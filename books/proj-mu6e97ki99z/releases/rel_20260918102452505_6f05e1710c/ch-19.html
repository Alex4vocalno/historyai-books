Chapter 19

The Cold Light on Collapsible B

Seen from above, the night wore on and the lifeboats drifted; across the water the survivors waited as, on the horizon, the lights of the Carpathia grew.

In Boat 6, twenty-eight people sat in a craft built for sixty-five. The sea was calm. The oars rose and fell. Quartermaster Robert Hichens held the tiller and told the women not to row back toward the swimmers. There was no point, he said. They were only dead bodies out there now. The boat pulled away into the darkness, its passengers safe, its seats half empty, its crew unwilling to return.

Two miles astern, thirty men clung to an overturned lifeboat.

Collapsible B had floated off the roof of the officers’ quarters as the Titanic took her final plunge. The canvas-sided craft had capsized as it hit the water, trapping air beneath its hull. Now it floated upside down in the North Atlantic, a low dark shape almost flush with the surface. The men who had reached it stood kneel-deep in the sea, balanced on the upturned bottom. The water was twenty-eight degrees. The air was colder. And the sounds from the darkness around them were terrible.

The contrast was absolute. In the boats that had pulled away early, passengers argued about whether to return. On Collapsible B, men fought for a place on a hull that could not support them all.

Second Officer Charles Lightoller was the senior officer aboard the overturned boat. He had been sucked below the surface as the ship went down, held against a ventilation grille by the pressure of water pouring into the hull, then blown free by a burst of air from the boiler rooms. He swam for the collapsible. He pulled himself onto the overturned bottom. And he began to organize the men who were already there.

The time was approximately 2: 20 a. m. on 15 April 1912. The Titanic had just vanished beneath the surface. The ship’s lights were gone. The stern had slipped under. And now the ocean was filled with people.

The swimmers came from every direction. Some had jumped from the stern as it tilted upward. Some had been washed off the deck by what survivors would later describe as a giant wave that swept the ship when her angle increased rapidly around 2: 15 a. m. Some had been below decks and found their way out through hatches or windows. They swam toward Collapsible B because it was the only thing visible in the darkness. The overturned hull was low in the water. It offered nothing but a smooth, slippery surface to cling to.

Lightoller counted heads. There were about thirty men on the boat. The collapsible was designed to carry forty-seven people right-side up. Upside down, it could support perhaps half that number. The air trapped beneath the hull gave it buoyancy, but every man who climbed aboard compressed that air and lowered the freeboard. The water rose over their knees. It rose toward their waists.

He ordered the men to stand and distribute their weight along the gunwales.

The men had to spread themselves along the edges of the overturned hull. If they clustered in the center, the boat would sink beneath them. If they moved to the sides, they could use the gunwales—the rigid wooden edges of the hull—as a kind of balance beam. They stood with their feet on the canvas bottom, the water sloshing around their thighs, their hands free to pull others from the sea.

Harold Bride, the junior wireless operator, was among them. He had come up from the wireless room after the ship’s angle increased, had helped launch Collapsible B from the roof of the officers’ quarters, and had swum for it when the wave washed over the deck. He reached the overturned boat and was pulled aboard. He would later testify that the collapsible was swamped, with the men standing in water up to their waists.

The cold was the immediate enemy. Water conducts heat away from the body roughly twenty-five times faster than air. In water at twenty-eight degrees Fahrenheit—four degrees below freezing, because the salt content lowers the freezing point—a human being loses consciousness in fifteen minutes or less. Death follows shortly after. The men who swam toward Collapsible B were already feeling the effects: numbness in their extremities, confusion, a growing lethargy that made it harder to keep swimming.

Lightoller knew the mathematics of hypothermia, not from medical training but from years at sea. He had seen men pulled from cold water. He knew that the swimmers had minutes, not hours. And he knew that the collapsible could not take them all.

He called out for other officers. None answered. He asked for seamen. A few voices responded.

The men identified themselves as best they could in the darkness. Lightoller began to organize them. He needed them to work in shifts, pulling swimmers from the water while others maintained balance on the hull. The boat was drifting. The current was carrying them away from the wreckage site. Every minute put more distance between them and the people in the water.

The cries were deafening. Hundreds of voices called out in the darkness. Some screamed for help. Some called for boats. Some prayed. The sound rose from the water like a chorus, a wall of noise that surrounded the overturned collapsible on every side.

Lightoller ordered his men to pull aboard as many swimmers as they could reach. Hands grasped at the canvas sides. Men tried to climb onto the hull. The boat tilted. The water rose. Those already aboard had to lean backward to compensate, shifting their weight in a desperate calculus of balance.

They pulled men aboard until the hull was so low that the water reached their chests. Then Lightoller made a decision. He told the men to stop pulling. The boat could take no more. If they tried to add another person, the entire craft would sink.

The swimmers who reached the collapsible after that point were turned away. There was no room. The men aboard had no oars, no raft, no rope to offer. They could only watch as the swimmers drifted past, their voices growing weaker, their strokes slowing, until they stopped moving altogether.

The cries continued for perhaps forty minutes. Then they began to fade.

The cold killed the swimmers one by one. Hypothermia progresses through distinct stages. First comes shivering, the body’s attempt to generate heat through muscle activity. Then comes vasoconstriction, the narrowing of blood vessels in the extremities to preserve core temperature. Then comes confusion and disorientation, as the brain is deprived of oxygen and blood sugar drops. Then comes unconsciousness. Then comes death.

The swimmers in the water around Collapsible B passed through these stages in real time. Lightoller and the others heard it happen. The voices that had called for help grew quiet. The splashing of arms and legs grew still. The prayers trailed off into silence. By 3: 00 a. m., the ocean was almost quiet.

Almost.

One voice kept calling for boats to come back.

The voice belonged to a man who had found something floating—a door, a piece of wreckage, a deck chair—and was clinging to it in the darkness. He called out to the boats that had pulled away. He called out to the collapsible. He called out to anyone who could hear.

Lightoller and his men had no way to reach him. They had no oars. They had no paddles. They were adrift, at the mercy of the current, unable to steer or propel their overturned hull. They could only listen as the voice called out, over and over, into the empty air.

The voice grew hoarse. It grew faint. It called out less frequently. And then it stopped.

The silence that followed was worse than the cries had been.

On Collapsible B, the men shifted their weight and waited for dawn. The cold was still killing them, even aboard the boat. Their wet clothes sucked heat from their bodies. Their feet were numb. Their hands were stiff. They had to keep moving to stay awake, shifting from foot to foot, rocking the hull in a slow rhythm that kept the blood flowing.

Lightoller took charge of the physical organization. He ordered the men to form two lines, one on each side of the hull, facing outward. They linked arms to create a stable platform. They sang hymns to stay awake. They called out names to take roll. They did whatever was necessary to keep from falling asleep, because sleep in those temperatures meant death.

Bride later described the scene in his testimony. They stood on the boat in water up to their waists. They had to keep moving to keep their feet warm. The cold was terrible.

The collapsible drifted. The current carried it away from the site of the sinking. The other lifeboats were scattered across a wide area, some two miles away, some closer, some farther. The men on Collapsible B could not see them. They could see nothing but the stars overhead and the black water beneath their feet.

The question of why they were there—why they were clinging to an overturned hull in the middle of the North Atlantic rather than sitting safely in a proper lifeboat—would occupy the inquiries for weeks. But the immediate answer was simple. The proper lifeboats had left half-empty.

Boat 6 had room for sixty-five people. It carried twenty-eight. Boat 1, the emergency cutter, had capacity for forty. It carried twelve—seven crew and five passengers. Boat 8 carried twenty-seven people in a boat built for sixty-five. The pattern repeated across the fleet of lifeboats that pulled away from the Titanic’s side. Boats left with empty seats because no one had ordered them filled. Officers loaded women and children first, but did not call for more when the supply of women and children ran short. Crewmen lowered boats before they were full because they feared the davits would not hold the weight. Passengers declined to board because the ship seemed safer than a small craft suspended above the black ocean.

The result was a fleet of lifeboats with room for hundreds more than they carried. And those hundreds remained aboard the Titanic until she sank beneath them.

The men on Collapsible B were the ones who had not found seats in the lifeboats. They were crew—stokers, trimmers, stewards, firemen—who had been below decks when the evacuation began and had emerged to find the boats gone. They were officers who had stayed to launch the collapsibles. They were passengers who had been in the wrong place at the wrong time. They were, in short, the surplus population that the lifeboats had left behind.

Now they stood on an overturned hull and waited for rescue or death.

The mathematics of hypothermia had not finished with them. Even out of the water, they were losing body heat. The air temperature was below freezing. Their wet clothes acted as a heat sink, drawing warmth from their skin. Their extremities were going numb. Their core temperatures were dropping.

Lightoller knew that they could not last indefinitely. He had been in the water himself. He had felt the cold grip his limbs. He knew that dawn was still an hour away, perhaps more. They needed to survive until then.

He organized the men into a tighter formation. They would stand close together, arms linked, bodies touching, to share what warmth they had. They would shift weight in unison, rocking the boat to keep it stable. They would sing, call out, talk to each other—anything to stay awake.

He asked again for officers. Firemen answered. Stewards answered. Seamen answered.

They were mostly crew. The passengers who had reached the collapsible were few. Most of the men aboard had worked below decks, keeping the ship running, and had emerged into the chaos of the final plunge with no assigned place in the evacuation.

Bride was among them. He had been in the wireless room until the end, sending distress calls with Jack Phillips. He had left when the water reached the deck above. He had helped launch Collapsible B from the roof of the officers’ quarters. He had swum for it when the wave came. And now he stood on the overturned hull, his wireless training useless, his survival dependent on his ability to stay awake and keep his balance.

Phillips was not there. Bride had last seen him heading aft as they split up on the roof of the officers’ quarters. He did not know what had happened to the senior operator. Later, Bride would insist that a body pulled from the water and taken aboard Boat 12 was not Phillips. He would discuss it with Lightoller. The Second Officer would agree with him. But for now, in the darkness on the overturned hull, he did not know.

The hours passed slowly. The men sang. They prayed. They called out to each other to stay awake. They shifted their weight in unison, a strange dance performed on a capsized hull in the middle of the Atlantic. They waited.

At some point, the weight became too much for one man. He slipped. He fell into the water. The others reached for him, but the darkness swallowed him before they could grab his hand. He was gone.

Another man died where he stood. The cold stopped his heart. His body remained upright, held in place by the arms linked on either side, until the men realized he was no longer moving. They lowered him into the water as gently as they could. There was nothing else to do.

The collapsible grew lighter as men died. The water level around their legs dropped. The hull rose slightly in the water. The survivors had more room to stand. But each death meant fewer arms to link, fewer voices to call out, fewer bodies to share warmth.

Lightoller counted heads again. They had started with about thirty. He did not know how many were left.

The sky began to lighten around 4: 00 a. m. The stars faded. The horizon turned gray. The men on Collapsible B could see each other for the first time—their wet clothes, their pale faces, their hands gripping the gunwales.

And then they saw something else.

A light on the horizon.

Titanic’s survivors were rescued around 04: 00 on 15 April by the RMS Carpathia, which had steamed through the night at high speed and at considerable risk, as the ship had to dodge numerous icebergs en route. Her lights were visible from miles away. She was coming toward them.

Carpathia’s lights were first spotted around 03: 30, which greatly cheered the survivors in the lifeboats, though it took several more hours for everyone to be brought aboard.

The men on the collapsible called out. They waved their arms. They tried to make themselves visible against the dark water. But the overturned hull was low and hard to see. They had no flares, no lights, no way to signal.

They watched the Carpathia approach. They watched her stop. They watched her begin to pick up survivors from the lifeboats that had reached her first. They watched and waited, balanced on their overturned hull, as the rescue operation unfolded a mile away. The Carpathia had arrived on the scene shortly after 04: 00.

Captain Arthur Rostron had pushed his vessel to her maximum speed, ordering extra lookouts and preparing his ship for survivors. He had no way of knowing that some of those survivors were clinging to an overturned lifeboat, drifting in the current, unable to signal their position.

The men on Collapsible B watched the rescue happen. They saw the lifeboats row toward the Carpathia. They saw survivors climb aboard. They saw the ship’s lights, the activity on her decks, the hope that seemed so close and yet so far.

They were drifting away from the rescue. The current was carrying them in the wrong direction. The Carpathia was stationary, picking up boats as they arrived. The collapsible was not arriving. It was moving away.

Lightoller looked at the light on the horizon. He looked at the men around him. He did the calculation. They could not row toward the Carpathia. They had no oars. They could not paddle with their hands—the water was too cold, their arms too weak. They could only wait and hope that someone on the rescue ship would see them.

The dawn grew brighter. The Carpathia grew more distinct. And the men on Collapsible B kept their balance and watched.

The disaster’s most lethal phase was not the sinking itself but the protracted exposure that followed. The ship had taken two hours and forty minutes to sink. The survivors in the water had minutes. The men on Collapsible B had hours, but those hours were measured in degrees of body heat and will. The lifeboats that had pulled away half-empty had condemned hundreds to this: not a quick death in the plunge, but a slow death in the cold.

The inquiries would later seek to quantify and explain this cost. They would count the dead—1, 496 souls lost, 712 saved. They would examine the lifeboat regulations, the Board of Trade’s outdated tonnage rules, the lack of boat drills, the confusion on deck. They would hear testimony from Lightoller, from Bride, from the men who had survived on the overturned hull. They would ask why the boats left half-full. They would ask why the Californian, visible on the horizon, did not come.

But for now, in the gray predawn light of 15 April 1912, those questions lay in the future. The men on Collapsible B had no interest in inquiries or regulations. They had only the light on the horizon, the cold in their bones, and the desperate need to stay upright until someone saw them.

The first distant lights were sighted by those clinging to Collapsible B, creating a new tension between hope and immediate peril.
