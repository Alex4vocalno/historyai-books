Chapter 20

Shifting the Primary Vantage Point

The men on Collapsible B clung to their overturned hull in a silence broken only by the lapping of water against the canvas bottom. Around them, scattered across two and a half miles of black ocean, eighteen lifeboats drifted or pulled at their oars—each one a separate world, each one making its own reckoning with the night. Some carried officers who knew where to steer. Some carried no one who could handle a tiller. Some carried twelve people in a boat built for forty, while others wallowed low in the water, overloaded and leaking, their gunwales inches above the surface.

The Titanic had launched her boats without a plan for what came next. Now the consequences of that omission played out in miniature across the dark Atlantic.

—-

Boat 1 carried twelve people. Sir Cosmo and Lady Duff-Gordon sat in the stern beside seven crewmen and a single additional passenger. The boat had been built for forty. It had room for thirty more. A few hundred yards away, Boat 14 wallowed under the weight of sixty or more women and children, water slopping over the gunwales with every roll, Fifth Officer Harold Lowe standing at the tiller trying to keep order. Two boats, two realities, both adrift in the same black sea.

The Board of Trade had required the Titanic to carry lifeboats. It had not required that anyone know how to use them. The regulations said nothing about where the boats should go after they left the ship, how they should stay together, or who should take command of the fleet. The result was visible now: a scattered archipelago of wooden hulls, each one making its own decisions, each one following whatever leadership it had managed to find.

—-

Fourth Officer Joseph Boxhall had fired the distress rockets from the Titanic’s bridge. He had watched the lights of a ship on the horizon and tried to signal her. Now those lights were gone, and Boxhall had a different problem. Boat 2 carried twenty people, mostly women, in a craft rated for forty. He had a sail and a lantern, and he had a compass. He also had a clear sense of what he should do.

Boxhall set the lantern burning and steered toward where he thought the Carpathia would come. The wireless messages had given him a position. The Cunarder was making steam toward the Titanic’s last known coordinates, and Boxhall intended to meet her halfway. He ordered the women to row—not because they needed the exercise, but because he needed their warmth. The work kept blood moving, kept hypothermia at bay a little longer. He rationed the sail. He watched the horizon.

In Boat 6, Quartermaster Robert Hichens had taken the tiller after the boat left the ship. He had been at the wheel when the iceberg struck. Now he had twenty-four people in a boat built for sixty-five, and he had no intention of going back. The cries from the water had started within minutes of the Titanic’s disappearance. Hichens heard them. So did the women at the oars.

“We should go back,” said one of the passengers. Margaret Brown, later famous as the “Unsinkable Molly Brown,” had found a place in the boat and had not lost her voice. She argued that they had room, that they could pull swimmers from the water, that it was the right thing to do.

Hichens refused. He told them the boat would be swamped, that swimmers would climb the sides and capsize them, that they would all drown if they tried. He spoke with the authority of a seaman, and he spoke from fear. The women at the oars looked at each other. Some stopped rowing. The boat drifted. The cries in the water grew fainter, then stopped.

Brown did not command the boat. Hichens did. The social order that had governed the Titanic’s deck—officers first, crew second, passengers last—had transferred itself into the lifeboat. Brown could argue, but she could not overrule. The boat stayed where it was, and the people in the water drowned within earshot.

—-

Fifth Officer Lowe had loaded Boat 14 with women and children, sixty or more in a craft meant for sixty-five, and he had fired his revolver to keep men from rushing the gunwales. Now he found himself responsible for an overloaded boat taking on water through the seams. The wooden hull had not been in the water for years. The planks had dried out. Now they were swelling, but not fast enough. Water seeped up between the boards, pooling at the bottom, soaking the feet of the passengers.

Lowe ordered the women to bail. They used their hats, their handkerchiefs, anything that could hold water and throw it over the side. He organized the rowing, setting a rhythm, keeping the boat moving toward where he thought rescue would come. He was twenty-eight years old, the youngest of the Titanic’s officers, and he had no intention of dying in a leaking lifeboat.

But he also had no intention of going back. Not yet. The cries from the water had reached him, and he had made the same calculation as Hichens: a boat this full could not take on swimmers without capsizing. He would wait until the noise stopped, until the dead and the dying had gone quiet, and then he would go back for whoever was left.

The calculation was practical. It was also a death sentence for the people treading water in the cold. Lowe knew this. He made the decision anyway.

—-

In Boat 1, the debate had taken a different form. The boat carried Sir Cosmo Duff-Gordon, his wife Lucy, and seven crewmen. It had been one of the first to leave the ship, lowered from the starboard side while the deck was still relatively calm. The crewmen rowed, but they did not row hard. They had no officer in the boat. They had no clear destination.

Someone mentioned the lights on the horizon—lights that had been visible earlier, lights that might have been a ship. The crewmen discussed rowing toward them. Someone else mentioned going back for swimmers. The discussion went nowhere. The boat drifted.

Lady Duff-Gordon later testified that she had heard the cries from the water and had wanted to go back. She also said that the crewmen had refused, arguing that the boat would be swamped. Sir Cosmo said nothing. He was a passenger in a boat that seemed to bear his name. He did not command. He did not argue. He sat in the stern and waited for someone else to decide.

Later, accusations would swirl around Boat 1. The crewmen would be accused of refusing to return because they feared for their own lives. Sir Cosmo would be accused of bribing them to stay away—five pounds each, a story that grew in the telling into something larger and more sinister. The British inquiry would examine the charge and find no evidence of bribery. But the fact remained: a boat with room for forty had carried twelve people to safety while hundreds died within reach.

—-

The lifeboat fleet had no coordination because the Titanic had no protocol. The ship’s officers had been given lifeboats and told to lower them. They had not been told what to do next. The Board of Trade required lifeboats. It did not require lifeboat drills. It did not require that officers know how many people each boat could hold, or how to row, or where to gather after the ship went down.

The result was visible now, in the scattered boats pulling in different directions across the dark sea. Boat 4, under Quartermaster Walter Perkis, had picked up swimmers from the water after the Titanic sank—eight men pulled from the freezing ocean, two of whom died before morning. Boat 14, under Lowe, eventually returned as well, transferring passengers to other boats and going back to search. But most boats did not go back. The decision was left to whoever happened to be in charge, and most of the people in charge decided that their responsibility was to the living, not to the dying.

The passengers had no say. The women who wanted to return, who begged to turn back, who offered to man the oars themselves—they were overruled by seamen who invoked the authority of experience and the logic of self-preservation. The social order that had put officers in command of the ship’s boats had also silenced the passengers in them.

—-

By 3: 00 a. m., the cold had become the central fact of existence. The air temperature hovered just above freezing. The water temperature was below twenty-eight degrees—cold enough to kill a swimmer in minutes, cold enough to creep into the boats through wet clothing and splashing oars. The passengers who had escaped in nightgowns and evening dress now huddled under blankets, pressed against each other for warmth, their teeth chattering too hard to speak.

In Boat 6, Hichens had begun to crack. The quartermaster who had been at the wheel when the iceberg struck now sat at the tiller of a lifeboat, responsible for twenty-four lives, and he had no idea where they were or where they were going. The lights on the horizon had disappeared. The stars wheeled overhead in their ancient patterns, useless for navigation to anyone who did not know how to read them. Hichens told the women to stop rowing. He told them to save their strength. He told them they might be out there for days.

Margaret Brown refused to listen. She had found a pair of oars and she meant to use them. She argued with Hichens, challenged his authority, insisted that they keep moving. Other women joined her. The boat moved, but it moved in circles, pulling first one way and then another, the passengers and the quartermaster fighting for control.

In Boat 8, the situation was calmer but no less desperate. Ida Straus had refused to leave her husband, and now Isidor Straus’s body was somewhere in the Atlantic, while his wife had chosen to die with him rather than take a place in a lifeboat. The boat that might have carried them pulled away under the command of Able Seaman Thomas Jones, who had been ordered by Captain Smith himself to make for the lights and come back.

Jones meant to follow orders. He set the women to rowing and steered toward where the lights had been. But the lights had vanished, and the sea was empty, and there was nothing to row toward. He kept the women working, not because he had a destination, but because the work kept them warm, and warmth was the only currency that mattered now.

—-

At 3: 30 a. m., a light appeared on the horizon. It was a green light, low in the water, moving toward them. The Carpathia had made the run at her maximum speed of 17 knots, pushing her engines to their limits, threading through the same ice field that had trapped the Titanic. She had reached the edge of the ice field by 2: 45 a. m., and for the next two hours dodged icebergs as small growlers of ice ground along her hull plates. Now Captain Arthur Rostron’s ship approached the lifeboats.

The green light, low in the water, moved toward them. The word passed from boat to boat, shouted across the dark distances between them. Someone was coming. A ship had arrived. The green light meant rescue.

The Carpathia had made the run at seventeen knots, pushing her engines to their limits, threading through the same ice field that had trapped the Titanic. Captain Arthur Rostron had ordered every preparation: hot soup, blankets, medical supplies, crew standing by at the gangways. Now his ship approached the lifeboats, and the lifeboats began to row toward her.

But the rowing was slow. The passengers were exhausted. The crewmen who had been rowing for hours had little left to give. The boats that had drifted apart now converged on a single point, but they converged at different speeds, and the Carpathia could not take them all at once.

—-

Boat 2 reached the ship first. Boxhall had kept his lantern burning, had steered toward the rescue ship, had brought his passengers in with purpose. The Carpathia’s crew lowered ropes and hauled the survivors aboard. Boxhall stepped onto the deck and reported to Rostron. He was the first officer from the Titanic to do so.

The other boats followed, but they followed slowly. Boat 6 had drifted far from the rescue ship, and Hichens had given up. Brown and the other women took the oars and rowed toward the green light themselves. They pulled alongside the Carpathia at last, and Hichens helped tie off the boat, but he had lost whatever authority the quartermaster’s rank had given him. The women had saved themselves.

Boat 14 arrived with Lowe still in command. He had transferred passengers to other boats and returned to the scene of the sinking, pulling four men from the water—men who had found something to hold onto, a door or a piece of wreckage, and who had survived long enough for Lowe to reach them. One of them died before the boat reached the Carpathia. The other three lived.

The process took hours. The Carpathia’s crew worked systematically, bringing each boat alongside, lowering ropes, hauling survivors aboard. The injured and the hypothermic went straight to the ship’s hospital. The able-bodied went to the dining saloon, where hot soup waited. The dead were taken below, their bodies tagged and stored.

—-

By 5: 45 a. m., most of the boats had been recovered. Collapsible B, the overturned hull that had supported thirty men through the night, had been spotted and its survivors transferred to other boats. Collapsible A, half-swamped and drifting, still held three living passengers and one dead body when it was found. The dead man was buried at sea. The living were taken aboard.

At 8: 30 a. m., the Carpathia lifted her last lifeboat from the water. The rescue was complete.

The fleet that had left the Titanic had been a collection of individual decisions, uncoordinated and often contradictory. Some boats had gone back. Most had not. Some had rowed toward rescue. Some had drifted. Some had been commanded by officers who knew what they were doing. Some had been commanded by crewmen who panicked, or by passengers who had no authority at all.

The inquiries would later ask why the boats had left half-full. They would ask why no one had organized the fleet, why no one had taken command of the rescue operation from the water. They would ask why the Board of Trade had never required lifeboat drills, why the regulations had never addressed what happened after the boats were lowered.

The survivors had no answers. They had only their own experience: a night in an open boat, the cold and the dark, the cries from the water and the silence that followed, and finally the green light on the horizon, the ropes coming down, the warmth of a ship’s deck under their feet.

—-

The Carpathia turned toward New York. The lifeboats that had carried the survivors through the night were lashed to the deck, evidence now, not rescue craft. They would be examined, counted, measured. Their capacities would be calculated against the number of people who had died. The mathematics of survival would become the mathematics of blame.

Now those numbers would travel back to New York, and from New York to Washington and London, where the inquiries would ask what they meant.

—-

The rescue ship, arriving at 08: 30, had just finished picking up the last of Titanic’s survivors. After communicating with the Californian, Carpathia left the area, leaving Californian to search for any other survivors. Californian, however, only found scattered wreckage and the silence of an empty sea.

The lifeboats that had carried the survivors through the night now rested on the Carpathia’s deck, their wooden hulls dried by the morning sun, their capacities visible to anyone who cared to count. The empty seats told their own story. So did the overcrowded boats that had nearly sunk under the weight of too many passengers. The evidence of the evacuation’s chaos was now preserved in wood and canvas, waiting to be measured.

The inquiries would examine that evidence. They would call the officers and the crewmen, the passengers and the wireless operators. They would ask about the boats that left half-full and the boats that returned for swimmers. They would ask about the lights on the horizon and the rockets that had been fired. They would ask why the Titanic had sped through an ice field at twenty-two knots, why the wireless warnings had gone unheeded, why the Board of Trade had regulated lifeboats by tonnage instead of by souls.

But first, the survivors had to reach land. The Carpathia had seven hundred and five people on her decks, and she had three days of sailing ahead. The inquiries would have to wait.
