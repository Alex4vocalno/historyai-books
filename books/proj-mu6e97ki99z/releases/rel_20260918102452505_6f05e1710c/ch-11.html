Chapter 11

The Californian’s Silent Deck (15 April, 00:00–02:20)

The first lifeboat pulled away from the Titanic’s side with fewer than thirty people aboard. The pattern had been set. Each subsequent launch would repeat it.

On the bridge of the SS Californian, Third Officer Charles Groves stood alone. The hour was just after midnight on 15 April 1912. The ship had stopped for the night, her engines silent, surrounded by field ice that stretched in every direction. Groves had relieved Captain Stanley Lord at 11: 10 p. m., taking the watch while the master retired to the chartroom below. The night was clear and cold, the sea calm, the horizon sharp where the stars met the water. Somewhere to the south, a steamer’s lights caught his attention.

Groves studied the approaching vessel through binoculars. He could see her port light and her masthead light, green and white against the darkness. She appeared to be a passenger liner, moving through the ice field with more confidence than prudence. He estimated her distance at five or six miles, perhaps less. She was close enough that he could see her lights clearly, close enough that he expected her to alter course when she sighted the Californian. But she kept coming.

At 12: 10 a. m., Groves called down the speaking tube to Captain Lord in the chartroom. He reported the steamer approaching from the east. Lord asked if she was close. Groves said she was. Lord told him to call the wireless operator if the vessel got close enough to be a concern. Groves returned to the bridge and watched.

The steamer stopped. She had come within three or four miles of the Californian, according to Groves’s estimate, and now her lights hung motionless on the horizon. She had stopped for the ice, he thought, just as they had. A reasonable assumption. A prudent decision. He did not call the wireless operator. There was no need. The steamer was stopped, the danger had passed, and the night resumed its quiet.

What Groves could not know was that the steamer he watched was the Titanic. She had stopped not by choice but by catastrophe. She was sinking.

The Californian’s wireless operator, Cyril Evans, had gone off duty at 11: 30 p. m. He had tried earlier that evening to warn the Titanic of the ice field ahead. Evans sent a message to the White Star liner reporting that the Californian was stopped and surrounded by ice. The Titanic’s wireless operator, Jack Phillips, cut him off sharply. Phillips was working to clear a backlog of passenger messages through the Cape Race station and found Evans’s interruption irritating. Evans heard the irritation and closed his set. He went to bed.

The wireless cabin was silent now. Evans slept in his bunk, the headphones hanging on their hook, the receiver cold. The Californian carried only one wireless operator. There was no one to maintain a night watch.

On the bridge, Groves continued his observations. At about 12: 15 a. m., he saw the steamer’s lights change. Her green port light disappeared, replaced by her red starboard light. She was turning, he thought, or perhaps swinging at anchor. He did not know what to make of it. He called Lord again at 12: 25 a. m. And reported that the steamer now showed five lights, including two masthead lights and her red sidelight. Lord asked if there were any deck lights visible. Groves said no. Lord suggested the vessel might be a tramp steamer. Groves disagreed. He thought she was a passenger ship.

The disagreement was never resolved. Groves returned to his watch, and Lord remained in the chartroom, half-awake, half-asleep, waiting for morning.

What happened next would become one of the most contested sequences in the entire disaster. At about 12: 45 a. m., Groves saw a white light burst above the steamer’s masthead. A rocket. Then another. He counted them as they rose and fell, white stars against the black sky. He did not know what they meant. He called Lord again.

The captain’s response was casual. Lord asked if the rockets were white. Groves said yes. Lord said they might be company signals. Company signals were private rockets used by shipping lines to identify their vessels. They were not distress signals. There was no international standard for what constituted a distress rocket. The Board of Trade required ships to carry rockets for signaling, but the rules did not specify what colors or patterns indicated an emergency. A white rocket could be a distress signal. It could also be a company signal. It could be anything.

Groves accepted the explanation. He returned to the bridge and continued to watch. The rockets kept coming. He counted seven or eight over the next hour, fired at irregular intervals from the steamer’s deck. Each one arced upward, burst into white light, and faded. Each one was visible from the Californian’s bridge. Each one was seen, reported, and dismissed.

At 1: 15 a. m., Second Officer Herbert Stone relieved Groves on the bridge. Groves mentioned the steamer and the rockets before going below. Stone took up the watch. He too saw the rockets. He counted them, recorded them, and wondered about them. At about 1: 40 a. m., he called Lord on the speaking tube and reported that the steamer had fired five rockets. Lord asked what color they were. Stone said white. Lord asked if he was sure. Stone said yes. Lord said nothing more. He did not come to the bridge. He did not order the wireless operator awakened. He did not attempt to signal the mystery ship.

Stone returned to his watch. The apprentice officer, James Gibson, joined him on the bridge. Together they watched the steamer and her rockets. Gibson counted eight white rockets between 1: 40 and 2: 00 a. m. He saw the steamer’s lights begin to move, or seem to move, as if she were steaming away. He reported this to Stone. Stone called Lord again at about 2: 00 a. m. And told him the ship appeared to be leaving, firing rockets as she went. Lord asked if there were any colors in the rockets. Stone said no, they were all white. Lord said nothing. He did not ask why a ship would fire rockets while steaming away. He did not ask why a ship would fire eight rockets in the middle of the night. He did not ask anything at all.

At 2: 20 a. m., the Titanic sank. The Californian was between ten and nineteen miles away, depending on whose estimate of the positions one accepted. The rockets had stopped. The lights had vanished. The sea was dark and silent.

Stone and Gibson continued their watch. They saw nothing more. At 4: 00 a. m., Chief Officer George Stewart relieved Stone. Stewart noticed the absence of the steamer and asked what had happened. Stone told him about the rockets. Stewart went below and woke Captain Lord. Lord came to the bridge and asked what was wrong. Stewart told him the ship they had been watching was gone, and that she had been firing rockets during the night. Lord asked what kind of rockets. Stewart said white rockets. Lord asked if there were any colors. Stewart said no.

At 4: 30 a. m., Lord went to the wireless room and woke Evans. He told the operator to find out what had happened to the ship they had seen during the night. Evans switched on his set and began to listen. Within minutes, he heard the news. The Titanic had struck an iceberg and sunk. The Carpathia was on the scene, picking up survivors. The Californian was less than twenty miles away.

Evans reported the news to Lord. The captain stood on the bridge and processed what he had heard. The ship they had watched, the ship that had fired rockets, the ship they had dismissed as a tramp steamer signaling her company colors—that ship was the Titanic. She had been sinking while they watched. She had been firing distress rockets while they debated. She had been calling for help while their wireless operator slept.

Lord ordered the Californian under way. The crew cut the engines and began to work the ship through the ice field toward the Titanic’s last reported position. They arrived at 7: 30 a. m., after the Carpathia had already picked up the survivors. There was nothing left but wreckage and bodies. The Californian searched the area for an hour, then turned toward Boston.

The ship arrived in Boston on 18 April 1912, the same day the Carpathia docked in New York with the survivors. No one paid much attention to her arrival. The world’s attention was fixed on the Titanic, on the survivors, on the dead. The Californian’s role in the disaster was not yet known.

That changed on 22 April. The United States Senate inquiry, led by Senator William Alden Smith, had opened in New York on 19 April. Smith was a relentless questioner. He wanted to know everything about the disaster, and he wanted to know it immediately. On 22 April, his inquiry discovered that a ship had been seen near the Titanic during the sinking. Witnesses from the Titanic described a vessel whose lights were visible on the horizon, a vessel they had hoped would come to their rescue. Smith demanded to know what ship it was.

The inquiry subpoenaed Captain Lord and his officers. They testified in Washington, D.C., beginning on 26 April. Lord’s testimony was defensive and evasive. He admitted that his ship had been stopped within sight of the Titanic. He admitted that rockets had been seen and reported to him. He admitted that he had not acted. But he denied that he had known the Titanic was in distress. He said he thought the rockets were company signals. He said he thought the ship was a tramp steamer. He said he had no reason to believe there was an emergency.

The officers’ testimony was inconsistent. Groves said the steamer had been a passenger liner. Lord said he thought it was a small tramp. Stone said the rockets had been white. Lord said he had asked about colors. Gibson said he had reported eight rockets. Lord said he could not recall how many. The discrepancies piled up. Smith pressed Lord on each point. Why had he not gone to the bridge to see for himself? Why had he not ordered the wireless operator awakened? Why had he not attempted to signal the ship? Lord had no good answers. He said he had seen no need. He said he had relied on his officers’ reports. He said he had done what any reasonable captain would have done.

The British Wreck Commissioner’s inquiry, led by Lord Mersey, opened in London on 2 May. Lord testified again. His testimony was again inconsistent, sometimes contradictory. The British inquiry examined the Californian’s logbooks, the officers’ statements, the wireless records. They heard from Titanic survivors who had seen the lights of a nearby ship. They heard from the Californian’s crew about the rockets and the failure to respond.

Mersey’s conclusion was scathing. He found that the Californian had been closer to the Titanic than Lord had admitted—between five and ten miles, not nineteen. He found that the rockets seen from the Californian’s bridge were distress signals from the Titanic. He found that Captain Lord had failed to take action that might have saved lives. The commissioner wrote that the Californian could have pushed through the ice to the open water without serious risk and so have come to the assistance of the Titanic. Had she done so she might have saved many if not all of the lives that were lost.

Lord never accepted the verdict. He spent the rest of his life trying to clear his name. He petitioned for a reopening of the inquiry. He gathered affidavits and statements. He argued that the ship seen from the Californian was not the Titanic but another vessel, a sealer or a tramp. He claimed the rockets were not distress signals but some other kind of signal. He maintained his innocence until his death in 1962.

The controversy never died. In the decades that followed, historians and researchers revisited the evidence. Some defended Lord, arguing that the Californian had been farther away than Mersey concluded, that the rockets had been ambiguous, that the failure to respond was understandable given the circumstances. Others condemned him, pointing to the consistent testimony of his own officers, the clear sight of the rockets, the obvious need to investigate. The debate continues to this day.

But the facts of that night are not really in dispute. The Californian was stopped in the ice within sight of the Titanic. Her officers saw rockets. They reported them to the captain. The captain did not go to the bridge. He did not wake the wireless operator. He did not attempt to signal the ship. He did nothing. The rockets continued. The Titanic sank. More than 1, 500 people died.

The question is why.

The answer lies not in deliberate negligence but in a cascade of small failures, each one understandable in isolation, each one compounding the others. The Board of Trade had no clear standard for distress rockets. The Californian carried only one wireless operator, who could not maintain a night watch. The captain had been awake since early morning and was exhausted. The officers were uncertain about what they were seeing. The rockets were white, not colored. The steamer’s lights were ambiguous. The night was quiet and cold, and nothing seemed urgent.

The confidence cascade had reached its purest form. The Californian’s crew had been trained to follow procedures, and the procedures said nothing about white rockets fired from an unknown vessel at midnight. The regulations required no specific response. The wireless operator went off duty at 11: 30 p. m. because that was his schedule. The captain stayed in the chartroom because no rule required him to be on the bridge. The officers reported what they saw, and the captain interpreted their reports through the lens of his own assumptions. Each decision followed from the one before. Each one seemed reasonable at the time.

The result was paralysis. The Californian’s deck remained silent while the Titanic’s crisis entered its final phase. The wireless operator slept while Jack Phillips sent CQD into the void. The captain rested while the rockets rose and fell. The officers watched while the lifeboats lowered and the ship went down.

No malice drove the failure. It was a failure of imagination, of training, of regulation. The Californian’s crew did not know what they were seeing because no one had taught them to recognize it. They did not act because no one had told them to act. They followed the procedures they had been given, and the procedures were inadequate.

The United States Senate inquiry and the British Wreck Commissioner’s inquiry would eventually rewrite those procedures. The investigations led to new regulations requiring ships to maintain a continuous wireless watch, to carry enough lifeboats for all passengers and crew, to respond to any distress signal without hesitation. The lessons were learned. The rules were changed. But for the 1, 500 people who died that night, the lessons came too late.

The Californian’s officers would spend the rest of their lives explaining what they had seen and what they had not done. Groves testified that he had believed the steamer was a passenger liner, that he had reported the rockets, that he had done what he could. Stone testified that he had seen the rockets, that he had reported them to the captain, that he had followed orders. Gibson testified that he had counted eight white rockets, that he had watched the steamer disappear, that he had told the captain what he saw. Lord testified that he had asked about the rockets, that he had been told they were white, that he had thought they were company signals.

None of it changed what had happened. The rockets had been seen. The wireless had been silent. The captain had stayed in the chartroom. The ship had sunk.

The Californian’s wireless operator remains asleep, its deck silent as the Titanic’s crisis enters its final phase.
