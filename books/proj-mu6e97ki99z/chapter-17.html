Chapter 17

The Last Boat Away (15 April, 02:05–02:18)

Seen from above, across the whole map of that night, inquiries would afterward dissect every decision made. But on the sloping boat deck, in the final minutes before the end, there was no time for decisions: there was only the last boat and the next wave and the water climbing the rails.

Two facts stood in opposition at 02: 05. The first was the rule: women and children first, the ancient protocol of the sea, enforced by officers with loaded revolvers. The second was the reality: Collapsible D, the last lifeboat to leave the Titanic properly, descended with only 44 people aboard out of a capacity of 47, while men with guns held back a crowd that had nowhere else to go. The gap between rule and reality was not a failure of will. It was the physical exhaustion of the evacuation itself: the women were gone from the decks, the seamen were scattered in boats already lowered, and the time remaining could be counted in single digits. The last boat to be launched was collapsible D, which left at 02: 05 with 25 people aboard; two more men jumped on the boat as it was being lowered.

Second Officer Charles Lightoller had supervised the lowering of lifeboats from the port side since shortly after midnight. He had put fourteen boats into the water. Now he stood at Collapsible D, one of the wooden-hulled boats with canvas sides that could be stored flat and erected in an emergency. The boat hung from the davits, ready to lower. The deck around it was crowded. But the crowd was mostly men.

Lightoller called for women. He had shouted the same thing at boats 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12. But this time, few women came forward. They had already gone. Some had climbed through the windows of the promenade deck to reach earlier boats. Some had been lowered from the boat deck while their husbands stayed behind. Some had refused to leave and disappeared into the ship. Lightoller later told the British inquiry that he had walked along the deck calling for women before D was lowered. He found none within earshot.

The boat held twenty-five people when it began its descent. That was the figure Lightoller gave in testimony. Other witnesses offered different numbers, but the British inquiry accepted the officer’s count. As D dropped toward the water, two men jumped from the deck and landed in the boat. They were not challenged. The fall was short, the distance to the water slight, and the moment too confused for anyone to enforce protocol. The boat pulled away from the ship with roughly forty-four people aboard. It had room for three more.

The inquiries would later ask why D left half-empty. The question assumed that the empty seats reflected a failure of organization or nerve. But the evidence pointed elsewhere. By 02: 05, the Titanic’s stern had risen until the propellers were visible above the waterline. The deck sloped at an angle that made walking difficult. Most of the women who had been on the boat deck were already in boats. Those who remained were either unwilling to leave or unable to reach the davits. Lightoller had sent boat after boat away with empty seats because he could not find enough women to fill them. The shortage was not of boats. It was of people who could board them in time.

The problem of manpower ran deeper than the shortage of passengers. Each boat needed seamen to row and steer. The Titanic had carried roughly 900 crew members. Most were stewards, firemen, and engineers—men who knew the ship’s interior but not the handling of small boats in open water. The deck crew, the able seamen and ordinary seamen who could lower and row, numbered fewer than sixty. Many had already been dispatched in earlier boats. Fifth Officer Harold Lowe had taken a crew of seamen in boat 14. Quartermaster Robert Hichens had taken the wheel of boat 6. Lookouts Frederick Fleet and Reginald Lee had gone in boats 6 and 13. By the time D was ready, Lightoller struggled to find enough trained hands to man the falls.

He found two. Quartermasters Arthur Bright and Walter Perks helped lower D from the davits. They were among the last able seamen remaining on the ship. When the boat reached the water, they jumped down to man the oars. Lightoller remained on deck. He had already decided, he later testified, that he would not leave the ship. He would stay until the last boat was clear and then, if possible, save himself. The decision was not heroism. It was the logic of his position. An officer’s duty ended when there was nothing left to command.

But there was still one more boat.

Collapsible D had been stored beneath one of the standard lifeboats, ready for emergency use. But Collapsible B remained on the roof of the officers’ quarters, lashed in place. The roof was nearly forty feet above the boat deck. The angle of the ship made the climb treacherous. But the boats were the last remaining.

Lightoller organized a small party to retrieve Collapsible B. The boat was an Englehardt collapsible—canvas sides stretched over a wooden frame, with a flat bottom that could float even if capsized. Lightoller had sent men to the roof earlier to free the remaining collapsibles. They had struggled with the lashings and the oars stored beneath the boats. The work was slow. The ship was sinking faster.

By 02: 10, the party had managed to slide Collapsible B down from the roof onto the boat deck. The deck was now so steeply inclined that the boat threatened to slide into the water on its own. Lightoller and the men with him tried to attach it to the davits. But the davits were already occupied. The falls from boat 2, the last standard lifeboat lowered from the port side, still hung from the blocks. The men tried to hook Collapsible B to those falls. They worked quickly, or as quickly as the angle allowed.

The ship’s angle was now increasing with visible speed. Survivors described a sudden change at about 02: 15. The bow, which had been slowly settling, seemed to plunge. At about 02: 15, Titanic’s angle in the water began to increase rapidly as water poured into previously unflooded parts of the ship through deck hatches. Her suddenly increasing angle caused what one survivor called a “giant wave.” It was not a wave from the ocean outside. It was the Atlantic coming aboard.

The water reached Collapsible B before the boat could be properly secured. The rush of water lifted the boat from the deck and carried it away. The boat flipped upside-down as it went. The canvas sides collapsed. The wooden frame floated, but inverted, offering only a flat surface to cling to. Lightoller was caught in the wash. He went under the water, he later said, and came up beneath the overturned boat. He found an air pocket and pushed his head into it. He could hear others around him, also trapped, also breathing the last air in the hull.

The boat was not launched. It was washed away. The distinction mattered. Every previous boat had been lowered by the davits, controlled by the falls, supervised by an officer. Collapsible B left the ship by accident, carried off the deck by the inrush of water. The men who survived on it, Lightoller among them, had not been assigned places. They had simply been close enough to grab the hull when the wave came.

Harold Bride, the junior wireless operator, told the American inquiry that he had been near Collapsible B when the water reached it. He had been helping to free the boat from the roof. When the wave came, he was washed beneath the overturned hull. He survived by clinging to the bottom.

The boat floated. That was all it did. It could not be rowed. It could not be steered. It offered a platform of perhaps thirty square feet of inverted wood and canvas, slippery with water, crowded with men trying to pull themselves up. Lightoller organized the survivors into a line along the keel. He made them stand in two rows, facing outward, balancing the boat. When a wave threatened to swamp them, he ordered them to shift their weight. The boat held.

Collapsible A, on the starboard side, met a similar fate. It had been stored on the roof beside B. First Officer Murdoch had supervised its removal earlier. But like B, it was washed from the deck before it could be properly launched. Unlike B, it remained upright. It floated off with a few people aboard. Estimates varied, but the number was small. Most of those who reached A had been standing near it when the water came. They climbed in as the boat drifted away. The boat was later found by another lifeboat, with a few survivors and several bodies. The bodies were left in the boat, which was set adrift. It was recovered a month later by the White Star liner Oceanic. The remaining bodies were buried at sea.

The fate of the collapsibles illustrated what the earlier lifeboat launches had only suggested. The evacuation of the Titanic was not a single process with a single cause of failure. It was a sequence of improvisations, each constrained by time, by the ship’s angle, by the shortage of trained crew, and by the dispersal of passengers. The early boats had left with few passengers because no one believed the ship would sink. The middle boats had left half-full because the officers could not find enough women. The last boats had left, or been washed away, because there was no time left to launch them at all.

The regulations in force in 1912 measured lifeboat capacity by cubic tonnage, not by the number of souls aboard. The Board of Trade had last updated its requirements in 1894, when the largest passenger steamers were a quarter of the Titanic’s size. The ship met the requirement. She exceeded it. She still carried boats for fewer than half the people on board.

But even that inadequate capacity was not fully used. The boats that left the Titanic carried roughly 700 people. They could have carried 1, 178. The gap—nearly 500 empty seats—reflected not the shortage of boats but the shortage of time and the failures of execution. Women had refused to board. Men had been held back. Children had been separated from parents. Crew members had been sent away in boats to row them, leaving fewer hands for the next launch. Each decision had seemed reasonable at the moment. Each had contributed to the final count.

At 02: 15, the angle of the ship increased sharply. The bow plunged deeper. The stern rose higher. The water that had been washing the forward well deck now reached the bridge. Captain Smith was last seen there, by some accounts, though the testimony conflicted. He had released the wireless operators from duty at around 02: 10, telling them they could not do any more. He had given no final order. There was no final order to give. The ship was sinking. The boats were gone. The people left on deck would have to save themselves or not at all.

Thomas Andrews, the ship’s builder, was last seen in the first-class smoking room at approximately 02: 05. He had been there earlier, staring at a painting, not speaking. Witnesses who saw him at that hour said he made no attempt to leave. He had spent the night helping passengers into boats, directing them to the upper decks, explaining where the life jackets were stored. Now he sat. He had designed the ship. He knew how many compartments could flood and still float. He knew the ship was doomed. He did not move. Thomas Andrews was reportedly last seen in the first-class smoking room after approximately 02: 05, apparently making no attempt to escape.

The musicians had stopped playing. They had performed throughout the evacuation, first ragtime, then hymns. Survivors disagreed about the final tune. Some said “Nearer, My God, to Thee.” Others said “Autumn.” The disagreement did not matter. The music had ended. The men had set down their instruments. They stood on the deck, in their life jackets, with nothing to do.

The water reached the boat deck. It flowed along both sides of the ship, rising as it went. People who had been standing aft now moved toward the stern, trying to stay above the waterline. The stern itself rose higher, until the ship was nearly vertical. The lights still burned. The generators continued to run, powered by the steam from the boilers that had not yet been flooded. The light made the scene visible in detail—the people climbing the rails, the boats pulling away in the darkness, the water swallowing the decks.

At about 02: 18, the lights flickered and went out. The ship was now a black shape against the stars, visible only by the sounds it made—the groaning of metal, the shouts of people, the rush of water. Then the ship broke. Survivors described a sound like an explosion, or like a train wreck, or like nothing they had words for. The hull split between the third and fourth funnels. The forward section, already underwater, sank. The stern section settled back briefly, then rose again, perpendicular to the surface. It held for perhaps a minute. Then it slid beneath the water.

The time was approximately 02: 20. The Titanic had been afloat for two hours and forty minutes after striking the iceberg. In that time, eighteen boats had been launched or floated away. Two of the collapsibles had been washed off the deck. One of them, B, was upside-down. The other, A, was half-swamped. The remaining sixteen boats were scattered across a mile of ocean, rowing away from the wreck or standing off to wait.

The people left on the ship—fifteen hundred or more—were now in the water. The water was cold enough to kill in minutes. Most did not know how to swim. Most had no life jacket, or had put them on incorrectly. The life jackets were made of cork blocks sewn into canvas, designed to keep a person afloat, but not to protect against cold. In water near freezing, the body lost heat faster than it could generate it. Unconsciousness came in ten minutes. Death in fifteen.

The boats that had pulled away heard the cries. Fifth Officer Lowe, in boat 14, later testified that he had heard “a continuous wailing chant” from the direction of the wreck. He waited until the sounds faded before rowing back. He explained to the inquiry that he had feared being swamped by swimmers. Other boats gave similar reasons. They had no room. They had no crew. They were afraid. The reasons were not excuses. They were facts. The boats were small, the water was cold, and the people in it were desperate.

Lightoller, on the overturned Collapsible B, organized the men on the hull. He counted them. There were about thirty. The boat was designed for forty-seven, but inverted it could barely support the weight it carried. Every time a wave lifted the boat, water washed across the bottom. The men had to grip the sides and shift their weight to keep from sliding off. Lightoller gave orders. He had no authority left—no ship, no crew—but the men obeyed. They had no other choice.

Harold Bride was among them. He had been in the wireless room until the captain released him. He had helped Phillips send distress signals until the power failed. He had made his way to the roof of the officers’ quarters, where Collapsible B was stored. He had helped free the boat. When the wave came, he was washed beneath it. He found the air pocket. He heard others breathing in the dark. He pushed his way out and climbed onto the hull. He survived.

The boat drifted. The men on it sang, or talked, or stayed silent. Some prayed. Lightoller kept them in line, shifting their weight, calling out when a wave approached. They had no oars, no food, no water. They had the clothes they wore and the cold that seeped through the canvas. They could see the lights of the other boats, far away. They could not reach them.

The inquiries would later ask why Collapsible B had not been properly launched. The question assumed that proper launch was still possible. The evidence showed otherwise. The boat had been on the roof. The angle of the ship made access difficult. The davits were occupied. The time was too short. The wave that swept the boat away was not a failure of procedure. It was the physical collapse of the ship’s operational spaces, the point at which the vessel ceased to be a platform for action and became a hazard to those on it.

The same was true of Collapsible D. The last boat properly launched had left with empty seats because the people who could have filled them were not there. The women had been dispatched in earlier boats. The men had been held back. The seamen who could have rowed had been sent away. The deck was too crowded, too steep, too close to the water. The launch was orderly by comparison with what followed. But it was still a failure—a failure of capacity, of time, of organization.

The lifeboats of the Titanic have been remembered as a symbol of inadequate regulation. They were that. But they were also a symbol of something more uncomfortable. The regulations had failed because they measured the wrong thing: tonnage instead of people.

But even the inadequate boats provided were not fully used. The failure was not only of rules but of execution. The officers did not fill the boats because they could not find enough women, or because they did not trust the boats to hold more, or because they believed the ship would stay afloat long enough for rescue. The passengers did not board because they did not believe the ship would sink, or because they refused to leave their husbands, or because they could not reach the deck. The crew did not man the falls because there were too few of them, and they had been sent away in the early boats.

Each decision had a reason. Each reason was comprehensible at the moment. The accumulation of decisions produced the final count: 712 survivors out of more than 2, 200 aboard. The number has been debated. The inquiries settled on 712. Later research adjusted it slightly. The exact figure did not matter. The gap between the saved and the lost was roughly fifteen hundred people. That gap was the measure of the failure.

The last boat away was not a boat at all. It was an overturned hull, floating on the water, with thirty men clinging to the bottom. The men on it survived because the boat floated, because they could balance, because the Carpathia came before they died of exposure. The men who did not reach the boat, the hundreds who had been on deck when the ship went down, were in the water. They did not survive.

Collapsible D rowed into the darkness. The men with oars pulled hard, trying to put distance between themselves and the sinking ship. They did not know that the ship would break. They did not know that the stern would rise perpendicular and slide beneath the surface. They only knew that the ship was sinking and that the suction might pull them down. They rowed. They stopped rowing when the lights went out. They watched.

The other boats watched too. They were scattered across a mile of ocean, some rowing, some drifting, all facing the direction of the wreck. They saw the lights flicker. They heard the noise when the hull broke. They saw the stern rise and fall. They heard the cries from the water. They did not go back. Not yet.

The inquiries would ask why. The witnesses would give reasons. The reasons would not satisfy. How could they? Fifteen hundred people had died in the water, within sight of boats that could have held them. The boats had room. The water was cold. The people were drowning. The boats stayed away.

The last boat away had left at 02: 05. The ship had sunk at 02: 20. In those fifteen minutes, the people on deck had tried to reach the remaining collapsibles. They had tried to climb the roof. They had tried to hook the falls. They had tried to stay above the water. Most had failed. The wave that swept Collapsible B away had also swept them. They were in the water now, swimming or drowning or already dead.

The boats that had been lowered earlier—boats 1 through 16—were scattered in the darkness. Some were close to the wreck. Others were a mile away.

Some had crews of trained seamen. Others had no one who knew how to row. Some were full. Others held fewer than twenty people. The distribution had no pattern.

It reflected only the accidents of position and timing. The first boats had left from the forward end of the boat deck. The last boats had left from aft. The passengers who had been forward when the order to lower was given had a better chance than those who were aft.

The passengers who had been on the port side had a different experience than those on starboard. The port-side boats had been supervised by Lightoller, who enforced the women-and-children rule strictly. The starboard-side boats had been supervised by Murdoch, who allowed men to board when no women were waiting.

The difference in policy had produced different outcomes. Men had survived from the starboard side who would have been turned away from the port.

The inquiries would examine these differences. They would ask why Lightoller had been stricter than Murdoch. They would ask why some boats had left half-empty. They would ask why no general alarm had been sounded, why no drill had been held, why the crew had not known the boat assignments. The witnesses would answer as best they could. The answers would point in different directions—to the regulations, to the training, to the decisions of individual officers, to the time that was not enough.

But the final answer was simpler. The ship had sunk faster than the boats could be loaded. The twenty lifeboats—sixteen standard, four collapsible—had been designed for a ship that could stay afloat long enough to fill them. The Titanic had not stayed afloat. The watertight compartments had flooded one after another, pulling the bow down, raising the stern, overwhelming the pumps. The ship had been built to float with four compartments flooded. Five had opened to the sea. The fifth had been the margin between survival and disaster.

The last boat away was a symbol of that margin. Collapsible D had left with forty-four people. Collapsible B had been washed off the deck upside-down. Between them, they represented the end of the evacuation—the point at which procedure collapsed into accident. The people who had followed the rules, who had waited for the boats, who had trusted the officers, had been given places if they were women or children. The people who had not followed the rules—who had jumped, who had climbed, who had swum—had survived if they were lucky. The people who had done neither had died.

The inquiries would produce verdicts. The British inquiry would find that the ship had been lost due to collision with an iceberg, and that the lifeboats had been lowered as quickly as possible. The American inquiry would find that the speed maintained despite ice warnings had been excessive, and that the lifeboats had been insufficient. Both inquiries would recommend changes—more lifeboats, better wireless watches, an ice patrol. The recommendations would be adopted. The regulations would change.

But on the night of 15 April 1912, none of that had happened yet. The boats were in the water, pulling away from the wreck. The people were in the water, drowning in the dark. The Carpathia was still four hours away. The Californian was stopped in the ice, her wireless operator asleep, her officers watching the distress rockets and not understanding what they saw.

Hundreds were left on the sloping decks with no boats remaining, watching the last lifeboat disappear into the night.
