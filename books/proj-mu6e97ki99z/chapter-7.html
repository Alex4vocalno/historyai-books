Chapter 7

The First Call from Boat Deck

The collision had occurred. Water was entering the ship at a catastrophic rate, and the damage handed the officers an immediate and irreversible crisis requiring evacuation.

The telegraphs on the bridge rang to “Stop.” The vibration that had accompanied the ship’s passage fell away. In its place came a silence that those present would later struggle to describe. Fourth Officer Joseph Boxhall stood at his station. First Officer William Murdoch had already given the order to hard-a-port, trying to swing the stern clear of the ice. Now the levers were back. The engines were still. The ship drifted.

Captain Edward John Smith emerged from his cabin almost immediately. He had been in bed when the grinding came. He entered the wheelhouse and asked Murdoch what had happened. The answer was brief. An iceberg. Starboard side. Murdoch had ordered the helm hard over and had closed the watertight doors. Smith asked if the warning had been rung from the crow’s nest. Yes, Murdoch replied, three strokes on the bell. The order to turn had come, but not soon enough.

Smith did not ask further questions on the bridge. He did not call for a damage report over the voice pipe or wait for a subordinate to bring word. He went below. The time was approximately 11: 40 p. m. on the night of 14 April 1912. The Titanic was sailing about 400 miles south of the Grand Banks of Newfoundland. The ship had struck an iceberg and had begun to take on water.

The captain’s descent into the ship’s interior set in motion the first organized response to the disaster. What he found there, and what he did with that knowledge, would determine the shape of the evacuation. Within minutes, a gap opened between what the ship’s designer understood about the vessel’s condition and what the officers on deck believed they were preparing for. That gap would widen. By the time the first lifeboat was swung out on its davits, the operation would be proceeding under a profound misapprehension of the time available.

Smith moved quickly. He went first toward the lower decks, where the mail clerks were already struggling to save the registered post. The mail room on G Deck was among the first compartments breached. Water had entered through the ruptured hull plates and was rising fast. The mail clerks—five men employed by the British Post Office—had been working when the collision occurred. They dragged sacks of letters up the stairways, trying to keep the bags dry. They reported to anyone who asked that water was coming in. Smith saw the flooding. He continued downward.

Thomas Andrews, the ship’s builder, had also been in his cabin when the collision occurred. He was traveling as part of the guarantee group—a team of Harland & Wolff craftsmen and engineers aboard to observe the vessel’s performance and address any defects. Andrews had designed the ship’s general arrangement. He knew her compartments, her bulkheads, her capacity. He had been in bed, reading, when the impact shook the vessel. He dressed and went to the bridge almost as quickly as Smith.

The two men met on the bridge, or near it, and then went below together. Their descent was a tour of the damage. They visited the mail room. They went forward to the cargo holds. They descended to the boiler rooms. In each compartment, the evidence accumulated.

Boiler Room 6 was the first of the boiler spaces to flood. A leading fireman on duty there later reported that water had rushed in through a gash in the ship’s side, forcing him and his men to scramble up the ladder to the escape. The fires were drawn—raked out of the furnaces to prevent explosions—but the room was already lost. He reported to Smith and Andrews that water was coming in at a rate that could not be controlled.

In Boiler Room 5, the situation appeared more stable. The bulkhead held, for the moment. But the water was rising elsewhere. The mail room, the cargo holds, the forward compartments—all were taking water. Andrews made a calculation as he walked. He knew the design. He knew how many compartments could flood before the ship’s buoyancy failed. The Titanic was built to survive the flooding of any two compartments. She might remain afloat with four. But the water was entering more than that.

The ship’s hull had been breached in the first five compartments. The forepeak, the three forward cargo holds, and Boiler Room 6 were all open to the sea. As the bow settled, water would spill from one compartment into the next. The bulkheads did not reach the deckhead. They were walls, not ceilings. Once the water rose high enough, it would flow over the top of each bulkhead and into the compartment behind it. That geometry was fatal.

Andrews gave Smith his assessment. The ship would sink. He estimated the time available: an hour, perhaps two. The calculation was not precise, but the conclusion was absolute. The Titanic could not be saved. The pumps might slow the flooding, but they could not stop it. The vessel was mortally wounded.

Smith absorbed this information. He asked no questions that Andrews later recalled. He did not argue or protest. He accepted the judgment. Then he returned to the bridge.

The time was approximately midnight. Less than thirty minutes had passed since the collision. The ship was still moving through the water, though the engines had stopped. The lights were on. The band had not yet begun to play. Most passengers were still in their cabins, or in the public rooms, waiting for an explanation. They felt the ship stop. They felt the vibration cease. Some went on deck to see what had happened. Most stayed where they were.

On the Boat Deck, the officers were beginning to act. Chief Officer Henry Wilde had overall responsibility for the deck crew. First Officer Murdoch was responsible for the starboard side. Second Officer Charles Lightoller was responsible for the port side. The three men had been on duty, or near duty, when the collision occurred. Now they were receiving orders.

Smith’s first order, after returning from below, was to uncover the lifeboats. The instruction was simple. Get the boats ready. Swing them out. Lower them level with the deck. The order did not say why. It did not say the ship was sinking. It did not say abandon ship. It said prepare.

The order was transmitted by word of mouth. Smith spoke to Wilde. Wilde spoke to Murdoch and Lightoller. Murdoch and Lightoller spoke to the boatswain, who called the deck crew. The boatswain’s mates went forward and aft, rousing the sailors and trimmers who made up the deck department. The men climbed out of their quarters and went to the Boat Deck.

The lifeboats were stored under canvas covers, lashed to the davits. The covers had to be removed. The falls had to be cleared. The boats had to be swung out over the side. The deck crew did this work. They had practiced it once, in Southampton, as a drill. The Board of Trade required a lifeboat drill before departure. The crew had lowered two boats, rowed them around the harbor, and hoisted them back. The exercise had taken perhaps an hour. It had not been conducted at night. It had not been conducted with passengers aboard. It had not been conducted under pressure.

Now the crew began the work again. The time was approximately 12: 05 a. m. The order had been given. The boats would be uncovered and swung out. The operation would proceed.

But the operation proceeded under an assumption that no longer matched reality. Smith knew the ship was sinking. Andrews had told him. But the order he gave did not convey that information. He told his officers to prepare the boats. He did not tell them that the preparation was all that stood between two thousand souls and the freezing ocean.

The gap between knowledge and communication would shape everything that followed. Smith was a captain of the White Star Line. He had commanded passenger vessels for decades. He had never faced a sinking. His training, his instincts, his understanding of his role—all told him to maintain order, to prevent panic, to project confidence. He did not announce that the ship was doomed. He did not call for immediate evacuation. He called for preparation.

The officers on the Boat Deck received the order and interpreted it according to their own understanding. They knew the ship had struck something. They knew water was entering. They did not know, not yet, that the vessel would sink. They assumed the preparation was precautionary. The ship was damaged. The boats might be needed. But they might not. That was the frame they brought to the work.

Wilde, Murdoch, and Lightoller began to organize the deck. The boats were arranged in two rows. The emergency boats, numbers 1 and 2, were already swung out, ready for immediate use. They were small, with a capacity of about forty people each. The remaining boats—fourteen wooden lifeboats and two collapsible canvas boats—were stored inboard, under the davits. The davits were the cranes that lowered the boats. They were designed to swing out over the side, lowering the boats to the water.

The crew began to remove the covers. They unhooked the falls—the ropes that raised and lowered the boats—and coiled them for use. They swung the davits out, positioning the boats over the side. The work was physical and methodical. The men knew what to do. They had done it before.

But the boats were not full. They were not even ready to receive passengers. The order was to prepare. The next order—to load—had not yet come.

Smith, meanwhile, was dealing with other matters. He went to the wireless room. The operators, John George Phillips and Harold Sydney Bride, were at their posts. Phillips was the senior operator. Bride was the second. They worked for the Marconi Company, not the White Star Line. Their job was to send and receive messages for passengers and to handle ship-to-shore communications.

Smith gave Phillips an order. Send a distress signal. The time was approximately 12: 15 a. m. Phillips began to transmit. The signal was CQD—the standard distress call at that time. It meant all stations: distress. Phillips sent the ship’s position: 41°46’ N, 50°14’ W. The position was approximate. The ship had been moving. The exact coordinates would be refined, but the first signal went out.

Harold Sydney Bride was a British merchant seaman and wireless operator who served as the second operator aboard the RMS Titanic during her ill-fated maiden voyage. After the Titanic struck an iceberg at 11: 40 pm on 14 April 1912, Bride assisted his senior, John George Phillips, in sending distress signals to nearby ships. The wireless room was on the Boat Deck, aft of the officers’ quarters. Smith could walk there in less than a minute. He gave the order and left. Phillips continued to transmit. Bride began to prepare the backup equipment.

The distress signal was a statement of fact, but it was not a public announcement. Smith did not use the ship’s public address system. The Titanic had no such system. The only way to communicate with passengers was through stewards and stewardesses, going cabin to cabin, or through verbal announcements on deck. Smith had not yet ordered either. He was still working through the chain of command. He was still trying to understand what he faced.

The gap between what Smith knew and what the passengers knew would widen in the coming hour. The passengers felt the ship stop. They felt the engines fall silent. Some went on deck. They saw nothing unusual. The ship was still lit. The sea was calm. The stars were bright. There was no visible damage from the deck. The iceberg was gone, passed astern. The damage was below the waterline.

Passengers asked crew members what had happened. The answers were vague. A propeller blade had broken, one steward said. The ship had struck ice, another suggested. Nothing serious. Go back to bed. The information was wrong, but it was not malicious. The crew did not know either. They had received no official word. They were guessing.

On the Boat Deck, the work continued. The boats were being uncovered. The davits were being swung out. The falls were being coiled. The deck crew worked efficiently. They were sailors. They knew their jobs.

But the operation was not proceeding with the urgency that the situation required. The officers did not know the ship was sinking. They thought they were preparing for a contingency. The ship was damaged. The boats might be needed. But there was time. There was no rush.

The misapprehension lay there. The ship was sinking. There was not time. The rush was necessary. But the order had not conveyed that. The order had said prepare. The officers prepared. They did not load. They did not call passengers. They did not fill the boats.

The gap between technical reality and procedural assumption would prove fatal. Andrews knew the ship would sink. Smith knew. But Smith did not communicate that knowledge to the officers who would manage the evacuation. He did not communicate it to the crew who would load the boats. He did not communicate it to the passengers who would climb into them.

The reasons for this silence are not fully documented. Smith left no memoir. He died in the sinking. His actions must be reconstructed from the testimony of others. What is clear is that he gave orders that were accurate in form but incomplete in substance. He told his officers to prepare the boats. He did not tell them why.

The result was a lifeboat operation that began without a clear understanding of its purpose. The officers thought they were conducting a precautionary drill. They did not know they were conducting an evacuation. They did not know that every boat they lowered would be one of only twenty available for more than two thousand people. They did not know that every empty seat would mean a death.

The Board of Trade regulations had required the Titanic to carry lifeboats for only 1, 060 people. The ship carried 2, 224. The regulations were based on tonnage, not on the number of souls aboard. The Titanic was a large ship. The regulations had not kept pace with her size. The ship carried more boats than required—twenty in total, including the collapsibles—but not enough for everyone.

This fact was known to the officers. They knew how many boats they had. They knew the capacity of each. They had drilled on the boats in Southampton. They had lowered them and hoisted them. They knew the numbers.

What they did not know was how little time they had. Andrews had estimated an hour, perhaps two. The ship was taking on water faster than the pumps could handle. The bow was settling. The water was rising. The compartments were flooding one by one. The ship was sinking.

The officers on the Boat Deck did not know this. They worked steadily, but not urgently. They uncovered the boats. They swung out the davits. They prepared the falls. They waited for further orders.

Smith was elsewhere. He was on the bridge. He was in the wireless room. He was below decks, checking the flooding again. He was trying to manage a situation that no captain had faced before. The largest ship in the world was sinking on her maiden voyage. There was no procedure for this. There was no protocol.

The protocol that existed was designed for a different kind of emergency. The Board of Trade regulations assumed that a ship in distress would have time to summon help. The wireless would bring other ships. The boats would be used to transfer passengers, not to abandon ship entirely. The regulations assumed that the ship would stay afloat long enough for rescue to arrive.

The protocol lagged behind the ship. The rules had been written for ships of a different era. The Titanic was larger, faster, and more complex than any vessel that had come before. But the rules had not changed. The assumptions had not been updated. The gap between regulation and reality was about to become visible.

At approximately 12: 20 a. m., Smith gave the order to load the boats. The instruction went to Wilde, who passed it to Murdoch and Lightoller. The order was specific: women and children first. The phrase was custom, not law. There was no regulation that required it. But it was the practice of the sea. In an emergency, the women and children would go first.

The order was transmitted. The officers began to call for passengers. They used their voices. They sent stewards below to rouse people from their cabins. The announcement was informal. There was no general alarm. The ship’s whistles had not sounded. The bells had not rung. The word passed from mouth to mouth, deck to deck.

Passengers began to emerge. They came up the grand staircases. They came up the crew ladders. They came in dressing gowns and overcoats. They carried lifebelts and jewelry. They carried nothing at all. They came onto the Boat Deck and looked around.

The scene was calm. The ship was lit. The band had begun to play. The musicians had come up from the lounge and taken their places on the deck. They played ragtime. They played waltzes. The music was meant to soothe. It succeeded. The passengers stood in groups. They talked quietly. They looked at the boats. They waited.

The boats were being lowered to the deck level. The davits swung out. The falls paid out. The boats hung in the air, level with the Boat Deck, ready to receive passengers. The crew stood by the falls. The officers stood by the boats. The passengers stood back.

The loading began. Murdoch took the starboard side. Lightoller took the port side. Wilde moved between them. Smith was on the bridge, or nearby. He observed. He gave orders. But the detailed work—the calling of passengers, the filling of boats, the lowering away—was done by the officers.

The first boat to be lowered was Number 7, on the starboard side. Murdoch oversaw the loading. He called for women and children. Passengers stepped forward. The boat filled slowly. There were empty seats. The passengers hesitated. They did not believe the ship was sinking. They did not want to leave. The boat seemed small. The ocean seemed cold. The ship seemed safe.

Murdoch urged them. He filled the boat, but not to capacity. There were empty places. The boat was lowered. It reached the water. The falls were released. The boat pulled away.

The time was approximately 12: 45 a. m. The first boat had gone. It carried fewer than thirty people. Its capacity was sixty-five.

The pattern would continue. Boat after boat would leave with empty seats. The passengers would hesitate. The officers would not force them. The boats would go down half full. The empty seats would never be filled.

The gap between knowledge and action had opened. Smith knew the ship was sinking. He had known since Andrews told him, shortly after midnight. But the passengers did not know. The crew did not know. The officers did not know, not fully. They thought they were preparing for a contingency. They thought there was time.

The misapprehension was structural. The order to prepare the boats had not conveyed the urgency of the situation. The order to load had come later, but still without a clear statement of the ship’s condition. The officers worked according to the information they had. The passengers reacted according to what they saw. The ship looked fine. The lights were on. The band was playing. Why leave?

The answer lay below the waterline. The compartments were flooding. The bow was settling. The water was rising. The ship was sinking. But that answer was not visible from the deck. It was not communicated in the orders. It was not understood by the people who needed to understand it.

The result was an evacuation that proceeded without urgency. The boats left half full. The passengers stayed on deck. The officers loaded the boats without filling them. The time passed. The water rose.

Smith had returned to the bridge after his tour with Andrews. He stood there now, or paced nearby. The wireless continued to send. The distress calls went out. Ships were responding. The Carpathia was turning toward them, steaming from fifty-eight miles away. But fifty-eight miles was four hours at full speed. The time Andrews had estimated was less than that.

The captain of the largest ship in the world faced a calculation he had never been trained to make. He had enough boats for roughly half the people aboard. He had perhaps two hours before the vessel foundered. He had passengers who did not understand the danger and officers who did not know the stakes. He had a wireless that could call for help but could not make that help arrive faster.

In the first five minutes after the collision, the die had been cast. Andrews had diagnosed the wound. Smith had accepted the diagnosis. But the translation of that knowledge into action had been filtered through the habits of command, the assumptions of procedure, and the absence of any protocol for a ship that must be evacuated in minutes rather than hours.

The first lifeboat is swung out on its davits, a physical action initiated under a profound misapprehension of the time available.
