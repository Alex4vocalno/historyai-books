Chapter 12

King Turns His Field Glasses

The engraved certificates arrived from the printer on October 15, 1872, their borders crowded with eagles and locomotives and the promise of ten million dollars in capital stock. A clerk from the Bank of California supervised their transfer to the vault, checking each number against the registration ledger while William Ralston watched from the doorway. The paper was still fragrant with ink. Outside on California Street, the afternoon fog was beginning to roll in from the bay, dissolving the outlines of the new palaces of finance. The clerk turned the key. The Diamond Peak Mining Company now existed in a form that could be bought, sold, leveraged, and sued.

Four hundred miles to the northeast, in a canvas tent pitched beside a stream that drained the western slope of the Medicine Bow Mountains, Clarence King smoothed a topographical map across a packing case that served as his field desk. The map showed country he had already walked, measured, and described: the drainage of the Green River, the uplifted ridges of the Uinta Mountains, the high plateaus where volcanic rock lay over sedimentary beds in sequences his survey had spent five years documenting. He weighted the corners with tin cans and a revolver. The autumn light was failing early. His assistant Samuel Franklin Emmons stood at the tent flap, listening to a prospector who had ridden in that afternoon with pack mules and a story.

The story concerned a mountain where diamonds lay on the ground like gravel. The prospector had not seen this mountain himself. He had talked to a man in Rawlins who had talked to a man who had been hired to pack supplies to a secret camp. The camp was somewhere in the Colorado Territory, perhaps near the Utah line, perhaps in the Uncompahgre country. The men who worked it were silent. Their backers were San Francisco millionaires. The stones they brought out were being graded in the offices of Charles Tiffany in New York City.

King listened without interrupting. Twenty-nine years old, already the author of a geological report that Congress had praised, already accustomed to having his judgment consulted on matters of western mineralogy. The Fortieth Parallel Survey, which he had directed since 1867, was nearing completion. Its publications would fill seven volumes. Its maps would guide the construction of the Union Pacific and the settlement of the interior West. King had designed the survey to be comprehensive, systematic, and authoritative—an answer to the rumor-driven chaos of western mining promotion, where every quartz ledge became a bonanza in the telling and every bonanza collapsed in the absence of scientific confirmation.

The survey had found no diamonds. It had crossed the Colorado Territory from east to west, from the plains to the Sierra Nevada, and its collections contained no indicator minerals, no kimberlite pipes, no geological formations that would suggest diamond occurrence. The report King was preparing would state this negatively but emphatically: the region’s geology did not support the presence of diamonds in commercial quantities. He had written these conclusions before hearing the prospector’s story. Now the story existed in the same space as his science, and the juxtaposition created a pressure that was not yet cognitive but physical, a tightening of attention across his shoulders and in his hands as they held the map.

Emmons came inside. The prospector had gone to the fire for coffee. King asked him what he made of the tale.

Emmons was younger, trained at Harvard and the École des Mines, already King’s most trusted field companion. They had mapped the Comstock together, had measured the granite domes of the Sierra, had survived a winter in the high Rockies when their provisions failed. Emmons understood the survey’s methods as King did: the triangulation of peaks, the plotting of strata, the correlation of fossil beds across hundreds of miles. He understood also the survey’s jurisdiction. The federal government had funded their work to replace speculation with knowledge. A diamond discovery in their mapped territory, unreported by them, by their methods, would constitute a professional failure of the first order.

They should find out where this mountain was.

King agreed. But the prospector did not know. The Rawlins man did not know. The secret was being maintained by men who had learned, in the hard schools of western mining, that information was the most valuable commodity and that its scarcity could be manufactured. King had seen this before: the locked assays, the guarded mine entrances, the promoters who spoke of fabulous ore bodies while refusing to disclose their locations. Usually the secrecy concealed nothing. Occasionally it concealed fraud. The diamond story had the familiar structure of both possibilities—too wonderful to believe, too specific to dismiss.

He turned to his files. The survey maintained a clipping service, packets of newspapers forwarded from Denver and San Francisco and Salt Lake City, and King had fallen behind in reading them during the summer’s fieldwork. Now he sorted through the stack, looking for references to diamonds, to San Francisco capital, to any of the names the prospector had mentioned. The light from his lamp attracted moths. Outside, the stream ran cold with snowmelt from the high peaks.

The first mention appeared in a Denver paper dated September 20: a brief notice of a “remarkable discovery” in northwestern Colorado, precious stones of extraordinary quality, capitalists from California investigating. The language was cautious, the location unspecified. A later issue, October 5, carried a longer account: Tiffany & Co. had examined specimens, had pronounced them genuine, had valued them at extraordinary figures. The San Francisco Bulletin was quoted. The names of the discoverers were withheld “for business reasons.” King also knew of the Attorney General’s opinion of August 31, 1872, in which George H. Williams had specifically ruled that the terms “valuable mineral deposits” in the mining laws included diamonds.

King read these paragraphs twice. He knew Henry Janin by reputation—a mining engineer of established credentials, a graduate of the École des Mines, a man who had directed successful operations in California and Nevada. Janin had examined the field. Janin had reported favorably. This was not the language of frontier rumor but the apparatus of professional validation. The survey’s own authority was being challenged by a parallel authority, one that operated through different channels—banking houses, jewelry firms, stock exchanges—yet claimed the same ultimate ground: the western earth, its contents, its value.

Emmons had the maps out now, the completed sheets and the sketches in progress. They spread them across the tent floor, kneeling to trace the country the newspapers described. Northwestern Colorado. The Uinta Mountains. The drainage of the Green. These were names from their own work, coordinates they had measured and plotted. If diamonds existed in this territory, they should have found them. If they had not found them, the diamonds did not exist. The syllogism was simple. Its consequences were not.

King stood up. His back ached from the day’s riding. He was aware, as he often was in moments of decision, of the multiple scales on which his life operated: the immediate physical discomfort, the professional obligation, the larger architecture of reputation and institution that his survey represented. Before Congress, he had argued that western development required scientific guidance. He had persuaded Gardiner to fund the Fortieth Parallel work. Something was being built that would outlast his own field seasons—a body of knowledge, a methodology, a claim to public trust. Now that claim was being tested by a story that had already traveled farther and faster than any survey report.

He would not dismiss it. That was the first principle. The history of geology was full of legitimate discoveries that had seemed improbable—petroleum in Pennsylvania, silver in Nevada, gold in the Klondike. King had himself found mineral deposits where established opinion said none existed. He would not commit the sin of the closed mind, the expert who knows too much to learn.

But he would verify. That was the second principle, and it ran deeper than professional caution. Verification was the survey’s reason for being. The West was too large, too various, too full of competing interests to be left to the verification shortcuts of the market—Tiffany’s certificate, Janin’s report, Ralston’s capital. Each of these was a substitute for the arduous, expensive, time-consuming work of actually going to the place, walking the ground, examining the rock. King understood why the substitutes were attractive. He understood also why they were dangerous. A signature on a report, however eminent the signer, could not see what the ground concealed.

He picked up the Denver paper again. The date was October 12. The story had been in print for three weeks. In San Francisco, the incorporation was proceeding. In New York, Tiffany’s valuation was being cited in conversations between men who would never visit the field themselves. The verification shortcut was complete: the stones had been seen, the engineer had reported, the bank had committed. The only element missing from the chain was the ground itself, and the ground was being protected by secrecy.

This secrecy was what made the story professionally urgent. A legitimate mining operation, in King’s experience, did not hide its location from the federal survey. The survey could assist with land titles, with water rights, with the geological information that made extraction efficient. Only operations that could not survive examination avoided examination. The diamond discoverers were not seeking the survey’s help. They were relying on the market’s substitutes.

King made his decision. He would treat the diamond story not as rumor to be ignored or fraud to be exposed but as a scientific problem to be investigated. The survey had resources: men, horses, instruments, the accumulated knowledge of five years’ mapping. These resources would be diverted from their scheduled program. He would find the mountain that the newspapers could not name.

He told Emmons to prepare for a detour.

The next morning, October 16, King wrote to his superior in Washington, explaining that routine operations would be suspended pending investigation of reports concerning diamond occurrence in territory adjacent to their present camp. The letter was careful, provisional, avoiding any suggestion that the reports were false. King was not yet prepared to make that claim. He was prepared only to say that the reports required his personal attention, that they fell within or near the survey’s mapped territory, and that their verification was a matter of scientific and governmental interest.

They broke camp on October 17. King left most of his party to continue the scheduled survey work, taking only Emmons and two packers. They rode south and west, following the stream courses that would lead them toward the Uinta Mountains and the country of rumor. The weather held clear and cold. The aspen had turned yellow on the slopes above them, and the meadows were brown with frost-killed grass. They passed no other travelers. This was country that the survey had already mapped, already described in the reports that King was preparing for publication. He knew its geology intimately: the sedimentary sequences, the volcanic intrusions, the faults and folds that had shaped the landscape. He knew what rocks could contain diamonds and what rocks could not.

The kimberlite pipes that produced diamonds in South Africa, in India, in Brazil, were volcanic formations of specific character. They broke through the crust in narrow columns, carrying fragments of the deep mantle to the surface. They were rare, distinctive, and geologically young. King had found nothing resembling them in the Colorado Territory. The formations he had mapped were older, metamorphosed, stripped of their original volcanic structures. The possibility of diamond occurrence in such country was not theoretically zero—geology reserved its surprises—but it was low enough that any claim required extraordinary evidence.

They reached Fort Bridger on October 20, trading news with the garrison and the mountain men who gathered there. The diamond story was known. Everyone had heard something. No one knew where the mountain was. The San Francisco men had been through, buying supplies, hiring packers, paying generously for silence. They had gone north, some said. East, said others. Into the Uintas, or beyond them, into the broken country where the Colorado Territory met Wyoming and Utah in a confusion of ridges and canyons.

King studied his maps again. The survey’s coverage was densest along the railroad corridor, thinner in the remote mountain country. There were blank spaces where his parties had not yet traveled, where the triangulation was incomplete. If the diamond mountain lay in one of these blanks, its absence from his reports would be explained but not excused. The survey claimed comprehensive knowledge. A discovery in a mapped blank would be embarrassing. A discovery in mapped territory would be worse.

They pushed on, following the Bear River into the high country. The nights were below freezing now. The horses struggled in snow that had fallen on the north-facing slopes. King and Emmons took turns breaking trail, their boots crunching through crust that supported the animals only in the early morning hours. They were traveling without the survey’s full equipment, moving fast and light, dependent on their own observations rather than the systematic measurements of the formal program.

This was how King preferred to work when investigation replaced routine. He had learned the method in the Sierra Nevada, where sudden storms and impassable terrain had taught him that geological insight often arrived in moments of physical extremity. The body registered the landscape before the mind could analyze it: the angle of a slope, the texture of an outcrop, the vegetation that indicated particular soils. He trusted these registrations. They had saved his life more than once.

On October 23, they encountered a pack train coming out of the mountains. The leader was a Mormon settler from Ogden, hauling furs and a small quantity of ore samples that he refused to discuss. King bought coffee from him and asked about the diamond country. The Mormon had heard the stories. He believed none of them. In his experience, men who found wealth in the mountains either stayed to work it or sold their knowledge openly. Secrecy was the mark of something else—not wealth but its imitation.

King noted the observation without comment. He was gathering data now, building a chain of evidence that would lead him to the truth. The newspaper reports were one link. The survey’s own geological knowledge was another. The testimony of experienced mountain men was a third. Each source narrowed the field of possibility. Each raised the stakes of his own eventual judgment.

They found the first solid information on October 25, at a trading post on the Henry’s Fork of the Green River. A packer who had worked for the San Francisco syndicate was drinking there, spending wages he had earned carrying supplies to a camp he was sworn not to locate. The whiskey loosened him. He described a mesa, not a mountain, standing above a valley where antelope grazed. The mesa was volcanic, capped with basalt, surrounded by eroded badlands. The diamonds were found in the decomposed rock at the base of the cliffs, mixed with rubies and other stones. The discoverers were two Kentuckians, rough men who kept to themselves. The engineers and capitalists came and went by night, blindfolded for the final approach.

King listened to this description with the attention he would give to a particularly puzzling outcrop. The geology was wrong. As a geologist, King was aware that diamonds and rubies formed under different conditions and would never be found together in a single deposit. Diamonds formed in the deep mantle, under pressures that destroyed rubies. Rubies were aluminum oxide, products of contact metamorphism in limestone. Their coexistence in the same deposit would require geological conditions that King could not imagine, that his reading suggested did not exist.

But he kept this judgment to himself. The packer was still talking, describing the machinery that had been freighted in, the shaft that was being sunk, the excitement of the men who had seen the stones. King paid for another drink and asked if the packer would guide them to the mesa. The packer refused. He had been paid for silence, and silence he would keep. But he described the approach well enough: up the Green River, through Brown’s Hole, into the country of the Yampa Plateau.

King and Emmons studied their maps that night by lamplight. The Yampa Plateau was within the survey’s mapped territory. King had crossed its southern margin in 1869, had described its volcanic formations in his field notes. The mesa the packer described sounded like one of the isolated volcanic remnants that dotted the plateau, eroded pillars of basalt standing above the softer sedimentary rocks. He had seen such formations before. He had found no diamonds in them.

Yet the coincidence of location was striking. The diamond discoverers had chosen, whether by accident or design, a region that King had already examined and pronounced barren of precious stones. This was either extraordinary luck on their part or something else—something that would require King himself to verify, to witness, to judge.

He made his decision. They would go to the Yampa Plateau. They would find the mesa. They would examine the diamonds with their own eyes and instruments. Whatever they found would become part of the survey’s record, part of the authoritative knowledge that King had committed his career to building.

The journey took four more days. They traveled by night when they could, following the packer’s directions, avoiding the trails that the syndicate’s supply trains used. King wanted to arrive unannounced, to see the ground before anyone knew that the survey had taken an interest. This was not deception, he told himself, but methodology. The survey’s presence would alter what it observed. Promoters who had hidden their location from competitors would hide it also from government geologists, or would stage a performance for their benefit. King wanted neither concealment nor theater. He wanted the ground as it was.

They reached the plateau on October 29, 1872. The mesa was visible from miles away, a dark silhouette against the pale sky, its basalt cap standing in vertical columns above the eroded slopes. King studied it through his field glasses, noting the geological structure, the drainage patterns, the absence of any surface indication of mineral wealth. The volcanic rock was ancient, weathered, stripped of any original minerals that might have been present. He had seen a hundred such formations in his western travels. None had contained diamonds.

They made camp in a draw below the mesa, hiding their horses in a grove of cottonwoods. King sent Emmons to scout the approach while he remained with the instruments, preparing the careful observations that the survey required. The afternoon sun slanted across the plateau, turning the volcanic rock red and gold. He worked methodically, measuring angles, sketching profiles, comparing what he saw with the maps and notes he had carried from his earlier survey.

Emmons returned at dusk. He had found the camp. The syndicate’s operation was concentrated on the north face of the mesa, where a shaft had been sunk into the decomposed volcanic rock. There were tents, a forge, machinery for hoisting ore. The discoverers were there, the Kentuckians, along with engineers and laborers. Security was loose. No one had challenged him.

King nodded. They would approach in the morning, openly, identifying themselves as the federal survey. They would request permission to examine the field, to collect specimens, to make the independent verification that scientific standards required. If permission was refused, they would note the refusal and continue their observations from public land. The survey’s authority did not depend on the cooperation of claimants.

But he did not sleep well that night. The mesa loomed above their camp, its outline visible against the stars, and King found himself rehearsing the possible outcomes of the next day’s work. If the diamonds were genuine, he would report them as genuine. The survey’s prior negative findings would require revision, explanation, perhaps apology. His own reputation would suffer, but the science would advance. If the diamonds were not genuine—if they had been placed there, planted, salted—then something else would follow. Exposure. Accusation. The destruction of a financial edifice that had already absorbed hundreds of thousands of dollars and was preparing to absorb millions more. He knew that exposing a fraud of this scale could make him an international celebrity.

He turned on his side, watching the stars move above the mesa. The verification shortcut had brought the market to this remote place. Tiffany’s certificate, Janin’s report, Ralston’s capital—each had substituted for the actual examination of the ground. Now the ground would be examined. The shortcut would be completed or it would be broken. King did not know which. He knew only that his own presence here, his field glasses and his maps and his trained observation, represented something that the market’s substitutes had tried to bypass: the direct encounter between scientific method and material reality.

In the morning, they saddled their horses and rode toward the mesa. The sun was rising behind it, casting long shadows across the plateau. King could see the syndicate’s camp now, the smoke from breakfast fires, the figures of men moving among the tents. He adjusted his field glasses and studied the scene, noting the arrangement of the workings, the disposition of the machinery, the geological formations that the shaft had exposed.

Something was wrong. He could not yet say what. The volcanic rock was as he remembered it from his earlier survey: ancient, weathered, barren of the indicator minerals that accompanied diamond pipes. The working was too shallow, too haphazard, for a genuine discovery of the scale that the newspapers described. The men moved with the energy of laborers who knew their business, but the business itself seemed staged, performed for an audience that was not yet present.

King lowered his glasses. He would need to see the stones themselves. He would need to examine the matrix in which they were found, to trace their geological context, to apply the methods that had guided his survey through five years of western exploration. The verification shortcut had ended here, at the base of this volcanic mesa, where a government geologist with field glasses and a trained eye was preparing to look at what others had only certified.

He spurred his horse forward. Emmons rode beside him, saying nothing. The morning air was cold and clear. The mesa waited, its secrets still intact, its basalt columns standing like the ruins of some ancient fortress against the brightening sky. King felt the familiar tightening of attention, the focus that came when observation replaced speculation. He did not know what he would find. He knew only that finding it was now his responsibility, his jurisdiction, his professional reason for being.

They reached the camp as the sun cleared the mesa rim. A man emerged from the largest tent, buttoning his coat against the cold. King recognized him from newspaper descriptions: one of the Kentuckians, the one called Arnold, the brains of the operation according to rumor. Arnold’s face showed nothing—neither surprise nor concern nor the guilt that King half-expected. He simply waited, standing between the federal geologist and the shaft that had made him wealthy, while the morning light fell equally on both men and the mesa rose behind them in its ancient, eroded silence.

King dismounted. He identified himself and his survey. He stated his purpose: to examine the diamond field, to collect specimens, to verify the reports that had reached the government concerning mineral deposits on public lands. His voice was level, professional, carrying none of the suspicion that had begun to form in his mind.

Arnold nodded. He said they were always glad to welcome scientific gentlemen. The field was open for inspection. The stones spoke for themselves.

King turned his field glasses toward the shaft. The verification would begin now, not with certificates or reports but with his own eyes, his own hands, his own trained judgment of what the western earth contained and what it did not. Two systems of knowledge—one built on systematic survey, one on market certification—were preparing to test each other against the same ground. King walked toward the workings, Emmons following with the sample bags. The sun was fully up now, the shadows shortening, the day’s heat beginning to rise from the eroded rock. He moved with the deliberate pace of a man who had learned that haste was the enemy of accuracy, that the ground revealed its truths only to those who took the time to look.

The shaft gaped before him, its timbered sides showing the decomposed volcanic rock that the packer had described. King knelt at its edge, adjusting his glasses, preparing to see what was there. Behind him, the mesa stood in its silence, and beyond it the plateau stretched away to the horizon, mapped and unmapped, known and unknown, while the federal survey and the Diamond Peak Mining Company prepared to measure their competing claims against the same patch of western ground.
