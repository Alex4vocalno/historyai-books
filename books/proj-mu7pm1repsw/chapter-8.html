Chapter 8

The Government’s Parallel Track

The barometer hung from a tripod at the lip of a dry wash, its brass case dulled by the high-altitude sun. Clarence King stood beside it, notebook open, pencil ready. He watched the mercury settle, recorded the reading, then walked fifty measured paces to where his assistant Samuel Franklin Emmons crouched at an outcrop of crumbling shale. Emmons had his hammer raised. The sound of steel on stone cracked across the empty valley and died without echo.

It was September 1872. They were completing the final season of the Geological Exploration of the Fortieth Parallel on the eastern slope of the Uinta Mountains.

King marked the time: 10:47. He noted sky conditions, wind direction, temperature. These entries would be transcribed, checked against readings from other stations, reduced to sea-level equivalents, and plotted on the contour sheets in his field desk. The survey had run for five years, producing seven volumes of reports, forty-three atlas sheets, and the most accurate geological cross-section of the American West ever attempted. King was twenty-nine. He had spent his entire adult life on this work.

Emmons straightened, examining the fragment he had chipped free. “Ferruginous sandstone. Same as the ridge above.”

King took the sample, slotted it into a canvas specimen bag numbered in india ink, and added it to the two hundred others collected that season. Back in camp, they would be laid out on a rubber sheet, described in formal lithological terms, correlated with stratigraphic columns. Eventually they would travel to Washington, to the new museum building on the Mall, becoming part of the federal government’s official knowledge of its western territories.

This was how the West was being made legible: not by the solitary prospector with his pan and intuition, but by teams of trained men moving in systematic grids, reducing complexity to standardized notation. Congress had authorized the Fortieth Parallel Survey in 1867, funding it at levels reflecting post-war determination to replace rumor with information. King’s mandate ran from California’s eastern border to the western edge of the Great Plains—territory encompassing Sierra granite, Idaho lava plateaus, Wyoming coal measures, Colorado red sandstone. He hired men who could endure hardship and maintain discipline: Emmons, mining engineer James Hague, topographer Charles Hoffmann, others who rotated through the seasons.

They worked in pairs or threes, separated by miles, converging at predetermined rendezvous where pack animals waited with fresh supplies. The isolation was total. News arrived in newspaper bundles cached at supply points, weeks or months stale. King read these with the detachment of a man for whom current events had become theoretical. He noted Northern Pacific progress, Nevada silver strikes, San Francisco money market fluctuations. None affected his daily rhythm.

On the evening of September 3, at a camp on the White River, King unfolded a Rocky Mountain News from Denver, dated August 15. A brief item on page three caught his attention: diamonds discovered in northwestern Colorado, extraordinary richness reported, location kept secret. The language was familiar, the form conventional. Every season brought such reports, most dissolving under examination. King read it twice, noted the absence of specific location data, and set the paper aside. The diamond hoax of 1872 had triggered a brief prospecting craze across the western territories, but this report felt different—it spoke of investors, not just prospectors.

Emmons, cooking bacon over the fire, asked what was interesting.

“Someone’s found diamonds. Northwest Colorado. No location given.”

Emmons considered this. The survey’s territory included all of Colorado west of the 105th meridian. A diamond discovery there would require their assessment, would appear on their maps if verified.

“Placer or pipe?”

“The report doesn’t distinguish.”

They ate in silence. The sun dropped behind the Uinta ridge; the temperature fell rapidly. King made his day’s notes, checked the aneroid barometer against the mercurial standard, and turned in without mentioning the diamonds again.

But the item stayed with him. In the morning, breaking camp, he found himself calculating distances, reviewing the geological character of country north and west of their position. The White River plateau, the Yampa valley, the rugged country toward the Green—he knew these terrains from previous seasons. He had mapped the Danforth Hills coal measures, traced eastern Uinta fault systems, collected Eocene fossil plants. Nowhere had he encountered the ultrabasic igneous rocks that carried diamond pipes. Northwestern Colorado geology was sedimentary: sandstone, shale, limestone, coal. Its structural history was gentle uplift and erosion, not deep magmatic intrusions.

This was not intuition. It was accumulated systematic observation—the thousands of station points and sample localities constituting the survey’s emerging map. King had learned to read the western landscape as continuous text, each outcrop connecting to the next through traceable stratigraphic relationships. The diamond report, read against this knowledge, presented a classification problem rather than a question of belief.

They moved camp twenty miles northeast, following the White River toward its confluence with the Green. Work continued: barometric readings every two hours, stratigraphic sections measured bed by bed, fossils collected for dating. King maintained his usual pace, but listened differently at evening fires, questioning teamsters who brought flour and bacon from supply stations.

Had they heard more about the diamond discovery?

The teamsters had heard plenty. A strike of extraordinary richness, guarded by armed men, investors arriving from San Francisco with cash. The location lay in high mesa country, accessible only by difficult trail. Some said the diamonds were surface finds, scattered like gravel. Others described vertical pipes of blue ground, the classic African formation. No one had seen the actual site. Secrecy was absolute.

King received this with his usual reserve. But he noticed a pattern distinguishing this rumor from ordinary mining excitement. The investors were not local speculators or Denver promoters. They were men of established position: William Ralston of the Bank of California, New York jeweler Charles Tiffany. Reputable mining engineer Henry Janin had been sent to examine the property. The investment scale suggested someone had already satisfied himself of the discovery’s authenticity. This was the verification shortcut in action: remote location, difficult access; few witnesses, selected for credibility; a chain of authoritative endorsements—engineer, jeweler, banker—each substituting for direct examination. The system was self-reinforcing: the more respectable the investors, the less necessary to question their judgment.

But the survey’s mandate made no such distinction. King’s authority derived from congressional appropriation, published reports, triangulation stations fixing positions to fractions of a second of arc. His business was description, and description, carried far enough, became judgment.

On September 12, at a station on the Diamond Fork of the White River, King decided to detach Emmons to continue scheduled work while he investigated the rumors. The survey would suffer delay, but ignoring a major mineral claim within his district was professionally untenable. He wrote instructions specifying stations to occupy, contacts to map, specimens to collect. They would rendezvous in three weeks at the supply camp on the Duchesne River.

Emmons accepted without comment. He understood the survey’s dual nature: scientific enterprise and government function, answerable to standards of completeness overriding individual convenience. If diamonds existed in northwestern Colorado, the survey needed to know. If not, that knowledge was equally required.

King packed light: field book, hammer, compass, a week’s provisions. He followed the White River downstream to the Green, then turned north into broken country rising toward the Colorado-Wyoming line. Terrain grew more difficult. He entered the uninhabited zone, mapped only in reconnaissance. His aneroid showed seven thousand feet, then eight. Vegetation thinned to sage and scattered juniper. Water became scarce, found only in potholes or cliff-base seeps.

He traveled alone, against survey regulations. But speed mattered more than safety; a party would have moved more slowly. He walked fifteen to twenty miles daily, navigating by compass and sketch maps carried in memory. The country was a maze of eroded ridges and dry washes, impossible to cross straight. He learned to read subtle signs: soil color changes indicating buried strata, vegetation alignment following fault traces.

On the fifth day, he encountered a trail.

Not an Indian path or game trail—too wide, too recently used, cut into a slope deterring casual travel. King followed it upward through a sandstone defile, emerging onto a high mesa extending for miles. Wind-polished gravel covered the surface, the characteristic desert pavement of the Colorado Plateau. In the distance, dust rose from beyond the northern horizon.

He concealed himself in a draw and waited. After two hours, six well-mounted riders appeared on the skyline, pack animals trailing. They passed within half a mile without detecting him. He noted their direction—toward the Green River settlements—and waited until they vanished before continuing.

The trail led north across the mesa. King followed on foot, keeping to low ridges. After three miles, he found recent activity: moist horse manure with shod hoof prints; canvas scrap on a juniper branch; campfire remains in a shallow depression.

He approached with caution learned from reading landscapes for human presence. The fire had been extinguished with water from some distant source, ashes carefully scattered. But the ground showed compressed tent impressions, parallel ruts of wagon or heavy cart. Someone had camped here recently, in numbers, with equipment suggesting days rather than hours.

King sketched the site in his field book, noting position by compass bearing and estimated distance from known landmarks. The location lay approximately forty miles northwest of his last triangulation station, near the 110th meridian in country his maps labeled “unexplored.”

He continued north. The trail grew more defined, worn by repeated traffic into a visible track across the desert pavement. Other signs appeared: dropped nail, broken glass fragment, butchered animal bones. The mesa top was remarkably flat, extending miles without relief. Walking was easy, navigation simple. He understood why someone might choose this for secret operation: accessible enough for determined travel, isolated enough to escape casual discovery.

In late afternoon, he sighted his objective.

First a geometric anomaly on the horizon: straight line where nature offered only curves, vertical plane against horizontal sweep. Approaching, the line resolved into a pole, the plane into canvas—a white tent, military in its regularity, pitched in a cleared area. Around it: a corral with horses, a second tent, equipment piles glinting in slanting light.

He stopped two miles out, finding cover in a shallow arroyo. The sun lowered toward the western rim. He would not approach in daylight. Instead, he reconnoitered surrounding terrain, identifying advance and retreat routes, water sources, observation positions. Survey methods applied as readily to human activity as geological structure: both were phenomena to map, measure, understand spatially.

He found a hollow beneath an overhanging ledge, invisible from all directions, to wait out the night. No fire. He ate cold bacon and hardtack, drank sparingly, and watched stars emerge in a sky unmarked by human light. The mesa was silent except for wind and occasional nighthawk cries. He thought about the camp, the investors who had committed money and reputation to whatever happened there, the gap between scientific knowledge and financial desire.

In the morning, he would learn what he could. For now, he recorded position, checked instruments, and slept fully dressed, hand on his belt revolver.

Dawn came cold and clear. King moved before full light, approaching from the north in a wide circle using terrain for concealment. Halfway there, he saw first evidence of mining activity: excavated earth pile, the source hole still visible. He examined without touching. The soil was wind-blown mesa-top deposit, unconsolidated, lacking weathered profile of natural exposure. The hole was shallow, irregular—more hurried digging than systematic exploration.

He continued. The camp was now fully visible, apparently unoccupied. Tents stood empty, flaps tied back. Horses were gone from the corral. He moved closer, keeping to scattered boulders, until he saw details: cold cooking fire, hours dead; tool pile of picks and shovels; wooden box with hasped lid.

He waited another hour. No one appeared. Silence was absolute.

King emerged and walked openly into the camp. He was trespassing on legally acquired, actively worked property. But his mandate overrode such considerations. The survey was authorized to examine all mineral resources within its assigned territory, and this location fell within that assignment. If challenged, he would identify himself. If unchallenged, he would observe and record.

The camp was orderly, professional in arrangement. The larger tent held bedding for four, a portable desk with writing materials, a box of survey instruments he recognized as standard mining engineer’s equipment. The smaller tent held provisions: canned goods in quantity, flour and bean sacks, a water barrel. The wooden box contained dynamite and blasting caps, properly stored.

He found excavation sites on the mesa rim, a hundred yards from camp. Three shallow pits with scattered excavated material. He examined pit walls, soil character, absence of bedrock exposure. The geology was uniform: wind-deposited sand and gravel, unconsolidated, extending to excavation depth without change. No indication of blue ground characterizing diamond pipes, no evidence of ultrabasic igneous rocks carrying such formations.

King took out his hammer and chipped a pit wall. Material crumbled easily, lacking weathered rock coherence. He collected a sample, bagged it, moved to the next excavation. The pattern was identical. He began understanding what he saw: not mining in any geological sense, but simulation performed on a surface that could not possibly contain the sought minerals.

But the simulation had secured major investment. That required explanation. King walked back to camp, examining ground with greater attention. Near the largest excavation, he found what he had missed: scattered small stones, unusually bright against dull soil. He picked one up, turned it, felt characteristic hardness and weight.

It was a diamond. Or rather, a stone appearing to be diamond, roughly crystalline, showing adamantine luster. He found three more in the immediate vicinity, all similar, all lying on excavated pile surfaces as if freshly exposed.

King sat on his heels and considered. The stones were not native to this geology. They had been placed here, deliberately, recently. The excavation supposedly discovering them was shallow, amateurish, conducted in material showing no structural features indicating mineral deposit. The conclusion was inescapable: diamonds planted, discoveries staged, investors deceived.

But by whom? To what scale? The camp suggested serious purpose, substantial resources. The occupants’ absence was significant—they had left hurriedly, or on scheduled rotation, or for purposes King could not determine. He needed more information before acting.

He searched more thoroughly. In the desk, he found a notebook belonging to Henry Janin, the mining engineer named in newspaper reports. Entries were dated, detailed, recording diamond finds at specific locations with estimated weights and qualities. The language was technical, professional—the work of a man believing he documented genuine discovery. King read with growing comprehension of the deception’s sophistication. Janin was not party to the fraud. He was its victim, his expertise enlisted to provide authoritative endorsement securing investment.

King copied selected entries into his field book, noting dates, locations, described quantities. He replaced the notebook exactly as found. In the portable desk, he also discovered correspondence: letters from San Francisco, New York, referring to mining company organization, ten-million-dollar capitalization, officer and director appointments. The enterprise scale exceeded anything he had imagined. This was no local swindle but a financial operation of national significance, involving institutions and individuals whose credibility would be severely damaged by exposure.

He had seen enough. King gathered equipment and retreated, following a different route than his approach. By midday, he was miles away, moving south across the mesa toward broken country concealing his trail. He did not know if his intrusion was detected, if camp occupants would return to find evidence of his visit. These uncertainties were secondary to his carried information: fraud location, method, scale, names involved.

He walked through afternoon and evening, stopping only when darkness made travel dangerous. In the morning, he resumed, navigating by sun and compass toward the rendezvous where Emmons waited. His mind worked the problem as he walked, fitting discovery into larger western development patterns. The survey had been created to prevent exactly this deception: substitution of speculation for knowledge, promotional narrative for geological fact. But survey methods were slow, publications delayed by years of processing. The fraud exploited this gap between scientific knowledge and commercial urgency, building false confidence in the interval before authoritative information arrived.

King reached the Duchesne River camp on September 21, two days early. Emmons was there with pack animals and fresh supplies. King said nothing until they established evening camp and checked animals. Then, by firelight, he laid out his field book and described what he found.

Emmons listened without interruption. When King finished, he asked only: “What will you do?”

King had considered this during his march south. The obvious answer: report immediately to Washington superiors, initiate official fraud exposure. But reporting channels were slow, bureaucratic process uncertain. By the time his report reached appropriate officials, the company might have completed its stock offering, investments committed, damage irreversible.

Alternatively, he could approach investors directly, present evidence, offer opportunity to withdraw before public scandal. But this risked alerting perpetrators, giving them time to escape or destroy evidence. And it assumed investors would listen to a government geologist against their own hired expert’s contrary testimony.

There was a third possibility. The survey’s mandate included mineral resource assessment for public policy. Attorney General George H. Williams had issued an opinion on August 31, 1872, specifically stating that “valuable mineral deposits” included diamonds. Requested in connection with the 1872 Mining Act governing mineral land disposal, King had received a copy through survey channels. Now he recognized its significance. The diamond claim, if genuine, would fall under federal mineral law. If fraudulent, it represented theft of public confidence implicating government’s own geological authority.

Method would govern his response. The survey had taught him that much. He would document findings with rigor withstanding legal and scientific examination: precise location identification, sample collection, evidence chain connecting planted stones to excavation sites. Only then would he act, designed to expose fraud with maximum authority and minimum evasion opportunity.

King opened his field book to a blank page and began drafting the formal report that would eventually reach the Secretary of the Interior, the Attorney General, the press. He wrote by firelight in the precise hand survey regulations required, describing location, geology, artificial placement evidence, mineral policy implications. Emmons sat across, cleaning his rifle, saying nothing. The night was cold, stars brilliant in unmarked sky.

Three days later, they broke camp together. Plane table and theodolite were packed with chain and pins, specimen boxes and labels. They would measure and map with accuracy that had become the survey’s signature, creating undisputable record, geological description standing against any promotional narrative however skillfully constructed.

The fraud had operated in the space between scientific knowledge and commercial desire. King intended to close that space. His survey, with congressional mandate and published authority, represented verification that could not be purchased or intimidated. The men who planted diamonds in Colorado sand had made a fundamental error: they chose a location falling within the district of the most systematic geological examination ever attempted in North America. The vulnerability Harpending had sensed—the corporate asset in the path of the government survey—was now being measured.

Clarence King, armed with maps and authority, had turned his survey toward the coordinates of Diamond Peak.
