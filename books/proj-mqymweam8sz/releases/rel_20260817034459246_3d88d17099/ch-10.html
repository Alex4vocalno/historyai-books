Chapter 10

The Developer Revolt and Web Standards

In the spring of 2003, the browser that had ignited the internet revolution lived on life support inside AOL Time Warner. Netscape Navigator, the product that had turned Marc Andreessen into a symbol of the new economy and had once commanded over 80 percent of the browsing market, had been reduced to a line item in a sprawling conglomerate’s budget. The numbers told a grim story: Internet Explorer now controlled more than 95 percent of users, and the antitrust remedies that were supposed to restore competition had done little to change that. The consent decree required Microsoft to disclose some APIs and allow computer manufacturers to remove the Internet Explorer icon, but it did not force the company to separate the browser from Windows. The operating system remained a distribution weapon, and the browser remained welded to it. AOL executives, focused on subscriber retention and advertising revenue, saw the browser as a cost center, a relic of a war that had been lost years earlier. The decision was made to end commercial development of Netscape and dispose of the asset. It was a threshold moment, not because a company was retreating from a market, but because of what would rise from the ashes.

AOL’s exit was not a simple shutdown. The company, recognizing the value of the codebase and the open-source community that had been nurturing it since 1998, agreed to provide a two-million-dollar grant and transfer the intellectual property to a new entity. In July 2003, the Mozilla Foundation was born, a nonprofit organization with a mission to steward the open web. It was an unusual outcome: a corporation, having failed to monetize a product, essentially handed it over to a community that saw it as a public utility. The foundation was small, underfunded, and staffed by a core of engineers who had been working on the open-source Mozilla project for years. But it had something Microsoft did not: a growing army of contributors who believed the browser was worth fighting for, not as a business, but as infrastructure.

The stagnation of Internet Explorer had created a crisis for the web, and the crisis was not merely about market share. It was about the platform itself. Microsoft, having achieved dominance, had little incentive to invest in the browser. The Internet Explorer team was largely disbanded after the release of version 6 in 2001. The browser received only occasional security patches. New web standards published by the World Wide Web Consortium—CSS2, XHTML, the Document Object Model—went largely unimplemented. The result was a web that was fractured. Internet Explorer 6 contained a host of proprietary features and nonstandard behaviors that web developers were forced to accommodate. Its implementation of Cascading Style Sheets, the language that controlled the visual presentation of web pages, was notoriously buggy and incomplete. Its JavaScript engine deviated from the ECMAScript specification in ways that made cross-browser scripting a nightmare. Developers who wanted to build sophisticated sites found themselves writing layer upon layer of workarounds to accommodate IE’s quirks. The cost was enormous, not in dollars but in time, frustration, and the erosion of the web’s promise as an open platform. The web was stuck, and the people who built it were growing angry.

It was into this vacuum that the Web Standards Project stepped. Founded in 1998 by a group of developers led by Jeffrey Zeldman, the WaSP was not a company or a formal organization. It was a loose coalition of designers, front-end engineers, and independent web builders who had grown tired of the fragmentation. They understood that the web’s power lay in its universality, and that proprietary lock-in was a threat to that universality. Their tool was not code but advocacy. They launched campaigns, published articles, and lobbied browser makers directly. Their most famous effort, the Browser Upgrade Campaign, encouraged developers to stop supporting obsolete browsers and to build sites using web standards. They also engaged with Microsoft, pressing the company to fix the CSS bugs that plagued Internet Explorer 6. As Zeldman later recalled, “Between 1998 and today, we persevered, and ultimately the browser makers listened.” The WaSP’s persistence was a form of collective action. It did not have capital, distribution, or legal power. What it had was the moral authority of the people who actually built the web, and the growing recognition that the browser was not a product to be sold but a platform to be defended.

The fragmentation that the WaSP fought against had a clear economic cause. Microsoft, having won the browser war, had no incentive to fix its bugs or adopt new standards. The browser generated no revenue. It was a cost, and a source of regulatory headaches. The rational business decision, however, had a devastating effect on the web. Without competitive pressure, the platform ossified. The World Wide Web Consortium, the standards body led by Tim Berners-Lee, published specifications that no browser maker supported. The web was not evolving; it was decaying. The developer revolt was not a reaction to a single product failure. It was a response to the structural threat of a proprietary gatekeeper that had stopped innovating. The internet, which had been built on the principle of open protocols, was at risk of becoming a closed, single-vendor platform.

The seeds of the alternative had been planted earlier, in 1998, when Netscape made the radical decision to release its source code to the open-source community. The resulting Mozilla project was ambitious but chaotic. The original Netscape code was a tangled mess, the product of years of rapid development and competitive pressure. The community, after months of struggle, made a painful choice: they would throw out the old rendering engine and build a new one from scratch. The result was Gecko, a fast, standards-compliant layout engine that was completed in 2002. But the Mozilla browser itself remained a heavy, complex application, burdened by the legacy of the Netscape suite, which included a mail client, a newsreader, and other components. A small group of developers within the Mozilla community decided to break free. They took the Gecko engine and built a slender, fast browser around it, stripping away the bloat. The project was called Phoenix, later renamed Firebird, and finally Firefox. It was designed to be a simple, secure, and standards-compliant browsing experience.

When the Mozilla Foundation was formed in 2003, the Firefox project became its central focus. The foundation’s engineers, many of them veterans of the Netscape wars, understood that the browser was not a product to be sold but a piece of infrastructure to be defended. They poured their effort into making Firefox not just a technical achievement but a movement. The foundation’s mission statement read like a manifesto: “to preserve choice and innovation on the internet.” It was a direct challenge to the idea that a single company could control the gateway to the web. The foundation was small, but it had a powerful asset: the open-source community. Thousands of contributors tested code, reported bugs, and spread the word. The Web Standards Project, which had been demanding a standards-compliant browser, suddenly had a champion.

The launch of Firefox 1.0 in November 2004 was a watershed. There was no massive marketing budget, no bundling deal with computer manufacturers. Instead, the foundation relied on the grassroots energy of the web community. The “Spread Firefox” campaign turned users into evangelists. A full-page advertisement in the New York Times, paid for by donations from thousands of supporters, listed the names of the contributors. The campaign was a throwback to the early days of Netscape, but with a crucial difference: it was not about taking market share from a competitor. It was about reclaiming the web for its users. Within a year, Firefox had been downloaded tens of millions of times. Its market share climbed from a rounding error to a measurable percentage, and the cracks in Internet Explorer’s dominance began to show. The browser, in other words, was no longer a solved problem. It was a battlefield again, but this time the terms of the fight had changed. The competition was not about proprietary extensions or platform lock-in. It was about standards compliance, security, and user experience. The developer revolt had succeeded in shifting the industry’s logic.

The shift was not just about Firefox. It was about the broader recognition that the web could not be owned. The Mozilla Foundation’s success proved that a nonprofit, community-driven organization could build a browser that was technically superior to the product of the world’s largest software company. It demonstrated that standards compliance was not a burden but a competitive advantage. Web developers, who had been forced to write workarounds for Internet Explorer, began to build sites that worked perfectly in Firefox, and then added fixes for IE. The power dynamic had inverted. The day-to-day reality of building for the web in the early 2000s was a grind of patching and pretending. The Internet Explorer 6 rendering engine, known as Trident, was riddled with deviations from the W3C specifications, none more infamous than its broken box model. The CSS box model dictates how an element’s width and height are calculated: in the standard model, the content area is separate from padding and borders. Trident, however, placed padding and borders inside the declared width, a decision that broke the layout of any site that expected the standard behavior. To work around this, developers had to resort to a “box model hack,” a piece of CSS code that exploited a parsing bug in Trident to feed it different dimensions than those sent to other browsers.[1] The hack was a clever but fragile solution, and it epitomized the broader problem: the web’s most widely used browser was a minefield of exceptions, and the act of building a site that looked the same across browsers required not just skill but a deep memorization of every quirk. Beyond the box model, IE6’s handling of floats, absolute positioning, and the z-index property generated countless hours of debugging. Its JavaScript engine did not fully support the DOM Level 2 standard, forcing developers to use IE-specific ActiveX objects for dynamic content.[2] The cumulative effect was a tax on innovation, a hidden overhead that drained the energy of the web’s creative class. A single layout that could be described in a few dozen lines of clean CSS for a compliant browser often required hundreds of lines of workarounds for IE6, along with conditional comments that branched the codebase. The sense of injustice was palpable: the web had been designed as an open, universal medium, but it was being held hostage by a single vendor’s implementation errors.

The Web Standards Project, which had been founded in 1998 by a core group including Jeffrey Zeldman, Glenn Davis, and George Olsen, saw this state of affairs as a disaster.[3] Zeldman, a designer and writer who had been building websites since the medium’s infancy, became the movement’s most visible voice. In his 2003 book “Designing with Web Standards,” he argued that the only way forward was to abandon the “write once, test everywhere” chaos and demand that browser makers adhere to the standards they had agreed to support.[4] The book became a manifesto for a generation of web professionals, selling tens of thousands of copies and providing the intellectual foundation for the revolt. The WaSP’s strategy was to shift the burden of compatibility from the developers to the browser vendors. The Browser Upgrade Campaign, launched in 2001, was a direct intervention. It encouraged developers to place a small message on their sites, informing visitors that they were using an outdated browser and offering links to upgrade.[5] The campaign was controversial because it risked alienating users, but it was a deliberate act of pressure.

The message was not just technical; it was political. It told users that their browser choice had consequences, and it told browser makers that the market would punish non-compliance. The WaSP also engaged in direct lobbying. In 2001, a group of its members, including Zeldman and Eric Meyer, a leading CSS expert, met with representatives of the Internet Explorer team at Microsoft’s Redmond campus.[6] They presented a detailed list of the most egregious CSS bugs in IE6, complete with test cases and suggested fixes. The meeting was cordial, but the results were minimal. Microsoft’s browser team had been gutted, and the remaining engineers were focused on security patches, not on improving standards support. The WaSP’s frustration was compounded by the fact that Microsoft’s own Macintosh Business Unit had built a superb, standards-compliant browser, Internet Explorer 5 for Mac, released in 2000. The Tasman rendering engine in that browser was a model of compliance, supporting CSS1 fully and even pioneering features like the DOCTYPE switch that allowed developers to opt into standards mode.[7] The Mac team had demonstrated that Microsoft could build a great browser, but the Windows division, which controlled the IE product, showed no interest in backporting those improvements.

The revelation was bitter: the browser monopoly was not a technical necessity but a corporate choice. The WaSP’s members, many of whom were independent consultants and small studio owners, had no institutional power, but they had the moral authority of the people who actually built the web. Their campaign was a form of collective action that harnessed the distributed nature of the web itself, using mailing lists, blogs, and personal websites to spread the word.[8]

Meanwhile, the standards movement found a visual ally in the CSS Zen Garden, a project launched in 2003 by Canadian developer Dave Shea.[9] The Zen Garden was a single HTML page with a fixed structure; designers were invited to submit CSS files that would completely transform its visual appearance without altering the markup. The results were stunning, showcasing the power of separating content from presentation. The project became a global phenomenon, with hundreds of designs submitted from around the world, and it proved definitively that web standards could produce beautiful, complex layouts. It was a direct counter to the argument that IE’s quirks were necessary for advanced design. The Zen Garden, along with Zeldman’s book, created a cultural shift within the web design profession. Standards-based design was no longer just a technical preference; it was an aesthetic and ethical stance. The revolt was not just about making sites work; it was about reclaiming the web as a creative medium unencumbered by proprietary lock-in. The WaSP also created the Acid Test, a small webpage that tested a browser’s compliance with several key CSS features. The original Acid1 test, released in 1999, was a simple page that, when rendered correctly, would display a smiley face. If a browser failed, the face would be distorted.[10] The test became a benchmark for the industry, a simple visual check that could be run by anyone. It was a clever piece of advocacy: it turned an abstract standards debate into a concrete, shareable image. The Acid1 test was followed by Acid2 in 2005, which targeted more advanced CSS features, but even the first test stood as a symbol of the movement’s demand for accountability.[11] The WaSP’s “CSS Samurai” group, a volunteer task force led by developer Todd Fahrner, maintained a database of bugs in major browsers and tracked their resolution.[12] They were relentless, filing bug reports, publishing test suites, and publicly shaming vendors who failed to fix their errors. The work was unpaid, often done after hours, but it was driven by a conviction that the web was a public good that required guardianship.

The open-source counterweight to Internet Explorer was being built in parallel, and its development was a story of technical audacity. When Netscape released its source code in 1998, the Mozilla project was born, but the codebase was a mess.[13] The engineers, led by Mozilla. org’s initial staffer Jamie Zawinski, quickly realized that the existing rendering engine, originally written for the quick-and-dirty Netscape 4, could not be salvaged.[14] The code was a tangle of legacy patches, and attempts to modernize it proved futile. The decision was made to build a new engine from the ground up, designed to support the W3C standards from the start. This engine, named Gecko, was a radical departure. It was built to parse HTML and CSS strictly according to the specifications, and it would render pages in “standards mode” by default, only falling back to quirks mode for legacy content.[15] The team, which included figures like Brendan Eich, who had joined Netscape in 1995 to work on JavaScript, and L. David Baron, a key contributor to the CSS specification, embedded a philosophy of compliance into the code.[16] The development was arduous: the first milestone releases of the Mozilla browser were slow, memory-hungry, and unstable.

The community, which had been promised a drop-in replacement for Netscape 4, grew frustrated with the delays. The Mozilla 1.0 release in 2002, while a technical achievement, was a suite of applications that included a browser, mail client, and IRC chat. It felt bloated compared to the lean, fast browsers that users remembered from the early days of the web. The real breakthrough came when a small group of developers, frustrated with the bloat, decided to strip the suite down to its essentials. Blake Ross, a young developer who had started working on Mozilla as a teenager, and Dave Hyatt, a former Apple engineer who had been a key contributor to the Safari project, began experimenting with a standalone browser built on Gecko.[17] They called it Phoenix, a name that symbolized the rebirth from the ashes of Netscape. The project was later renamed Firebird and then, after a trademark dispute, Firefox. The browser was designed to be lean, fast, and secure. It introduced features that were novelties at the time: a built-in pop-up blocker, tabbed browsing, and a search bar that integrated with multiple engines.[18] It also had a modular extension system that allowed third-party developers to add functionality without bloating the core.

The philosophy was simple: the browser should do one thing well, and it should respect the user’s privacy and intelligence. The development team, which included Ben Goodger as the lead engineer, operated with a transparency that was alien to the proprietary software world. Bug reports were public, and nightly builds were available for anyone to test.[19] The community became a massive quality assurance force, finding and fixing issues faster than any corporate testing team could.

The launch of Firefox 1.0 in November 2004 was not just a product release; it was a community event. The Mozilla Foundation, lacking a marketing budget, turned to the grassroots for support. The “Spread Firefox” website was launched, a hub where volunteers could sign up to promote the browser.[20] The campaign’s centerpiece was a full-page advertisement in the New York Times, which was funded entirely by donations from the community. The ad, which ran on December 16, 2004, featured a bold message: “Are you fed up with your web browser? You’re not alone. We’re Firefox, and we’re here to help.” It listed the names of the 10, 000 donors who had contributed between $10 and $30 to make the ad possible, a physical manifestation of the distributed power of the open-source movement.[21] The ad cost $250, 000, a sum that would have been a rounding error in Microsoft’s marketing budget, but the fact that it was raised through small donations made it a symbol of the movement’s legitimacy. The ad was a declaration of independence from the corporate monopoly. It told the world that a browser built by a nonprofit and funded by its users could challenge the world’s largest software company. The response was overwhelming: Firefox 1.0 was downloaded over a million times in its first 24 hours, and within a year it had captured more than 10 percent of the global market.[22] The Spread Firefox campaign also included viral tactics like “Get Firefox” buttons that webmasters placed on their sites, linking to the download page. These buttons, often styled with a proud, counter-cultural flair, became a badge of membership in the open web movement. The campaign was a throwback to the early days of the internet, when word-of-mouth and personal recommendation drove adoption, but it was amplified by the network effects of the blogosphere. The monopoly was not broken overnight, but the psychological barrier had been shattered. Internet Explorer was no longer inevitable.

The impact of Firefox on the web development community was profound and immediate. For years, the default practice had been to build a site for Internet Explorer, and then, if time permitted, add a few hacks to make it work in other browsers. Firefox inverted that logic. Developers began to build sites that were standards-compliant first, using Firefox as their primary testing environment, and then they wrote conditional stylesheets to fix the display in IE6. The phrase “IE6 is the new Netscape 4” began to circulate, a wry recognition that the browser that had once been the center of the universe was now the legacy outlier.[23] The Web Standards Project’s Acid Test became a benchmark for the industry. As Firefox and other browsers passed it, the pressure on Microsoft to improve its rendering grew. The developer revolt was not just a matter of protest; it was a change in the production process. The web was being built by people who chose their tools based on standards support, and that choice had economic weight. The sites that looked best in Firefox were the ones that worked best across all platforms, and the competitive advantage of the open web was becoming visible to every user who switched. The security crisis of the IE6 era, which saw a seemingly endless stream of vulnerabilities exploited by malware, also drove users to Firefox. The browser’s pop-up blocker, its isolated security model, and its rapid patch cycle made it a safer choice, and the Mozilla Foundation’s transparent handling of security issues built trust. The shift was not instantaneous, but it was unmistakable. The monopoly had been contested not by another corporation offering a proprietary alternative, but by a collective of developers, designers, and users who insisted that the web itself was the platform worth protecting.

The chapter of the developer revolt closed with a new order taking shape. The Mozilla Foundation had proven that a nonprofit could sustain a browser ecosystem through a combination of community contributions, corporate partnerships, and a search engine deal with Google, which provided revenue for the default search bar integration. The Web Standards Project, having achieved its primary goal of forcing browser makers to commit to standards, began to shift its focus to education and advocacy for the next generation of web technologies. The browser wars were not over, but the mechanism of innovation had been fundamentally altered. No longer would the web’s evolution be dictated by the strategic calculations of a single platform vendor. The open architecture of the web, embodied in the specifications of the W3C and the interoperable implementations of the open-source community, had become the new engine of progress. The revolt had been a decentralized, multi-year insurgency, fought not in boardrooms or courtrooms, but in markup, style sheets, and user downloads. And it had won. The web was now a public good, guarded by the people who built it.
