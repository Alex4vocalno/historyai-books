Chapter 7

The Ad Hoc Committee (October 16-17, 1962)

A military aide stepped into the office of the Special Assistant for National Security Affairs and placed a single sheet of paper on the desk. McGeorge Bundy looked up from the other cables he was reviewing. The paper was blank except for a routing slip and a timestamp; the aide then handed him a second, sealed envelope.

Inside was a one-paragraph memorandum, already stamped ‘TOP SECRET—EYES ONLY.’ Bundy broke the seal. The time was 3: 00 p. m. EDT on Tuesday, October 16, 1962.

The subject line was “Establishment of Executive Committee of the National Security Council.” This was not a report or a transcript. It was an instruction, and its arrival transformed Bundy’s role in that instant. He was no longer merely an advisor who had been in a chaotic meeting; he was now a designated member of a standing committee, tasked by this document with helping to “direct the day-to-day conduct of the government’s affairs during the present crisis.”

The first action of the newly born bureaucracy was this physical delivery, putting the charter into the hands of the man who would help run it.

The memorandum, National Security Action Memorandum (NSAM) 196, was brief and direct. “The President has today established an Executive Committee of the National Security Council,” it stated, “to advise him on all matters relating thereto.” It named its members: the President, the Vice President, the Secretaries of State and Defense, the Attorney General, the Director of Central Intelligence, the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the Special Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs, and the Secretary of the Treasury.

It was a list of titles, not personalities, formalizing the ad-hoc gathering of the morning into an official body. Copies were distributed to each man named. The routing was precise, the distribution list tiny.

This was the first brick in a wall of secrecy that would separate the committee’s deliberations from the rest of the government, and from the world.

Its issuance was an administrative act, but its effect was constitutive: it turned a temporary discussion into a permanent fixture with rules, a name, and a purpose.

The memo did not emerge from a vacuum. It was the direct, paper consequence of the assignments President Kennedy had given at the end of that first, chaotic meeting. The group had dispersed with no policy, but each member carried away a specific assignment to generate documents. The Secretary of Defense was to prepare detailed military options. The Secretary of State was to draft diplomatic warnings and explore alliance reactions. The Attorney General was to examine the legal basis for action. The Director of Central Intelligence was to intensify surveillance and refine estimates. They had left not with a decision, but with homework.

NSAM 196 was the administrative answer to that scattering of effort. It pulled those disparate threads back into a single, sanctioned channel. It declared that the homework would now be collected and graded in a dedicated, secure classroom called the Executive Committee.

The establishment of the Executive Committee, or ExComm, was an exercise in improvised bureaucracy. The crisis had revealed no pre-existing structure capable of handling it. The standard machinery of the National Security Council was too large, too slow, and too leaky. The need was for a small, flexible, and utterly secret cell.

The paperwork did not merely describe this new entity; it called it into being. The memo’s existence authorized its members to meet, to receive classified intelligence, to command resources, and to produce recommendations under the mantle of presidential authority. From this point forward, every discussion, every option, every disagreement would be funneled through this committee and recorded in its name. The paper trail was no longer just a record of events; it was becoming the framework within which events would be made.

The first order of business for this new framework was to ensure its own survival. Secrecy was not an abstract principle; it was a tactical necessity.

If news of the missile discovery leaked before the administration had chosen a response, it would lose all control over the political and diplomatic tempo.

The initial security protocols were therefore draconian. Meetings were to be held in the Cabinet Room or the President’s private quarters. No written agenda would circulate beyond the members. All notes taken by aides were to be collected and destroyed at the end of each session. The only permanent record would be the secret tape recordings President Kennedy had authorized and the formal memoranda that resulted from decisions. Even the name “Executive Committee” was a bland cover, designed to attract no special attention if glimpsed on a schedule. The committee would live in a bubble of classified paper, its reality known only to those whose names were on the distribution list for documents like NSAM 196.

This bureaucratic improvisation immediately generated its own paper stream. The first structured agenda for an ExComm meeting, crafted on Wednesday, October 17, replaced the freewheeling debate of the previous day with assigned topics.

It listed items like “Evaluation of Latest Intelligence,” “Status of Military Options,” and “Allied Consultations.” Each topic was assigned to a member or a sub-group. The agenda was a tool for imposing order on chaos, for translating shock into a procedural checklist. It acknowledged that the problem was too vast to be swallowed whole; it had to be digested in pieces, each piece assigned to a different part of the bureaucratic digestive tract.

The most significant procedural innovation was the rapid formation of sub-committees. The full ExComm was too large for detailed technical work. Almost immediately, it spawned smaller working groups focused on military, diplomatic, and intelligence problems. These groups were not created by a grand design, but by immediate necessity. The military sub-committee, for instance, coalesced around Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara and the Joint Chiefs. Their task was to convert the abstract concept of an “air strike” or an “invasion” into actionable plans with timetables, force levels, and casualty estimates. They did not debate whether such actions were wise; they worked to make them possible on paper.

Each sub-committee became a specialized factory, taking in raw intelligence and producing polished option papers. The paper produced by these factories began to shape the reality they were analyzing.

A memorandum from the military group detailing the steps for a “surgical air strike” against the missile sites did not just describe an option; it made that option more concrete, and therefore more plausible, for the policymakers who read it. The very act of drafting the plan required the military to answer questions it might otherwise have avoided: How many sorties? What munitions? What is the expected rate of success? What are the probable Soviet casualties? The answers, once committed to paper, took on a weight of their own. They hardened a concept into a potential reality.

Similarly, a State Department paper outlining the text of a speech to the Organization of American States seeking support for a “quarantine” started to give legal and diplomatic form to what had been, only hours before, merely an idea voiced in frustration by the Attorney General.

This torrent of paperwork served a psychological function as much as an administrative one. Activity substituted for certainty. Generating documents gave the men in the Cabinet Room a sense of momentum, of progress. They were doing something, even if that something was largely writing and discussing other things they might do. The machinery gave them roles: they were not just advisors in a room, but chairmen of sub-committees, authors of memoranda, reviewers of draft cables. The crisis was not just happening to them; they were building an apparatus to process it.

This improvised bureaucracy became a mechanism for managing their own fear and uncertainty. The facade of command, constructed from meeting minutes and option papers, helped them believe command was possible.

The documents also reveal the profound gaps in understanding that this busywork was trying to bridge. The intelligence sub-committee, for example, grappled with a fundamental and terrifying unknown: were the nuclear warheads for the Soviet missiles already in Cuba? The photographs showed launchers and missiles; they could not show warheads.

Meanwhile, other strands of government activity, not yet fully absorbed into the new ExComm system, continued on their own trajectories.

The covert action program against Cuba, codenamed Operation Mongoose, was a case in point. Attorney General Robert Kennedy directed this program with the goal of destabilizing the Castro regime.

It was a separate paper stream—a separate bureaucracy—that now intersected catastrophically with the missile crisis. As the ExComm was being formalized on October 16 and 17, Mongoose operations were still underway. The operation was suspended on October 30, 1962, but three of ten six-man sabotage teams had already been deployed to Cuba.

The historian Dominguez writes that Kennedy put a hold on Mongoose’s actions as the Cuban Missile Crisis escalated, as pictures of Soviet nuclear weapons stationed ninety miles from Florida made earlier plans for subversion seem tragically quaint. But in those first two days of the crisis, the machinery of sabotage was still running, its operators unaware that the president’s attention had been seized by a threat that rendered their work not just irrelevant but dangerously provocative. The right hand was frantically designing a committee to control nuclear escalation, while the left hand was still sending men ashore to blow up a power plant. The paper trails had not yet converged.

This disconnect illustrates a central tension. The improvised bureaucracy of the ExComm was designed to centralize control and rationalize decision-making under extreme pressure. Yet its very creation could not instantly corral all the moving parts of a vast government. Older paper trails, authorizing older missions, continued to unwind until explicitly countermanded by new documents from the new committee.

Control was not established by fiat, but through the gradual, sometimes lagging, issuance of superior paperwork that redirected or halted existing processes. The crisis management was therefore not a clean switch from disorder to order, but a messy overlap where new procedural systems raced to overtake older operational ones.

By the end of Wednesday, October 17, the improvised bureaucracy was fully operational. It had a founding charter, a secure meeting space, a formal agenda, and a set of working groups churning out papers. The initial shock had given way to a deliberate, almost frantic, generation of procedural documents. The energy had shape now—a tightening spiral of analysis and recommendation.

But for all its activity, the committee had not yet made a single decision about what to do. It had only decided how to decide. The real test would come when the products of this new machinery demanded a choice.

The pressure point shifted subtly. It was no longer the paralyzing shock of discovery. It was now the impending deadline imposed by their own machinery. The sub-committees had received assignments.

The proliferation of option papers from these sub-committees did more than just inform the debate; it began to construct the very pathways along which a decision would have to travel. A draft speech for the Organization of American States, for instance, was not merely a collection of paragraphs. In committing the concept of a “quarantine” to a formal text, lawyers had to parse the nuances of international law, diplomats had to gauge the reactions of allied capitals, and speechwriters had to calibrate phrases for global audiences. Each edited draft, circulated on secure distribution lists, subtly cemented the idea’s legitimacy. It moved from a speculative notion in the Attorney General’s mind to a documented course of action, complete with legal justifications and procedural steps.

This was bureaucracy not as a passive recorder, but as an active composer of reality. Similarly, the military option papers forced precision onto terrifying abstractions. To write the plan for a “surgical strike,” Pentagon planners had to designate specific squadrons, ordnance loads, and flight paths. The resulting document presented the President not with a vague concept of war, but with a meticulously timed sequence of events, each box on a Gantt chart representing the moment an American pilot might die or a Soviet technician might be killed. The paper gave the option a terrifying tangibility, making the hypothetical feel inevitable.

This frenetic document production also served to mask a deeper, more unsettling truth: for all its structured activity, the committee was operating within a cage of profound uncertainty. The intelligence sub-committee’s papers meticulously categorized what was known, what was suspected, and what was merely feared. Yet the most critical questions—Khrushchev’s ultimate intent, the location of nuclear warheads, the Soviet Union’s precise red lines—remained shrouded.

The bureaucracy, in its diligent way, formalized this ignorance. Charts listed gaps in knowledge alongside confirmed facts, creating an illusion of comprehensive understanding where none existed. The committee was, in effect, building a detailed map of a dark room, carefully sketching the few walls they could touch while vast, unseen spaces stretched around them.

This procedural diligence was a defense mechanism against the void of the unknowable, a way to maintain operational morale by focusing on soluble, technical problems rather than the grand, terrifying imponderables of nuclear brinkmanship.

The formalization of the ExComm also created an immediate, if unintended, hierarchy of information and influence. The distribution list for NSAM 196 and subsequent agendas was a map of power.

Their reports were coming due. The military group would have to present its strike plans with concrete numbers. The diplomatic group would have to produce a draft ultimatum to Khrushchev. The intelligence group would have to deliver its latest—and still incomplete—assessment of Soviet capabilities and intentions.

These papers would land on the Cabinet Room table not as abstract ideas, but as finished products of institutional labor, each with the weight of professional consensus behind it. They would demand comparison, evaluation, and choice.

The bureaucracy, invented to manage uncertainty, was now producing its own certainties—plans on paper that begged to be used. The act of creation had generated its own imperative. Having built a factory to produce options, the men in charge now had to face the products coming off its line, knowing that selecting one would set in motion events far beyond the controlled environment of their secret meetings.

The paperwork had given them a framework for control. Now it would present them with the burdens of command. The next meeting would not be another exploratory discussion.

It would be a review of finished staff work, where every option had received flesh and bone from the very committee created to devise them. The paper was no longer just a tool for thought; it was becoming a force for decision.
