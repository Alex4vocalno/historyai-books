Chapter 15

The Second Letter from Moscow

The long, typed message arrived in Washington as a document before it became a signal. Its routing was official: from the Kremlin, through Soviet and American diplomatic channels, to the White House, where it was translated in the early hours of Saturday, October 27. Its dateline was Moscow, October 26, 1962.

Its author was identified as N. Khrushchev. Its recipient was President Kennedy. The paper itself, with its lines of Cyrillic typescript and the official stamps of its journey, was the first concrete fact. Its text, once rendered into English, began not with a statesman’s formalities but with a confession of fear.

“I think you will understand me correctly if you are concerned about your country, as I am about mine,” the letter stated. It spoke of the “catastrophic means of modern warfare” and invoked a sensory image of doom: “the smell of burning.” The prose was emotional, discursive, and deeply personal. It recounted the history of Soviet aid to Cuba, framed not as aggression but as the fulfillment of a pledge to protect a small nation from invasion.

It insisted the weapons were defensive. But then, in its winding narrative, it offered a way out.

The proposal was specific. “If assurances were given that the President of the United States would not participate in an attack on Cuba and the blockade lifted, then the question of the removal or destruction of the missile sites in Cuba would be an entirely different question.” Khrushchev elaborated: “We are willing to remove from Cuba those means which you regard as offensive. We are willing to carry this out and make this pledge in the United Nations.”

The condition was a public American pledge never to invade Cuba. In return, under UN supervision, the missiles would be withdrawn.

For the exhausted men of the Executive Committee, convening that Saturday morning, this private letter from the Soviet Premier appeared to be the breakthrough they had been waiting for. It was not a formal diplomatic note but a personal appeal, a signal from one leader to another, bypassing the hardened rhetoric of state radio and official press releases.

Its tone suggested a man under pressure, seeking a face-saving exit. Its terms were clear and, from the American perspective, manageable: a no-invasion pledge in exchange for the removal of the missiles. It aligned perfectly with the public position Kennedy had staked out. Here was a document that seemed to map a path away from the abyss.

The document’s arrival through formal channels found an echo in another line of communication, one that was entirely unofficial. Since Thursday, October 25, ABC News correspondent John Scali had been meeting secretly with Aleksandr Fomin, a senior official at the Soviet Embassy in Washington who was widely understood to be a senior KGB officer. Fomin had approached Scali with a proposal that mirrored, in simpler terms, the offer Khrushchev would make in his letter: remove the missiles under UN inspection in return for a no-invasion pledge. Scali had relayed this to the State Department.

On Friday night, as Khrushchev’s letter was being transmitted, Secretary of State Dean Rusk asked Scali to meet Fomin again. Rusk wanted Scali to convey that the U.

S. Government saw “real possibilities” in the proposal, but that time was desperately short. Scali did so at a coffee shop near the State Department. Fomin, according to Scali’s report, had seemed “exhausted” and “emotionally overwrought,” repeating that war seemed only hours away.

This backchannel whisper seemed to corroborate the desperate sincerity of the official letter. The paper trail and the human contact told the same story: Moscow was ready to deal.

The ExComm meeting that began at 10: 00 a. m. On October 27 opened with this document as its centerpiece.

The transcript of their discussion captures a moment of cautious, weary relief. They analyzed Khrushchev’s language. President Kennedy noted the letter’s emotional character. Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara observed that it contained “not a word about Turkey or anything else.” It was, they agreed, an offer they could work with.

Instructions were drafted for a reply accepting the basic framework: if the USSR removed its offensive weapons under UN verification, the United States would end the quarantine and give assurances against an invasion of Cuba.

The machinery of the American government began to orient itself toward this single, seemingly clear signal. The pressure of the last eleven days seemed, for a few hours, to have produced a coherent resolution.

Then the second document arrived. It did not come through diplomatic cables or secret meetings. It came through the air, on public radio waves, broadcast to the world.

At 9: 00 a. m. Eastern Daylight Time—while the ExComm was already meeting—Radio Moscow began transmitting a new message from Chairman Khrushchev to President Kennedy.

This message was formal, cold, and utterly different in substance from the private letter of the night before. The broadcast text, dated October 27, 1962, proposed a trade.

“You are worried over Cuba. You say that it worries you because it lies at a distance of 90 miles across the sea from the shores of the United States. However, Turkey lies next to us,” it stated. “You have stationed devastating rocket weapons, which you call offensive, in Turkey, literally right next to us.”

The solution it offered was a blunt quid pro quo: “The Soviet Union, in turn, would promise to remove from Cuba the means which you regard as offensive… and would make a pledge in the United Nations to that effect. The United States, on its part, would promise to remove its analogous means from Turkey.”

The juxtaposition was immediate and catastrophic to the morning’s fragile consensus. The private letter had proposed a bilateral deal focused solely on Cuba. The public broadcast now linked the Caribbean crisis directly to the NATO arsenal on the Soviet Union’s southern flank.

One document offered a simple exit. The other demanded a complex and politically explosive bargain.

They could not both be true final positions. One had to be wrong, or superseded, or a lie.

The ExComm transcripts record the sound of understanding shattering. “This is a completely different track,” said one voice. “This is a much tougher one,” said another. The discussion spiraled into confusion and alarm. They had been analyzing a personal appeal for peace.

Now they were confronting what sounded like a public ultimatum for a missile swap. Which document represented the true will of the Kremlin? Had the private letter been a genuine offer that had now been overruled by a hardened faction in the Presidium? Was the public broadcast the real position, and the private letter merely a feint or a trial balloon? Or was this evidence of a chaotic internal Soviet debate, with different documents reflecting different factions scrambling for control?

John Scali was summoned to the State Department and sent back to Fomin in a fury. According to Scali’s later account, he accused Fomin of a “stinking double cross.” Fomin, bewildered, could only plead ignorance of the new broadcast and mutter about “poor communications” within his government. His explanation was dismissed in Washington as not credible.

The backchannel, which had seemed to corroborate the first letter, now only deepened the mystery. It revealed not coordination, but disarray. The American analysis, as captured in the ExComm tapes, settled on a specific interpretation.

The shift likely stemmed from an internal Soviet debate. The private letter of October 26 reflected one impulse—probably Khrushchev’s own—toward a quick, face-saving resolution using only the Cuban context. The public letter of October 27 reflected another—a harder line, possibly from military or political rivals arguing that the USSR should not retreat from Cuba without extracting a tangible, equivalent concession from the United States. The pressure of time and competing bureaucratic voices within the Soviet leadership had produced this dangerous documentary fracture. It was not a calculated strategy of duplicity. It was improvisation under extreme stress, and the paper trail showed two governments, not one, struggling to interpret its meaning. The two letters now forced a brutal choice upon President Kennedy. Accepting the private proposal meant a swift end to the crisis on terms favorable to the U.S. Public posture: a no-invasion pledge for missile removal.

Accepting the public proposal meant openly bargaining away NATO’s Jupiter missiles in Turkey under the explicit threat of nuclear war—a move Kennedy believed would shatter alliance credibility and appear as a capitulation to blackmail. Yet rejecting both paths meant proceeding with a military invasion of Cuba, an action everyone in the room believed would trigger Soviet military retaliation and a high probability of general war.

Kennedy’s judgment, recorded on tape, was succinct. He would be in an “insupportable position” if he ignored the public offer from Moscow and attacked Cuba. The world would see that he had refused a proposed trade and chosen war instead.

The documentary clash had transformed the crisis. It was no longer simply about removing missiles from Cuba. It was now a test of interpreting Soviet intent amidst conflicting signals, where a decision based on a misinterpretation could lead to catastrophe.

The paperwork did not clarify the situation; it multiplied its perils. While the ExComm debated these two documents, other papers continued to flow, representing governmental machines operating on their own logic and timetables.

A cable arrived from the U.S. Embassy in Turkey reporting that its officials had just begun, that very morning, the complex and physically obvious process of de-activating the Jupiter missiles there for routine maintenance. In Moscow, it would be afternoon. To any Soviet intelligence officer watching the Turkish bases, it might look like the United States was beginning to implement the swap broadcast on Radio Moscow before it had even been accepted—a potentially catastrophic misreading of a bureaucratic coincidence.

And another document was being prepared, one that would land with its own terrible force later that same afternoon: a photo-interpretation report confirming that surface-to-air missile sites in Cuba were now fully operational, and that work on the medium-range ballistic missile bases was accelerating, not stopping. The pressure was not pausing for interpretation. It was increasing.

The documentary dissonance exposed a central reality of the crisis. The Soviet government was not a monolith executing a single plan but an ensemble of competing voices—the Presidium, the military, the Foreign Ministry, the KGB—each capable of generating authoritative-looking communications.

The private letter of October 26 bore the hallmarks of Khrushchev’s personal style and his immediate desire to halt the slide toward war. Its routing through official channels gave it weight.

The public broadcast of October 27 carried the tone of a state pronouncement, designed for domestic and international audiences, and it introduced a substantive demand that reflected long-standing Soviet grievances about NATO missiles on its borders.

That this second document emerged less than twenty-four hours after the first suggested not mastery but panic, a frantic effort to improve the terms of retreat after the first offer had already been dispatched.

The American government faced the same institutional fragmentation. The ExComm debated which Soviet document to believe while the Navy maintained the quarantine under rules of engagement that could force a confrontation at any hour, while the Air Force updated invasion plans assuming no deal would be reached, while the State Department drafted cables based on the assumption that the private letter was genuine.

Each branch operated on its own documentary fuel—its own orders, its own intelligence reports, its own procedural mandates. The gap between what the leaders said in the Cabinet Room and what their agencies did in the field was widening with every passing hour. This was governance by paperwork under extreme compression. The documents did not record decisive control; they revealed its absence.

The two letters from Moscow forced Washington to become not just a decision-maker but an archivist and textual critic, trying to authenticate which piece of paper held the real authority. Success or failure would hinge on this act of interpretation.

The two contradictory Soviet positions now forced the definitive, immediate question: which document would the United States believe and answer? To answer the private letter was to risk ignoring what Moscow had proclaimed to the world. To answer the public broadcast was to accept a trade the President found unacceptable. To craft a response that somehow addressed both was an exercise in diplomatic and political triage for which there was no precedent.

The need for a decisive interpretation was no longer an analytical exercise. It was an operational imperative. President Kennedy could not afford to choose wrongly. He could not afford to wait for clarification. The converging pressures of military timelines, diplomatic signals, and political perception had narrowed to this point: a choice between two pieces of paper, each claiming to speak for the other side of the world, with no arbiter but his own judgment of which document was real and which was noise. The next document to emerge from the White House would have to resolve that ambiguity, knowing that any answer would set in motion forces that might already have outpaced the paperwork that described them.
