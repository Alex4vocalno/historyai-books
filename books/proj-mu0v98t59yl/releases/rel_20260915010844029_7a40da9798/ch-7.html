Chapter 7

Against the Rising Tide

────────────────────────────────────────

Dawn light, weak and grey, seeps over the concrete monolith of the station, revealing the intake channel as a deep, damp wound in the earth. Iona stood at the concrete lip with the tide table calculations folded into her jacket pocket, her breath forming small clouds in the February chill. Behind her, the four witnesses arranged themselves in an uneasy semicircle, their faces catching the first flat light. None of them had wanted to come. She had insisted.

The channel below lay exposed, a rare gift of the spring tide. In an hour, perhaps less, the sea would return. For now, the concrete gullet showed its teeth—green algae slick on the slanted walls, rust stains bleeding from the inspection ladder, and at the bottom, a thick layer of black silt that had accumulated over decades. The smell rose up to meet them: seaweed and decay, the estuary’s ancient breath.

Alistair Rossiter stepped forward, his station manager’s posture deliberately casual, hands in the pockets of his waxed jacket. “Miss Kerr, I must reiterate. The silt down there is unstable. You could be trapped. Let me call the harbour master’s office, and we can arrange a proper dredging operation in the summer months when—““I’ve calculated the window.” Iona cut him off without turning. “Lowest tide of the year. Forty-seven minutes remaining before the water returns to this level. By summer, whatever lies in that silt will have shifted. You know that, Ross. You managed this station for twenty-three years.”

The use of his shortened name, the one the old crew used, made his jaw tighten.

Catriona MacAulay stood apart from the men, her wispy grey hair escaping its clips in the morning breeze. She clutched her heavy locket with one hand, her blue eyes fixed on the channel as though hypnotized. “The water… it was just so fast that night,” she whispered. “Callum always said the tide didn’t forgive mistakes.”

Fraser Munro shuffled his feet, his weathered face creased with confusion. “If the tide was present at the time, how could you possibly have witnessed what you claimed to have witnessed?” He scratched his head. “I got it all mixed up. Always did. But I remember the lights. The turbine hall lights were on, even after.”

Ewan Stirling stood rigid, his former duty engineer’s bearing unchanged by retirement. He had confessed to the forgery in the archive the night before, his precise handwriting exposed in the rewritten logs. Now he watched Iona with something like relief, as though he had been waiting decades for someone else to take the next step.

“The log is the authority,” he murmured, almost to himself. “What is written, happened. That’s what we told ourselves. But the log was a lie, and I wrote it.”

Alistair turned on him. “Ewan, you don’t need to—““I do.” Ewan’s voice hardened. “You asked me to record Callum’s shift as normal. You told me the harbour master needed clean logs for the insurance inquiry. I believed you. I wrote what you told me to write, and I’ve carried that for thirty-eight years.”

The word hung in the salt air: you. Not we. You.

Iona felt the familiar pressure in her chest, the archivist’s compulsion toward completeness. The forged logs proved Callum Reid couldn’t have been on duty when the official record claimed—he had drowned hours before. Ewan’s confession proved the forgery was deliberate. But neither explained why Callum had been near the intake channel in the first place, or what had happened in those final minutes. The missing piece lay somewhere below, in the silt that had swallowed the engineer and his toolkit on February 3rd, 1986.

She turned to face them, four people bound by a night none of them could fully remember or fully forget. “I’m going down. You can watch from here, or you can help. Either way, you’ll witness what I find.”

“The silt is unstable,” Alistair repeated, but his voice had shifted. The concern now sounded like desperation. “You could be trapped. People have died in estuary mud. It’s not worth the risk for some… some fanciful annotations in a catalogue.”

“This isn’t about the catalogue.” Iona pulled on her gloves. “Callum Reid’s body was recovered thirty metres from where we’re standing. His toolkit was never found. The accident report says he was checking the intake screens during a surge event. But the tide tables show the water level that night was wrong for a surge. Something else happened, and that toolkit is the only evidence that hasn’t been touched by human hands since that night.”

Catriona made a small sound. “He was a careful man. He wouldn’t have gone down there without reason.”

“Then let’s find the reason.” Iona moved to the inspection ladder, its rungs orange with rust but solid enough. She tested the first rung with her weight. It held.

Alistair stepped forward, reaching as though to grab her arm, then stopped himself. “Miss Kerr, please. Think about what you’re doing. Whatever you find down there, it won’t bring him back. It won’t change anything that matters.”

“That’s not your decision to make.”

She descended.

The ladder rungs were slick with moisture, the metal cold enough to bite through her gloves. The channel walls rose around her, blocking out the grey sky, narrowing her world to the concrete and the smell and the growing darkness below. Eight rungs. Twelve. Fifteen. Then her boot touched softness, and she sank.

The silt accepted her like a cold embrace, rising past her ankle immediately. She had expected this—had read the harbour master’s reports on the channel composition—but reading was different from feeling the suck and pull of decades of accumulated sediment. It moved like a living thing, thick and reluctant, holding her foot when she tried to lift it.

Iona looked up. The five faces peered over the edge, framed by the rectangle of grey sky. Alistair’s was tight with something that might have been fear or might have been calculation. Catriona’s locket caught the light. Fraser had turned away, muttering to himself. Ewan stood motionless, his confession apparently complete, his role in the night’s drama seemingly finished.

She was alone in the wound of the station.

The calculations said the toolkit should be here, somewhere in a five-metre radius from where Callum’s body had been found. The current patterns, the tide height, the position of the intake screens—all of it pointed to this section of the channel. If Callum had been carrying his tools when he fell, they would have sunk into the silt within moments. Metal and canvas, heavier than a body. They would still be here, buried.

Unless someone had retrieved them.

Iona took a step, feeling the silt shift and settle around her boot. The cold seeped through her trousers, numbing her calves. She moved forward, probing with each foot, searching for anything that wasn’t silt.

Forty-one minutes.

“You don’t have to do this,” Alistair called down. His voice echoed strangely in the concrete throat. “Whatever you think you’ll find, it’s been decades. The tides move everything. Silt shifts. Metal corrodes. You’re risking your safety for nothing.”

Iona didn’t answer. She took another step, feeling her way through the darkness. The channel floor was uneven, hollows and ridges where the water had carved patterns over the years. Her boot scraped against something—a rock, perhaps, or a piece of debris—and she moved on.

The silence from above was heavier than the silt. Four people watching, four people waiting. Four people who had lived with this night for thirty-eight years, each carrying their own version of the truth. Ewan, who had forged the logs. Fraser, whose confused memories kept circling back to lights and tides. Catriona, who had loved the dead man and then learned to love the people who had failed him. And Alistair, who had managed the station then and managed the witnesses now, whose smooth authority had cracked the moment Iona mentioned the channel.

She thought about the logbooks in the archive, the precise handwriting that wasn’t Callum’s. Someone had decided to rewrite those pages. Someone had decided that the official story mattered more than the truth. Ewan had held the pen, but Ewan was a follower, a man who believed in duty and authority. He hadn’t acted alone.

Thirty-six minutes.

The silt deepened as she moved toward the center of the channel. It rose past her knees now, each step a negotiation with the mud. She had to lift her leg high, break the suction, place her foot carefully. The smell intensified, rot and salt and something metallic underneath. Her fingers brushed the surface, feeling the cold grit, and she thought about Callum Reid, about his final moments in this same channel, the water rising, the darkness closing in.

Had he been alone? Had someone been with him? Had someone pushed him, or held him under, or simply watched him drown?

The questions circled like the gulls overhead. Iona pushed them away. She was an archivist, not a detective. Her job was to find the record, not to judge it. The toolkit was the record. The toolkit would speak for itself.

She took another step, and her boot struck something hard.

The sound was different from the scrape of rock, different from the crunch of shell. It was a dull, metallic thunk, muffled by the silt but unmistakable. Iona froze, her heart suddenly loud in her ears.

“Miss Kerr?” Alistair’s voice came from above, sharp with tension. “What is it? What’s happening down there?”

She didn’t answer. She stood perfectly still, feeling the shape beneath her boot. It was long, narrow, partially buried. She shifted her weight, testing. The object didn’t move. It was solid, fixed in the silt, waiting.

Iona knelt, feeling the cold water seep through her trousers, soaking her knees. The silt rose to her waist now as she crouched, pressing against her chest. She reached down with both hands, pushing through the thick mud, searching for the thing her boot had found.

Her fingers touched canvas, sodden and disintegrating. The fabric fell apart at her touch, rotten after decades underwater. She pushed past it, feeling for what lay inside.

Metal. Cold and pitted with corrosion, but unmistakably shaped. A wrench, perhaps, or a spanner. The forms were familiar from her weeks in the archive, surrounded by the station’s equipment records. She knew the inventory of a tidal engineer’s toolkit: adjustable wrench, pipe grips, screwdrivers, inspection mirror, torch. The standard set for intake maintenance.

She dug deeper, her hands numb with cold, her breath coming in short gasps. The silt fought her, pulling at her arms, trying to reclaim what she had found. But she had it now, her fingers closing around a handle, feeling the weight of decades of accumulated sediment and rust.

Iona pulled.

The silt resisted, holding its prize. She pulled harder, feeling the strain in her shoulders, the burn in her muscles. Something shifted, loosened, and then the object came free with a wet, sucking sound that echoed off the concrete walls.

She held it up, a dark shape in the grey light. A canvas roll, or what was left of one, disintegrating as the water drained from it. Tools tumbled from the rotting fabric—a wrench, a screwdriver, a torch with its glass cracked and dark. They fell back into the silt with soft splashes, but Iona kept her grip on the largest piece.

A wrench, heavy and rusted, its adjustable jaw frozen in place. She turned it, feeling the weight, and wiped her thumb across the handle where the corrosion was thickest.

The initials emerged one letter at a time. C.R.

Carved into the metal with the careful precision of a man who wanted to mark his possessions. Callum Reid. His toolkit, buried in the silt where it had fallen, untouched for thirty-eight years.

Iona looked up. The faces above had gone still, their expressions frozen in the moment before understanding. She raised the wrench higher, letting them see.

“Catriona,” she said. Her voice came out hoarse, cracking. “Catriona, look at this. Do you recognize it?”

The older woman had pressed her hand to her mouth. Her eyes were fixed on the wrench, on the initials that emerged from the grime as Iona wiped away more silt. The locket clutched at her throat, her knuckles white.

“That’s his,” Catriona whispered. The sound was barely audible, a choked confession of recognition. “He bought that wrench the summer we got engaged. He said he wanted good tools for the work ahead. He engraved all his things. He was… he was a careful man.”

The words fell into the channel like stones into water. That’s his. The toolkit was his. Callum Reid had been here, in this channel, carrying these tools, on the night he died.

And the official logs said he had been on duty three hours later, signing the register in a hand that wasn’t his own.

Iona began to move toward the ladder, the wrench clutched against her chest. The silt tried to hold her, pulling at her legs, but she fought through it. Twenty-eight minutes remained. The water would be returning soon, creeping back up the channel walls. She needed to get out, to get the evidence to solid ground.

“Help her,” Catriona said, her voice stronger now. “Someone help her out of there.”

Fraser moved first, his confusion momentarily cleared by the urgency of the moment. He knelt at the channel’s edge, extending his hand. Ewan followed, his former engineer’s hands steady despite everything. Iona reached the ladder, thrust the wrench into Fraser’s grip, and began to climb.

The rungs were slicker now, coated with the silt from her gloves. Her arms burned with the effort, her legs numb from the cold. But she climbed, rung by rung, until Fraser’s hand closed around her wrist and hauled her over the lip onto solid concrete.

She lay on her back, gasping, the grey sky spinning overhead. The smell of the silt clung to her, thick and cloying. Her hands were shaking.

The wrench lay between them, its rusted surface dark with moisture. The initials C.R. caught the flat morning light, visible to everyone who cared to look.

Alistair Rossiter had not moved. He stood apart from the group, his face ashen, his hands trembling in his pockets. The smooth manager’s composure had cracked and fallen away, leaving something raw and frightened underneath.

“Where,” he said, and his voice came out hoarse. “Where exactly did you find that?”

“Three metres from the intake screens. Where the current deposits heavy objects during surge events.” Iona sat up, wiping silt from her face. “Where Callum Reid’s body was recovered. Where he shouldn’t have been if the official logs were accurate.”

“The log is the authority,” Ewan said quietly. “What is written, happened. That’s what you told me, Ross. That’s what you said.”

Alistair turned on him. “Ewan—““No. No more.” Ewan’s voice was steady, but his hands were clasped in front of him, white-knuckled. “I wrote what you told me to write. I said Callum was on duty at midnight. But he was already dead. We both knew it. We both knew it, and I wrote the lie anyway.”

Fraser was muttering again, his hands pressed against his head. “If the tide was there, how could you have seen what you said you saw? I remember the lights. I remember Callum going down to the channel. I remember you coming back up, Ross. You were wet. You said you’d tried to save him, but you were wet, and Callum was—““Fraser.” Alistair’s voice cut through the old man’s confusion. “You got it wrong. You said so yourself. You always get it mixed up.”

“Did he?” Iona stood, her legs unsteady beneath her. She picked up the wrench, holding it between them like an accusation. “Fraser remembers you coming up from the channel, wet, on the night Callum died. Ewan remembers you asking him to forge the logs. Catriona remembers the water being ‘so fast that night,’ faster than the tide tables say it should have been. And I’ve found Callum’s toolkit in the silt, exactly where it would be if he’d been working on the intake screens when someone opened the surge gates.”

The words fell into silence. The only sound was the distant cry of gulls and the faint whisper of the returning tide, water beginning to seep back into the channel below.

Catriona stepped forward, her face pale, her locket clutched in her fingers. “They were all so good to me, after. Why would you want to drag all that up?” Her voice cracked. “But I need to know. I’ve needed to know for thirty-eight years. What happened to him? What really happened in that channel?”

She turned to Alistair, and her eyes were clear, perceptive, seeing through decades of gratitude and grief to the truth underneath. “You told me it was an accident. You told me Callum slipped, that the water took him before anyone could reach him. You told me you tried to save him. But Fraser says you came up wet. And now there’s a toolkit in the silt that proves Callum was where the logs say he couldn’t have been.”

“Catriona.” Alistair’s voice was barely a whisper. “Please. You don’t understand what was at stake.”

“Then make me understand.” She didn’t shout. Her voice was steady, almost gentle, and that steadiness was worse than anger. “Make me understand why my fiancé died. Make me understand why you’ve lied to me for thirty-eight years. Make me understand why I’ve been thanking you for your comfort when you were the one who needed forgiving.”

The water was rising in the channel now, dark and cold, reclaiming the wound in the earth. The toolkit lay on the concrete between them, its rusted surface dripping, its initials catching the light. Callum Reid’s mark, carved into metal, speaking from beyond the grave.

Iona watched Alistair Rossiter’s face. She saw the struggle there, the decades of lies pressing against the need to explain, to justify, to be understood. His smooth authority had abandoned him. What remained was a frightened man who had carried a secret for too long.

“The surge gates,” he said finally. His voice was flat, mechanical, as though reading from a report. “They were malfunctioning. Callum went down to check them. I was supposed to be monitoring the control panel. The water rose faster than we expected.”

“That’s not what the tide tables show,” Iona said. “The water level that night was wrong for a natural surge.”

“No.” Alistair closed his eyes. “It wasn’t natural. Someone opened the gates manually. I don’t know if it was a mistake or… or something else. But the water came in, and Callum was in the channel, and by the time I got down there, it was too late. He was gone. The current had him.”

“So you let him die?” Catriona’s voice was still steady, but tears were running down her face.

“I tried to reach him. I swear I tried.” Alistair opened his eyes, and they were wet. “But the water was too fast, and I was afraid. I climbed back up, and I told Ewan what had happened, and he said… he said we had to get the story straight. The harbour master would ask questions. The insurance would investigate. If they knew the gates had been opened manually, if they knew I’d been on duty when it happened…”

“You rewrote the logs,” Iona said. “You had Ewan forge the records to show Callum on duty hours after his death. You created a story where the accident happened during normal operations, not during an unauthorized manual override.”

“It was for the station.” Alistair’s voice cracked. “Kessock was new. Tidal power was experimental. One death could have shut us down. I told myself I was protecting jobs, protecting the future of the technology. I told myself it was a noble lie.”

“And you’ve been protecting that lie ever since,” Iona said. “Managing the witnesses. Keeping Fraser confused. Keeping Catriona grateful. Keeping Ewan complicit.”

“I protected all of them.” Alistair’s voice hardened, defensive. “Ewan kept his pension. Fraser kept his job. Catriona… I made sure she was taken care of. I became her friend, her support. I’ve spent thirty-eight years trying to make up for what happened.”

“You can’t make up for murder,” Catriona said softly.

“It wasn’t murder!” Alistair stepped forward, his composure shattering. “It was an accident. A terrible, stupid accident. I didn’t kill Callum. I just… I just failed to save him. And then I failed again, by lying about it. By making all of you complicit in the lie.”

The water was rising faster now, dark tendrils creeping up the channel walls. The silt that had held Callum’s toolkit for thirty-eight years was disappearing beneath the surface, hiding its secrets once more. The tide was returning, as inevitable as the truth.

Iona looked down at the wrench in her hands. It was heavier than she had expected, the rust rough against her palms. The initials C.R. were clear now, the proof that would force the final reckoning.

“We need to document this,” she said, her archivist’s voice returning. “All of it. The toolkit, the location, the condition. Ewan’s confession. Fraser’s testimony. And what you’ve just told us, Ross. All of it goes into the record.”

“You can’t.” Alistair’s voice was desperate. “The statute of limitations—there’s no murder charge, not after this long. But the civil liability, the damage to reputations, to the station’s legacy—““The catalogue will be finalized as-is.” Iona met his eyes. “With everything we’ve found. Every document, every testimony, every piece of physical evidence. That’s my job. That’s what I do.”

She turned away from him, toward the grey light of the morning, the wrench heavy in her hands. Behind her, the four witnesses stood in their separate silences—Catriona weeping softly, Fraser muttering fragments of memory, Ewan rigid with the weight of confession, Alistair ashen with the collapse of his careful lies.

The tide continued to rise, filling the wound in the earth, covering the place where Callum Reid had died. But the truth was out now, pulled from the silt at the year’s lowest tide, and it would not be buried again.

The question was no longer what had happened in the turbine hall on February 3rd, 1986. The question was who would speak first, and what they would say, and whether any of them could live with the answers.

Iona walked toward the station, carrying the dead man’s wrench, and did not look back.
