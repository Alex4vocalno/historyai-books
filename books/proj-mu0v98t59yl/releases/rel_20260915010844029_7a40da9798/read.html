Chapter 1

The Anomaly in Ink

────────────────────────────────────────

A single drop of condensation fell from a pipe onto the leather cover of the 1986 logbook.

Iona Kerr watched it strike, the dark spot spreading across the cracked binding like a bruise. Above her head, the pipe sweated in the damp chill of the archive basement, another symptom of this place’s slow surrender to the estuary that had birthed it. The Kessock Tidal Station had been built to harness the water; now the water was taking it back, one rusted fitting and weeping joint at a time.

She folded her hands on the table, her white cotton gloves lying beside the logbook, and opened it to the first page. January 1st, 1986. The handwriting was cramped, hurried, the letters slanting aggressively to the right. Duty Engineer: C. Reid. The entries were terse, functional. Turbine output nominal. Intake clear. Pressure holding. Each line ended with the same initials, the same hasty scrawl.

Iona turned the pages with careful fingers, her pen hovering over her inventory sheet. The routine of it settled her into a rhythm. January 15th. January 20th. January 28th. The same hand, the same slant, the same hurried pressure of ink into paper. She could see Reid growing tired as the month progressed, the letters becoming looser, the lines slanting more steeply, as if the act of writing was something to be finished quickly so the real work could continue.

Then she turned to February.

Her finger stopped on the first entry of the month. February 1st. Duty Engineer: C. Reid. But the handwriting had changed. She leaned closer, adjusting the angle of the desk lamp. The letters were different—precise, controlled, each one formed with deliberate care. The slant was gone. The hurried pressure was gone. Instead, the script was uniform, almost mechanical, as if someone had taken pains to make each letter match the one before it.

She turned back to January, flipping through the pages she had already inventoried. January 28th: hurried, slanting, tired. January 30th: the same. January55st: the same. Then February 1st: neat, controlled, careful.

Iona sat back, her chair creaking against the stone floor. The difference was unmistakable. She had spent fifteen years working with historical documents, fifteen years learning to read the story that handwriting told beyond the words themselves. This was not the same hand.

She pulled the February log closer, her eyes tracing the entries. February 1st. February 2nd. February 3rd. The same precise script throughout, the same careful uniformity. Each entry dutifully recording turbine readings, intake levels, pressure gauges. Each one signed with the same initials: C. Reid.

But the handwriting wasn’t Reid’s.

Iona’s finger hovered over the February 3rd entry. Duty Engineer: C. Reid. 22: 00 to 06: 00. Turbine output nominal. Intake clear. Pressure holding. The words were calm, routine, professional. Someone had written them with care, someone had signed Reid’s initials, someone had created a record of a man at his post.

She reached for the personnel file included in the archival materials provided for her cataloging work. The folder was thin, the station’s records sparse and poorly maintained. She flipped through it until she found the page she was looking for.

Callum Reid. Senior Duty Engineer. Hired 1978.

And beneath that, in a different hand, a single line: Deceased 3rd February 1986.

Iona’s breath slowed. She looked from the personnel file to the logbook, her mind working through the timeline. Reid had died on February 3rd. But the log showed him working the night shift on February 3rd, from 22: 00 to 06: 00. She turned back to the personnel file, scanning for more information. The death record was sparse, almost dismissive. Cause of death: drowning. Location: intake channel. No time given.

She set the file down and pulled the tide almanac from the stack of reference materials beside her. The 1986 tables were printed on thin paper, the numbers small and dense. She ran her finger down the column until she found February 3rd.

Low tide: 02: 14. High tide: 08: 31. Low tide: 14: 47. High tide: 20: 56.

Iona spread the almanac open beside the logbook, her eyes moving between them. The log showed Reid on duty from 22: 00 on February 3rd to 06: 00 on February 4th. But if he had drowned in the intake channel, he would have been found at low tide—when the water was low enough to reveal the body. The first low tide on February 3rd was at 02: 14 in the morning. The second was at 14: 47 in the afternoon.

She pulled another reference volume from the stack, a slim folder of incident reports from the station’s operating years. Her fingers trembled slightly as she opened it, the paper dry and brittle. There was no formal incident report for Reid’s death—just a brief notation in the station’s operational summary for February 1986.

But there was something else. A photograph, slipped loose inside the back cover. Iona lifted it carefully, holding it under the lamp’s glow. The image was grainy, black and white, but the scene was clear enough. The intake channel, the water drained to a thin stream running between the stone walls. Two figures standing at the edge, their faces blurred by motion or time. And in the foreground, a shape half-covered in silt, the outline unmistakably human.

She turned the photograph over. On the back, someone had written in pencil: “03/02/86. Channel drained for recovery. 06: 45.”

Iona set the photograph down and pressed her palms flat against the desk. The timeline was forming in her mind, each piece clicking into place. Reid’s body had been recovered at 06: 45 on February 3rd, after the morning low tide. That meant he had drowned during the night, when the tide was low, when the channel was accessible. The first low tide had been at 02: 14.

But the log showed him working from 22: 00 on February 3rd to 06: 00 on February 4th.

The math was impossible. Reid had died sometime before 02: 14 on February 3rd, when the tide was low enough to trap him in the channel. By 22: 00 that night, when the log claimed he was starting his shift, the tide had risen and fallen again. The channel would have been submerged at high tide, then draining again. And Reid had been dead for nearly twenty hours.

Iona looked at the logbook again, at the neat, precise script that filled the February entries. Someone had written these entries after Reid died. Someone had signed his initials, created a record of his presence, pretended he was still alive and working. The handwriting mismatch was proof of that much. But the timeline was proof of something worse.

Someone had forged a dead man’s log.

She reached for her inventory sheet, her pen poised to note the anomaly. But her hand stopped. The professional thing to do would be to flag the discrepancy and move on. Handwriting inconsistency noted. Recommend cross-referencing with personnel records. A routine observation for a routine digitization entry.

But this wasn’t routine. The handwriting was only the surface. Beneath it, the timeline revealed something deliberate. Someone had not only written in Reid’s name—they had written entries for shifts Reid could not have worked. They had created a false record, and they had done it carefully, precisely, with the kind of uniform script that suggested someone trying to hide their own hand.

Iona set the pen down. She pulled the February logbook closer, her eyes tracing the entries again. February 1st. February 2nd. February 3rd. Each one in the same careful script. And then she turned the page.

February 4th. February 5th. February 6th.

The handwriting changed again.

She leaned in, her pulse quickening. February 4th showed a different hand altogether—looser, rounder, the letters less controlled. February 5th, the same. By February 6th, the entries were back to something closer to Reid’s original January style, hurried and slanting, though still not quite right.

Iona sat back, her mind racing. The pattern was clear now. Someone had written the February 1st through 3rd entries in a careful, disguised hand. Then, starting February 4th, a different person had taken over—someone with a naturally rounder script, someone who wasn’t trying to hide. By February 6th, the entries had settled into a rhythm that was meant to look like Reid’s but wasn’t.

But Reid had died on February 3rd. The log should have shown someone else on duty from February 4th onward. Instead, it showed Reid’s initials continuing through the entire month.

Iona pulled the personnel file again, scanning the list of station personnel from 1986. There were three duty engineers listed: Callum Reid, Malcolm Douglas, and Alistair Rossiter. She turned to the logbook, checking the signatures for late January. January 28th: C. Reid. January 29th: M. Douglas. January 30th: A. Rossiter. January 31st: C. Reid.

The signatures matched the names. Douglas’s entries were in a loose, round hand—the same hand that appeared on February 4th and 5th. Rossiter’s entries were in a sharp, angular script, each letter precise and deliberate.

Iona’s finger traced the page. Someone had written the February 1st through 3rd entries in a hand that matched neither Douglas nor Rossiter. Someone else entirely.

She was so focused on the page that she didn’t hear the footsteps until they were at the door.

“Impressive collection, isn’t it?”

Iona’s head snapped up. Alistair Rossiter stood in the doorway, his frame silhouetted against the fluorescent light of the corridor beyond. He was older than his personnel file photo, his hair now more silver than brown, but the sharp features were the same. He wore a cable-knit sweater under a waxed jacket, the uniform of a man who had spent his life on the water.

“Mr. Rossiter.” Iona’s voice was steady, but her hand moved instinctively to the logbook, sliding a blank inventory sheet over the open page. “I didn’t expect you today.”

“Routine check.” Rossiter stepped into the archive, his eyes moving across the shelves with the familiarity of long acquaintance. “The station may be decommissioned, but the records are still the property of the board. I like to make sure they’re being handled properly.”

“Of course.” Iona kept her hands visible, her posture professional. “I’m making good progress on the 1986 logs. The earlier years are already inventoried.”

“Excellent.” Rossiter’s gaze lingered on the desk, on the papers spread before her. “Have you reached the mid-eighties yet?”

The question was casual, but something in his tone made Iona’s shoulders tighten. “I’m working through February now.”

Rossiter’s expression didn’t change, but his eyes flickered to the logbook, then back to her face. “February was always a difficult month. The tides, the weather. The channel could be treacherous.”

Iona kept her face neutral. “I noticed some inconsistencies in the entries. Routine discrepancies. I’ll note them in my report.”

“Inconsistencies.” Rossiter repeated the word slowly, as if tasting it. “What kind of inconsistencies?”

“Handwriting variations. Nothing significant.” Iona reached for her pen, her movements deliberate. “The kind of thing you find in any historical document. Different clerks, different shifts. Standard procedure, I suppose.”

Rossiter was quiet for a moment. Then he smiled, a thin expression that didn’t reach his eyes. “You’re very thorough, Miss Kerr. It’s a commendable quality.”

“Thank you.”

“The board appreciates diligent work.” He turned toward the door, then paused, his hand on the frame. “The estuary can be dangerous, you know. The tide comes in faster than people expect. It’s easy to get caught.”

Iona met his gaze. “I’ll be careful.”

“See that you are.” Rossiter’s voice was light, but the words carried weight. “We wouldn’t want any accidents.”

He left, his footsteps echoing down the corridor. Iona didn’t move until the sound faded entirely.

Then she pulled the logbook back toward her, her hands steady despite the cold settling in her chest. She turned to the tide almanac, scanning the columns until she found what she needed.

The next extreme low tide—the kind that would drain the intake channel completely—was in two days. February 15th, if she remembered the current date correctly. 03: 47. The water would be at its lowest, the channel exposed.

Iona checked her watch. Two days. She had two days to verify the site, to see for herself what the records only suggested. Two days before the tide rose again and sealed the channel, hiding whatever evidence might remain in the silt and stone.

She gathered the papers, stacking them neatly, her movements precise. The forgery was certain now, visible on the page in black and white. The handwriting didn’t match. The timeline didn’t match. Callum Reid had died in the intake channel on the night of February 3rd, 1986, and someone had spent the following days writing duty logs in his name, signing his signature, creating a record of a man who could no longer speak for himself.

Iona tucked the gloves from the table into her bag. The question was why. Someone had logged Callum Reid’s shift for 22: 00 that same night, hours after the low tide had already claimed him.

And the answer, she suspected, lay buried in the channel, waiting for the lowest tide.

She switched off the lamp and walked out, the darkness closing behind her. What else had the silt preserved?
