Chapter 9

A True Record

────────────────────────────────────────

Dawn crept through the high windows of the Control Room, pale and reluctant, as if the day itself hesitated to witness what had been set in motion. The light fell across the desk in long, slanting bars, illuminating the chaos of papers and artifacts that had accumulated over nine days of investigation. Dust motes drifted in the golden shafts, suspended in the stillness.

A rust-stained toolkit lay open near the desk’s edge, its leather flap curled back to reveal wrenches and calipers still marked with the reddish-brown of the turbine hall’s decay. Beside it sat the forged logbook, its cover warped, pages fanned open to February 1986. The handwritten entries marched across the page in a careful hand that belonged to no one who had been there that night. A photograph of the turbine maintenance tag had been paper-clipped to a manila folder, the clip’s metal teeth leaving small indentations in the aging paper. The folder itself bore a typed label: “Kessock Station - Incident Documentation.”

Coffee rings stained the desk’s surface in uneven circles, evidence of long hours and insufficient breaks. A portable hard drive sat connected to the laptop, its blue indicator light blinking in slow rhythm, containing digital copies of everything. The air smelled of old paper and cold stone, undercut by the metallic tang that seemed to permeate this place, as if the turbine’s rust had worked its way into the very atmosphere. The fluorescent tubes overhead had been left off, and in the natural light, the room looked smaller, more intimate, a witness box where judgment had already been rendered.

Iona stood before the desk and let the silence settle around her. She had not slept. The confession lay before her, three typed pages signed in Alistair Rossiter’s trembling hand, and she had read it so many times the words had begun to lose meaning. She picked it up anyway.

The paper felt flimsy between her fingers. Rossiter’s signature sprawled at the bottom of the final page, unsteady, the marks of a man who had held a pen with shaking hands.

“Statement of Alistair Rossiter, former Operations Supervisor, Kessock Tidal Station,” she read aloud, her voice flat in the quiet room. “Regarding events of February 3rd, 1986.”

The words washed over her, familiar now. Rossiter had written of the turbine failure, the catastrophic blade fracture that should have been caught during routine maintenance. He had written of Callum Reid, the young engineer who had been dispatched to the intake channel to check the emergency shutoff valves. He had written of the decision to falsify the duty logs, to claim Reid had been working elsewhere, to hide the negligence that had led to a man’s death.

“The maintenance schedule had been deferred,” Rossiter had written. “Budget constraints. We believed we could extend the intervals. We were wrong.”

Iona set the pages down. The confession was thorough, methodical, the kind of document an archivist might produce when finally forced to confront the gaps in their own record. Rossiter had not asked for forgiveness. He had simply told the truth.

She turned her attention to the evidence spread before her. The forged logbook sat open, its false entries a testament to a different kind of methodical work. Someone had carefully rewritten history, matching dates and times to create an alibi that had held for thirty-seven years. The hand was steady, practiced. The signature at the bottom of each page purported to be Reid’s, but she knew now it was not.

The rust-stained toolkit had belonged to Reid. It had been found in the intake channel, recovered from the very spot where his body had been pulled from the water. The logs claimed he had been working in the control room that night, but the toolkit told a different story. It had been left where he died.

Catriona MacAulay’s statement lay in a stack beside the toolkit. The retired administrator had remembered Reid leaving the control room, heading toward the turbine hall. Her memory had contradicted the logs from the beginning.

Fraser Munro’s testimony supported the official record, but only because he had trusted what he was told. He had not been there. He had signed off on reports prepared by others.

Ewan Stirling’s engineering assessment had confirmed what the logs denied: the turbine blade had failed due to deferred maintenance, and the intake channel had been the only place Reid could have been sent to address the emergency.

The four witnesses. Their truths had seemed contradictory at first, but now Iona saw how they fit together. Catriona’s memory of a man walking toward his death. Fraser’s inherited lie, believed in good faith. Ewan’s technical proof, buried for decades. And Rossiter’s confession, the final piece that made sense of all the others.

She began to sort the evidence into a single stack. The forged logbook went on the bottom. Then the photograph of the maintenance tag, the rust-stained toolkit, the witness statements arranged chronologically, and finally Rossiter’s confession on top. The pile was uneven, messy, nothing like the ordered archives she had been hired to create. But it was true.

Iona pulled the laptop closer and opened a new document. Her fingers hovered over the keyboard. She could write a sanitized version. She could describe the forgery without naming names, could frame it as an historical anomaly, a clerical error discovered during routine cataloguing. The archive would remain intact. The station’s sale would proceed. Her professional reputation would be preserved.

She looked at the stack of evidence. The rust on the toolkit had oxidized into patterns like dried blood. The logbook’s false signatures seemed to mock her from across the desk.

“Finalize the catalogue as-is,” she muttered, the phrase hollow now. She had said those words so many times in her career, taking pride in the clean completion of each project. But this catalogue could not be finalized. Not as-is. Not as-it-had-been-written.

She began to type.

“Report of Archival Anomaly Investigation: Kessock Tidal Station. Submitted by Iona Kerr, contracted archivist, to the Marine Accident Investigation Branch.”

The words came slowly at first, then faster. She described the discovery of the forged logs, the analysis of handwriting and ink, the cross-referencing with tide tables that proved the timeline impossible. She detailed each witness statement, explaining how Catriona MacAulay’s memory had been the key that unlocked the others. She included Fraser Munro’s testimony and its limitations, Ewan Stirling’s engineering assessment, and the physical evidence of the rust-stained toolkit.

Then she came to Rossiter’s confession.

Iona paused. Her finger rested on the mouse, the cursor blinking at the end of a sentence. She could summarize the confession, could soften its edges, could preserve some dignity for the man who had finally chosen to speak.

She looked at his signature again. The trembling hand. The man had waited thirty-seven years to tell the truth. The least she could do was record it faithfully.

She attached the full confession as an appendix and continued.

“The official duty logs for February 1986 have been determined to be fabricated,” she typed. “The signature attributed to Callum Reid does not match verified samples of his handwriting. The timeline described in the logs is physically impossible given the tidal conditions recorded for that date. The witness statements and physical evidence corroborate an alternative sequence of events.”

She wrote of the turbine failure, the deferred maintenance, the decision to conceal negligence. She wrote of Reid’s death, his body found in the intake channel where he had been sent to address an emergency the logs denied. She wrote of the cover-up that had allowed the station to continue operating, that had protected careers and reputations at the cost of one man’s memory.

“The archive as it existed prior to this investigation presented a coherent but false narrative,” she concluded. “The ordered appearance of the records was itself an artifact of the cover-up. A true record must be established, even if that truth renders the archive disordered.”

She read through the document once more. It was brutal in its clarity. There were no elegant turns of phrase, no archival flourishes. Just facts, arranged in sequence, leading to an unavoidable conclusion.

Iona navigated to the Marine Accident Investigation Branch’s digital portal. She had used it before, for routine submissions, for cataloguing updates that required official registration. The interface was familiar, almost banal.

She attached the report file. She attached the scanned evidence, the photographs, the witness statements, Rossiter’s confession. She filled in the required metadata fields: date, location, incident type, fatalities.

The cursor blinked on the final button. “SUBMIT.”

Her hand hovered over the mouse. The room was cold, and she could see her breath in the air. The station’s heating had been shut down days ago, part of the decommissioning process. She had worked through it, wearing her coat indoors, typing with cold fingers.

One click. That was all it would take. One click and the report would be filed with the government’s accident investigation authority. One click and the official record would be permanently altered. One click and her work here would be over, not in completion but in consequence.

She thought of the archive she had been hired to create. Neat rows of boxes, labeled and catalogued, a testament to order and professional competence. That archive was impossible now. The truth had ruined it.

Good.

Iona clicked submit.

The screen changed. A progress bar appeared, filled, and vanished. Then a confirmation message: “Report Filed. Kessock Tidal Station Archive - Complete.”

The word “Complete” felt like a lie. The archive was not complete. It was broken open, its contents scattered and reassembled into something messier, harder, more honest.

She printed a copy for her own records, the printer whirring in the silence. The pages emerged warm from the machine, the ink still drying. She placed them in her bag beside the portable hard drive.

Then she began to pack.

Her personal belongings were few. A coffee thermos, a spare sweater, a notebook she had used for observations during her first days here. She had brought no personal mementos to this place. She had expected to leave with a completed catalogue, a professional accomplishment, a line on her resume.

Instead, she was leaving with a terminated contract and a government investigation pending.

The archive room around her showed the evidence of her work. The shelves that should have held ordered boxes stood empty. The boxes themselves were stacked in the corner, their contents pulled and redistributed according to the demands of truth rather than aesthetics. The room smelled of dust and displacement.

The Four Witnesses had done their work. Catriona, who had trusted her memory when the records lied. Fraser, who had accepted truth even when it complicated his inherited narrative. Ewan, whose technical expertise had cut through decades of obfuscation. And Rossiter, whose final confession had closed the circle.

The archive’s ruin was complete. But from that ruin, something true had been built.

Iona sealed the evidence room’s door behind her. The lock clicked into place. She walked through the station’s corridors one last time, past the turbine hall where rust bloomed across ancient machinery, past the intake channel where the tide rose and fell as it had on that February night in 1986.

The gravel crunched beneath her boots as she walked to the parking area. The salt wind caught her hair, pulled it across her face. She could taste the sea on her tongue, could hear the water against the pilings. The tide was rising.

She reached her car and slid into the driver’s seat. The steering wheel was cold beneath her hands. The engine turned over on the first try, a reliable sound in a day of uncertainties.

She sat for a moment, looking out at the gray water beyond the station’s jetty. The sky hung low and heavy with the promise of rain. The station receded in her rearview mirror as she pulled away, its concrete facade growing smaller, its windows dark.

The sale would be complicated now. The buyers would have questions. The investigation would take months, perhaps years. Rossiter would face whatever consequences remained for a man nearing the end of his life. The Reid family might finally learn what had really happened to their son.

None of that was Iona’s concern anymore. Her work was done.

She drove toward the main road, the weight of the true record in her bag beside her. The ledger was closed. A new one had begun.
