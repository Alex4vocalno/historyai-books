Chapter 5

Breaking the Seal

────────────────────────────────────────

The official seal across the Control Room door had bleached to the color of old bone, but the warning text remained legible: CONDEMNED – NO ENTRY.

Iona Kerr stood before it, her breath forming small clouds that vanished almost as they formed. The fluorescent tube overhead flickered with an arrhythmic pulse—bright, dim, bright—casting the yellowed notice in alternating visibility. She had walked past this door twelve times since arriving at Kessock Tidal Station, always with the same professional detachment she afforded any restricted area. Safety and liability, the official report had claimed. Structural instability. Asbestos. Environmental hazard.

None of that explained why someone had layered discouragement upon discouragement: the notice, yes, but also the physical barriers, the rusted hasp, the visible wear patterns on the door frame that suggested decades of hands pressing against wood that would not yield.

She adjusted her grip on the torch from the archive office. In her other hand, her identification badge caught the weak light: ARCHIVAL ACCESS – AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. The laminated edge pressed a sharp line into her palm. She was authorized to catalogue, to preserve, to document. She was not authorized to break into sealed rooms on the basis of two contradictory witness statements and the certainty, growing like a tumor beneath her ribs, that something was very wrong with February 1986.

Six hours until the next low tide. She had checked the tables that morning, her finger descending the column of predicted times with the mechanical habit she had developed since her first day. The tide governed everything—the station’s operation then, her investigation now. The channel beneath the turbine hall, where Callum Reid’s body had been found, would be accessible. She could walk its length, see the intake valves, measure the distance from where Reid was supposedly working to where his body was recovered.

But Catriona MacAulay had been certain: Reid in the Control Room that night, not near the channel. And Fraser Munro had been equally certain, equally confident, and completely incompatible.

Both truthful. Both detailed. Both impossible.

The falsified duty logs had been her first clue—the handwriting that didn’t match Reid’s signature, the timeline placing him on duty hours after his death. But falsification required a falsifier, and falsifiers required reasons. She needed the reasons now. The physical evidence that would explain why someone had constructed an alternative version of that night.

She looked again at the seal. The paper had curled where moisture seeped in, the adhesive weakened to the texture of dried mucilage. It would not take much. She had supplies in the archive that could recreate age, match color and texture. If she was careful—

If.

The word hung in her mind, heavy with implications she had spent her career avoiding. Archivists did not tamper. They preserved, protected, made accessible. They did not break seals, enter condemned spaces, prioritize curiosity over protocol.

But this was not curiosity. This was recognition that the record itself had been corrupted, that the documents she had been sent to catalogue contained deliberate falsehood. And if the record was false, then her duty to preserve it collided with her duty to the truth it was meant to represent.

Her grandfather’s journals surfaced unbidden. Seventeen when she found the entry—the careful copperplate describing events her own research had proven impossible. Places he had never visited, people who had never existed, rendered with the same meticulous detail that made his journals valuable. The discovery had planted something in her, a splinter of doubt she had learned to work around, to use as motivation rather than let it fester into paralysis.

Here, at Kessock, the witnesses were still alive, the documents extant, the space still standing. Here, she could find what had really happened, could restore the truth someone had tried to bury.

If she was willing to break the rules.

Her thumb found the archivist’s ID card in her pocket, traced its raised seal, then let it go. She reached into her bag and withdrew the crowbar from the maintenance shed. The metal was cold against her palm, its weight foreign and familiar. She had never used one for its intended purpose. Archivists worked with paper and ink, careful hands, precise instruments. They did not force their way into sealed rooms.

But archivists also did not ignore falsification. They did not accept contradiction as the cost of doing business with the past. They did not prioritize institutional convenience over historical truth.

Her thumb found the ink-smudged scar on her left hand, traced its familiar ridge. Twelve years old, opening a box of her grandfather’s papers after his death. The Stanley knife had been her first lesson in the physical reality of archival work—the way it could draw blood if you were not careful.

This felt like that. Like she was about to cut herself on something sharp, something hidden in the dark.

She positioned the crowbar at the edge of the seal, where wood met metal. The seal stretched tight across the junction. Get purchase underneath, pry the wood from the frame. The lock was old, mechanical. Once the seal broke, the lock would yield.

She pressed the crowbar into the gap. The wood was soft with age and moisture, the metal sinking more easily than expected. She adjusted her grip, found stability, and pushed.

The wood splintered with a sound like a bone snapping.

She froze. The noise echoed in the silent corridor. The fluorescent light flickered, and somewhere distant, the faint hum of remaining electrical systems, the rush of water through pipes not fully drained. No footsteps. No voices. No indication anyone had heard.

She was alone. The station had been empty when she arrived, would remain so until Monday’s maintenance crew. She had the weekend.

The seal hung in tatters now, its warning text torn across the middle. She had committed herself. No going back to the version of herself that had stood outside this door as a rule-following archivist. She had broken the seal, violated the institutional trust. If anyone found out, she would lose her position, the standing she had spent a decade building.

But she would have the truth.

She gripped the crowbar and pushed the door open.

The Control Room revealed itself in the thin beam of her torch.

Larger than expected. From outside, the door had suggested modest space. But this had been the heart of Kessock Tidal Station, the nerve center where operators monitored the massive turbines that converted tide to electricity. The torch beam found banks of monitoring equipment, displays dark, switches frozen in positions untouched for decades. The air was thick with dust, each breath carrying the taste of old metal and staleness. Ozone lingered, faint but unmistakable—the residue of systems that had once hummed with power.

She stepped inside, closed the door behind her. Darkness was absolute beyond the torch’s reach. She swept the beam across the room, taking in layout, equipment positions, filing cabinets and desk surfaces that might contain what she sought.

The turbine monitoring station dominated the far wall. Gauges and displays that would have shown real-time status of four turbines—rotational speed, power output, operational status. She approached slowly, her footsteps stirring dust that rose in small clouds around her boots.

The gauges were frozen, needles stuck at zero. But beneath the monitoring station, she found a row of drawers, each labeled with turbine number and date range. The log sheets. Operators recording status at regular intervals, creating continuous record of station operation.

She pulled open the drawer labeled TURBINE 3 – FEB 1986.

Empty.

She checked TURBINE 4 – FEB 1986. Empty. TURBINE 2 – FEB 1986. Empty.

Someone had removed the log sheets. Someone had entered this sealed room and taken exactly what she needed.

But they had not been thorough.

The torch beam caught something behind the monitoring station, tucked in the gap between equipment bank and wall. A corner of paper, yellowed and curled. She reached for it, fingers finding purchase on textured old stationery.

A log sheet, folded roughly, shoved into the gap as if someone had been in a hurry to hide it. She unfolded it carefully, torch illuminating the faded grid of printed lines and handwritten entries.

Header: TURBINE 3 – OPERATIONAL LOG. Date: February 14, 1986. Shift: NIGHT (2200-0600).

Across the bottom, stamped in red ink faded but legible: FAILURE.

Her breath caught. Not in fear. She whispered to the empty room: “Got you.”

She traced the date with her finger. February 14, 1986. Two days after the official record claimed Callum Reid died. Two days after falsified duty logs placed him on shift, hours after his body had been recovered from the intake channel.

But here, in this hidden log sheet: evidence that Turbine 3 had failed on February 14. Not February 12. Not the date official records claimed.

The implications cascaded. If the turbine failed on February 14, the official timeline was wrong—not simple error, but deliberate misdirection. Someone had moved the date, shifted events, constructed a version of history bearing no relation to what had happened.

She examined the log entries more closely. At 2300: T3 OPERATIONAL – OUTPUT 85%. At 0100: T3 OPERATIONAL – OUTPUT 82%. At 0300, the handwriting changed, became hurried: T3 VIBRATION ANOMALY – INVESTIGATING.

At 0347, a single line: T3 FAILURE – EMERGENCY SHUTDOWN INITIATED.

The signature at the bottom was barely legible, but she recognized the handwriting from other documents. Ewan Stirling. Junior Engineer. One of the four witnesses.

On duty that night. The one who initiated emergency shutdown. His signature on a document hidden, not destroyed, tucked behind a panel in a room sealed for decades.

She folded the log sheet carefully, tucked it into her jacket. Returned to the drawer, checking more thoroughly, fingers running along back and sides of empty space.

Her hand found another paper. Smaller. A maintenance tag, the kind attached to equipment needing repair. She pulled it free, brought it into torchlight.

TURBINE 3 – BEARING ASSEMBLY. MAINTENANCE REQUIRED – DO NOT OPERATE. Beneath, in the same cramped handwriting: Ewan Stirling. Date: February 10, 1986.

Four days before the failure. Four days before emergency shutdown. Stirling had signed off on a maintenance tag identifying Turbine 3’s bearing assembly problem, flagging it for repair before operation.

But Turbine 3 had continued operating. The log sheet showed 85% output on February 14. Someone had ignored the maintenance tag, kept the turbine running despite known problem.

And then it had failed.

She reached for the drawer again when her torch caught something else beneath the maintenance tag. A damage report, hastily filed, form creased and smudged as if handled in a hurry. She pulled it free, smoothed it against the equipment bank.

DAMAGE REPORT – TURBINE 3. DATE: February 15, 1986. DESCRIPTION: Bearing assembly failure resulting in turbine shutdown. Extensive damage to housing and intake mechanism. ESTIMATED REPAIR COST: £47, 000. RECOMMENDATION: Decommission pending assessment.

But her attention caught on INCIDENT DETAILS. Different handwriting here—not Stirling’s cramped script, but a more flowing hand she didn’t recognize. OPERATOR INJURED DURING SHUTDOWN PROCEDURE. TRANSPORTED TO INVERNESS REGIONAL HOSPITAL. CONDITION UNKNOWN.

Operator injured. Not operator dead. Not Callum Reid, whose body had been recovered from the intake channel on February 12, according to official record.

The damage report contradicted everything. Date of turbine failure, nature of incident, outcome for personnel. It painted chaos and injury, catastrophic mechanical failure that had nearly killed someone. But that someone was not Callum Reid.

Unless Reid had been killed not by drowning in the intake channel, but by the turbine failure itself. Unless the body in the channel had been moved there after death, placed to support a narrative of accidental drowning rather than mechanical catastrophe.

She thought of the intake channel, the body’s position as described in the recovery report from her first week. Found near the access grating, caught in water flow beneath the station. But if the turbine had failed, if the bearing assembly had shattered, debris would have been drawn into intake. Anyone in vicinity would have been caught in the same current.

Or would they?

She needed to see the channel herself. Needed to understand the relationship between turbine hall and intake, water flow patterns, the path a body would take if caught in machinery versus floating in the channel.

Six hours until the scheduled intake gate closure. She would be ready.

She tucked the damage report into her jacket with the log sheet and maintenance tag, her fingers brushing rough edges where someone had torn them from original locations. She had what she came for. Proof that official records were falsified, timeline deliberately manipulated, someone had gone to considerable lengths to hide the truth of February 14, cardiff 1986.

But she also had unexpected questions. About Stirling’s role, his signature on documents that should have been filed officially, his decision to hide rather than destroy evidence. About who had ordered Turbine 3 kept running despite the maintenance tag. About who had decided to falsify records, move the incident date, construct a narrative protecting someone from responsibility.

Questions that would have to wait.

She made her way to the door, torch beam cutting through darkness. The room seemed different now, equipment somehow aware of what she had taken. She pushed the feeling aside and stepped into the corridor.

The fluorescent light flickered overhead, casting long shadows. She pulled the door closed, checking if damage to the seal would be visible. The wood had splintered more than expected, frame showing clear signs of forced entry. She would need to address that, make it less obvious someone had been inside.

But not now. Now she needed to leave, get documents somewhere safe, process what she had found.

She walked quickly through the corridor, footsteps echoing in empty station. The archive office was on the ground floor, near main entrance. She could store documents there, add them to her catalogue, create a record preserving their existence even if someone tried to make them disappear.

Halfway down the stairwell, she heard it. Distant rumble of car engine, crunch of gravel under tires.

She froze, hand on railing, breath stopped in her throat.

Someone was arriving.

She moved to the window at the landing, positioning herself where she could see the car park without being visible. Evening light was fading, sky turning deep blue of twilight. Below, a vehicle pulled away from the main building, taillights glowing red in gathering dark.

She recognized it. Dark sedan, distinctive even in dim light. Same car she had seen in the station’s lot on her first day. Alistair Rossiter’s car. The site manager, who came to check archival progress.

Rossiter was leaving. Which meant he had been here, somewhere in the station, while she was breaking into the Control Room.

Had he heard her? Seen the splintered frame, the broken seal? Been in the building the entire time, monitoring her movements, waiting for her to reveal herself?

She stayed at the window until taillights disappeared around the access road curve. Then descended, more slowly, more carefully. She needed to check the seal, assess damage, decide whether she could hide what she had done or find another way.

The corridor outside the Control Room was empty. Fluorescent light still flickered, still cast irregular shadows. But approaching the door, she saw the damage was visible, clearly visible, to anyone walking past.

She had broken the seal. Violated professional trust. And now someone, possibly Rossiter, possibly someone else, knew the Control Room had been accessed.

The documents pressed against her ribs, their weight suddenly more significant than physical mass. She had the evidence she needed, but had exposed herself in ways unanticipated. The seal was broken, literally and metaphorically. She was no longer impartial archivist, cataloguing from safe professional distance. She was investigator, rule-breaker, someone who had crossed a line she could not uncross.

She thought of her grandfather’s journals again, the falsified entries she had discovered at seventeen, the questions never asked. She had spent her career trying to prevent that corruption, ensure records she preserved were accurate, authentic, true.

And now she had become the one who altered the record, removed documents from proper location, broke seals and entered condemned spaces.

The irony pressed against her sternum like a physical weight.

She made her way to the archive office, mind racing through implications. Turbine failure, hidden log sheet, maintenance tag with Stirling’s signature, damage report with contradictory account. Each document a piece of a puzzle she was only beginning to understand.

Tomorrow she would confront Ewan Stirling. Ask about Turbine 3, the maintenance tag, the decision to hide rather than destroy evidence. Use the documents she had taken to force truth into open.

But tonight, she needed to think, to plan, to understand what she had actually found.

The archive office was cold when she entered, heating long since disabled in most of the station. She crossed to her desk, unlocked the drawer where she kept working notes, catalogue entries, growing collection of documents that did not fit official narrative.

She added the log sheet, maintenance tag, and damage report to the file labeled FEBRUARY 1986 – ANOMALIES. Deliberately mundane label, designed to attract no attention. But contents were proof of cover-up, evidence of conspiracy, documents that could blow open a case closed for thirty years.

She thought of Catriona MacAulay’s testimony, her certainty Reid had been in the Control Room. Of Fraser Munro’s equally certain account of Reid working near the intake. Both telling truth as they knew it. Neither knowing about turbine failure, hidden log sheet, maintenance tag that should have prevented everything.

The truth was more complicated than either account. Involved mechanical failure and human decision, choices made under pressure and consequences someone had tried to bury.

And now she had documents to prove it.

She locked the drawer, gathered her things. The station was officially closed for the weekend, no reason to stay. But walking toward the exit, she found herself pausing at the window overlooking the turbine hall.

The massive structure was dark now, windows reflecting last evening light. Somewhere beneath, the intake channel ran its course, carrying tide in and out of station workings. In six hours, tide at lowest point. Channel accessible, walkable, visible.

She could see for herself the relationship between turbine and intake, understand how a body might have ended up in water, verify whether official account of Reid’s death was even physically possible.

She would come back. Walk the channel at low tide, measure distances, see with her own eyes what documents could only suggest.

And then she would know, once and for all, what had really happened in this place on a February night thirty years ago.

The cool evening air greeted her outside, carrying smell of sea, salt and seaweed permeating everything at Kessock. She walked to her car, bag heavy with stolen documents, mind heavy with their implications.

In her rearview mirror, the station receded behind her, dark silhouette against fading sky. She thought of broken seal, splintered wood, evidence of her violation.

The seal was broken. There was no going back.

She drove toward Inverness, toward the small hotel where she had been staying, toward the next phase of an investigation that had just become infinitely more complicated.

Tomorrow she would confront Ewan Stirling. Tomorrow she would walk the intake channel at low tide. Tomorrow she would take another step toward truth.

But tonight, she would protect the documents, hide them somewhere no one would think to look, ensure evidence she had risked everything to obtain would not be lost.

She thought of her grandfather again, journals she had never been able to reconcile with truth. Years trying to understand why he had lied, trying to find truth behind his fabrications.

Here, at Kessock, she had the chance to do what she had never been able to do for him. Chance to expose the lie, restore the truth, set the record straight.

The next low tide would reveal what the channel had kept hidden for thirty years.

She would be there to see it.
