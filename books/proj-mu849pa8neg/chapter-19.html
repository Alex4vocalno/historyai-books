Chapter 19

The First Shot

A logbook entry for HMS Volage on 3 November 1839 recorded position and wind, then noted a shift: Chinese war-junks had fired on a British merchantman. The frigate responded. The engagement lasted less than an hour before the junks withdrew, damaged, leaving the British ships with minor harm. The log did not interpret the sequence of the Royal Saxon’s attempt, the interception, the signal, the broadside, and the withdrawal; it merely documented it, the ink in places still damp on the captain’s table. Interpretation would come later, in dispatches and memoranda far from the water.

The memorandum James Matheson had finalized in October was already at sea, carried by the clipper that had departed Macao for London. Its arguments, framing the opium seizure as a national insult and demanding a forceful British response, had been crafted to shape policy. But events in the Pearl River estuary had moved faster than any dispatch could travel. The carefully constructed narrative now had to accommodate an unforeseen fact: British and Chinese forces had exchanged fire. The commercial crisis had become a military one.

The Royal Saxon had appeared in the morning light, her sails filled with the northeasterly wind that marked the turning monsoon. She was a British merchantman, armed against pirates, making for the passage that led to Canton. Her master had calculated that he could run the Chinese blockade. The war-junks stationed across the river mouth had other intentions. They moved to intercept, their guns run out, their orders clear from the imperial edict that had closed the port to British trade. The Chinese opened fire first.

Smith watched through his glass from the deck of Volage. The eighteen-gun frigate had been sent to the estuary at the request of Superintendent Charles Elliot, who had argued that British shipping required protection after Lin Zexu’s crackdown. The definition of protection had been ambiguous. The Admiralty’s instructions had been general. Now Smith faced a specific choice: to engage or to stand down.

The battle was brief and tactically indecisive. The Chinese junks, outgunned and outranged by the British frigate’s modern armament, took significant damage. Several were hit repeatedly. Some were disabled. They fell back from the passage. The British vessels, Volage and the smaller brig Hyacinth, sustained minor damage to rigging and hull. The Royal Saxon was seized and taken upriver by the Chinese forces. But the outcome that mattered was political, not tactical. British and Chinese forces had exchanged fire. The first shot of what would become the Opium War had been fired.

Why Smith chose to engage leads back through a chain of decisions, each rational in its own context, each narrowing the options for those who followed. Smith fired because he had been sent to protect British shipping. He had been sent because Elliot had requested naval support. Elliot had requested support because Lin Zexu had detained the British community in Canton, demanded the surrender of the opium, and closed the port. Lin had acted on imperial orders to end the trade. The emperor had issued those orders because the opium imports had been draining silver from China and the existing enforcement mechanisms had failed to stop the flow.

At each stage, the actors had made choices that seemed necessary. Smith, facing the decision to engage or abandon a British vessel to seizure, chose to engage. The alternative would have meant abandoning the principle that British ships were under British protection. That principle was the foundation of the entire trading system. Without it, the agency houses could not operate. Their vessels, their cargoes, their factors would be subject to the authority of local officials who did not recognize the same rules.

But the principle itself was not neutral. It had been built into the system by decades of precedent, by the accumulation of informal arrangements that had allowed the trade to grow beyond what the formal treaties permitted. The East India Company had established the pattern: Company ships flying the Company flag answered to Company authority and to the Crown. The private traders who followed had adopted the same expectation. They flew the same flag. They claimed the same protection. When the Chinese state challenged that claim, the British government found itself drawn into a dispute it had not chosen but could not easily abandon.

The Red Rover, built in 1829 as a 254-ton clipper constructed specifically for the opium trade, had exemplified the innovation that transformed the economics of the traffic. Modeled on the sharp-hulled American privateers from the War of 1812—specifically the blockade runner Prince de Neufchatel—she had been designed for speed rather than cargo capacity. Her purpose was specific: to beat the monsoon, to deliver Bengal opium to the receiving hulks at Lintin before market prices fell. She and her successors had changed the trade’s scale. Faster ships meant more chests delivered per season. More chests meant more silver flowing out of China. More silver meant greater pressure on the Qing administration to act.

Lin Zexu had arrived in Canton with imperial backing and a mandate to end the trade by whatever means necessary. He had demanded the opium. He had detained the merchants. He had destroyed more than twenty thousand chests. When the British refused to accept his terms, he had closed the port and blockaded the river. The system that had produced the fast clippers had now produced a response. The trade had grown to a scale that the Chinese state could no longer ignore.

The clash on 3 November was the moment when the private trading network built on speed met the imperial enforcement apparatus built on authority. The result was an improvisation by a naval captain facing a situation his orders had not fully anticipated. But the consequences would not be improvised. They would be determined by the structures that had produced the confrontation.

In Macao, the partners of Jardine Matheson received the news through the informal channels that connected the counting house to the waterfront. James Matheson had spent the previous weeks drafting the memorandum that framed the crisis for the British government. He had argued that the Chinese actions constituted an insult to the British flag, a violation of the rights of British subjects, a provocation demanding forceful response. William Jardine had departed for London to make the case in person. Now the first shot had been fired, and the argument they had crafted was becoming reality.

The firm’s correspondence in the days after the engagement reflected the new situation. The letters discussed the condition of ships, the disposition of cargoes, the movements of the Chinese fleet. The prospects for trade were essentially nil. The blockade was effective. The Chinese were serious. The British response, when it came, would have to be military.

The partners understood what this meant. A war would open the ports and secure the trade, establishing the legal framework the merchants had long desired. But it would also be expensive, uncertain, and dependent on decisions made in London by men who might not share their priorities. The agency houses had pushed for intervention. Now they would see what intervention looked like.

The immediate problem was the fleet. The opium clippers that had been running between India and China were now scattered, their trade interrupted, their crews idle. Some had taken shelter in Hong Kong harbor, where Elliot had established a temporary anchorage. Others had returned to India or hovered off the coast, waiting for news. The specialized logistics system that had moved thousands of chests per season was now a floating resource with nothing to move.

Elliot had tried to organize this fleet. He had issued instructions, arranged for supplies, attempted to maintain order among the captains and crews who found themselves without clear purpose. But he was a naval officer, not a merchant, and his authority over private ships was limited. The agency houses still owned the vessels. They still paid the wages. They still decided where the ships went and what they did.

The engagement changed the calculation. The Royal Navy was now committed. The frigates had fired on Chinese junks. The flag had been defended, in precisely the terms Matheson’s memorandum had specified. The next move would not be commercial. It would be military, and it would be made by the government, not by the traders.

Lin Zexu reported the clash to the emperor as a victory—the foreign ships had been driven off, the blockade held. He had no reason to describe it otherwise. The truth was more complicated. The British ships had withdrawn, but they had also demonstrated that they could engage the Chinese fleet and survive. The junks had taken damage. The British had not. The balance of naval power was not what Lin assumed.

But Lin’s assumptions were built into the Qing system, which had dealt with foreign traders for centuries by treating them as tributaries. The British, from the Qing perspective, were simply another group of barbarians who needed to be reminded of their place. That they had ships with guns did not change the fundamental relationship. It only meant enforcement would have to be more forceful.

The British perspective was equally structured. The Royal Navy existed to protect British interests, and British interests in China were defined by trade. The opium trade was not officially endorsed—the East India Company’s monopoly had ended, and the private trade operated in a legal gray area—but the principle of protection extended to all British subjects. The merchants in Canton might be engaged in trade the government found inconvenient, but they were still British. Their ships still flew the flag. Their property still mattered.

The clash on 3 November made the contradiction impossible to ignore. The British government could not simultaneously claim to protect its subjects and disavow their activities. The Chinese government could not simultaneously demand the end of the opium trade and allow the British to continue under the old rules. Something had to give.

In the days after the engagement, correspondence between the British ships and the shore reflected the uncertainty. Elliot wrote to London describing the situation and requesting instructions. Smith wrote his official report defending his decision. The merchants wrote to their agents, their insurers, their partners in India and Britain, explaining what had happened and what it might mean.

The letters from Jardine Matheson were particularly clear. The firm had been preparing for this possibility since Lin’s first moves. They had organized the surrender of the opium under protest, preserving their legal position. They had drafted the memoranda framing the crisis as an attack on British rights. They had sent Jardine to London to make the case. Now they watched as the situation they had predicted came to pass.

The difference was that they had expected the crisis to be resolved by negotiation, or by a show of force that would bring the Chinese to the table. They had not expected a shooting war. The engagement was small, a skirmish rather than a battle, but it was real. Shots had been fired. Damage had been done. The next step would be escalation or retreat, and retreat was not what the partners had in mind.

Why this particular clash escalated, when previous confrontations had been resolved without gunfire, leads back to the accumulation of pressure. The Lintin receiving hulks had been dismantled. The opium had been surrendered and destroyed. The merchants had been detained and expelled. The trade had been stopped. Each step had narrowed the options, reduced the space for maneuver, made the next confrontation more likely to turn violent.

The British position had hardened because the costs of backing down had increased. To accept the Chinese terms—to abandon the opium trade, to submit to Qing jurisdiction, to acknowledge that British subjects were subject to Chinese law—would have meant the end of the commercial system built over decades. The agency houses would have been ruined. The ships would have been worthless. The capital invested would have been lost.

The Chinese position had hardened for the same reason. Lin had been sent to Canton with a specific mission. He had destroyed the opium. He had closed the port. He had demonstrated imperial resolve. To back down now—to reopen the trade without securing the concessions he had demanded—would have meant admitting failure. The emperor did not tolerate failure.

Both sides were trapped by their own commitments. The British could not abandon their merchants. The Chinese could not abandon their principles. The clash at the river mouth was the moment when the trap closed.

The damage reports told the story. The Chinese junks had taken the worst of it. Several were heavily damaged. Some were sunk. The British ships had sustained minor damage—a few shot holes, some cut rigging. The technological gap between British frigates and Chinese war-junks was substantial. The British had better guns, better-trained crews, better ships. In a fair fight, the British would win.

But the fight was not fair, and it was not over. The Chinese had more junks, more men, more resources. They could absorb losses the British could not. They were fighting in their own waters, near their own bases, with their own supply lines. The British were far from home, dependent on monsoon winds, operating at the end of a logistical chain stretching back to India.

The partners in Macao understood this. A war would require major commitment from Britain. The frigates currently in the estuary could defend themselves and inflict damage. But they could not open the ports, lift the blockade, restore the trade. For that, a larger force would be needed. A fleet. An army. A full-scale expedition.

Matheson had argued for exactly this in his memorandum. Jardine would argue for it in London. Now, with the first shot fired, the argument had become urgent. The crisis was no longer theoretical. British and Chinese forces had exchanged fire. The flag had been defended, but the trade had not been restored.

The captains of the idle clippers waited for instructions. The crews—a mix of British officers and Lascar sailors—maintained the ships as best they could, but there was little to do. The trade was stopped. The monsoon was turning. The season that should have been the busiest was instead a time of waiting.

Some ships had been sent to other trades temporarily, carrying legitimate cargo between Asian ports. But the profits from these voyages were nothing compared to the opium runs. The specialized clippers were too fast, too small, too expensive for ordinary freight. They had been built for a specific purpose, and that purpose was now impossible.

The engagement was not the first time British and Chinese forces had faced each other. There had been confrontations before: disputes over jurisdiction, arrests of sailors, seizures of cargo. But those had been resolved through negotiation, through the informal mechanisms that had governed the Canton trade for decades. The Hong merchants, the licensed intermediaries between foreigners and officials, had smoothed over conflicts. The East India Company, with its official monopoly, had provided a structure for managing disputes. That structure was gone. The Company’s monopoly had ended in 1834. The Hong merchants had been sidelined by Lin’s direct action. The informal mechanisms had been replaced by imperial edicts and naval blockades. The system that had contained the contradictions of the trade had collapsed, and the contradictions had produced a crisis.

The partners of Jardine Matheson had positioned themselves for this moment. They had gathered evidence, drafted arguments, cultivated contacts. They had made the case that the British government had to act, that national honor was at stake, that the interests of British subjects required protection. They had done everything commercial men could do to shape policy.

Now they would see if they had succeeded. The dispatches were on their way. The first shot had been fired. The decision would be made in London, by ministers who might or might not share the merchants’ priorities.

The ships at anchor in Hong Kong harbor rode easily at their moorings. The crews went about their routines. The captains waited for orders. In the counting houses of Macao, the partners watched the correspondence pile up—reports from captains, letters from agents, official dispatches from Elliot.

The war that would follow was not yet inevitable. There were still steps that could be taken, negotiations attempted, compromises explored. But the engagement had made the path to war shorter and clearer. The first shot had been fired. The response would determine what came next.

Smith’s log recorded the events with naval precision. The positions, the wind, the movements of ships, the opening of fire, the damage sustained and inflicted. The log did not interpret. It did not argue. It recorded what had happened. The interpretation would come later.

But the fact was clear. On 3 November 1839, British and Chinese forces had exchanged fire in the Pearl River estuary. The Royal Saxon had attempted to run the blockade. The Chinese junks had intercepted her. Captain Smith had intervened. The guns had spoken.

The partners in Macao understood what this meant for their business. The trade was stopped. The ships were idle. The capital was frozen. The future depended on decisions made by others, in London, in Beijing, by men who might not share their interests. The system they had built—the fast ships and receiving hulks and network of agents—was now in the hands of governments and navies.

The memorandum Matheson had sent with Jardine would arrive in London in coming months. It would be read, discussed, debated. The government would decide how to respond. The fleet would be assembled, the expedition mounted, the war fought. When it was over, the partners would learn whether their investment in the crisis had paid.

The counting house closed for the evening. The clerks filed out, leaving ledgers and letters on the tables. The partners remained, reviewing the latest reports, discussing implications, planning for scenarios they could not fully predict. The trade had always been uncertain. The monsoon, the market, the officials, the competition: there had always been risks. But this was different. This was war, or the beginning of one, and the rules of commerce did not apply.

The lights went out in the counting house. The ships in the harbor were dark shapes against the water. The Chinese junks maintained their positions across the river, enforcing the blockade. The British frigates rode at anchor nearby, guns still loaded, crews watchful. The two fleets faced each other across dark water, and the peace that had held for decades was gone.

The partners walked home through the empty streets of Macao. The Portuguese governor had maintained neutrality, but the town was full of rumors—refugees from Canton, sailors and merchants and officials, all waiting to see what would happen. The monsoon was turning. The season was ending. The year was drawing to a close.

In London, the dispatches were still weeks away. In Beijing, the emperor was receiving reports that described a victory. In Canton, Lin Zexu was preparing for the next confrontation, confident the barbarians had been taught a lesson. In the Pearl River estuary, the British fleet waited for orders that would not arrive for months.

The first shot had been fired. The consequences would take years to unfold. But the transformation was already visible. The commercial blockade had become a military confrontation. The dispute over trade had become a question of sovereignty. The private system of fast ships and receiving hulks and agency houses had collided with the imperial system of edicts and enforcement, and the result was a war that would change everything.

The night settled over the estuary. The ships rode at anchor, the officials waited in their yamens, the merchants in their counting houses. All waited for the next move. The memorandum crafted in Macao would reach London in due course. The government would decide. The fleet would sail. The question now was whether the owners would survive the martial crisis their trade had created.
