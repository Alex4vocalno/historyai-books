Chapter 18

Matheson’s War Council

The memorandum lay on the table in the upstairs room of the Matheson godown, the paper still creased from the packet that had carried it down the river. James Matheson had written it himself, dated October 1839, and addressed it to the select committee of the House of Commons. The words were plain enough on the page, but their purpose was anything but plain. They framed the seizure of twenty thousand chests of opium not as a commercial loss but as a national insult, and they demanded a response that would transform a private trade into a public war.

Matheson had been writing versions of this document for weeks. The letter books of Jardine, Matheson & Co. showed the progression. In September, he had drafted a circular to the British merchants at Canton, laying out the legal case for compensation. In early October, he had refined the argument for transmission to London, adding details about the detention of the merchants, the conditions of their confinement, and the threat to their lives that Superintendent Elliot had cited in his forced bond. Now, in late October, he was preparing the final version, the one that would travel with the next ship and land on the desk of Lord Palmerston. The work was done in the upstairs room of the Matheson godown, a space that held the firm’s records and the quiet necessary for such a political composition.

The pressure had been building since Lin Zexu’s commissioners arrived in Canton the previous March. The Imperial Commissioner had given the foreign merchants no room to maneuver. He had surrounded the factories with troops, cut off their supplies, and demanded the surrender of every chest of opium in their possession. The surrender had taken weeks. The chests had come from the receiving ships at Lintin, from the clippers anchored in the estuary, from the godowns of every agency house in the trade. When the last chest had been handed over, Lin had ordered them all destroyed, the opium mixed with lime and salt and flushed into the sea.

The destruction had left the clipper fleet idle. Ships built for speed now sat at anchor, their holds empty, their captains waiting for news. The Red Rover, a 254-ton clipper built in 1829, had been designed for a single purpose: to carry Bengal opium quickly from Calcutta to Lintin, beating the monsoon to reach market before the season ended. The Aurora and the Sultana had followed her design. All of them now rode at anchor with nothing to carry, their purpose taken from them by a Chinese official who had understood the trade’s vulnerabilities better than the men who had built it.

Matheson had remained in Macao while his partner prepared to leave. William Jardine had been planning his retirement for years. Born in 1784 in the southwest of Scotland, he had graduated from Edinburgh University with a degree in medicine. In 1803, at the age of 19, he became a surgeon on the ships of the British East India Company, working the trading routes between London, China, and India. He had left medicine for trade, and by 1839 he was the senior partner of the largest foreign firm in Canton, a man whose opinion carried weight in London as well as Macao. He had seen the crisis coming, and he had decided that his presence in England would serve the firm better than his presence in China.

Jardine had left Canton on 26 January 1839, before Lin’s arrival but after the signs of the coming crackdown had become unmistakable. The timing had been careful, the succession planned. Matheson had returned to China three years earlier to assume control as Jardine prepared for retirement. What neither man had anticipated was that the trade itself would be destroyed before the succession could be completed.

Now Matheson sat in Macao, the de facto head of the firm’s operations in China, coordinating what had become a political campaign as much as a commercial one. The memorandum on his desk was part of that campaign. Its argument was simple: the British government owed the merchants compensation for the opium Lin had destroyed, and it owed them protection against future seizures. The argument required a reframing of what had happened. The opium had been surrendered under duress, Matheson wrote. The merchants had been held prisoner, their lives threatened, their supplies cut off. The Chinese authorities had acted not as legitimate enforcers of their own laws but as pirates, seizing property by force and destroying it without compensation. The British flag had been insulted. British subjects had been threatened. The government had no choice but to respond.

The argument was not universally accepted. In London, there were those who questioned whether the British government should go to war over a trade that many considered immoral. The opium trade had its defenders, but it also had its critics. The abolitionists had been campaigning against it for years. Some in Parliament saw the Chinese action as a legitimate exercise of sovereignty, a response to a trade that had been forced on China against its will. The debate was not yet settled, and Matheson knew that the outcome would depend on how the question was framed.

He had been preparing the ground for months. The Canton Register, the newspaper he owned and edited, had been publishing accounts of the crisis from the merchants’ perspective. The letters to the editors, the reports from the factories, the dispatches from Elliot—all of it had been crafted to present a consistent narrative. The Chinese were the aggressors. The British merchants were the victims. The government had a duty to act.

The memorandum was the latest iteration of that narrative. Matheson had shown it to the other merchants in Macao, seeking their signatures, their support. The response had been generally positive. The opium trade had been a collective enterprise, and its destruction had been a collective loss. The agency houses had all suffered. The question now was whether they could translate that loss into political pressure.

The mechanism for that translation was already in motion. Jardine had arrived in London in May, and he had been meeting with government officials, with MPs, with anyone who would listen. His message was the same as Matheson’s, but his audience was different. In London, he could speak directly to the men who made policy. He could present the case for war in terms they understood: the financial stakes, the national honor, the precedent that Chinese action would set if it went unanswered. The opium seized by Lin had been worth millions of dollars. The merchants who had lost it were owed compensation, and the only way to secure that compensation was through force.

The plan Jardine presented to Palmerston was detailed. In 1839, Jardine successfully persuaded the British Foreign Minister to wage war on China, giving a full detailed plan for war, including strategic maps, battle strategies, the indemnifications and political demands from China, and even the number of ships and troops required. The plan was a political one. It laid out what the British government should demand in exchange for peace: compensation for the seized opium, the opening of new ports to trade, the establishment of a British resident in Peking, and the cession of an island where British merchants could trade under their own laws.

The demands were ambitious. They represented a fundamental transformation of the relationship between Britain and China. For decades, the British had traded on Chinese terms, confined to Canton, subject to Chinese laws, dealing with Chinese merchants through the Co-hong system. The war Jardine proposed would end that system. It would open China to British trade on British terms. It would replace the old monopoly with a new order, one in which the agency houses could operate freely, without the constraints that had bound them since the East India Company’s charter had first restricted them to the Canton factories.

The transformation had been coming for years. When the Company’s monopoly ended in 1834, the agency houses had rushed to fill the vacuum. Jardine, Matheson & Co. Had been among the first to seize the opportunity. They had expanded their operations, increased their shipments, built new ships to meet the demand. The clippers had been part of that expansion. The Red Rover had been the model for a generation of ships, all built for speed, all built for the same trade.

Now that trade had been destroyed, and the ships that had served it were idle. Matheson could see them from his window in Macao, the masts of the clipper fleet rising above the harbor. They were not warships, but they could be made to serve a war. Their speed made them useful for carrying dispatches, for scouting enemy positions, for supporting naval operations. The captains who had sailed them knew the coast better than any naval officer. The pilots who had guided them through the estuary knew the channels, the shoals, the approaches to Canton. If war came, they would be useful.

Matheson’s job was to make sure that war did come. The memorandum was part of that work. So were the meetings with naval officers, the consultations with other merchants, the coordination of intelligence from up the coast. The Chinese had not stopped at destroying the opium. They were still demanding that the merchants sign a bond promising never to trade opium again, on penalty of death. Elliot had refused, and the stand-off continued. The factories remained closed. The trade remained suspended. The ships remained idle.

The pressure was building on both sides. Lin had made his position clear: the opium trade had to end, and the British had to accept Chinese law or face the consequences. Elliot had made his position equally clear: British subjects could not be subjected to Chinese justice, and the British government would not accept the humiliation of its nationals. The two positions were irreconcilable. The only question was whether the conflict would be resolved through negotiation or through force.

Matheson had no doubt about the answer. Negotiation had failed. Lin had shown no willingness to compromise, and the British government had shown no willingness to accept Chinese terms. The only path forward was war. The memorandum on his desk was an argument for that war, a justification for the use of force, a case for intervention that would transform a commercial dispute into a national cause.

He picked up his pen and made a final correction to the text. The language had to be precise. The argument had to be clear. The document would be read by men who knew nothing of the trade, nothing of the conditions in Canton, nothing of the system that had produced the clippers and the agency houses and the receiving ships at Lintin. It had to explain all of that in terms they could understand, and it had to do so in a way that made the case for war seem not just reasonable but necessary.

The work took hours. The light from the window shifted across the page. The clerk who kept the letter books waited in the outer office, ready to copy the final version for the packet. Matheson wrote, and revised, and wrote again. The argument took shape on the page, paragraph by paragraph, the case building toward its conclusion.

When he was finished, he called for the clerk. The document was copied, sealed, and placed with the other letters for the next ship. The ship would sail within the week, weather permitting, carrying the memorandum to London, where it would join the other dispatches, the other petitions, the other arguments that were converging on Palmerston’s desk.

The process of translation had begun months earlier, when the first reports of Lin’s demands had reached Macao. The merchants had met, had debated, had drafted their first protests. The crisis had escalated through the spring and summer, the detention in Canton, the surrender of the opium, the destruction at Chuenpi. Elliot had issued his proclamation, ordering all British subjects to leave Canton and assemble at Macao. The clipper fleet had gathered at Hong Kong, a visible reminder of British presence in the estuary. The political pressure had built, layer by layer, argument by argument, until it had become a force that could not be ignored.

Matheson’s role in that process had been central. He had coordinated the merchants’ response, drafted the petitions, managed the correspondence. He had kept the lines of communication open between Macao and London, between the merchants and the government, between the commercial world and the political one. The memorandum was the latest product of that work, but it would not be the last.

The question of compensation remained unresolved. The merchants had submitted claims for the opium they had surrendered, claims that totaled millions of dollars. The British government had not yet accepted responsibility for those claims, but it had not rejected them either. The debate in Parliament had been fierce, with critics arguing that the government should not go to war for a trade that many considered immoral, and defenders arguing that the issue was sovereignty and the protection of British subjects abroad.

Matheson understood the distinction, and he used it. The memorandum did not defend the opium trade. It defended the rights of British merchants to trade without fear of arbitrary seizure, to be protected by their government when their property was taken by force. The moral question was left to others. The argument Matheson made was a legal and political one: the Chinese government had violated the rights of British subjects, and the British government had an obligation to respond.

The argument was not new. It had been made before, in the wake of the Napier affair in 1834, when Lord Napier had been expelled from Canton and had died soon after. Matheson had been involved in that debate as well, traveling to England to argue for a stronger British presence in China. He had returned in 1836, preparing to take over the firm as Jardine prepared to retire. The seeds of the current crisis had been planted then, in the failure of the British government to respond decisively to Chinese intransigence.

Now the crisis had come to a head. Lin’s destruction of the opium had been the most dramatic action any Chinese official had ever taken against the foreign trade. It had demonstrated the vulnerability of the system the agency houses had built, the fragility of the arrangements that had allowed them to operate for decades. The clippers had been built for speed, but speed offered no defense against an Imperial Commissioner with the Emperor’s backing and the will to use it. The receiving ships could store the opium, but they could not protect it from seizure. The pilots and the compradors and the corrupt officials who had facilitated the trade could not stand against a determined crackdown.

The system had been exposed. Its weaknesses had been revealed. The question now was whether it could be restored, and on what terms.

Matheson’s answer was that it could be restored, but only through force. The Chinese government would not negotiate away its right to enforce its own laws. It would not accept the opium trade as a legitimate enterprise. It would not open new ports or allow a British resident in Peking or cede an island for foreign settlement. Those demands could only be achieved through war, and war could only be achieved if the British government could be persuaded to fight.

The memorandum was part of that persuasion. So were the letters, the reports, the intelligence that flowed from Macao to London. Matheson coordinated it all, the collection of information, the drafting of arguments, the transmission of dispatches. He was not a military man, but he understood that wars were won in the minds of those who decided to fight them.

The work continued through October and into November. The ships came and went, carrying letters, carrying news, carrying the arguments that would shape the debate in London. Matheson met with naval officers, discussed strategy, shared intelligence about Chinese defenses. He corresponded with other merchants, coordinating the claims, building the case for compensation. He wrote and revised and wrote again, the words accumulating on the page, the argument taking shape.

The clipper fleet remained at anchor, visible from the shore, a reminder of what had been built and what had been lost. The Red Rover and her sisters had been designed for a world that no longer existed. They had been built to carry opium, to run the monsoon, to serve a trade that had been destroyed by a Chinese official with the will to enforce his Emperor’s laws. The ships were still there, but their purpose had been taken from them.

Matheson looked at them sometimes, when the work allowed. He thought about the system they represented, the capital that had been invested in them, the crews that had sailed them, the trade they had served. All of it had been directed toward a single end: the delivery of opium to the Chinese market. That end had been achieved year after year, through innovation, through investment, through the relentless pressure of competition. The clippers had been the fastest ships in the trade, and the trade had been the most profitable in the East.

Now the trade was gone, and the clippers were idle, and the question was what would come next.

Matheson’s answer was war. The memorandum made that clear. The arguments he had crafted, the intelligence he had gathered, the coordination he had managed—all of it pointed toward the same conclusion. The British government had to act. It had to demand compensation, it had to demand protection, it had to demand the opening of China to British trade. And it had to be prepared to enforce those demands with force.

The pressure had been building for months. Now it was approaching its breaking point. The dispatches Matheson had sent were on their way to London. The arguments they contained would be heard in Parliament, in the Foreign Office, in the corridors of power. The decision would be made there, not in Macao. But the case for that decision had been made here, in the letter books of Jardine, Matheson & Co., in the memoranda and the petitions and the reports that had flowed from this small room in the Portuguese settlement.

Matheson set down his pen. The work was done, for now. The memorandum was finished, the packet was ready, the ship would sail. All that remained was to wait for the response, and to prepare for what would follow.

The clerk came in to collect the papers. He counted them, checked the seals, and placed them in the leather satchel that would travel to the waterfront. The light was fading outside. The masts of the clipper fleet were silhouetted against the evening sky, motionless, waiting.

The pressure had been handed off. Matheson’s dispatches were en route to London, carrying the crafted narrative that would justify war.
