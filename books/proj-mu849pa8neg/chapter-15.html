Chapter 15

The Twenty Thousand Chests

A memorandum dated the twenty-first day of the third month of the nineteenth year of Daoguang lay on the table. It delivered a final demand, requiring the surrender of all foreign-held opium for destruction. The blank tally sheet before the assembled merchants awaited its numbers, ready for the clerk’s pen and wet inkstone to transform wealth into concrete accounting. The ships at Lintin rode at anchor, their holds full of contraband, their captains awaiting orders that would never come from the man who had once given them. Now, with William Jardine gone to England, the leadership of the trade fell to James Matheson, who faced the crisis alone.

The merchants argued. Some advocated resistance, trusting that the British government would intervene, that the Royal Navy would arrive, that pressure would break before they surrendered the commodity that paid for the tea Europe demanded. Others calculated costs: the value of the opium, the credit extended by Indian merchants, the insurance that might or might not cover confiscation by a foreign government. The number, when it finally came, would be 20, 283 chests.

—-

Lin Zexu had arrived in Canton on the tenth day of the third month, and from the moment his procession entered the city gates, the rhythm of the trade began to unravel. The commissioner carried the full authority of the emperor, granted in response to a crisis building for years. The outflow of silver to pay for opium had reached levels threatening imperial stability. Memorials crossing the emperor’s desk described millions of taels draining annually, a hemorrhage of specie distorting the economy and corrupting officials tasked with stopping it. Lin had been chosen for his reputation of incorruptibility and his understanding that edicts alone could not stop the trade. It required physical destruction of the drug and permanent removal of the merchants profiting from it.

His first act was arresting the Chinese merchants serving as links between foreign traders and the domestic market. The Hong merchants, licensed intermediaries handling all legal trade, found themselves imprisoned in their own guildhall, held responsible for opium flowing through the system they ostensibly controlled. Lin understood that foreign merchants could not operate without Chinese cooperation. By seizing the Hong merchants, he cut the trade’s nervous system.

Then he turned to the foreigners themselves.

The edict reaching the factories on the eighteenth day of the third month was unambiguous. Foreign merchants were to surrender all opium in their possession within three days. They were to sign a bond pledging never again to bring the drug to China, a bond carrying the penalty of death for any violation. The language was precise: any foreign ship bringing opium would see that ship and all its goods confiscated, and the foreigners would be put to death according to the laws.

The merchants refused. Protests were written. Appeals were made to principles of international commerce, sanctity of property, and rights of British subjects under Crown protection. A substantial fine was offered, measured in silver, that would have enriched the provincial treasury without destroying the trade. Lin rejected every proposal. The opium itself was the object. Nothing else would satisfy the emperor’s command.

Now the factories were surrounded. Chinese servants had been withdrawn. Fresh food supplies had stopped. Soldiers could be heard outside the windows, banners of imperial troops draped across barricades sealing the streets. The merchants were prisoners in their own counting houses.

—-

The tally sheet waited.

James Matheson had argued for resistance. He had returned to China in 1836 to prepare taking over the firm as William Jardine arranged his temporarily delayed retirement. Jardine had left Canton on the twenty-sixth of January 1839, sailing for England with intention of presenting the China question directly to the British government. In his absence, Matheson faced the crisis Jardine had predicted but neither man had expected to arrive with such speed.

Matheson understood the numbers better than anyone in the room. Jardine, Matheson & Co. Controlled roughly half of China’s foreign trade by value. Operations stretched from Calcutta to Canton, from auction houses where the East India Company sold Bengal opium to receiving ships at Lintin where the drug was stored before sale to Chinese smugglers. The clippers Jardine had commissioned—the Red Rover and her successors—had been built to outrun the monsoon, to deliver cargo before the season turned, to compress time between production and sale into weeks rather than months. Speed was the competitive advantage. Speed had allowed the firm to dominate the trade.

Speed meant nothing now.

The clippers at Lintin rode at anchor, holds full of a commodity that could not be moved. The receiving ships—hulks serving as floating warehouses at the Pearl River’s mouth—contained thousands of chests purchased on credit from Indian merchants, insured against loss at sea, entered in ledgers as assets against which the firm had borrowed to expand. Every chest represented a chain of obligations stretching back to Calcutta and forward to London. Surrendering the opium meant breaking those chains, absorbing losses that would cripple credit, admitting that the fastest, most efficient, most profitable opium operation in the history of the China trade had been halted by a Chinese official with an imperial mandate.

The argument continued through the night. The merchants were not unified. Partners of Jardine, Matheson & Co. Held the largest share of opium at Lintin. Representatives of Dent & Co., the firm’s chief rival, were present, their head Lancelot Dent specifically named in Lin’s edicts as a target for arrest. Smaller operators, independent traders who had ridden the boom, faced ruin. Captain Charles Elliot, the British Superintendent of Trade, had arrived in Canton with neither authority to negotiate treaties nor military force to back his presence.

Elliot had tried to mediate. He wrote to Lin, explaining that British merchants could not simply hand over property belonging to investors in India and England. He attempted invoking principles of international law, appealing to the commissioner’s sense of justice, finding some formula that would satisfy imperial demand without destroying merchants’ credit. Lin’s responses were patient but implacable. The opium must be surrendered. The bond must be signed. There was no other way.

—-

On the morning of the twenty-first day, the merchants reached their decision. They would surrender the opium. Continued detention, the possibility of execution, complete shutdown of all legal trade—the alternative was unacceptable. The tally sheet could no longer remain blank.

The clerk dipped his pen in ink.

Jardine, Matheson & Co. Pledged seven thousand chests. Dent & Co. Pledged two thousand. Other firms, smaller traders, independent operators added contributions. Numbers accumulated on the page, each line representing a shipment that had crossed the Bay of Bengal, a voyage testing clippers against the monsoon, a transaction transferring wealth from one side of the world to the other. The total reached 20, 283 chests.

The merchants signed. Elliot, as representative of the British government, attached his signature. The tally sheet, now complete, was delivered to Lin Zexu’s headquarters.

The commissioner received it without comment. He had expected more, demanded more. But 20, 283 chests was a beginning. It was nearly all the opium stored at Lintin and other receiving stations in the Pearl River estuary. It was enough to demonstrate his strategy was working.

Now came the logistics.

—-

The transfer of 20, 283 chests from receiving ships to government custody required planning, coordination, and cooperation of the very merchants surrendering the drug. Lin Zexu ordered the opium brought to Humen, the Bocca Tigris, the narrow passage where the Pearl River emptied into the estuary. There, at the foot of forts guarding the approach to Canton, chests would be counted, verified, and prepared for destruction.

The receiving ships at Lintin were anchored some distance from Humen. Chests had to be loaded onto smaller vessels, transported down the estuary, and transferred to government junks waiting at the forts. Foreign merchants were required to oversee the process, ensuring every chest was accounted for, that no substitution or concealment occurred. Chinese officials watched the foreigners. Foreigners watched the Chinese. Opium moved from ship to ship in a slow, deliberate procession.

The clerk who had filled the tally sheet now recorded a different set of numbers. Each chest bore a mark identifying its origin—the name of the agency house that had consigned it, the number assigned in the firm’s ledgers. As chests came off receiving ships, the clerk wrote down marks. As they went onto government vessels, he checked them against his list. The work required precision. Lin Zexu had made clear that any discrepancy, any missing chest, would be treated as evidence of bad faith.

The operation took days. Weather was hot. The estuary was crowded with vessels—receiving ships with cargo of contraband, government junks with armed crews, foreign merchant ships ordered to wait, small craft ferrying between them. Sailors—Chinese and foreign, lascar and British—worked under eyes of officials watching for any sign of deception. Opium was too valuable for ordinary handling. Every chest was a small fortune in silver. Every chest was also evidence of a crime.

—-

Lin Zexu watched from his headquarters. Memorials sent to the emperor described the process in detail: the number of chests, names of firms, quantities of opium in each consignment. The commissioner understood that destroying the drug was only part of his mission. The other part was demonstrating—to the emperor, to officials who had failed to stop the trade, to foreign merchants who had profited—that the Qing state retained power to enforce its laws.

The memorials also revealed Lin’s strategy for dealing with foreigners. He had studied the trade, understood its structure. He knew foreign merchants depended on Chinese intermediaries, on Hong merchants handling legal trade and smugglers moving opium from receiving ships to shore. By arresting Hong merchants, cutting off supply chains, surrounding factories and detaining foreigners, he had turned the trade’s own complexity against it. The system moving opium from India to China with such efficiency had no mechanism for resistance. Clippers could outrun the monsoon, but they could not outrun an imperial edict.

Lin’s dispatches also revealed his understanding of broader context. He knew foreign merchants would appeal to their government. He expected British protest, demands for compensation, threats of force. But he calculated that the British government, faced with facts of the trade—violation of Chinese law, corruption of Chinese officials, drain of silver from the empire—would find it difficult to justify military intervention. Opium was contraband. Its seizure was legal. Merchants had no standing to complain about enforcement of laws they had willfully broken.

—-

The last of the 20, 283 chests reached Humen on the twenty-fourth day of the fifth month. Receiving ships were empty. Foreign merchants had fulfilled their obligation. Now they waited to see what Lin would do next.

The commissioner had ordered construction of three great trenches on the beach at Humen. Each trench was lined with stone and connected to the river by channels that could be opened and closed. Opium was to be destroyed by water and fire, a method Lin devised to ensure none of the drug could be salvaged or sold. Chests would be opened, opium balls broken apart, powder mixed with water and lime, resulting sludge flushed into the river.

Destruction would take weeks. The process required labor, supervision, and constant attention of officials tasked with preventing theft. Opium was valuable enough that even soldiers guarding it might be tempted to steal a ball, a fragment, a handful of powder. Lin ordered trenches watched day and night, workers searched when leaving the site, any attempt at theft punished with immediate execution.

Foreign merchants were invited to watch. Lin wanted them to see what had happened to the drug they had brought to China, to understand that the trade was finished, that the system they had built had been dismantled, that clippers and receiving ships and smugglers and credit networks had been rendered useless by application of imperial power.

Some merchants accepted. They stood on the beach at Humen watching chests being opened, opium dumped into trenches, water and lime doing their work. They saw physical substance of their trade reduced to sludge and washed away. Wealth that had crossed oceans and continents disappeared into the Pearl River.

—-

Destruction of the opium was a victory for Lin Zexu. It was also victory for the system he represented: imperial bureaucracy, Confucian order, principle that the state had right and power to regulate commerce in interests of the people. The commissioner had achieved what generations of officials had failed. He had stopped the trade.

But the victory contained seeds of its own reversal.

Merchants who had surrendered opium had not accepted justice of Lin’s actions. Capitulation came under duress, and compensation was expected. Credit financing the trade, advances from Indian merchants, insurance from British underwriters, capital invested by partners in London, remained unpaid. Firms that had dominated the trade, Jardine, Matheson & Co. chief among them, had absorbed losses that would be presented to the British government as basis for claim against China.

William Jardine was already in London. He had arrived in England in April, carrying detailed knowledge of trade operations, connections to merchants and manufacturers depending on the China market, determination to present the case for intervention. There, Jardine presented Lord Palmerston with a comprehensive plan including strategic maps, battle strategies, indemnifications and political demands from China, and the number of troops and warships required. The document became known as the Jardine Paper.

Merchants in Canton did not know Jardine had already begun his campaign. They knew only that they had surrendered opium, signed the bond, complied with Lin’s demands. They expected release. They expected resumption of legal trade in tea and silk. They expected the crisis would pass, that opium trade would find new routes and new methods, that the system would adapt and survive.

Lin Zexu had other plans.

—-

The commissioner’s next edict arrived at the factories the day after the last chest was counted at Humen. Foreign merchants were to sign a new bond, a permanent bond, pledging never to bring opium to China again, on penalty of death. The language was explicit: any foreigner bringing opium to China would suffer death, and goods would be confiscated. The bond was not mere formality. It was a legal document binding merchants to Chinese law, subjecting them to Chinese penalties, stripping them of protections claimed as British subjects.

Merchants refused. Opium had been surrendered. Detention had been endured. But lives would not be signed away to foreign jurisdiction. Elliot supported them. The bond was unacceptable, he wrote to Lin. British subjects could not be bound by Chinese criminal law. Penalty of death applied to foreigners was violation of principles governing relations between nations.

Lin’s response was patient but firm. The bond was not negotiable. Merchants had violated Chinese law, brought poison into the empire, corrupted the people and drained the silver. If they wished to trade in China, they must accept laws of China. If they refused, they must leave.

The commissioner gave them three days to decide.

—-

Refusal of the bond was unanimous. Merchants understood signing meant end of independence, subjection to arbitrary power of a legal system they did not control. They also understood refusal meant expulsion. Lin had made it clear: no bond, no trade.

Three days passed. Merchants packed belongings, closed counting houses, arranged passage on ships waiting in the estuary, ships with no cargo to carry, ships with no purpose in a China without trade.

On the third day, Lin issued his final order. Foreign merchants were to leave Canton within three days, proceed to Macao, the Portuguese settlement at the mouth of the Pearl River, and await further instructions. They were not to return to Canton until they had signed the bond.

Merchants complied. They left factories that had been home and workplace, buildings housing operations of great agency houses, rooms where ledgers had been kept and letters written and deals struck. They walked through Canton streets, past crowds watching in silence, past soldiers at barricades, past shops and tea houses and temples of a city that had been center of their world.

They boarded boats taking them down the river. They passed forts at Humen, where last traces of opium were being washed out of trenches. They passed Lintin, where receiving ships rode empty at anchor. They passed the mouth of the estuary, where clippers waited for orders that would never come.

—-

The fleet that had carried opium to China now lay idle. The Red Rover and her successors, fast ships built to beat the monsoon, had nothing to carry. Receiving hulks at Lintin had no cargo to store. Fast crabs and scrambling dragons that had taken chests ashore had no work. The system moving drug from India to China with such efficiency had been halted by determination of a single official.

Merchants who had owned ships and operated trade faced a void. Capital invested in opium was gone. Credit sustaining operations was exhausted. Relationships connecting Calcutta to Canton to London had been severed. Trade that had made them rich had been abolished by stroke of a pen and labor of men with shovels on a beach at Humen.

The void was not empty. It was filled with anger, calculation, determination to recover what had been lost. Merchants had surrendered opium under duress. They had not accepted justice of the surrender. They had not forgiven humiliation of detention. They had not forgotten value of what had been destroyed.

Jardine was in London. Matheson was in Macao. Ships were in the estuary. Opium was gone. The question facing merchants, facing the British government, shaping the next decade of relations between China and the West, was what would happen next.

Clippers had been built for speed. They had been designed to outrun the monsoon, to deliver cargo before the season turned, to compress time and distance into profit. They had succeeded. They had made trade faster, more efficient, more profitable than anyone had imagined.

They had also made trade more visible, more concentrated, more vulnerable to the kind of attack Lin Zexu had launched. Receiving ships at Lintin had been convenient: floating warehouse at the river’s mouth, close to market, easy to access, easy to defend against pirates and smugglers. They had also been impossible to hide. Clippers racing to deliver cargo had left trail of records, bills of lading, insurance policies, letters documenting every voyage, every sale, every transaction. When Lin demanded opium, merchants could not pretend they did not have it. The system itself provided evidence of their guilt.

Speed that had been trade’s greatest advantage had become its greatest weakness. Efficiency making clippers profitable had made them visible. Concentration allowing agency houses to dominate the market had allowed the commissioner to seize supply in a single, coordinated operation.

Twenty thousand chests had been surrendered. Twenty thousand chests had been destroyed. The system producing them, carrying them across oceans, storing and selling and counting profits they generated, that system had been dismantled in weeks.

Clippers rode at anchor in the estuary, holds empty, crews waiting for orders. Merchants who had owned them sat in Macao, calculating losses and planning next moves. The commissioner who had destroyed the trade returned to dispatches, writing to the emperor that the opium menace had been eliminated, foreigners expelled, coast clear.

The coast was not clear. The void left by destruction of trade demanded to be filled. Losses demanded to be recovered. Humiliation demanded to be avenged. Ships that had carried opium would carry something else: soldiers, guns, force of a British empire presented with cause and plan by the very merchants who had lost the most.
