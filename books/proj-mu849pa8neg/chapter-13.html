Chapter 13

The Commissioner's Edict

A document dated 10 January 1839 arrived in Canton, bearing the red seals of the Imperial Censorate and the Board of Punishments. This memorandum from the Emperor conveyed unambiguous authority, appointing Lin Zexu as Imperial Commissioner with plenipotentiary powers to suppress the opium trade. It specified his superior authority over provincial officials and the penalties of death for traders, severe punishment for facilitators, and disgrace for negligent officials. The edict was part of a long tradition; successive Chinese emperors had issued numerous edicts restricting trade with foreigners under what was known as the Canton System, concerned at what they saw as the encroachment of ‘barbarians’ in their Celestial Kingdom.

Twenty thousand chests of opium lay stored in the receiving hulks at Lintin and scattered among other anchorages along the coast. That inventory represented the accumulated investment of a season’s trade, the capital of the agency houses and their correspondents in India and Britain. It sat in the holds of ships that had been designed to outrun the monsoon, positioned beyond the effective reach of the Canton authorities, protected by a system of bribes and facilitation payments that had proven resilient for decades. The memorial did not mention Lintin by name. It did not have to. The imperial bureaucracy knew where the opium lay, and the foreign merchants would learn soon enough that their concentrated asset had become a target.

Lin Zexu had already departed Huguang. The memorial announced that he would travel with speed, that he would arrive in Canton before the end of the month, and that he expected full cooperation from all parties. The language of the document was administrative, not rhetorical. It listed the ranks of the officials who would accompany him, the scope of the authority granted to him, and the penalties for obstruction. It concluded with a directive: the Viceroy and the Governor were to prepare detailed reports on the state of the trade, the names of the foreign merchants involved, and the quantities of opium currently held in the receiving ships. The information was to be ready upon the Commissioner’s arrival.

The memorial passed through the yamen of the Viceroy of Liangguang, then to the offices of the Hoppo, the superintendent of maritime customs, and finally to the Hong merchants, the licensed Chinese traders who served as the only legal intermediaries between the foreign factories and the imperial administration. By evening, the contents were known in the Thirteen Factories. The foreign community did not receive the document directly. They learned of it through their Hong merchants, who came to the factories that afternoon with faces that signaled trouble.

Howqua, the senior Hong merchant, called upon William Jardine at Number 4, Hong Kong. The conversation was brief. Howqua conveyed the essential facts: the Emperor had appointed a special commissioner, a man of reputation for severity, who would arrive within weeks with powers to demand the surrender of all opium and to punish those who refused. Jardine asked what form that punishment might take. Howqua replied that the memorial specified death for Chinese subjects who traded in opium. For foreigners, the penalty was unclear, but the Commissioner would have the authority to cut off trade, to detain ships, and to hold the Hong merchants responsible for the actions of their foreign correspondents. Howqua left without making any request. His visit was a warning, not a negotiation.

Jardine wrote to James Matheson that evening. The letter, preserved in the Jardine Matheson letter books, recorded the facts as he understood them and assessed their implications for the firm’s operations. He noted the appointment of Lin Zexu, the scope of his authority, and the expected date of his arrival. He noted that the Hong merchants had been ordered to prepare reports on the quantities of opium held by each foreign house. He observed that the language of the memorial was more severe than any previous imperial pronouncement on the trade, and that the grant of plenipotentiary authority to a single commissioner represented a departure from the usual pattern of provincial administration. He concluded that the situation required immediate attention.

The options were limited. The firm could continue to sell opium from the receiving ships at Lintin, accepting the increased risk of seizure or arrest. It could reduce its operations, withdrawing ships and inventory from the coast until the Commissioner’s intentions became clear. Or it could attempt to negotiate, using the Hong merchants as intermediaries to offer cooperation in exchange for some form of accommodation. Jardine’s letter did not commit to any of these courses. It asked Matheson, then at Lintin supervising the receipt of the season’s opium, to return to Canton for consultation.

The Canton Register, the English-language newspaper published in the factories, carried the news in its issue of 14 January. The editor, John Slade, had obtained a translation of the memorial’s key passages, and he printed them alongside commentary that reflected the foreign community’s mixture of alarm and skepticism.

The Register noted that imperial pronouncements against opium had been issued before, in 1796, in 1800, in 1814, and at intervals since. Each had been followed by a period of heightened enforcement, and each had ended with the resumption of trade under modified arrangements. The bribes had increased. The precautions had tightened. The trade had continued.

The Register asked whether this time would be different, and it answered with a qualified maybe. The appointment of a special commissioner with plenipotentiary powers was unusual. The personal involvement of the Emperor, suggested by the language of the memorial, was significant. But the fundamental constraints remained: the imperial government needed the revenue from the Canton trade, and the foreign merchants held the leverage of controlling the supply of opium. The Register advised its readers to wait, to watch, and to avoid hasty decisions.

The foreign community had reason for skepticism. The trade had operated under the shadow of imperial prohibition for decades, and it had grown under that shadow into an enterprise of unprecedented scale. The clippers that carried opium from Calcutta and Bombay to Lintin had been designed to outrun both the monsoon and the patrols of the Chinese navy. The receiving hulks at Lintin had been positioned beyond the effective reach of the Canton authorities. The system of bribes and facilitation payments that connected the foreign merchants to the Hong merchants, and through them to the officials of the maritime customs, had proven resilient under pressure. The question was whether Lin Zexu represented a change in kind or merely a change in degree.

The Chinese sources suggested a change in kind. The memorial appointing Lin had been drafted in response to a series of memorials from officials in the coastal provinces, each describing the spread of opium addiction and the drain of silver from the imperial economy. The Emperor had read these documents, and he had concluded that the situation required extraordinary measures. He had selected Lin Zexu because Lin had a reputation for competence and incorruptibility, and because Lin had successfully suppressed opium cultivation in Huguang through a combination of enforcement and moral suasion. The Emperor’s edict granting Lin his powers was explicit: the opium trade had become a threat to the stability of the realm, and its suppression was a matter of imperial priority.

Lin Zexu arrived in Canton on 25 January. He traveled with a retinue of officials and secretaries, and he proceeded directly to the yamen of the Viceroy, where he received the reports that had been prepared for his review. The reports detailed the quantities of opium held at Lintin, the names of the foreign merchants who owned it, and the names of the ships that had carried it. The numbers were substantial. The reports estimated that approximately twenty thousand chests of opium were held in the receiving ships at Lintin and other anchorages along the coast. The foreign merchants named in the reports included Jardine, Matheson and Company, Dent and Company, and the other major agency houses. The ships named included the clippers that had been built to serve the trade: the Red Rover, the Water Witch, the Sylph, and others.

Lin reviewed the reports and issued his first edict on 30 January. The edict was addressed to the foreign merchants of Canton, and it was delivered to them through the Hong merchants. It began with a statement of the imperial position: opium was a poisonous drug, its importation was prohibited by imperial decree, and its continued trade was a crime against the Emperor and the people of China. It continued with a demand: the foreign merchants were to surrender all opium currently held at Lintin and elsewhere within three days. It concluded with a warning: failure to comply would result in the severest penalties, including the detention of foreign merchants, the seizure of their ships, and the permanent closure of the Canton trade.

The edict was written in classical Chinese, and it was translated for the foreign merchants by their Hong merchants. The translation was imperfect, but the meaning was clear. Lin was demanding the surrender of the entire inventory of opium held by the foreign merchants at Lintin. The inventory represented the capital of the trade, the accumulated investment of the agency houses and their correspondents in India and Britain. Its surrender would mean financial ruin for the merchants who complied.

The foreign merchants met on 1 February to discuss their response. The meeting was held in the rooms of the British Factory, and it was attended by representatives of the major agency houses. The minutes of the meeting, recorded in the Jardine Matheson letter books, show a community divided between those who advocated compliance and those who advocated resistance.

The argument for compliance was practical. The Commissioner had the authority to cut off trade, and the foreign merchants had no means to compel him to relent. Their ships were fast, but they could not force their way into Canton against the will of the imperial government. Their capital was mobile, but it was tied up in the opium held at Lintin, and that opium could not be moved quickly or secretly. If they refused to surrender the opium, they risked the seizure of their inventory and the permanent loss of access to the China market. If they complied, they would lose the opium, but they might preserve the trade.

The argument for resistance was also practical, but it rested on a different calculation. The foreign merchants had faced imperial edicts before, and they had survived by holding firm, by offering bribes, by waiting for the pressure to ease. If they surrendered the opium now, they would establish a precedent that would make future trade impossible. The Commissioner would know that he could extract concessions by threat, and he would continue to extract them until nothing remained. Better to refuse, to call his bluff, to trust that the imperial government would not risk the loss of the Canton trade over a question of opium.

Jardine spoke for resistance. He had built the trade over two decades, and he understood its economics. The opium at Lintin represented the investment of the current season, and its loss would be substantial. But the greater loss would be the surrender of the principle that the foreign merchants could operate independently of imperial interference. Jardine argued that the Commissioner’s demands were an opening position, not a final offer. If the merchants held firm, they might negotiate a settlement that preserved their capital and their position. If they capitulated, they would have nothing left to negotiate with.

The meeting ended without a decision. The merchants agreed to request an extension of the deadline, and they drafted a reply to the Commissioner stating that they would consider his demands and respond in due course. The reply was delivered through the Hong merchants on 2 February.

Lin Zexu’s response was immediate. He summoned the Hong merchants to his yamen and berated them for their failure to convey the seriousness of his demands. He reminded them that they were responsible for the conduct of the foreign merchants, and that they would suffer the consequences if the foreigners refused to comply. He ordered them to return to the factories and to demand a definitive answer within one day.

The Hong merchants returned to the factories that evening. Their demeanor had changed. Howqua and the other senior merchants had weathered imperial disapproval before, but they had never faced a commissioner with Lin’s powers and Lin’s evident determination. They conveyed the Commissioner’s message: the foreign merchants must surrender the opium, or the Hong merchants would be held accountable. The penalty for their failure would be severe, potentially including execution.

The pressure on the foreign merchants had shifted. The question was no longer how to negotiate with a distant imperial government. It was now how to confront a commissioner who held the power of life and death over the Hong merchants, and through them over the foreign community. The calculus of resistance had changed.

The Canton Register reported on the confrontation in its issue of 4 February. The tone had shifted from skepticism to alarm. The editor noted that the Commissioner had backed his demands with the threat of force, and that the Hong merchants had been placed in an impossible position. The foreign merchants, the Register observed, were the guests of the Chinese government, and their status as guests provided them with certain protections. But those protections were only as strong as the government’s willingness to honor them. If the Commissioner chose to treat the foreign merchants as criminals rather than guests, there was little they could do to stop him.

The Jardine Matheson letter books from this period show the partners grappling with the implications of Lin’s demands. Jardine wrote to Matheson on 5 February, acknowledging that the situation had become more serious than they had initially assessed. He noted that the Hong merchants were now personally at risk, and that their fear would make them unreliable intermediaries. He suggested that the firm should begin to consider contingency plans, including the possibility of withdrawing its ships from the coast and suspending its operations until the crisis passed.

Matheson, who had returned to Canton from Lintin, replied the following day. He argued that withdrawal would be premature. The firm had invested heavily in the season’s opium, and the opium was still on the ships at Lintin. If they withdrew now, they would abandon that investment to whatever fate the Commissioner chose for it. Better to continue the negotiation, to seek some form of accommodation that would allow the trade to continue.

The negotiation took the form of a proposal. The foreign merchants offered to surrender a portion of the opium held at Lintin, approximately one thousand chests, as a gesture of good faith. They asked in return that the Commissioner allow the remaining opium to be sold under modified arrangements, with increased payments to the imperial treasury and stricter controls over the retail trade in China. The proposal was delivered through the Hong merchants on 7 February.

Lin Zexu’s response was to reject the proposal and to escalate the pressure. He issued a second edict on 10 February, this one addressed directly to the foreign merchants rather than through the Hong merchants. The edict reiterated the demand for the surrender of all opium, and it added a new requirement: the foreign merchants must sign a bond, a written pledge, promising not to trade opium in the future and accepting the penalty of death for any violation. The edict specified that trade would not resume until the opium was surrendered and the bonds were signed.

The bond was the crucial element. Lin understood that the surrender of the current inventory would not prevent the arrival of new opium in future seasons. The trade had proven too profitable, and the foreign merchants had too much invested in its continuation. The only way to stop the trade permanently was to extract a commitment from the merchants themselves, a commitment backed by the threat of personal liability. The bond would make each merchant individually responsible for any opium found on his ships, and it would subject him to Chinese law in the event of a violation.

The foreign merchants refused to sign. The bond would have required them to accept the jurisdiction of Chinese courts and the application of Chinese penalties, including execution, for violations of imperial law. They had never accepted such terms, and they were not prepared to accept them now. Their position had always been that they were subject to their own laws, not to the laws of China, and that any disputes arising from their trade should be resolved through negotiation, not through submission to Chinese authority.

The refusal to sign the bond was communicated to the Commissioner on 12 February. Lin’s response was to detain the Hong merchants in his yamen, holding them as hostages for the compliance of the foreign merchants. Howqua and the other senior merchants were confined to the yamen grounds, forbidden to return to their homes or their businesses, and informed that they would remain in detention until the opium was surrendered and the bonds were signed.

The detention of the Hong merchants transformed the situation. The foreign merchants had previously calculated that their status as guests provided them with some protection. But the Hong merchants were not guests. They were subjects of the Emperor, and they had no protection against a commissioner acting with imperial authority. If Lin was prepared to detain them, he might be prepared to go further. The foreign community in Canton was small, perhaps two hundred people in total, and they were surrounded by a population of millions who owed them no loyalty.

Lin Zexu issued a third edict on 18 February. This one was addressed to the foreign community as a whole, and it specified the consequences of continued non-compliance. The edict declared that all foreign merchants who refused to surrender their opium and sign the bond would be considered enemies of the state. Their trade would be permanently prohibited. Their factories would be seized. Their persons would be subject to arrest and detention. The edict concluded with a warning: the Emperor’s patience was limited, and the Commissioner’s authority was absolute.

The edict was posted in the streets of Canton, and copies were distributed to the Hong merchants for delivery to the factories. The foreign community read it with a mixture of fear and disbelief. The language was unprecedented. Previous imperial pronouncements had threatened penalties for specific violations, but they had not declared the foreign merchants to be enemies of the state. The declaration implied that the protections of guest status no longer applied.

The Canton Register published a translation of the edict in its issue of 21 February. The editorial that accompanied the translation reflected the growing sense of crisis. The Register noted that the Commissioner had moved from demands to threats, and from threats to declarations. The next step would be action. The foreign merchants had a choice: they could comply, accepting the loss of their capital and the humiliation of submission, or they could resist, accepting the risk of arrest, seizure, and possibly death. The Register offered no advice. There was no good advice to offer.

Lin Zexu issued his fourth edict on 25 February. This one ordered the detention of the foreign merchants in Canton. The factories were to be surrounded by Chinese troops. The foreign merchants were forbidden to leave their compounds, forbidden to communicate with the ships at Lintin, forbidden to conduct any business until the opium was surrendered and the bonds were signed. The edict specified that the detention would continue indefinitely.

The troops arrived that evening. They surrounded the factories, posting guards at each entrance and establishing patrols along the narrow lanes that connected the foreign enclave to the rest of Canton. The foreign merchants found themselves prisoners in their own homes. They could move within the factory compounds, but they could not leave. They could communicate with each other, but they could not communicate with the outside world. Their ships at Lintin were beyond their reach, and their opium was beyond their control.

The Jardine Matheson letter books record the situation with a combination of outrage and resignation. Jardine wrote to Matheson on 26 February, noting that the firm was now effectively under house arrest. The Chinese troops had cut off all communication with the ships, and the partners had no way to issue orders or to receive intelligence. The opium at Lintin was sitting in the receiving hulks, vulnerable to seizure, while the partners who owned it were confined to their rooms in Canton.

The foreign merchants remained in detention for weeks, unable to leave their compounds, unable to communicate with their ships, unable to conduct their business. The Canton Register continued to publish, but its issues were filled with speculation and rumor. The Jardine Matheson letter books grew thin, as the partners had little to report and less to discuss. The trade had stopped. The ships were idle. The opium sat in the holds of the receiving hulks, waiting for a decision that would determine its fate.

Lin Zexu had achieved what no previous imperial official had achieved: he had brought the opium trade to a halt. The foreign merchants were detained. The ships were immobilized. The capital was trapped. The system that had operated for decades, that had grown from a trickle to a flood, that had built fortunes and financed empires, had been stopped by a single man with the authority of the Emperor behind him.

The foreign community waited in their factories, surrounded by Chinese troops, cut off from their ships, uncertain of their fate. The clippers rode at anchor off Lintin, their holds full of contraband, their captains awaiting orders that could not come. The partners in the agency houses faced a choice that was not really a choice: they could surrender their opium and sign the bond, accepting financial ruin and personal humiliation, or they could continue to resist, accepting the risk of indefinite detention. The fleet idled offshore, awaiting a decision that would fracture their leadership.
