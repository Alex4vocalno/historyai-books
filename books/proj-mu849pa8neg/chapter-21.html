Chapter 21

Lin’s Fortifications

Dated 1840, a memorandum dispatched from Canton reached the Emperor’s quarters in Peking, its pages outlining a strategy of denial. It detailed the chains forged for the river channels at the Bogue, the militia raised from coastal villages, and the fortifications under construction. The document argued that British ships, however fast, could not fight without supplies, endure without fresh water, or maintain themselves along a hostile coast with no safe harbor. It cited the opium clippers that had fled and the foreign merchants who had surrendered their stocks as evidence of a system broken. The commissioner who composed it, having revised it through several nights and set his seal to the paper that spring, believed denial would defeat firepower. He did not know that the fleet being assembled at Singapore and Calcutta carried soldiers and artillery, that the calculations made in London had already exceeded the scale he imagined.

Lin had arrived in Canton in March 1839 with extraordinary powers and a clear mandate. The emperor had appointed him specifically to suppress the drug trade draining silver from the empire and corrupting the people. The initial edicts had failed; the provincial officials had colluded; the foreign merchants had evaded. Lin was the court’s answer to administrative failure—a man with a reputation for incorruptibility and the authority to act across bureaucratic boundaries.

He had used those powers decisively, surrounding the foreign factories, detaining the merchants, demanding the surrender of the opium. Twenty thousand chests were delivered. The drugs were destroyed in trenches dug outside the city walls. The clippers and their crews scattered to anchorages along the coast.

The Lintin receiving hulks were abandoned. The fast crabs and scrambling dragons that had carried the chests ashore were burned or seized. The system that had moved Bengal opium from Calcutta to the Pearl River Delta in a matter of weeks, that had beaten the monsoon with sharp-hulled ships and bribed officials, had been dismantled in a single season.

But dismantling the trade and securing the coast were different matters. Lin understood that the foreign merchants would not simply accept their losses. Their ships lingered beyond the reach of his authority, the clippers anchored in the outer islands, the merchants who had retreated to Macao and were now, he heard, sending letters to their governments. The British possessed formidable naval vessels. His memorial to the emperor acknowledged their speed and their firepower, noting that the opium clippers had been built specifically to outrun the imperial junks that tried to intercept them. The Red Rover and her successors had demonstrated what hull design and sail area could achieve. Lin did not dismiss the technical capability of the barbarian ships. What he questioned was their endurance.

The theory of victory that Lin developed in his memorials rested on three pillars. First, the British fleet, whatever its strength, could not operate indefinitely along a hostile coast without secure bases. The opium clippers had relied on the receiving hulks at Lintin, on the Portuguese neutrality of Macao, on the tacit cooperation of provincial officials who had been paid to look away. Lin had eliminated those advantages. The hulks were gone. The officials who had taken bribes had been executed or exiled. The Portuguese authorities in Macao had been warned that harboring British ships would bring consequences. Without shelter, without fresh water, without the ability to repair damaged vessels, the British fleet would have to withdraw or fight under impossible conditions.

Second, the coastal defenses Lin was building would make any attempt to force the river entrances prohibitively costly. The Bogue forts—Humen, Weiyuan, Jingyuan—commanded the narrow channels where the Pearl River met the sea. Lin had ordered the installation of additional guns, the construction of barriers that would force attacking ships into the range of the heaviest artillery, the training of gunners who could hit moving targets. He had commissioned chains to be stretched across the river mouth, barriers that could be raised or lowered to control passage.

The forts had been designed generations earlier, when the primary threat had been pirates and the occasional Portuguese or Dutch vessel, but Lin believed they could be adapted. He sent memorials to the emperor describing the works in progress, the numbers of guns being mounted, the depth of the channels being blocked. The figures were meant to reassure the throne that the barbarians would not simply sail up to Canton as they had done in the past.

Third, Lin placed his faith in the mobilization of local forces. The imperial army was a blunt instrument, designed to suppress rebellions and guard frontiers, not to patrol hundreds of miles of coastline against fast-moving ships. The opium clippers had exploited this limitation, landing their cargoes at isolated beaches and inlets where no garrison existed. Lin’s answer was to create a naval militia, a force of local boats and fishermen who knew the waters, who could be paid and organized to watch for foreign vessels and report their movements. He wrote to the emperor of the “brave and skilled” boatmen of the delta, men who could be trusted to defend their own shores if given proper leadership and compensation. The memorials described a network of watchers, signal towers, and rapid response boats that would make it impossible for any foreign ship to approach the coast without being observed.

The commissioner’s confidence in these measures was genuine, but it rested on assumptions about the threat that would prove catastrophically wrong. Lin believed that the British response would be proportionate to the commercial losses they had suffered. The merchants wanted compensation for the opium that had been destroyed. They wanted their trade restored. They wanted access to Canton and the other ports where they had conducted business. Lin assumed that the military force being assembled would be designed to secure these commercial objectives—to intimidate the imperial court into reversing his policies, to force a negotiation that would restore the old system under new terms. He did not imagine that the British government had decided to use the destruction of the opium as a pretext for a larger war, one designed to humble the Qing dynasty and force open the entire Chinese coast, Canton included.

The intelligence reaching Lin in the spring of 1840 was fragmentary and often contradictory. Agents in Macao reported that ships were gathering in the straits, that soldiers were being embarked, that a fleet larger than any seen in these waters was preparing to sail. But the reports came through intermediaries, were filtered through merchants with their own interests, arrived weeks or months after the events they described. The British expeditionary force assembling under Admiral George Elliot and his cousin Charles Elliot, the chief superintendent of British trade in China, included not just the frigates and sloops that had patrolled these waters for years but transports carrying thousands of troops, artillery designed for siege warfare, and instructions from Lord Palmerston that went far beyond commercial compensation.

Jardine had departed Canton on January 26, 1839, under the pretense of retirement. The Qing government had been pleased to hear of his departure, being more familiar with Jardine as the trading head and quite unfamiliar with Matheson, and had proceeded to stop the opium trafficker.

But Jardine’s retirement had been a feint. He had traveled to London, where he had begun the lobbying campaign that would transform a commercial dispute into a national war.

By the close of 1839, Jardine had persuaded Lord Palmerston to commit to military action against China, providing the foreign minister with comprehensive plans for the campaign, including strategic maps, proposed battle strategies, the indemnifications and political demands to be extracted from China, and specifications for the number of ships and troops required. Matheson had returned to Canton three years earlier to assume control of the firm while Jardine prepared his departure for England. The partners had coordinated their efforts across oceans, one in London pressing the government to act, the other in Macao managing the firm’s affairs and preparing the claims that would compensate them for their losses.

Lin knew nothing of the specific instructions Palmerston had issued to the expeditionary force. He did not know that the British government had demanded compensation for the opium, the cession of an island, the opening of new ports, the establishment of diplomatic relations on terms of equality, and an indemnity that would cover the value of the destroyed drug and the costs of the war itself. The commissioner’s memorials to the emperor reflected his belief that the British were essentially merchants who would use force to secure commercial advantage, not a modern industrial power that had decided to use military force to remake the terms of engagement with China.

The fortifications at the Bogue illustrated both the strengths and the limitations of Lin’s approach. The forts themselves were substantial structures, built of stone and earth, positioned on hillsides that commanded the river channels. Their guns were a mix of older Chinese artillery and some pieces acquired from foreign sources, capable of firing solid shot at approaching vessels. Lin had ordered the channels buoyed and marked, but in a way that would mislead unfamiliar pilots, directing them into the heaviest concentrations of fire. He had commissioned the construction of fire ships, small vessels loaded with combustibles that could be sent against an enemy fleet at anchor. He had organized the local population into militia units, distributing weapons and assigning responsibilities for coastal watch.

But the defenses suffered from fundamental weaknesses that Lin’s memorials did not acknowledge. The guns in the forts were fixed in position, designed to fire down the channels at approaching ships but unable to track vessels that maneuvered quickly or approached from unexpected angles. The fire ships required favorable winds and currents to reach their targets; in the wrong conditions, they were useless. The militia were enthusiastic but untrained, armed with a mix of weapons that included ancient muskets, spears, and the occasional matchlock. Against the disciplined firepower of a modern naval force, their valor would count for little.

Lin’s greatest misjudgment concerned the nature of the British force itself. The opium clippers had been designed for speed, not combat. They carried small crews, minimal armament, and cargoes that made them valuable targets rather than effective fighters. The ships that Lin had faced in his suppression campaign had been merchants, not men-of-war. The Red Rover and her successors could outrun imperial junks, but they could not stand against a frigate’s broadside. Lin had extrapolated from this experience, assuming that the British fleet would be essentially a larger version of the merchant convoys he had scattered. He did not understand that the expeditionary force being assembled included ships of the line, frigates designed for fleet actions, and troops trained in the tactics that had defeated Napoleon.

The memorials Lin sent to the emperor in the spring of 1840 reveal the gap between his understanding and the reality he faced. In one document, he described the British as “mere merchants” who had been forced to call upon their government for assistance because they could not prevail against his measures. He wrote of their “greed and cunning” but dismissed their military capacity, noting that they had been unable to protect their own ships from the enforcement actions he had ordered. He assured the throne that the fortifications being built would be more than adequate to repel any attack, that the British would soon realize the futility of resistance and would come forward to negotiate terms.

In another memorial, Lin acknowledged that the British ships were fast and well-armed, but he argued that their speed was a liability in confined waters. A ship that moved quickly could not navigate narrow channels with care, could not avoid the obstacles he was placing in its path. The faster the ship, the more vulnerable it would be to the concentrated fire of the forts. The theory was not entirely wrong—a fast ship in a restricted channel was indeed at risk—but it assumed that the British would obligingly sail into the traps Lin had prepared, that they would attack where he was strongest rather than where he was weakest.

The commissioner’s intelligence operations extended beyond the coast. Agents in Macao reported on the movements of foreign ships, on the conversations overheard in the taverns and counting houses where the merchants gathered. Summaries of the Canton Register, the English-language newspaper that Matheson had founded, provided accounts of the political debates in London and the commercial calculations of the agency houses. But the information came filtered through interpreters who often did not fully understand the technical or political terms they were translating, and Lin himself had no direct access to English-language sources. He relied on Chinese merchants who dealt with the foreigners, on converted sailors who had worked on the opium clippers, on the fragmentary reports that could be gathered from a world he could not directly observe.

The result was a picture of the British that was accurate in some respects and dangerously incomplete in others. Lin knew that the Jardine-Matheson firm had been the driving force behind the opium trade, that its partners had organized the shipments from Calcutta, that they had owned or chartered the fastest clippers on the route. He knew that Jardine had left for London and that Matheson remained in Macao, managing the firm’s affairs. He knew that the British merchants were pressing their government for action. What he did not know was the extent to which Jardine had already succeeded, the degree to which the British government had committed itself to a war of coercion, the scale of the force that was being assembled.

The defensive preparations continued through the spring and into the summer of 1840. Lin toured the fortifications personally, inspecting the gun positions, testing the chains that would block the river channels, reviewing the militia units that had been raised. He wrote to the emperor of his satisfaction with the progress, of the “firm determination” of the local population to resist the barbarians, of the “impregnable” defenses that would greet any hostile fleet. The memorials were not simply propaganda; Lin believed what he wrote. He had seen the British merchants surrender their opium, had watched the clippers scatter before his enforcement, had witnessed the collapse of the system that had defied imperial authority for decades. The pattern of the past shaped his expectations for the future.

But the system Lin had broken had been designed for smuggling, not for war. The opium clippers had succeeded because they were fast enough to avoid confrontation, because they could land their cargoes before the imperial authorities could respond, because the trade had corrupted the officials who should have stopped it. Lin’s suppression campaign had worked because he had bypassed those corrupt officials, because he had used his extraordinary powers to confront the merchants directly, because the British had not been prepared for a determined crackdown. None of these factors would apply in a war against a British expeditionary force. The clippers had fled because fighting was not their purpose; the fleet that was approaching had been sent specifically to fight.

The summer brought reports that the British force had begun to move. Ships were leaving Singapore, sailing north toward the Chinese coast. The expeditionary force that had been assembling for months was now on its way. Lin’s agents reported the sightings—a large number of vessels, including warships of a size not normally seen in these waters, transports carrying what appeared to be troops and equipment. The commissioner sent urgent memorials to the throne, warning that the barbarian fleet was approaching, that the moment of confrontation was near. He expressed confidence that the defenses would hold, that the British would be repulsed, that the empire would prevail.

But even as he wrote, Lin was beginning to receive reports that suggested the situation might be more complex than he had assumed. The British ships were not heading directly for Canton, for the Bogue forts where his defenses were strongest. They were moving up the coast, toward the Yangtze River delta, toward the cities of the central Chinese coast. The strategy that Palmerston had adopted, based in part on Jardine’s recommendations, was not to attack the strongest fortifications but to bypass them, to seize key positions along the coast, to threaten the Grand Canal and the grain supply that fed Beijing. The war would not be fought on Lin’s terms, in the waters he had prepared, but on terms chosen by the British, in places where the defenses were weak or nonexistent.

Lin had concentrated his forces at the Pearl River Delta because that was where the opium trade had been centered, where the foreign merchants had operated, where the clippers had delivered their cargoes. He had assumed that the British would have to take Canton to achieve their objectives, that they would attack where the trade had flourished and where he had broken it. But the British strategy was more sophisticated. They understood that the Qing dynasty’s vulnerability lay not in Canton but in the north, in the grain tribute that moved along the Grand Canal, in the economic lifelines that connected the wealthy south to the political center in Beijing. By threatening those lifelines, they could force the imperial court to negotiate without having to overcome Lin’s fortifications at all.

The commissioner’s memorials in the late summer of 1840 began to show signs of the strain he was under. He continued to express confidence in the defenses he had built, but he also asked for additional resources, for more troops, for funds to expand the fortifications beyond Canton. He reported on the British movements along the coast, on the cities that were now at risk, on the need for a coordinated response that would require coordination with provincial authorities beyond his jurisdiction. The extraordinary powers that had allowed him to suppress the opium trade in Canton did not extend to the entire Chinese coast. The bureaucratic system that he had bypassed in the south would now have to be mobilized in the north, and the officials in those provinces had not been prepared for war.

Lin wrote to Queen Victoria, or attempted to—a remarkable document that tried to appeal to the British monarch’s sense of justice and morality. He described the damage that opium had caused to the Chinese people, the “poison” that was destroying families and draining the empire’s silver. He asked how a civilized nation could permit such a trade, how Christians could justify profiting from the degradation of others. The letter was never delivered, or if it reached British hands, it was dismissed as the naive plea of an official who did not understand the forces he was confronting. But the document reveals Lin’s fundamental assumption: that the British were essentially a moral people who had been misled by bad actors, that if their government understood the true nature of the opium trade, it would rein in the merchants and restore proper relations.

The assumption was not entirely unreasonable. There was opposition to the opium trade in Britain, including from figures who would later play important roles in British politics. The Anti-Opium Society and its allies argued that the trade was immoral, that forcing China to accept a drug it had banned was a stain on British honor. But these voices were a minority, overwhelmed by the commercial interests that had mobilized for war. The claims that Jardine and Matheson had prepared, the arguments they had made to Palmerston, the narrative they had constructed of British merchants illegally detained and property illegally seized—these had carried the day. The war that was coming was not about opium, the British government insisted, but about justice, about the rights of British subjects, about the refusal of the Chinese to treat other nations as equals.

British newspapers and parliamentary proceedings remained beyond Lin’s reach. The East India Company’s court of directors, while officially neutral, had been lobbied extensively by the agency houses that had grown rich on the China trade. The claims being prepared would eventually include compensation for the opium that had been destroyed and for the costs of the war itself, and the indemnities being demanded would far exceed the value of the seized drug. His information came from the merchants and sailors he could observe, from the reports of his agents, from the documents that fell into his hands. And those sources, while they told him much about the trade he had suppressed, told him little about the war that was coming.

The irony of Lin’s position would become clear only in retrospect. He had been appointed to suppress the opium trade, and he had succeeded. He had broken the system that had moved Bengal opium from Calcutta to Canton in weeks, that had used fast ships and bribed officials and receiving hulks to evade imperial authority. The Lintin anchorage was empty. The clippers had fled. The merchants had surrendered their stocks. The trade that had seemed unstoppable, that had corrupted officials from Canton to Beijing, had been stopped by a single determined commissioner with extraordinary powers and the backing of an emperor who had finally decided that enough was enough.

But the success had created its own failure. The merchants whose trade Lin had destroyed had not simply accepted their losses. They had organized, lobbied, and persuaded their government to respond with force. The speed and elusiveness that had made the clippers effective smuggling vessels had also made them symbols of the British capacity to defy Chinese authority. The trade that Lin had dismantled had been replaced by a war that he could not win. The fortifications he had built, the militias he had raised, the memorials he had written—all were directed at a threat that had already been superseded. The British were not coming to restore the opium trade. They were coming to remake the relationship between their empire and the Qing dynasty, to force open ports and extract concessions, to establish a new order in which Chinese authority would be subordinated to British interests.

Lin’s preparations had been thorough, given the assumptions under which he was operating. He had fortified the river approaches, mobilized the coastal population, organized the defense of Canton. He had done everything that a competent official could be expected to do when facing a hostile fleet. But he had misjudged the scale of the threat, the determination of the British government, and the nature of the war that was being waged. The defensive preparations were complete, but they were misaligned with the threat. The British expeditionary force was approaching the Chinese coast, and Commissioner Lin Zexu would soon discover that the system he had broken had been replaced by something far more dangerous than the opium clippers he had driven from the Pearl River Delta.
