Chapter 2

The Scream Before the Shot

The scream came first. Mary Lincoln’s cry cut through the laughter of Our American Cousin like a blade through canvas, and for a moment the audience at Ford’s Theater did not understand what had broken their evening. The pistol shot had been muffled, confused with stage noise or a firecracker. But the scream carried meaning. Henry Clay Ford, managing the theater that night, was standing near the orchestra when he heard it. He looked up toward the presidential box and saw a man in dark clothing at the balustrade.

What happened in the next ninety seconds would be argued over for months, rehearsed in courtrooms, and written into contradictory affidavits. The War Department would collect these fragments, pressing witnesses for precision they did not possess, trying to reconstruct a sequence that no single observer had seen whole. The result is not a narrative but a palimpsest: layers of testimony, each honest, none complete.

John Wilkes Booth, an American stage actor who assassinated United States president Abraham Lincoln at Ford’s Theater on April 14, 1865, had prepared his exit as he prepared his entrance. The hole in the door to the presidential box—bored not by Booth but by Harry Clay Ford, the manager, to allow the guard to look in—had let him survey his target. Now Booth vaulted the balustrade. Major Henry Rathbone, the president’s guest, rose from his seat and moved toward the assassin. Booth turned. He held a dagger in his left hand. The blade caught Rathbone in the left arm, slicing from elbow to shoulder, and the major fell back against the railing, bleeding, stumbling, still not comprehending.

Booth jumped.

The drop was eleven feet six inches to the stage. He landed with his weight forward, and something in his left leg gave way—a bone snapping at the fibula, or perhaps the tibia fracturing under the strain. Later he would write that he had broken his leg. Later still, Dr. Samuel Mudd, a Maryland physician and tobacco farmer, would set it. But in this moment Booth did not fall. He caught himself, straightened, and stood facing the audience with his arms raised. The gesture was theatrical, unmistakable. He had played this theater before. He knew how to hold a crowd.

What he shouted remains disputed. Some witnesses heard Sic semper tyrannis—thus always to tyrants, the Virginia state motto. Others heard only indistinct words, or nothing. Booth himself would claim the Latin phrase. The audience, in any case, did not respond as he might have wished. There was no uprising, no recognition. Only confusion, and then the beginning of pursuit.

Booth ran stage left, toward the wings. A man who worked as musical director stood in his path. Booth struck him with the butt of his knife, knocking him aside, and continued toward the rear door. A boy who worked about the theater was standing near the back entrance when he saw Booth coming. The actor shoved past him and out into the alley.

Behind him, the presidential box had become a scene of its own. Rathbone, bleeding heavily from his arm, reached the door and found it barred from within. Booth had prepared this too, wedging a wooden music stand against the inner handle. It took precious seconds to clear. When Rathbone finally emerged into the corridor, he found confusion rather than direction. The president’s footman had been stationed outside the box. Where he was during the shooting, and why he did not intercept Booth, would become matters of accusation and defense at the conspiracy trial. The footman would testify that he had stepped away to watch the play. Others would suggest negligence, or worse.

In the theater itself, the audience had begun to move. Some rose from their seats and pressed toward the stage, trying to see what had happened. Others remained frozen, uncertain whether this was part of the performance. The actors on stage stood paralyzed, caught between their roles and the violence that had invaded their fiction. One of them, the leading lady, would later claim that she had gone to the presidential box to comfort Mary Lincoln, and that she held the dying president’s head in her lap. This became part of the theater’s lore, though other accounts place her elsewhere in those first minutes.

The critical vacuum was not in the theater alone. It lay in the structure of information itself. No telegraph wire ran from Ford’s box. No military protocol existed for an attack on the president during a public performance. Washington, that porous and chaotic stage, had no mechanism for translating a local explosion into general alarm. The military cordons Stanton had established to guard the capital, designed against Confederate raiders rather than a single man with a broken leg, remained unalerted.

Booth knew this. He had studied the city’s geography, its checkpoints, its rhythms of patrol. The Navy Yard Bridge, his immediate objective, lay southeast of the theater, across the Anacostia River. He had perhaps twenty minutes before the chaos inside Ford’s would crystallize into organized pursuit. He needed every one of them.

He emerged from the alley onto F Street. His horse was waiting where he had left it, held by the boy’s brother, or by a stable hand—the accounts diverge. Booth mounted with difficulty, his left leg refusing to bear weight properly. He turned the horse south toward Pennsylvania Avenue, then east toward the bridge. Behind him, the first shouts were beginning to carry into the street.

At the theater, the fragments of witness were already diverging. A businessman sitting in the dress circle would testify that he saw Booth’s face clearly and recognized him immediately. Others saw only a figure in dark clothing, or could not say whether the man had a beard or was clean-shaven. Some heard a pistol shot before the scream; others heard only the scream. The conspiracy trial would rehearse these contradictions without resolving them, because they were not resolvable. Perception in moments of violence is not recording. It is interpretation, pressed through the sieve of expectation and fear.

What is certain is the delay. Major Rathbone, emerging finally from the box, shouted for someone to stop the man, but he did not know where the man had gone. Henry Clay Ford ran to the front of the theater and then to the back, searching for his brother Harry, for any authority, for any plan. The audience, released from its paralysis, began to stream into the streets, carrying with them versions of the event that would multiply and distort with each retelling.

The military patrols learned of the shooting not through any alarm system but through rumor. A captain commanding a detachment near the Capitol heard the news from a civilian who had heard it from another civilian who had been at the theater. The time was approximately 10: 20 p. m., perhaps twenty minutes after the shot. The captain sent a messenger to headquarters, but the message traveled no faster than a man on horseback.

At the War Department, Edwin Stanton was working late, as was his habit. The first report reached him not through official channels but through his own household. His twelve-year-old son had been at Ford’s Theater that night and came home with the news. The Secretary of War did not immediately credit it. He sent to the theater for confirmation, then began to gather what forces he could. But the machinery of response—telegraphs, cavalry, roadblocks—required time to engage. Time that Booth was spending.

The actor had reached Pennsylvania Avenue. The street was still busy with Friday evening traffic, carriages and pedestrians moving between the Capitol and the White House. Booth did not ride fast; a galloping man attracts attention. He kept to a canter, his damaged leg braced against the horse’s flank, and watched for the first signs of pursuit. None came. The avenue’s gas lamps cast their familiar glow, the same glow that had illuminated the theater’s facade, and for these minutes the city maintained its ordinary appearance. A man on horseback might be going anywhere, coming from anywhere.

At the Seward residence, three-quarters of a mile west of Ford’s, a parallel violence had already occurred and already failed. Lewis Powell, the tall, powerful Confederate veteran whom Booth had recruited for this night’s work, had entered the Secretary of State’s house on the pretense of delivering medicine. He had attacked William Seward in his bed, slashing at his face and neck with a revolver butt and a knife. But Seward’s neck brace, fitted after a recent carriage accident, had deflected the worst blows. Powell had wounded five people—Seward, his son Frederick, his daughter Fanny, a male nurse, and a State Department messenger—yet killed none. Then, like Booth, he had run.

David Herold, the twenty-two-year-old pharmacist’s assistant who had guided Powell to the Seward house, had been waiting outside with their horses. When Powell emerged, bloodied and alone, Herold asked what had happened. Powell’s answer, if he gave one, did not satisfy. Herold, frightened by the commotion inside the house, the screams, the knowledge that something had gone wrong, made a decision that would shape his fate. He rode away, leaving Powell to escape alone.

Herold’s flight took him not toward the Navy Yard Bridge but into the city, wandering, uncertain of his destination. He had been assigned to guide Powell and then to meet Booth; with Powell’s mission failed and Booth’s whereabouts unknown, he had no clear purpose. Here was the first fracture in Booth’s design. The conspirator who should have been waiting at the bridge to join the escape was instead adrift in Washington, while Booth rode alone toward their rendezvous.

Powell, abandoned, made his own way out of the city. He would wander for three days before being captured at Mary Surratt’s boarding house, still carrying the weapons of his failed assassination. His capture, and the evidence found on him, would help seal Mary Surratt’s fate. But that lay in the future. On this night, he was simply another wounded man in a city that did not yet know it was under attack.

Booth reached the Navy Yard Bridge at approximately 10: 30 p. m., by the account of the sentry who guarded it. The bridge was a military checkpoint, the last controlled crossing of the Anacostia before the open country of Maryland. The sentry was stationed there to prevent the passage of unauthorized persons into or out of the capital after nightfall. He had specific orders: no one to cross without permit after 9: 00 p. m.

Booth gave his name. He offered no permit. He said he was going into Maryland on business and would return before morning. The sentry, according to his later testimony, recognized Booth as an actor he had seen perform. He let him pass. The sergeant would be arrested for this negligence, then released when no evidence connected him to the conspiracy. He had simply failed to enforce an order that seemed, in the moment, trivial. An actor on a horse, going to Maryland for the night—what threat could that represent?

This was the critical head start. Booth had crossed the last military checkpoint out of Washington before the alarm could reach it. The information that would have stopped him—description of the assassin, order to detain all travelers—was still forming at the War Department, still traveling through messengers and confused reports. The gap between event and response, between the scream in the theater and the command on the bridge, measured approximately twenty-five minutes. Booth had used them precisely.

Behind him, the city was beginning to understand what had happened. At Ford’s Theater, a young army surgeon who had been in the audience had reached the presidential box. He found Lincoln slumped in his rocking chair, his head forward, blood draining from a wound behind the left ear. The bullet had entered the skull and lodged near the right eye. The surgeon knew immediately that the wound was mortal. He worked to clear the blood, to restore breathing, while others in the box wept or prayed or stood helpless.

The decision to move the president was made quickly. The theater was no place for a dying man, and the White House was too far. They would carry him to the Petersen house across the street, a boarding house kept by a German tailor. Soldiers formed a corridor through the crowd. The bearers—the young surgeon, another doctor, several officers—carried Lincoln’s long frame horizontally, his head cradled, his legs trailing. They crossed the street in the lamplight, a procession of the stunned and the urgent, and entered the Petersen house through its front door.

The room they chose was narrow, at the rear of the first floor, rented by a government clerk who was displaced for the night. They laid Lincoln on a bed too short for him, diagonally, his head supported, his wound still bleeding. Mary Lincoln was brought in, then removed when her grief became too violent. The cabinet began to gather: Stanton from the War Department, Secretary of the Navy Gideon Welles from his home, others as they learned the news. The vigil began that would last through the night and end at 7: 22 the following morning.

But this was not yet known. At 10: 25 p. m., as Booth rode into Maryland and Lincoln lay dying in a stranger’s bed, the information that would organize the manhunt was still scattered across the city in fragments. Some knew the president had been shot. Some knew a man had escaped from Ford’s Theater. Almost no one knew both, or connected them. The telegraph wires that Stanton would soon use to coordinate pursuit were still silent, or carried only rumor.

The actor had made his exit. He had played his final performance at Ford’s Theater, and now he was riding into a different drama, one whose script was not yet written. His left leg pained him with each stride of the horse. He had twelve days remaining, though he did not know their number. The Confederate underground that he had counted on for aid would prove hesitant, transactional, emptied of the ideological conviction that had driven him to the presidential box. The federal machinery that he had outraced would recover, redirect, and pursue him with growing efficiency. But for these minutes, in the dark of Good Friday, he had succeeded in the only measure that mattered to him. He had struck the tyrant. He had escaped the theater. The city behind him, with all its oil lamps and its bunting and its unalerted defenses, receded into the night.

With the city’s defenses still unalerted, Booth had gained his critical head start. The bridge was behind him, the sentries passed, the Maryland roads opening before his horse’s hooves. What he would find in that darkness—supporters or solitude, aid or betrayal—remained unknown. But the first measure of his escape was complete. The vacuum of authoritative information that had swallowed the minutes after the shot had deposited him, alone and wounded, on the far side of the Anacostia River, with the whole apparatus of federal pursuit still struggling to find its starting point.
