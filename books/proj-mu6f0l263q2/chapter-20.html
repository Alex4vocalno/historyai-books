Chapter 20

The Telegraph Orders from Baker’s Office

Willie Jett sat in the public room of the Rappahannock Hotel at Port Royal, Virginia, on the afternoon of April 24, 1865, with the ease of a man who believed the war’s end had returned him to civilian obscurity. He was twenty-two years old, a private in Mosby’s command, and for ten days he had moved through the broken infrastructure of Confederate mail service with the confidence of one who knew every ford and farm gate between the Blue Ridge and the Chesapeake. The hotel was a two-story frame building near the steamboat landing. Jett had taken a room there because it was convenient, because he had business in the neighborhood, and because nothing in his experience suggested that his name had entered any federal ledger.

Three hundred miles north, in Washington, Detective Lafayette Baker received a dispatch that named Jett among a cluster of Confederate contacts in the Rappahannock region who might know the whereabouts of John Wilkes Booth. The message reached Baker’s office at 16th and I Streets, where he had established headquarters in the days since his recall from New York. Baker was forty-four years old, his career threaded through the irregular channels of wartime intelligence—provost marshal duties, secret service operations, the surveillance of disloyal civilians. He understood that the collapse of Confederate authority had opened new sources of information even as it complicated the verification of any single report. The dispatch he read did not name Booth directly. It named Jett, and it named a location: the Garrett farm, near Bowling Green, some twelve miles west of Port Royal.

The contrast between these two men—one relaxed in a hotel public room, the other examining a telegram in a government office—measured the distance between the fugitive’s assumptions and the pursuit’s evolving methods. Jett believed himself invisible because the Confederate underground had always made him so. Baker had learned that invisibility ended when defeated men began to talk.

Baker had been recalled to Washington after Lincoln’s assassination. Within two days of his arrival, his agents in Maryland had made four arrests and obtained the names of two more conspirators, including the actual presidential assassin John Wilkes Booth. This initial success, however, had not translated into a capture; the fugitives had slipped into Virginia, and the machinery Baker commanded—civilian detectives, military provost marshals, paid informants, telegraph operators who copied suspicious messages—was now straining under an excess of information. Hundreds of tips, sightings, rumors, and denials poured into the War Department each day, each requiring evaluation against the others, each delay carrying the cost of the fugitive’s continued movement. Stanton had authorized a reward of $100, 000 for Booth’s capture, a sum that guaranteed enthusiastic response from the public but did not discriminate between genuine intelligence and the inventions of opportunists.

The dispatch naming Jett arrived at a moment when the official search was drifting. Lieutenant Dana’s cavalry patrol had swept Charles County, Maryland, on April 21 and found nothing. Other detachments had searched the lower counties of Maryland without result. The assumption that Booth would remain in familiar territory—Southern Maryland, where he had planned his escape routes, where Confederate sympathies ran deep—had governed the deployment of forces, and it had failed. The intelligence now reaching Baker suggested a different trajectory: Booth had crossed the Potomac, had been seen in the company of Confederate couriers in Virginia, was moving not toward the Deep South but toward the fragmented remnants of Lee’s army, which had surrendered at Appomattox nine days before.

Baker took the dispatch to Stanton. The Secretary of War had established his headquarters in the War Department building, sleeping on a cot in his office, receiving reports at all hours. The two men had a difficult history. Baker had served under Stanton during the war, had been dismissed and recalled, had made enemies with his aggressive methods and his willingness to arrest prominent civilians. But in the crisis of the assassination, Stanton needed Baker’s network of informants and his willingness to operate outside regular military channels. The dispatch about Jett was not conclusive—it named a Confederate courier, not an assassin—but it offered coordinates. The Garrett farm. Bowling Green. A specific place where men could be sent.

The decision to act on this intelligence involved a calculation of risk. Stanton and Baker had to weigh the possibility that Jett was a false lead against the certainty that every day without decisive action allowed Booth to move deeper into obscurity. They had to consider, too, the political cost of another failed raid, another empty farm, another telegram reporting that the fugitive had escaped. The reward notices were already drawing criticism for their indiscriminate language, their promise of payment to any informant regardless of information quality. The War Department needed a success, and it needed it before public memory of the assassination fixed into permanent grievance.

Baker authorized the mission. The orders went to the 16th New York Cavalry, stationed at Washington since the surrender. Lieutenant Edward P. Doherty, a twenty-seven-year-old Canadian-born officer, received command of the detachment: twenty-five men, with provisions for extended operation. Attached to the unit were two civilian detectives, Everton Conger and Luther Baker, the latter a cousin of Lafayette Baker who had served in similar capacities throughout the war. Conger was an intelligence officer with experience in counter-espionage, a man who understood Confederate courier networks and could evaluate the information they provided. The three men—Doherty, Conger, Luther Baker—would travel together, the military officer nominally in command, the detectives exercising authority derived from their direct connection to the War Department.

The telegraph orders from Baker’s office specified the objective with unusual precision. The detachment was to proceed to Port Royal, Virginia, and there to locate one Willie Jett, described as a Confederate courier believed to have information regarding the whereabouts of John Wilkes Booth. Jett was to be apprehended and interrogated. If his information proved reliable, the detachment was authorized to proceed to the Garrett farm, near Bowling Green, and there to arrest Booth and any accomplices. The orders were terse, imperative, the language of military bureaucracy adapted to police work, directing every exertion to secure the persons of the murderers.

The detachment embarked on the steamer John S. Ide at the Washington Navy Yard on the evening of April 24. The vessel was a coal barge converted to troop transport, slow and uncomfortable, but it offered direct passage down the Potomac to the mouth of the Rappahannock. The seventy-mile journey took through the night, the men sleeping on deck or in the hold, the horses restless in their slings.

Conger used the time to review what was known of the Confederate network in the region: the names of couriers, the locations of safe houses, the patterns of movement that had sustained mail and intelligence operations even as the armies dissolved. He knew that Jett was not a senior figure in this network, not a planner or decision-maker, but a young man who carried messages because he was trusted and because he knew the country. Such men were often the first to cooperate when the cause they served collapsed. They had no investment in martyrdom, no ideological commitment that would survive the end of organized resistance.

The John S. Ide reached Belle Plain, the landing below Fredericksburg, at dawn on April 25. The detachment disembarked, mounted, and began the overland march to Port Royal, some twenty miles to the south. The country was familiar to Conger from earlier operations: rolling farmland, pine woods, the occasional plantation house with its outbuildings and slave quarters. The roads were poor, churned by spring rains, and the horses moved slowly. The men had been riding for two hours when they encountered their first informant: a black man working in a field near the road, who confirmed that strangers had been seen in the neighborhood, that a man with a broken leg had been carried across a ferry, that Confederate soldiers were still moving through the area despite the surrender.

This was the pattern that would repeat through the day: fragments of information, each partial, each requiring correlation with the others. The detachment reached Port Royal in the early afternoon and located the Rappahannock Hotel. Jett was not there. He had left that morning, the proprietor said, traveling with another Confederate soldier and a civilian. They had gone toward Bowling Green, riding horses borrowed from a local stable. The proprietor did not know their destination, but he mentioned that the Confederate soldier had spoken of visiting his father, a former officer who lived near a farm owned by the Garrett family.

Conger made the connection immediately. The Garrett farm was the location named in Baker’s dispatch, the place where Booth was believed to be hiding. The coincidence of the soldier’s destination and the intelligence about Booth’s location suggested that the three men were traveling together, and that their journey was connected to the fugitive. The detachment pressed on, following the road west from Port Royal, questioning farmers and ferrymen, piecing together the route that the three riders had taken.

They found Jett at the Bowling Green Hotel, a larger establishment than the Rappahannock, situated at the junction of the main roads. He was sitting in the public room when Conger and Baker entered, and he made no attempt to escape. The detectives separated him from his companions and questioned him privately. Jett’s initial statements were evasive: he admitted knowing Booth, admitted having seen him in the company of Confederate soldiers, but denied knowledge of his current whereabouts. Conger, who had interrogated many such men, recognized the pattern of partial confession—the desire to appear cooperative without implicating himself in serious crime.

The breakthrough came when Conger mentioned the Garrett farm specifically. Jett’s reaction, as later reported, was visible: a hesitation, a shift in posture, the recognition that his interrogators knew more than he had assumed.

He asked what would happen to him if he provided information. Conger, operating without formal authority to grant immunity, made promises that exceeded his instructions: protection, reward, the favorable consideration of the authorities. These were the standard inducements of wartime intelligence work, offered and sometimes honored, sometimes forgotten. Jett accepted them.

He confirmed that Booth was at the Garrett farm, that he had been there since April 24, that he was traveling with a young man named David Herold who had been with him since the assassination. He described Booth’s condition: the broken leg, the crutches, the fever that had developed during the days of hiding in the pine thicket. He described the Garrett family: Richard Garrett, the farmer, his wife, their sons, the younger son who had served in the Confederate army and who had vouched for Booth as a wounded veteran named “James W. Boyd.”

The information was specific enough to act upon. Conger and Baker returned to Doherty, who had established a temporary camp outside the town with the cavalry detachment. The lieutenant received the report and prepared his orders for the following morning: a night march to the Garrett farm, a dawn raid, the capture of the assassin. The men rested as they could, the horses fed and watered, the weapons checked. The intelligence chain that had begun with arrests at Port Conway, that had passed through Baker’s office in Washington and the telegraph wires to Belle Plain, that had been verified through Jett’s interrogation, was now complete. The cavalry had a destination.

In Washington, Stanton received confirmation that the detachment had located its target. The telegram from Baker’s office was brief, stating that Booth had been found and that the detachment was moving to arrest him. The Secretary of War had maintained his vigil at the War Department for twelve days, sleeping in his clothes, receiving reports of false leads and failed searches, the accumulated weight of administrative failure. The message did not guarantee success—Booth might escape again, might fight, might be killed before capture—but it represented the first credible intelligence that aligned with the capacity for decisive action. Stanton authorized the payment of reward money to the informants, contingent upon the result. He prepared the announcements that would follow successful arrest: the restoration of order, the demonstration that the federal government could reach its enemies even into the rural fastnesses of the defeated South.

The night of April 25–26 was clear and cold. The cavalry moved out from Bowling Green at eleven o’clock, following a guide who knew the back roads to the Garrett farm. The twelve miles took four hours, the horses picking their way through woods and fields, avoiding the main roads where sentries might be posted. Conger rode with the advance party, his mind working through the contingencies: Booth might be armed, might resist, might have arranged some warning system with the Garretts. The intelligence had located him, but it had not described his state of mind, his willingness to surrender, his physical capacity for flight. The broken leg was an advantage for the pursuers, but Booth had demonstrated resourcefulness throughout his escape, and Conger did not underestimate the desperation of a cornered man.

They reached the farm before dawn. The house was dark, the outbuildings silent. The cavalry surrounded the property, cutting off escape routes, establishing positions from which to observe any movement. Conger and Baker approached the house with a small party, waking Richard Garrett, demanding admission. The farmer was confused, frightened, initially denying knowledge of any strangers. The detectives pressed him: they knew Booth was there, they knew the false name he had used, they knew that his presence implicated the entire family in the crime of harboring an assassin. Garrett collapsed under this pressure, admitting that Booth had been there, that he had left that evening, that he might be in the tobacco barn with Herold.

The barn was a large structure, used for curing tobacco and storing farm equipment, with a single door and no windows. The cavalry formed a cordon around it, their carbines ready. Conger approached the door and called out, demanding surrender. The response from inside was silence, then a voice—Herold’s, high and frightened—asking for terms. Booth’s voice followed, deeper, refusing to surrender, declaring that he would fight. The standoff that followed, the negotiations, the eventual decision to burn the barn, would become the subject of conflicting accounts, disputed memories, the competing claims of men who had been present and who understood that the manner of Booth’s death would determine their rewards and reputations.

But the essential fact was established before any of this occurred. The telegraph orders from Baker’s office had set the cavalry in motion toward a known destination. The intelligence apparatus that Stanton had assembled, imperfect and improvisational, had finally produced coordinates that matched the fugitive’s actual location. The capture that followed—whether by fire or by bullet, whether alive or dead—was the consequence of this alignment. The manhunt had not ended through the systematic coverage of territory, the methodical search that military doctrine would prescribe, but through the exploitation of a collapsing Confederate network, the willingness of defeated men to trade information for protection, the bureaucratic capacity to convert rumor into actionable orders.

The cavalry formed their cordon in the darkness, carbines trained on the barn door, while inside the two fugitives made their final calculations. The telegraph orders had done their work: they had transformed a twelve-day fugitive into a fixed target, a set of coordinates where military force could be applied. The Garrett farm was no longer a name in a dispatch but a place surrounded by armed men, its tobacco barn filled with dry curing racks and desperate choices. The issued telegraph orders set the cavalry in motion toward a known destination, handing off the narrative to the imminent confrontation at that location.
