Chapter 27

The Coffin in the Arsenal Cellar

Seen from above, the Washington Arsenal in May 1865 was a sprawling military complex of wharves and magazines, a landscape of ordnance and organized violence concentrated to wage war. This vast storage ground of shot and shell now assumed a quieter function, becoming a repository for what the state wished to make disappear.

The descent from aerial view to concrete fact lands in a windowless storage room beneath one of the Arsenal’s stone buildings. On the night of April 27, 1865, a wooden crate arrived here, carried by soldiers who had been sworn to silence. Inside lay the body of John Wilkes Booth, shrouded in a blanket, the bullet wound in his neck still visible, the broken left leg splinted but unmistakable. The official identification aboard the ironclad USS Montauk had concluded that morning: ten witnesses, including Surgeon General Joseph Barnes, had examined the corpse, compared its dental work with the actor’s known records, noted the tattoo of his initials J.W.B. on his left hand and a distinct scar on the back of his neck. The government had its confirmation. What it lacked was a protocol for what came next.

The burial was unceremonious by design. The body was placed in a simple wooden coffin, assigned a numbered tag, and interred in a storage room at the Arsenal Penitentiary—the portion of the complex reserved for military prisoners and, now, for the remains of those executed for conspiracy against the national life. This was not the interment of a man but the storage of evidence. Edwin Stanton, Secretary of War, had determined that no grave should become a site of pilgrimage, no marker should invite memory. The assassin’s body would be held in bureaucratic custody until the state decided what memory it could risk permitting.

The logic was transparent. In the weeks after Lincoln’s death, the War Department had received reports of mourning displays for Booth in scattered Confederate communities, of toasts offered to his name, of songs composed to celebrate the act. The fear was that Booth’s grave would attract sympathizers and become a node around which alternative narratives of the war’s meaning might crystallize. The state that had mobilized three million men and spent billions of dollars to preserve the Union could not permit its chief enemy’s corpse to become a monument. Secrecy was the instrument of this prevention, and the Arsenal’s storage rooms offered the architecture of disappearance.

Yet secrecy generates its own demands. The body buried without rite became an object of persistent inquiry. The Booth family, learning through intermediaries that John’s remains were in federal custody, began a campaign of letter-writing and petition that would extend across four years. Edwin Booth, the actor’s older brother, occupied a difficult position: he had publicly renounced his brother’s act, had pledged loyalty to the Union, had continued his theatrical career in New York under the shadow of the family name. His requests for the body were framed in the language of private grief and familial duty, but they carried political weight. To deny them indefinitely risked appearing vindictive; to grant them seemed to concede that the assassin remained, in some dimension, a person entitled to ordinary rites.

The War Department’s response was delay. Through 1865 and 1866, as the military tribunal concluded its work and eight conspirators were executed or imprisoned, Booth’s body remained in its storage room, neither acknowledged nor released. The official silence was broken only by necessity. In 1867, the War Department decided to tear down the portion of the Arsenal Penitentiary where the executed conspirators lay buried. Mary Surratt, Lewis Powell, David Herold, and George Atzerodt had been hanged on July 7, 1865, and interred near Booth’s temporary grave. The demolition required disinterment.

On October 1, 1867, the coffins were exhumed and moved to Warehouse No. 1 at the Washington Arsenal. The operation was conducted under military supervision. Wooden markers identified each burial. For Booth, this meant a second burial in a second anonymous location, his remains transferred from storage room to warehouse, from one form of bureaucratic custody to another. The documentation of this move survives in War Department records. The date appears: October 1, 1867. The location: Warehouse No. 1. The participants: military personnel acting under orders. What the records do not disclose is whether anyone examined the body during transfer, whether the condition of the remains was noted, whether the numbered tag was verified against the coffin’s contents. The chain of custody was maintained. The chain of attention was thin.

The 1867 exhumation and re-interment would prove significant beyond its immediate purpose. In the years following the assassination, rumors had circulated that Booth had escaped, that the body identified aboard the Montauk was a substitute, that the assassin lived under an assumed name in the West or in Mexico or in Italy. These rumors were not merely products of Confederate nostalgia; they reflected a genuine uncertainty about what the government had actually done with the body it claimed to possess. The secret burial, intended to prevent martyrdom, had instead created conditions for persistent doubt.

The War Department’s response to this doubt was administrative. In 1869, as pressure for the body’s release mounted, officials determined to conduct a positive re-identification. The remains were exhumed from Warehouse No. 1 and examined by medical authorities. The same markers noted in 1865 were verified: the dental work, the tattoo, the fracture of the left leg, the gunshot wound. The body that had been buried in haste, moved in secrecy, stored in anonymity was now subjected to the same clinical scrutiny that had preceded its first interment. The government required certainty before it could risk release.

The identification appears to have satisfied the authorities. On February 18, 1869, the remains were released to the Booth family. The transfer was conducted without public ceremony. The body was taken to the Weaver funeral home in Baltimore, where it was prepared for burial at Green Mount Cemetery. The family had secured what they had sought: the right to private mourning, to a marked grave, to the ordinary rites denied the assassin by his act.

The arrival of the coffin in Baltimore was witnessed by William M. Pegram, then serving as mayor of the city. Pegram’s account, published by the Maryland Historical Society in 1913, described the condition of the remains and the circumstances of their reception. The mayor had viewed Booth’s body upon its arrival at the funeral home, confirming what the War Department’s examinations had asserted: that the body in the coffin was indeed that of John Wilkes Booth, that the four-year custody had preserved this fact against doubt, that the family’s claim was satisfied by the actual remains of their brother and son.

The Green Mount burial was private. A burial ceremony was conducted by Fleming James, minister of Christ Episcopal Church, in the presence of more than forty people. No monument marked the grave initially; the family sought obscurity as the government had sought erasure, though for different reasons. The assassin who had aspired to historical memory through spectacular violence was finally interred in the condition he had most feared: obscurity, his name attached to an act that had failed to alter the political order he opposed.

The trajectory from storage room to warehouse to family custody spans 1, 400 days. This figure is not arbitrary. It measures the interval between the state’s initial claim on the body and its final relinquishment, between April 14, 1865 and February 1869. During this period, the public trial of conspirators had concluded, executions had been carried out, military reconstruction of South had begun and stalled and begun again. The body in Arsenal cellar was a persistent administrative problem whose meaning shifted with each political season.

The management of Booth’s remains reveals the costs of the War Department’s approach to consequence. The decision to withhold the body from ordinary rites, made in the immediate shock of assassination, committed the government to a sustained exercise of secrecy that proved more demanding than any public trial. Each year of custody required continued security, continued silence, continued justification to a family that would not abandon its claim. The state that had acted with such speed in the manhunt found itself engaged in a longer, more draining contest with the obligations it had assumed.

The parallel with the public trial is instructive. The military tribunal that convened on May 9, 1865, operated under rules designed for expedition: no civilian jury, no appeal to ordinary courts, no delay for the gathering of evidence that might complicate the narrative of conspiracy. Eight defendants were tried together, their fates bound by the logic of association rather than by individual proof of specific acts. The trial concluded on June 30; four defendants were sentenced to death, four to imprisonment. The speed was deliberate, the message unmistakable: the state would not permit the assassination to become a protracted legal spectacle, a forum for debating the war’s causes or the limits of executive power.

Yet the trial’s swiftness generated its own prolongations. The execution of Mary Surratt, in particular, became a focus of controversy that outlived the immediate judgment. Her son John Surratt, whose association with Booth had been well documented, remained at large until 1867; his eventual capture and trial in civilian court—resulting in a hung jury and release—suggested that the military tribunal’s verdict had rested on evidence insufficient for ordinary legal standards. The government’s choice of venue, intended to secure conviction, thus exposed its judgments to retrospective doubt.

The secret burial operated similarly. The initial decision to withhold Booth’s body from his family, made in the name of preventing martyrdom, committed the government to a custody that became increasingly difficult to justify. Each year that passed without release strengthened the family’s moral claim; each rumor of escape weakened the government’s factual authority. The 1869 release, when it finally came, appeared less as generosity than as the exhaustion of a policy that had served its immediate purpose but could not be sustained indefinitely.

The condition of the remains at each stage of this history merits attention. The body that had been dragged from the Garrett barn, tied to a farm wagon, transported by steamer and ironclad, examined by surgeons and dentists, buried and exhumed and reburied and exhumed again, was by 1869 a diminished physical fact. The processes of death and decay, accelerated by summer heat and interrupted by storage in cool stone cellars, had altered what the witnesses of 1865 had seen. Yet the identifying marks persisted: the dental work that had been compared with Booth’s known records, the tattoo that had been noted in life, the fracture that had been set by Dr. Samuel Mudd. The body remained legible to examination, even as its significance became increasingly contested.

The administrative documentation of this custody survives in fragments. War Department records note the dates of transfer, the locations of burial, the participants in examination. They do not record the conversations in which decisions were made, the arguments for and against release, the calculations of political cost that shaped the timing of the 1869 decision. The historian must infer these from the pattern of action: the initial secrecy, the prolonged delay, the eventual release when the pressure of family petition and public rumor became unsustainable.

The Booth family’s experience of this custody was, by contrast, documented in their persistent correspondence. Edwin Booth’s letters to the War Department, preserved in family papers and cited in subsequent biographies, trace a trajectory from formal request to urgent petition to something approaching demand. The brother who had publicly disavowed the assassin’s act found himself obliged to claim the body as a matter of familial right, to assert a connection that his public statements had sought to minimize. The government’s delay placed him in an impossible position: to abandon the claim would appear to confirm his brother’s guilt as absolute and unredeemable; to press it was to risk association with the act he had condemned.

The release of 1869 resolved this tension without resolving its underlying conflict. The family secured the body; the government secured the documentation of its identification; the public secured the confirmation that Booth was indeed dead, that the rumors of escape were unfounded, that the assassination had concluded with the death of its perpetrator. Yet each of these securities rested on a foundation of secrecy that could not be fully disclosed. The 1867 and 1869 examinations had been conducted without public witness; the records of identification remained in War Department files; the actual condition of the remains at each stage was known only to those who had viewed them.

This epistemic structure—knowledge concentrated in administrative hands, distributed to the public only in authorized form—characterized the entire management of the assassination’s consequences. The trial had been conducted in a sealed room at the Old Capitol Prison, with reporters admitted only to transmit official summaries. The burial had been conducted in storage rooms and warehouses, with witnesses sworn to silence. The identification had been conducted by medical authorities, with their conclusions accepted on the basis of institutional authority rather than public observation. The state that had been attacked in the person of its chief executive responded by concentrating the management of consequence in executive hands, treating information itself as an instrument of security.

The cost of this concentration became apparent only in retrospect. The rumors of Booth’s survival, which persisted into the twentieth century and generated multiple claimants to his identity, drew their plausibility from the very secrecy that had been intended to prevent them. The government’s refusal to permit ordinary observation of the body, ordinary rites for the dead, ordinary verification of identity, created the epistemic gap that conspiracy theories would fill. The state that had acted to prevent martyrdom had instead created the conditions for persistent doubt.

The 1, 400 days of custody thus measure more than a bureaucratic interval. They measure duration of policy that sought to manage memory through control of material fact—treating assassin’s body as object requiring security rather than rite. Policy succeeded immediate aim: no grave became shrine; no pilgrimage organized; no public monument marked assassin’s death. But it failed larger purpose securing authoritative memory: hidden body became object persistent speculation.

The final burial at Green Mount Cemetery closed this chapter without resolving its tensions. The family secured what they had sought; the government relinquished what it had held; the body passed from administrative to private custody. Yet the conditions of this transfer—the secret examinations, the undocumented custody, the delayed release—left a residue of doubt that would outlive the participants. The historian who examines this record confronts a pattern of action that was coherent in its immediate logic but costly in its long-term consequences, that solved the problem of the moment by creating problems of memory that would persist for generations.

Booth’s body was in federal custody for 1, 400 days after his death, a concrete fact that prefigures the book’s final reckoning with historical judgment and legacy.
