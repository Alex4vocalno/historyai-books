Chapter 7

The Price on the President’s Head

The fugitives would spend another night in the thicket, perhaps two, while Jones worked the river. In the pine barrens of the Zekiah Swamp, Thomas Jones moved through darkness with provisions wrapped in oilcloth, navigating by memory through undergrowth that would tangle an unfamiliar man in minutes. He had smuggled mail across the Potomac for three years, hidden deserters, moved contraband past Union patrols. But he had never before concealed a man whose capture would transform an informant’s life overnight, a man now the subject of the largest manhunt in U.S. history, involving thousands of federal troops.

That transformation was being drafted in Washington while Jones walked his wooded paths. Before dawn on April 17, 1865, the Secretary of War would commit to paper a sum that would restructure every calculation in southern Maryland.

—-

Edwin Stanton stood at his desk on the second floor of the War Department, a brick building at Seventeenth and F Streets that served as the military nerve center of the United States. The room smelled of coal gas and wet wool. Outside, Washington lay under a light rain that had begun Friday evening and continued through Saturday, the sky offering its own mourning.

Stanton was fifty-one years old, a former Democrat who had become Lincoln’s most aggressive cabinet member, the man who had organized the Union war machine through four years of slaughter. He was short, heavy, with a dense beard and eyes that witnesses described as penetrating or cold, depending on their politics. Those eyes had watched Lincoln die at 7:22 on the morning of April 15, had seen the surgeon fold the president’s arms across his chest, had heard Mary Lincoln’s keening from the front parlor of the Petersen boarding house across from Ford’s Theater.

Now he faced a different crisis. The murder of the president was an assault on the state itself, committed in the capital city five days after Lee’s surrender. The war was supposed to be ending. Instead, Stanton confronted the possibility that the conflict would regenerate into guerrilla warfare, Confederate die-hards, a decapitated government vulnerable to renewed attack.

At his desk, he began to write. The first orders had gone out by telegraph before midnight on April 14, even as Lincoln still breathed. They were reactive and fragmentary: close the bridges, stop the trains, detain suspicious persons. The earliest surviving telegram, dispatched at 11:00 p.m. on April 14, ordered the Washington Navy Yard bridge closed to prevent escape. The message was handwritten, amended in Stanton’s rapid script, time-stamped by the telegraph clerk who received it for transmission. It named no suspect and described no crime—only the imperative of containment.

By Saturday morning, Stanton had established a pattern he would maintain for twelve days. He would treat the manhunt as a military operation, using the infrastructure he had built to wage war against the Confederacy. The telegraph lines that had carried orders to Grant and Sherman would now carry descriptions of Booth and his accomplices. The detective apparatus that had tracked Confederate agents would redirect toward domestic criminals. The same administrative mind that had coordinated armies of a million men would now focus on two fugitives in a Maryland swamp.

The physical evidence of this transformation accumulated on his desk. Telegrams arrived from military governors, from port commanders, from railroad superintendents. Each required a response. Stanton wrote in a rapid, legible hand, his pen scratching across War Department stationery, the paper thin and slightly gray from wartime shortages. He annotated, amended, forwarded. The clerks who carried these messages observed a man operating at the edge of exhaustion but without hesitation.

By Saturday afternoon, April 15, Stanton had begun to assemble the machinery of pursuit. He summoned Lafayette C. Baker to Washington.

Baker was forty-four, a former detective for the Union Army who had operated behind Confederate lines, arrested disloyal civilians, and built a reputation for ruthless efficiency. He was also controversial. Accusations of brutality, of fabricated evidence, of personal enrichment, had followed him through the war. Stanton had used him before and distrusted him personally. But Baker possessed something the Secretary of War needed: an existing network of agents, informants, and interrogators, already organized and ready to deploy.

Baker arrived in Washington on Saturday evening. Within forty-eight hours, his agents in Maryland had made four arrests and identified two additional conspirators by name. The speed of this operation revealed both Baker’s capabilities and the vulnerability of Booth’s network. The Confederate underground in southern Maryland, which had smuggled supplies and intelligence for four years, began to collapse under systematic pressure. Farmers who had accepted Confederate gold for silence now calculated the odds of discovery.

Stanton worked through Saturday night and into Sunday morning. The reward proclamation took shape in these hours, moving from draft to final form through multiple revisions. The sum was unprecedented: $100, 000 total, with $50, 000 reserved specifically for John Wilkes Booth. The other $50, 000 was divided between rewards for David Herold and John Surratt, each named as assassins in the proclamation’s text. A Union private earned thirteen dollars per month; a skilled craftsman might make two dollars per day. The reward for Booth alone exceeded the annual federal budget of several states.

The proclamation was dated April 20, 1865, though preparations began days earlier. Its language combined legal precision with theatrical urgency. It described the crime, named the suspects, and promised payment to any person who would deliver them to military authorities. The document was printed in multiple copies, distributed by telegraph to every military district, posted in public buildings, published in newspapers. It transformed the manhunt from an administrative operation into a national spectacle.

This transformation carried risks that Stanton recognized but accepted. A reward of such size would generate false information. Every ambitious liar, every mistaken witness, every Confederate agent seeking to divert pursuit would flood the system with reports. The War Department would spend precious hours investigating sightings that led nowhere, chasing shadows across multiple states. But the alternative—relying solely on military and police resources—seemed inadequate to the crime. Stanton chose noise over silence, gambling that volume would compensate for inaccuracy.

The proclamation also created a moral hazard it could not control. The $50, 000 reward for Booth established a market for betrayal. Anyone who knew the fugitive’s location, anyone who might shelter him, anyone who could manufacture information convincing enough to secure payment now had a powerful incentive to act. Thomas Jones, feeding Booth and Herold in the Zekiah Swamp, could become wealthy by walking to the nearest Union post. The farmers who had seen strangers on horseback, the ferrymen who had crossed the Potomac, the doctors who had treated a man with a broken leg—all were now potential informants, their silence purchasable at a price the government had named.

Stanton understood this. His calculation was that the reward would produce more genuine information than fabricated, that the pressure of universal suspicion would constrict Booth’s movements more than it would confuse his pursuers. He was wagering on human greed against human loyalty, and four years of war had taught him how that wager usually resolved.

—-

While Stanton built his machinery, the fugitives waited. In the pine thicket, Booth and Herold passed the days of April 16 and 17 in enforced idleness. Jones brought food, newspapers, occasional intelligence. The newspapers were particularly important to Booth, who had expected to be celebrated as a tyrannicide and found instead that he was being described as a common murderer. The April 15 editions carried conflicting reports: some claimed he had been captured, others that he had escaped to Canada or Europe. The certainty of his identity emerged only gradually, as witnesses from Ford’s Theater provided descriptions and detectives traced his movements backward from the theater to the rooming house on Tenth Street.

Booth read these accounts with growing frustration. His leg, broken in the jump from the presidential box, throbbed continuously. The splint applied by Dr. Samuel Mudd allowed him to ride but not to walk comfortably. Herold, younger and uninjured, could have fled alone but chose to remain, bound by loyalty or uncertainty or the simple paralysis that affects accomplices when the crime exceeds their expectations.

The thicket offered concealment but not comfort. The pine barrens of southern Maryland were dense with undergrowth, difficult to search, impossible to cultivate. Jones had selected his hiding places over years of smuggling operations. He knew which thickets could be approached without leaving visible trails, which farmers’ eyes could be trusted, which times of day allowed movement. But he also knew that this knowledge was perishable. Every person who saw him carrying food into the woods, every curious neighbor who noticed his unusual movements, represented a potential leak in a system that had never been tested against serious opposition.

The reward proclamation, when it reached southern Maryland, changed his calculations. Jones was a Confederate loyalist, committed to the cause if not to Booth personally. But $50, 000 was a transformative sum, capable of purchasing land, security, a new identity. The proclamation did not merely offer money. It announced that the full weight of the federal government would concentrate on this single objective, that no resource would be spared, that the search would continue until successful. Jones had to decide whether his loyalty to a defeated cause justified the risk of discovery, torture, imprisonment, death.

He decided to continue. The reasons for this decision can only be inferred: personal honor, political commitment, fear of the consequences if he abandoned Booth and was later discovered. Whatever his motives, Jones maintained his supply runs through April 18, 19, and 20, while the War Department’s machinery accelerated around him.

—-

The machinery itself represented something new in American governance: the application of wartime administrative methods to domestic criminal investigation. The telegraph network, built to coordinate military operations, now carried descriptions and orders at the speed of electricity. The National Detective Police, created to suppress disloyalty, redirected toward specific named targets. The military occupation of the southern states, already organized for control, adapted to pursuit.

Lafayette Baker embodied this adaptation. His methods combined systematic intelligence gathering with improvised brutality. Agents were dispatched to southern Maryland with instructions to arrest anyone connected to Booth, to interrogate prisoners vigorously, to follow any lead regardless of its apparent reliability. The four arrests made within two days of Baker’s arrival suggest that his network already possessed substantial information about Confederate operations in the region. The identification of John Surratt as a conspirator indicated that the investigation had penetrated Booth’s planning circle, possibly through captured documents or interrogated associates.

The interrogations themselves became a subject of later controversy. Baker’s agents were accused of using physical coercion, of extracting confessions that mixed truth with invention, of constructing cases that served political purposes beyond simple justice. These accusations would resurface at the conspiracy trial in May and June, when defense attorneys challenged the admissibility of statements obtained under duress. For the present, in the urgent days of mid-April, such methods seemed to Stanton justified by the magnitude of the crime and the continuing danger of Confederate resurgence.

The military dimensions of the pursuit were equally significant. Stanton ordered the closure of Washington’s ports, preventing maritime escape. He alerted commanders at Norfolk, at Fortress Monroe, at every point where Booth might seek a ship. The Navy Yard Bridge, which Sergeant Silas Cobb had allowed Booth to cross on the night of the assassination, was now heavily guarded. The Potomac River, which Booth had expected to cross easily into Virginia, became a patrolled barrier.

These measures created the conditions for the manhunt’s eventual success. They did not guarantee it. The geography of southern Maryland and northern Virginia favored fugitives: forests, swamps, inlets, islands, a population of uncertain loyalty. Booth possessed local knowledge, Confederate connections, and the advantage of initiative. He had chosen his route before the pursuit organized; his pursuers had to discover that route while operating from incomplete information.

But the structural pressure Stanton applied constrained Booth’s options progressively. Every hour of delay increased the number of searchers. Every mile of travel exposed the fugitives to new witnesses. Every person who saw them, even without recognizing them, added to the intelligence that Baker’s agents could assemble. The $100, 000 reward accelerated this process by commercializing information, transforming private observation into public currency.

—-

The proclamation’s specific language mattered. It named three men: John Wilkes Booth, David E. Herold, and John Surratt. The inclusion of Surratt was significant. He had not been present at Ford’s Theater; his connection to the conspiracy was organizational rather than immediate. Naming him indicated that investigators had already identified the Confederate courier network in Canada, had traced Booth’s communications backward through multiple intermediaries, understood the assassination as a planned operation rather than a spontaneous act.

This understanding shaped the pursuit’s intensity. Stanton and Baker were not hunting a lone assassin but dismantling a conspiracy. Every arrest produced new names, every interrogation suggested new connections. The network that had supported Booth’s flight—Dr. Mudd, who treated his leg; Thomas Jones, who supplied his hiding place; the various Confederate sympathizers who provided horses, food, information—became targets of systematic investigation. The reward proclamation offered protection to those who cooperated and destruction to those who concealed.

The economic logic was implacable. A farmer who sheltered Booth for reasons of Confederate loyalty might maintain that loyalty against moderate pressure. The same farmer, confronted with the possibility that his neighbor might claim $50, 000 by informing, faced a different calculation. Trust dissolved under monetary temptation. The conspiracy’s security, always fragile, became unsustainable as the reward’s existence propagated through southern Maryland’s small communities.

Jones understood this logic precisely. His continued support of Booth required operational security as much as personal commitment: moving only at night, varying his routes, avoiding any pattern that might attract attention. The newspapers he carried to the thicket reported the spreading search, the growing reward, the arrests of suspected accomplices. Booth read these reports and understood that his window for escape was narrowing.

—-

The War Department’s telegrams from this period, preserved in the National Archives, reveal the pursuit’s administrative texture. Stanton sent instructions to General Christopher Augur, commanding the Department of Washington, directing him to use every available exertion to secure the murderers. He ordered Major General John A. Dix in New York to detain all persons who might have been concerned in the murder. He directed naval commanders to search vessels, railroad superintendents to examine passengers, customs officers to watch the borders. The language was imperative, the scope comprehensive, the tone urgent without panic.

These orders traveled through a communications infrastructure that had no precedent in American history. The telegraph, developed commercially in the 1840s, had become by 1865 an essential tool of government. Stanton could send a message to Philadelphia in minutes, receive a response within the hour, coordinate simultaneous actions across multiple states. The speed of this communication compressed decision cycles, allowing rapid adaptation to new information. When a report arrived suggesting Booth had been seen in a particular location, Stanton could redirect searchers before the fugitive could move beyond that location’s radius.

The system’s limitations were equally significant. Telegraph messages were brief, subject to transmission errors, vulnerable to interception. The volume of information generated by a nationwide search exceeded the capacity of any single office to process. Stanton’s clerks struggled to maintain records, to verify reports, to distinguish genuine sightings from fraudulent claims. The reward proclamation amplified this problem by incentivizing fabrication. Every ambitious liar with access to a telegraph office could send a message describing Booth’s supposed location, hoping to be first with accurate information or simply to confuse the pursuit.

Baker’s role in managing this information flow was crucial. His agents served as filters, evaluating reports before they reached Stanton, investigating promising leads, discarding obvious fabrications. But this filtering introduced its own distortions. Agents might pursue theories that confirmed their preconceptions, ignore evidence that contradicted established assumptions, construct narratives that served their institutional interests. The manhunt’s eventual success would depend partly on Baker’s ability to overcome these tendencies, to maintain flexibility in the face of contradictory information.

—-

By April 17, the pursuit’s basic structure was established. The reward proclamation had been issued, though its full distribution would take several more days. Military and naval forces were alerted. Baker’s agents were operating in Maryland. The telegraph network carried orders and reports. Booth and Herold remained hidden in the Zekiah Swamp, dependent on Thomas Jones’s continued loyalty and on the difficulty of searching terrain that had been selected specifically for concealment.

The asymmetry between these positions was striking. Stanton commanded resources that dwarfed anything available to his targets: thousands of troops, a national communication network, unlimited funds, the legitimacy of established government. Yet he faced the classic problem of counterinsurgency: finding enemies who did not wish to be found, in territory they knew better than their pursuers, supported by a population of uncertain loyalty. The reward proclamation addressed this problem by transforming the population itself into an instrument of surveillance, replacing military search with economic incentive.

Whether this transformation would succeed remained uncertain. The twelve days of Booth’s flight would test both the fugitive’s resourcefulness and the pursuer’s organizational capacity. Each day of evasion demonstrated the limitations of Stanton’s machinery; each day of pursuit demonstrated its relentless expansion. The conflict between these dynamics would determine the outcome.

What was already clear by April 17 was that the nature of the chase had fundamentally changed. Booth had begun his flight expecting to be sheltered by Confederate sympathizers, celebrated as a hero of the defeated cause, assisted by an underground network that had operated successfully throughout the war. He found instead that the war’s end had dissolved the political basis for that network, that loyalty to the Confederacy no longer outweighed personal survival, that the federal government possessed both the will and the means to purchase information at prices no private conspiracy could match.

The $50, 000 reward for Booth, the $25, 000 each for Herold and Surratt, represented more than money. They announced that the assassination had transformed a political conflict into a test of institutional capacity. The Confederate underground, which had resisted Union power for four years through evasion and concealment, would now face that power concentrated on specific named individuals. The result was not predetermined. But the terms of contest had been established, and they favored the side that could sustain pressure longest.

In the pine thicket, Booth read the newspapers Jones brought and understood these terms. His broken leg, his dependence on others, his isolation from the Confederate command structure in Canada—all constrained his options. Herold, less committed, less imaginative, represented an additional vulnerability. The thicket that sheltered them also imprisoned them, its concealment purchased at the cost of immobility.

Thomas Jones, moving between the swamp and his contacts on the Potomac shore, observed the tightening net. The reward proclamation had reached southern Maryland; he had seen copies posted at crossroads, heard farmers discussing the sums involved, calculated his own exposure. His decision to continue assisting Booth was not a single choice but a daily recommitment, renewed each time he carried food into the thicket, each time he lied to a neighbor, each time he avoided a Union patrol.

This recommitment could not continue indefinitely. The pressure Stanton had generated would force movement, error, discovery. The question was not whether Booth would attempt escape, but when, and whether the attempt would come before the search converged on his hiding place. The machinery of pursuit, once set in motion, could not be recalled. The official reward notices and military alerts had transformed southern Maryland into a watched zone; this pressure now forced Booth and Herold’s desperate and risky attempt to cross the Potomac.
