Chapter 19

The Cavalry Patrol’s Lost Day

The telegram reached the cavalry camp at Bryantown, Maryland, at half past nine on the morning of Friday, April 21, 1865. It had traveled down the wire from Washington through the night, accumulating urgency with each relay. Lieutenant David Dana, commanding a detachment of the 13th New York Cavalry, read the order twice. The War Department wanted a sweep of Charles County—every road, every swamp crossing, every farmstead between the Potomac and the Patuxent. The descriptions were familiar by now: one man tall, with dark hair and a broken left leg; the other slight, fair, younger, answering to the name Herold or Harold. Dana had thirty mounted men, enough to cover ground if they moved fast and split into parties.

At that same hour, forty miles to the south, John Wilkes Booth and David Herold were already standing on Virginia soil. They had crossed the Potomac River in the dark hours before dawn, guided by Thomas Jones through a maze of tidal inlets to a landing near Machodoc Creek. Booth’s diary, recovered later from his pockets, recorded the crossing with the satisfaction of a man who had beaten a clock he could not see. After a most anxious night he crossed the river before sunrise, he wrote. The Maryland shore was behind him. The Confederate underground of the Northern Neck lay ahead, a network of safe houses and sympathizers that would carry him south toward Richmond, toward Mexico, toward any destination that was not a federal prison.

The two facts sat in perfect contradiction. The most intensive manhunt in the nation’s history, directed from the War Department by Secretary Edwin Stanton himself, was about to sweep a county its quarry had already abandoned. The lag was not hours but a full day—more, counting the time required for Booth and Herold to reach the river, secure a boat, and make the crossing. The intelligence that sent Dana’s patrol into motion had been accurate when gathered. It was obsolete when acted upon. This was the pattern that would define the middle phase of the pursuit: a chase that moved at the speed of rumor and telegraph against a fugitive who moved at the speed of darkness and local knowledge.

This was the pattern that would define the middle phase of the pursuit: a chase that moved at the speed of rumor and telegraph against a fugitive who moved at the speed of darkness and local knowledge. The federal apparatus had resources—troops, money, the institutional weight of a victorious government—but it lacked the one asset that mattered most in the tidewater country of Maryland and Virginia. It did not know the ground.

—-

Dana divided his command into three parties. One squad pushed southeast toward the mouth of the Wicomico River, where Booth might seek a boatman willing to run the blockade. Another swung west toward the village of Newport, checking the farmsteads that lined the road to the Potomac. Dana himself took the center column, following the main road through Allen’s Fresh toward the Surratt tavern at Surrattsville, where the first solid clues had emerged.

The tavern sat at a crossroads twelve miles south of Washington, a two-story frame building with a barroom on the ground floor and sleeping quarters above. It had been searched once already, on the night of April 17, when soldiers arrested Mary Surratt and several of her boarders. The search had yielded weapons, ammunition, and papers that confirmed the tavern’s role as a Confederate mail drop and meeting place. What it had not yielded was any indication of where Booth had gone after leaving Dr. Samuel Mudd’s house on the morning of April 15. After the shooting, Herold had accompanied Booth to the home of Samuel Mudd, who set Booth’s injured leg. The two men then continued their escape through Maryland.

Dana’s men reached Surrattsville by noon. The tavern was shuttered, its proprietor in federal custody, its inventory of liquor and provisions under seal. The lieutenant questioned the neighbors—the blacksmith at the forge, a woman washing clothes at the pump, a boy herding cattle toward fresh pasture. None had seen strangers matching the descriptions. The blacksmith remembered a one-armed man who had passed through on Wednesday, seeking work. The washerwoman had heard shots in the distance on Saturday night, but that was three days past and likely hunters after spring turkey.

The patrol pressed on. Dana’s orders specified the swamps and by-roads of Charles County, a terrain that resisted systematic search. Southern Maryland was a country of inlets and estuaries, of tobacco fields giving way abruptly to cypress swamp, of roads that dissolved into corduroy bridges or vanished entirely at the high tide. The population was thin, scattered, and—Dana had reason to suspect—hostile. This was the Maryland that had supplied recruits to the Confederate army, the Maryland of the underground railroad in reverse, where escaped prisoners of war found shelter and guides to carry them across the river to Richmond.

By evening on April 21, Dana’s center column had covered twenty miles of road and questioned perhaps fifty inhabitants. They had found no trace of the fugitives. The squad that had pushed toward the Wicomico returned with a similar report: no boats missing, no unusual traffic on the river, no strangers at the landings. The western detachment had done better, in a sense. They had arrested a man named William B. Merrick, a Confederate agent known to have assisted blockade runners, and had seized a quantity of contraband medical supplies. But Merrick had not seen Booth, had not heard of Booth, and seemed genuinely surprised to learn that the actor was believed to be in the neighborhood.

Dana made his camp that night at Allen’s Fresh, bivouacking his men in an abandoned barn. He wrote a report for his superiors, summarizing the day’s operations and requesting instructions. The lieutenant was a professional soldier, West Point class of 1861, and he understood the difficulty of his position. He had been sent to find a needle in a haystack, with no certainty that the needle had ever been in this particular stack, or that it had not already been carried elsewhere by some means his search could not detect.

What Dana did not know—what no one in Washington knew—was that the intelligence directing his movements was already two days stale. Booth and Herold had left Dr. Mudd’s house on the morning of April 15, not April 17 or 18 as the investigators initially assumed.

They had spent two full days hidden in the pine thicket near the Zekiah Swamp, attended by Thomas Jones, a Confederate agent who knew the country intimately. Jones had fed them, treated Booth’s splinted leg, and—most critically—arranged their passage across the Potomac. The crossing had been attempted on the night of April 20 and failed, forcing them to hide another day on the Maryland shore. The successful crossing came on the night of April 20–21, hours before Dana’s telegram arrived at Bryantown. Booth’s diary, recovered later from his pockets, recorded the crossing with the satisfaction of a man who had beaten a clock he could not see.

The lag was structural, not merely accidental. The federal investigation had begun with the assumption that Booth would run north, toward the sympathetic territory of Maryland and Pennsylvania, or east toward the Atlantic ports. The detectives who first reached Mudd’s house had accepted the doctor’s story that two strangers had stopped briefly for medical attention and continued on their way. It took the arrest of Lewis Powell and the interrogation of other conspirators to establish that Booth had remained in the area, hidden by Confederate agents. By the time that intelligence reached Stanton’s desk and was translated into orders for Dana’s patrol, the fugitives had already exploited their head start to reach Virginia.

—-

The contrast between the two movements—Booth’s and the manhunt’s—grew sharper with each hour. On the morning of April 22, while Dana’s cavalry resumed their sweep of Charles County, Booth and Herold were moving slowly through the Virginia countryside, guided by a succession of Confederate sympathizers. They stopped at the farm of Richard Stuart, a wealthy planter and former Confederate agent, who fed them but refused to shelter them overnight. They pressed on to the home of William Lucas, a free black farmer, where they obtained a meal and directions. Booth’s leg pained him constantly; he rode when he could, walked when he must, and depended on Herold to manage the horses and negotiate with strangers.

The Virginia shore was not safe territory, exactly. Union gunboats patrolled the Potomac, and federal troops occupied the major towns. But the network of Confederate agents and sympathizers was still functional, still capable of moving men and information across a landscape that federal forces could not fully control. Booth had planned for this. His original scheme, developed in the months before the assassination, had included escape routes through Maryland and into Virginia, contacts who would guide him to Richmond or to a port where he could take ship for Cuba or Mexico. The assassination had come too soon—the war had ended while he was still preparing his flight—but enough of the infrastructure remained to carry him past the immediate danger.

In Washington, Stanton was receiving reports from multiple sources, each contradicting or complicating the others. Lafayette Baker, the chief of the War Department’s detective force, had agents in Baltimore, Philadelphia, and New York, tracking rumors that Booth had been seen in each city. The New York Herald published a dispatch claiming Booth had crossed into Canada. The Philadelphia Inquirer reported his capture near Port Tobacco, Maryland. The actual capture, when it came twelve days later, would surprise everyone who had been following these reports.

The War Department’s response to this confusion was to multiply the search. More cavalry detachments were ordered into Southern Maryland. Detectives were sent to question everyone who had been arrested in connection with the conspiracy, squeezing for names and locations.

Reward notices were posted in every town and landing along the Atlantic coast, offering $50, 000 for Booth’s capture and lesser amounts for his accomplices. The notices described Booth as five feet seven or eight inches high, slender build, high forehead, black hair, black eyes. They noted that he walked with a firm step and had the appearance of a man of determination. They did not note that he was already in Virginia, walking with a limp, dependent on crutches, and moving through a landscape where the notices would never be posted.

Lafayette Baker, recalled to Washington after the assassination, had within two days made four arrests and obtained the names of two more conspirators, including Booth, but the fugitives’ trail had already grown cold in Maryland.

—-

Dana’s patrol continued its work through April 22, extending the search to the eastern shore of the Wicomico and the lower reaches of the Patuxent. The method was thorough and futile. The cavalrymen stopped at every farmhouse, examined every outbuilding, questioned every inhabitant who would speak to them. They arrested several men on suspicion of disloyalty—one for possessing Confederate currency, another for making treasonable utterances about the late president. None had information about Booth.

The frustration was palpable in Dana’s reports, which grew shorter and more pointed as the day wore on. No trace of fugitives, he telegraphed from a relay station near Benedict. The country was hostile. The population refused information. The hostility was real enough. Southern Maryland had been occupied territory for four years, its economy wrecked by the blockade and the emancipation of its enslaved labor force. The assassination of Lincoln was not universally mourned in this country, and the presence of federal troops searching for the assassin was received as further humiliation.

But the hostility was not the main reason for Dana’s failure. The main reason was that he was searching in the wrong place. Booth and Herold had crossed the river. They were already moving through King George County, Virginia, toward the Rappahannock River and the deeper South. The intelligence that would redirect the manhunt toward Virginia—specifically, the testimony of William Rollins, a ferryman who had seen two men matching the descriptions cross the Potomac on April 22—would not reach Washington for several days. Until then, the search would continue to concentrate on Maryland, where the fugitives had never been and where they would never return. After a 12-day search, on April 26, Booth and Herold would be located at a tobacco farm in King George County, Virginia.

—-

The gap between perception and reality had consequences beyond the immediate failure to capture Booth. It shaped the federal response to the conspiracy, directing suspicion toward individuals and networks that had not in fact assisted the assassin’s escape, while allowing those who had actually helped him to escape notice. Dr. Samuel Mudd, whose treatment of Booth’s leg was established fact, would be arrested and charged with conspiracy. Thomas Jones, who had hidden Booth for two days and arranged his crossing, would not be identified as a conspirator for months, and would never be brought to trial. The Surratt tavern, a minor waystation in the Confederate mail system, would become a focus of prosecutorial attention, while the more substantial Confederate intelligence networks in Virginia would escape systematic investigation.

This was the cost of fighting an information war with the tools of conventional military pursuit. The federal government had the power to arrest, to interrogate, to offer rewards that dwarfed any ordinary fortune. It did not have the power to see through the opaque surface of a divided society, to distinguish accurate report from deliberate misdirection, to move faster than the rumors that multiplied with each telegraph transmission. The manhunt for Booth would eventually succeed—would locate him at the Garrett farm and run him to ground in a burning tobacco barn—but its success would come from a breakthrough in intelligence, not from the methodical sweep that Dana’s patrol exemplified. That breakthrough, involving the interrogation of the former Confederate private Willie Jett, was still several days away.

That breakthrough was still several days away on the afternoon of April 22, 1865, when Dana gathered his men for the return march to Bryantown. The patrol had covered nearly sixty miles of road and swamp, had questioned hundreds of inhabitants, had arrested a dozen men on various charges of disloyalty. They had found no trace of John Wilkes Booth or David Herold. They had not found them because the men they sought were already in another state, moving through a different network of safe houses, carrying with them the knowledge that they had beaten the first, most intensive phase of the pursuit.

Dana filed his final report that evening. It was a document of professional disappointment, carefully worded to avoid the admission of failure. The area had been searched thoroughly. The fugitives were not located. He requested further instructions. The instructions, when they came, would send other patrols over the same ground, repeating the same search with the same results, until the intelligence finally caught up with the reality and the manhunt was redirected toward Virginia. The lost day—April 21, the day when Booth crossed the river while Dana received his orders—would not be recovered. It would become part of the pattern of delay and misdirection that allowed the assassin twelve days of freedom, twelve days of movement through a countryside that remained, despite all the resources of the federal government, essentially opaque to those who sought him.

The patrol rode out at dawn on April 23, leaving Charles County to its farmers and its secrets. Behind them, the swamps closed over their passage. Ahead of them, in Washington, the telegraph wires were already carrying new orders, new rumors, new certainties that would prove as fragile as the old. The manhunt continued. The gap between what was known and what was true remained, and the pressure for a new, accurate intelligence breakthrough was building with each failed sweep. Willie Jett sat in the public room of the Rappahannock Hotel at Port Royal, Virginia, on the afternoon of April 24, 1865, with the ease of a man who believed the war’s end had returned him to civilian life, unaware that his name had entered a federal ledger.
