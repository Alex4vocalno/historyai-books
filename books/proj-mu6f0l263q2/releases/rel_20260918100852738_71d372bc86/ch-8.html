Chapter 8

After Days of Hiding

The pine thicket had become a cage built of Thomas Jones’s patience and the tightening radius of federal search parties. By the evening of April 21, 1865, Jones understood that his provision of food and intelligence could not continue indefinitely. The reward notices had multiplied across southern Maryland. The War Department’s telegraph lines hummed with coordinates. Every creek mouth and landing on the Potomac between Point Lookout and Alexandria now carried the risk of encounter.

At Dent’s Meadow, on the shoreline near Jones’s fish farm, the Confederate agent handed over a small wooden boat. The vessel was perhaps twelve feet long, narrow, built for one man to tend oyster beds or run a trotline. Its oars were short, worn smooth by years of handling. Jones gave what instructions he could: cross to the Virginia shore, make for the mouth of Machodoc Creek, seek out men who might still remember Confederate loyalties. The current would carry them southward. They must row against it to hold their bearing.

The darkness was complete. The river at this hour was a black surface without margin. The Maryland shore dissolved ten feet from the bank. Somewhere across a mile of moving water, the Virginia shore existed as theory rather than sight.

Booth’s broken left leg, splinted crudely after Mudd’s ministrations, made the boat an instrument of fresh pain. He settled into the stern, his leg extended, while Herold took the oars. The younger man had never navigated the Potomac at night. His experience was of the small streams and inlets of southern Maryland, where landmarks held still and the tide ran predictable. Here the river’s breadth swallowed sound. The splash of the oars returned no echo. The men could measure progress only by the strain in Herold’s shoulders and the cold accumulation of minutes.

They had aimed for Virginia. The crossing was meant to carry Booth beyond the immediate jurisdiction of Stanton’s manhunt, into the collapsed Confederacy where a famous name might still open doors, where the assassination could be reframed as a military act rather than a murder. Booth believed the American Civil War remained unresolved despite Lee’s surrender four days earlier, because the Army of Tennessee under General Joseph E. Johnston continued fighting. The plan assumed that the river was a boundary, that crossing it would transform their condition. That assumption would cost them twelve hours and deliver them back to the shore they had left.

The current was stronger than Jones had warned, or stronger than Herold could calculate. The boat drifted southward as it crossed, the oars insufficient to hold a true bearing. In darkness, without visible landmarks, the men could not correct. They rowed toward what they believed was the Virginia shore and found themselves instead at a bend in the broad Potomac, the river’s own geography defeating their intent. Exhaustion compounded error. Herold was twenty-two years old, slight, without the stamina for prolonged exertion. Booth could not assist. The boat moved through the night as a vessel without destination, the oars dipping and rising in a rhythm that carried them nowhere certain.

Dawn came with the particular cruelty of mid-April, the light gathering slowly through mist that lay on the water like a second surface. The men saw the shore they had approached, and they saw that it was Maryland. The coastline was familiar: the configuration of Nanjemoy Creek, the inlet where Jones had launched them hours before. They had described a curve through the darkness and returned to the point of departure. The Virginia shore remained visible across the water, approximately where it had been, unattained.

The psychological cost of this failure is documented in Booth’s own hand. His diary, kept intermittently through the flight, records the crossing attempt without self-pity but with a precision that reveals its weight: the night on the river, the return to Maryland soil, the recognition that their position had worsened without advancing. The entries are brief, factual, but they accumulate toward a portrait of diminishing options. Each documented action—each movement, each contact, each hour of exposure—narrows the field of possibility.

Herold’s later testimony, given under very different circumstances, confirms the physical circumstances: the cold, the confusion of currents, the failure of navigation. The two men had spent their strength and their night of opportunity to achieve nothing. They were now stranded not merely in Maryland but in a specific condition of exposure. The boat could not be used again in daylight; the shoreline near Nanjemoy Creek was inhabited, farmed, subject to the same federal patrols that had made the pine thicket untenable. Their presence was unexplainable. Two men in a small boat, one clearly injured, emerging from the river at dawn, carried the marks of flight without the cover of legitimate purpose.

They concealed the boat and sought shelter, improvising where plan had failed. The Confederate network that had sustained them—Jones, Mudd, the Surratt tavern—was behind them, its reach exhausted. Ahead lay only the uncertainty of whether any equivalent structure existed in Virginia, whether the assassination had destroyed the possibility of Confederate sanctuary or created it. Booth believed, or needed to believe, that his act would be understood in the South as the necessary completion of a war that Lee’s surrender had left unfinished. The failed crossing forced a confrontation with the possibility that this understanding would not be forthcoming.

The day of April 22 passed in hiding. The men found what cover they could in the marshy ground near Nanjemoy Creek, waiting for darkness and a second attempt. This interval was not rest but suspended anxiety. The failed crossing had demonstrated the difficulty of the river; a second attempt would face the same current, the same darkness, the same navigational challenge, now compounded by exhaustion and the knowledge of failure. Herold’s confidence, never robust, eroded further. Booth’s diary entries from this period show him constructing a narrative of justification, addressing himself to an imagined posterity that would recognize the grandeur of his motive. The prose is theatrical, self-dramatizing, the voice of a man who had always performed and who now found himself in a role without audience.

The second attempt, made on the night of April 22–23, succeeded in reaching Virginia. The difference between the two crossings is instructive: not a change in capability but in conditions. The men had rested, however poorly. They had studied the river by daylight, marking landmarks, calculating the angle of approach. Jones, who had remained in contact, may have provided additional guidance. Or simply the repetition of effort, the refusal to accept the first failure as final, carried them across. They landed at the mouth of Machodoc Creek on the Virginia shore, approximately where Jones had directed them, and made contact with the sons of a local farmer whose name had been supplied as a Confederate sympathizer.

This arrival was not the transformation Booth had imagined. Virginia in late April 1865 was not a sanctuary but a landscape of defeat. The Army of Northern Virginia had surrendered at Appomattox nine days before. The civil authority that might have protected a Confederate agent no longer existed. The local contacts, when found, were cautious, noncommittal, aware that the war’s end had changed all calculations of risk and reward. They provided food, information, the use of a wagon. They did not offer sustained concealment or passage farther south. The network of Confederate loyalty, which Booth had assumed would function automatically, proved to be conditional, situational, dissolving under the pressure of federal victory.

The fugitives moved inland, seeking the Rappahannock River crossing that would carry them deeper into Virginia, toward the imagined safety of more remote counties. Their progress was slow, marked by hesitation and misdirection. Booth’s leg prevented walking; he traveled by wagon, a visible invalid in a region where strangers attracted attention. Herold, who might have moved faster alone, remained with him, a loyalty that compounded their joint exposure. The assassination had bound them together more tightly than any prior conspiracy; Herold’s knowledge of the crime made his separation from Booth unthinkable, his continued presence increasingly desperate.

The federal pursuit, meanwhile, had not remained static. Stanton’s War Department had processed the intelligence from Maryland, the reports of Jones’s activities, the pattern of Confederate contacts. Lafayette Baker was recalled to Washington after the assassination, and within two days of his arrival, his agents in Maryland had made four arrests and had the names of two more conspirators. The manhunt was becoming systematic, a bureaucratic operation applied to a physical search. The telegraph carried descriptions, rewards, instructions to military commanders and civilian authorities. The twelve-day window that Booth had imagined as sufficient for disappearance was closing under the pressure of institutional persistence.

The contrast between Booth’s self-conception and his actual circumstances sharpened through these days. He had understood himself as an instrument of historical necessity, the man who would rekindle Confederate resistance by a single decisive act. The diary entries maintain this construction, addressing “the country” and “posterity” with the assumption of future vindication. But the practical record—the failed crossing, the cautious reception in Virginia, the dependence on marginal figures who offered only provisional assistance—suggests a different interpretation. Booth was not directing events but responding to them, his choices increasingly constrained by the machinery of pursuit that Stanton had assembled.

The geography of his flight traces this constraint. From the Potomac crossing, the fugitives moved toward Port Royal, then inland toward Bowling Green, then toward the farm of Richard Garrett, a location selected for its isolation and the possibility of concealment. Each movement was shorter, more tentative, more obviously driven by immediate necessity rather than strategic purpose. The Garrett farm, when they reached it on April 24, represented not a destination but a terminus, the last point before capture or death.

The chapter of the failed crossing thus stands as a pivot in the narrative of pursuit and flight. Before April 21, Booth retained the possibility of successful escape: the Confederate network functioned, the river was a passable boundary, the future held multiple paths. After April 23, when he finally reached Virginia, the possibilities had narrowed to survival and concealment. The two days between—the night on the river, the return to Maryland, the second crossing, the cautious progress through defeated territory—consumed the margin of error that might have carried him to safety.

The sources for this reconstruction are partial and sometimes contradictory. Booth’s diary provides the fugitive’s own accounting, shaped by his need for self-justification. Herold’s testimony, given after capture and under the pressure of prosecution, confirms certain details while possibly distorting others. The statements of those who assisted them, the observations of Union search parties, the physical evidence of the boat and the landing sites—these pieces must be assembled without the certainty of complete knowledge. What emerges is not a seamless narrative but a probable one, the record of two men struggling against darkness, current, and their own miscalculation to achieve a crossing that would not, in the end, deliver what they sought.

The boat itself became a physical record of their miscalculation. Its hull, scraped by the submerged snags of Nanjemoy Creek, carried the marks of a journey that had gone nowhere. Herold had to drag it into the marsh grass, his hands numb from the cold water, while Booth watched from the bank unable to assist. The vessel that was meant to carry them to freedom now required concealment. They overturned it among the reeds and weighted it with stones and driftwood, a burial of evidence that took precious minutes of gray morning light. Hiding the boat was itself a confession: no legitimate traveler would need to erase his passage so thoroughly.

The immediate landscape offered little refuge. Nanjemoy Creek drained broad agricultural flats where tenant farmers worked fields visible for half a mile in any direction. The creek’s marshy margins, thick with phragmites and cattail, provided cover of a kind, but cover that would not survive systematic search. Booth and Herold moved inland from the landing, keeping to the lowest ground, their progress slowed by Booth’s dependence on crutch and Herold’s supporting shoulder. The broken leg, never properly set, had begun to show signs of complication: swelling, intermittent fever, an odor from the wound that Booth’s diary would later record without comment. These were not conditions for sustained flight; sustained flight was their only option.

The day of April 22 imposed a particular discipline of invisibility. They could not risk fire for warmth, could not move in daylight, could not speak above a whisper. The sounds of ordinary life—ax blows from a woodlot, a dog’s bark, the distant passage of a wagon—measured their isolation and their proximity to danger. Herold, the more practical of the two, foraged what he could: spring watercress from the creek margins, the remains of Jones’s provisions. Booth occupied himself with the diary, writing by insufficient light, constructing the narrative that would survive him. The entries from this period show an increasing density of self-reference, as if the act of recording had become the only action available to him, the only performance he could still command.

The Confederate network that had sustained them to this point had been designed for different circumstances—wartime, when risk carried reciprocal obligation, when the cause created bonds of mutual protection. That war was now over. The men who might have assisted Booth and Herold made their calculations differently. Assistance to a fugitive assassin was not the same as assistance to a Confederate agent; the former carried no promise of future reciprocity, only the certainty of federal punishment. The network, never as robust as Booth had assumed, was dissolving in real time, its nodes going silent or denying connection.

Thomas Jones, who had delivered them to Dent’s Meadow, made his own calculations. He would be questioned, searched, possibly arrested. His knowledge of the fugitives’ movements was a liability that required management. The pattern of his subsequent testimony—what he revealed, what he withheld, when he chose to speak—suggests a man reconstructing his own narrative of involvement, minimizing his role while preserving enough credibility to satisfy investigators. He had done what he promised: delivered the boat, provided the means of crossing. That the crossing had failed was not, strictly, his responsibility. Yet the failure bound him to the fugitives in new ways, implicated him in the hours of exposure that followed.

The second crossing, made on the night of April 22–23, benefited from specific preparation that the first had lacked. Herold had spent the daylight hours studying the river’s surface, tracking the movement of debris, noting how the current set at different points along the shore. He had identified a bearing that would compensate for drift, a point of aim far upstream of their actual destination. The method was sound; what had been missing the first night was the opportunity to apply it. Darkness again concealed their departure, but darkness now informed by observation, by the memory of failure.

The physical conditions of the second crossing were, if anything, more demanding. Booth’s fever had risen through the day of hiding; he was semi-delirious by nightfall, capable of sitting in the boat but not of useful counsel. Herold rowed alone, as he had before, but now with the burden of confirmed knowledge. The river had defeated him once. Its currents and its darkness were not abstract threats but experienced facts. The psychological weight of this—rowing into the same blackness that had returned him to shore—must have been considerable. Yet the record shows no hesitation. The boat pushed off from a different point, on a different bearing, toward a destination that Herold had fixed in his mind through hours of daylight study.

They reached Virginia near dawn, not at the precise point Jones had indicated but close enough to recognize the changed topography: the different configuration of the shoreline, the absence of familiar landmarks. The confirmation of success came slowly, through the accumulation of small differences—the quality of the light on the water, the sound of the waves against a different shore. Booth, rousing from his fevered half-sleep, would have understood before Herold spoke. The diary records the crossing with characteristic restraint: “Crossed to Va. shore.” The brevity itself suggests exhaustion, the collapse of dramatic possibility into mere fact.

The Virginia they encountered was not the sanctuary of Booth’s imagining. The machinery of federal victory was already in motion across the state. Military governors had been appointed. Loyalty oaths were being administered. The Confederate currency that might have purchased assistance was worthless; the Confederate identity that might have commanded respect was now a liability. The local contacts Jones had named—men who had maintained Confederate sympathies through four years of war—faced new calculations. The war was lost. The cause was defeated. Assistance to Booth offered no future advantage, only present danger.

The sons of the farmer at Machodoc Creek who received them exemplified this transformed condition. They provided what was asked—food, information, the use of a wagon—without enthusiasm or commitment. Their assistance was transactional, minimal, designed to move the fugitives through their territory rather than to sustain them within it. The farmer led them to his son-in-law, Col. John J. Hughes, who provided the fugitives with food and a hideout until nightfall, for a second attempt to row across the river to Virginia. The network of Confederate loyalty, which Booth had expected to function automatically, proved to require conditions that no longer obtained. The assassination had not rekindled the cause; it had accelerated its dissolution, made every former Confederate face the question of where his interest now lay.

The wagon ride inland, with Booth stretched among sacks of grain or farm tools, marked a further reduction in his condition.

The machinery of pursuit had forced movement. Movement had produced error. Error had consumed time and confidence and the last reserves of Confederate assistance. By the morning of April 23, when Booth and Herold finally stood on Virginia soil, they were not escaped but displaced, carried by exhaustion and improvisation toward a confrontation that neither could any longer prevent. The river had been crossed. The safety it promised had dissolved with the Confederate cause itself. What remained was the bare fact of survival, measured in hours, dependent on the forbearance of strangers and the continuing blindness of the search.

Stranded, exhausted, and exposed on the Maryland shore after their failed crossing, Booth and Herold were now desperate for immediate shelter and must soon make a final, urgent push for Virginia. The machinery of pursuit, once set in motion, could not be recalled. The official reward notices and military alerts had transformed southern Maryland into a watched zone; this pressure now forced Booth and Herold’s desperate and risky attempt to cross the Potomac.
