Chapter 24

The Surratt Boardinghouse Siege

Seen from above, Washington resembled a vast command center. Its telegraph network stretched from the War Department like static rigging, channeling orders outward while signal lights flickered and columns of dust and smoke traced movements across the landscape. Below this web of communication and control, the urgent national pursuit had already narrowed, contracting to the scrutiny of a single, ordinary street address.

541 H Street stood three blocks from the Patent Office, a three-story brick townhouse with a small yard fronting the sidewalk. On the afternoon of Monday, April 17, 1865, three men in civilian clothes occupied positions near this house without appearing to occupy them. One sat on a stoop across the street, newspaper unfolded to a story he did not read. Another walked past at intervals, studying the ground as if searching for a dropped coin. The third waited in a carriage at the corner of Sixth Street, reins loose, watching the upper windows. They were detectives assigned to Colonel Lafayette Baker’s National Detective Police, though nothing on their persons identified them as federal agents. Their orders had come through the Provost Marshal’s office sometime after midnight, triggered by information that had accumulated across the seventy-two hours since John Wilkes Booth fired his pistol at Ford’s Theater.

The house itself gave little outward sign of its recent history. Mary Surratt, the widow who owned the property, had converted it to a boardinghouse in the autumn of 1864, after her husband’s death and the sale of their Maryland tavern. She lived here with her twenty-two-year-old daughter Anna, her son John when he was not traveling, and a shifting cast of paying guests. The arrangement was common enough in a city swollen by war bureaucrats, temporary officers, and the hangers-on of Confederate sympathizers who had found Washington more hospitable than Richmond. The Surratts were sympathetic to the Confederate States of America and often hosted fellow Confederate sympathizers at their tavern. What distinguished 541 H Street was its connection to the man now the subject of the largest manhunt in American history.

That connection had emerged in fragments. On the morning after the assassination, detectives searching Booth’s room at the National Hotel found correspondence pointing to Surratt’s tavern in Surrattsville, Maryland, as a way-station on the escape route. The tavern’s manager, John Lloyd, was brought to Washington and questioned. He revealed that on the afternoon of April 14, Mary Surratt had delivered to him a package of field glasses and told him to have shooting irons ready that night: two Spencer carbines hidden under the floorboards. She had also mentioned that her son John would be calling for them after dark. The weapons had indeed been collected, Lloyd said, by a man on horseback sometime after midnight, accompanied by another man on a sorrel. The description matched Booth and David Herold.

More significant for the immediate surveillance of the H Street house was the testimony of Louis Weichmann, a boarder who had occupied a room on the second floor since November. Weichmann was twenty-three, a clerk in the Commissary General of Prisoners office, a Northern-born Catholic with Confederate sympathies that had led him to seek out the Surratt household. He had become, without entirely intending it, a witness to the conspiracy’s formation.

In interviews with detectives on April 15 and 16, he described Booth’s frequent visits to the house (sometimes two or three times a week), his private conversations with John Surratt in the parlor, and a particularly suspicious meeting on December 23, 1864. On that occasion Weichmann had driven Mary Surratt, her son, and Booth to a restaurant on the Seventh Street pike; they then continued to Booth’s room at the National Hotel. Weichmann had not been admitted to the room; but several hours passed before they emerged.

These details, assembled in Baker’s office and relayed to Stanton’s War Department, had produced the surveillance now in its second day. The detectives had orders to watch, to note all visitors, and to arrest anyone who attempted to leave. But they had not yet been authorized to enter. That authorization would depend on developments elsewhere in the city, and on the judgment of officials who were still uncertain whether the conspiracy was a contained plot or the opening move of a broader Confederate operation.

Inside the house, fear and confusion thickened by degrees. Mary Surratt was forty-two years old: heavy-featured and devout; she understood by Monday morning that her name was circulating in connection with the assassination. She had spent Sunday in prayer and denial; she told her daughter and boarders that accusations against her were fabrications; she insisted her son John was innocent of any involvement; she said visits to Surrattsville concerned only routine business. But no official inquiry came: no knock at the door; no warrant served; nothing but silence from authorities who might have ended uncertainty one way or another.

Anna Surratt proved more volatile than her mother; she spent Sunday weeping and arguing by turns; she had been close to her brother; she shared his Confederate loyalties and social connections within Washington’s Confederate underground; news that Booth was indeed an assassin (still uncertain Saturday) became confirmed fact in Sunday newspapers; Booth had been frequent visitor parlor sometimes sat mother private conversation brother seen him days before April14 all carried weight she could not calculate

The other boarders moved through the house with the self-consciousness of people aware they were being observed. Honora Fitzpatrick, a young woman from Baltimore, kept to her room. Louis Weichmann, the source of so much damaging information, had not returned to the house since his interviews with detectives. His absence was itself a message that the others read correctly. Only one recent arrival seemed untroubled by the tension: a large, slow-spoken man who had taken a room on the first floor on Saturday, April 15, introducing himself as a Baptist preacher named Reverend Wood. He was, in fact, Lewis Powell, the twenty-one-year-old Confederate veteran who had attacked Secretary of State William Seward on the night of the assassination, and who had come to the Surratt house seeking refuge when his planned escape to Baltimore had collapsed.

Powell’s presence in the house represented a failure of coordination that would have consequences for everyone inside. He had been recruited by Booth in January, had trained with the others in the conspiracy’s final form, had accepted the assignment to kill Seward as his contribution to the strike against the federal government. The attack had failed—Seward, already injured in a carriage accident, had survived Powell’s knife—and Powell had wandered the streets of Washington for two days, sleeping in a tree and attempting without success to find Booth or any of the other conspirators. He had come to 541 H Street because it was the only address he knew, the only connection he had in a city now swarming with soldiers and police.

Mary Surratt had received him with caution. She knew him as a friend of Booth’s, had seen him at the house before, but his appearance on Saturday afternoon—disheveled, without luggage, asking for a room—had alarmed her. She had let him stay, perhaps from Confederate solidarity, perhaps from the paralysis of a woman who did not know what refusal would imply. By Monday, she must have understood that her house now harbored a man who had attempted to murder the Secretary of State. The detectives outside had not yet identified him. But his presence turned the boardinghouse from a node of association into a shelter for a known perpetrator.

The surveillance continued through Monday without incident. The detectives noted Mary Surratt’s departure for a brief errand, her return, the movements of servants. They noted Anna Surratt’s agitation, visible even from the street. They did not know about Powell. What broke the stasis was a decision made at the War Department in the late afternoon, and a coincidence of timing that would shape the trial to come.

Colonel Baker had spent Monday consolidating his control of the investigation. His agents had made four arrests in Maryland by Sunday night—Samuel Arnold, Michael O’Laughlen, Edman Spangler, and George Atzerodt—and had identified two more conspirators still at large: John Surratt and David Herold. The focus on 541 H Street had intensified as Weichmann’s testimony accumulated. By late afternoon, Baker had secured authorization for a raid. The timing was determined by a separate development: the discovery of Booth’s abandoned horse.

The horse, a bay mare, had been found in a stable off Tenth Street, not far from Ford’s Theater. It was identified as belonging to John Burroughs, a stableman who had been with Booth at the theater on April 14. The horse carried a saddle and bridle of military pattern, and its presence in Washington—rather than on the escape route to Maryland—suggested that someone had returned to the city after the assassination. That someone could only be Powell, who had lost his own horse in the confusion of the Seward attack. The connection between Powell and the Surratt house, already suspected from Weichmann’s description of Booth’s associates, now seemed probable enough to justify immediate action.

At approximately ten o’clock on the night of April 17, the surveillance became a siege. Baker himself led a party of detectives and soldiers to 541 H Street, arriving in a closed carriage. The street was quiet, the houses dark except for lamps in upper windows. The detectives who had been watching all day emerged from their positions to join the assault force. Baker posted men at the rear of the house to prevent escape, then approached the front door with three others.

The knock was loud and official. Mary Surratt answered it herself, a lamp in her hand, her face composed in the rigid mask of a woman who had been expecting this moment without knowing when it would come. Baker identified himself and his authority. He asked if she knew John Wilkes Booth. She said she did, that he had been a frequent visitor, that she had no knowledge of his whereabouts since April 14. Baker asked about her son John. She said he was traveling, that she did not know where, that he had nothing to do with the assassination.

The questioning moved inside. Baker and his men entered the parlor, a narrow room with horsehair furniture and a portrait of Pius IX on the wall. Anna Surratt had come downstairs, wrapped in a shawl, her face pale. The other boarders emerged from their rooms, pressed against the walls, watching the federal agents search their house. Baker’s men moved through the ground floor, then mounted the stairs to search the upper rooms.

What happened next has been reconstructed from multiple accounts that agree on essentials while differing in emphasis. Powell was in his room on the first floor when he heard the knock. He had spent the day in a state of suspended existence, unable to leave the house without exposing himself, unable to remain without endangering his hosts. The arrival of Baker’s party forced a decision he was not prepared to make. He opened his door, looked into the hallway, saw the federal agents, and retreated. Then, according to the testimony he would later give and the accounts of those present, he made a choice that seems inexplicable except as the product of exhaustion, confusion, or a final, distorted loyalty to the mission Booth had assigned him.

He walked out of his room, down the hallway, and into the parlor where Baker was interrogating Mary Surratt. He carried nothing—no weapon, no bundle, no prepared story. He was a large man, six feet tall, broad-shouldered, with a face that even in normal circumstances attracted attention. In the lamplight of the parlor, his appearance must have seemed to the federal agents like an apparition, or a trap.

He told Baker that he was a poor man who wanted to hire out as a hand.

The words hung in the air. Mary Surratt looked at him without recognition, or with recognition so complete that she dared not show it. She told Baker that she did not know him, that he had come to the house that day asking for work, that she had never seen him before.

This denial, made in the presence of federal agents who would remember it, would cost Mary Surratt her life. At her trial, the prosecution would present it as proof of her guilt: she had lied to protect a known assassin, had preferred perjury to cooperation with lawful authority. The defense would argue that she had spoken from terror, from the reflexive solidarity of a household under assault, from genuine uncertainty about who this stranger was and what he wanted. The military commission would accept the prosecution’s reading. But in the moment, in the parlor at 541 H Street, the denial created only confusion and delay.

Baker was not deceived. He had seen Powell’s photograph in the files of suspected Confederate agents. He had read descriptions of the Seward attacker: large, powerful, dressed in dark clothing. The man before him matched those descriptions precisely. He ordered Powell seized. The detectives closed in. Powell did not resist. He was searched, found to be unarmed, and placed under guard in the hallway.

The arrest of Powell transformed the raid from an investigative probe into a major capture. Baker now had in custody one of the two men who had struck at the federal government on April 14. The other, Booth, was still at large, but his network was being dismantled piece by piece. The Surratt house, which had seemed a minor node of association, was revealed as a shelter for a principal conspirator.

Mary Surratt was arrested immediately after Powell. The charge was not yet specified—conspiracy to assassinate the President, harboring a fugitive, lying to federal officers—but the fact of arrest was decisive. She was taken from her house in the custody of female guards, placed in a carriage, and driven to the Old Capitol Prison, a converted school building on the site of the present Supreme Court. Anna Surratt was detained briefly, then released to the care of relatives. The other boarders were questioned and dismissed. The house at 541 H Street was sealed, its contents inventoried, its role in the conspiracy now a matter of official record.

The significance of these events extended beyond the individuals involved. The raid on the Surratt boardinghouse represented the moment when the federal investigation shifted from a search for Booth to the construction of a conspiracy case against multiple defendants. Stanton and his subordinates had begun with a assumption of organized Confederate action—a plot authorized by Richmond, supported by the Confederate secret service, extending through multiple agents in Washington and beyond. The evidence they had gathered supported a more modest conclusion: a small group of Confederate sympathizers, led by an actor with theatrical ambitions and political delusions, acting without official sanction but with the tacit support of a network of safe houses and sympathizers.

The Surratt house was the first of these safe houses to be exposed. Its exposure established a pattern that would govern the subsequent investigation: the identification of a location, the extraction of testimony from those associated with it, the arrest of everyone who could be connected to the conspiracy’s planning or execution. This pattern was not unique to the Lincoln assassination investigation. It was the standard procedure of military occupation, applied now to a civilian population in the capital of a nation still technically at war. But its application to Mary Surratt—a widow, a boardinghouse keeper, a woman with no direct part in the violence of April 14—raised questions about the boundaries of guilt that would persist through the trial and beyond.

Why this house? Because it was a known node in Booth’s network, a place where he had planned, recruited, and found shelter. Why arrest everyone? Because Stanton’s bureaucracy needed a conspiracy theory that would justify the extraordinary measures of the manhunt—the suspension of habeas corpus, the military arrest of civilians, the creation of a special detective force operating outside normal legal constraints. The Surratt house provided the first substantial evidence that such a conspiracy existed, that it had involved multiple actors over an extended period, that it had been planned in the drawing rooms of Washington as well as the taverns of Maryland.

The atmosphere inside the house in the hours before the raid—fear, denial, the paralysis of waiting—contrasted with the methodical expansion of the dragnet outside. This contrast would be repeated in other locations as the investigation proceeded: the Mudd farm in Maryland, where Samuel Mudd had set Booth’s broken leg; the home of George Atzerodt in Washington, where the would-be assassin of Andrew Johnson had abandoned his assignment and his nerve; the various boardinghouses and hotels where the conspirators had met and dispersed. In each case, the federal presence arrived as a systematic force, armed with information extracted from previous arrests, operating under emergency authority that brooked no resistance.

The arrest of Powell was particularly significant because it provided a living witness to the conspiracy’s scope. Unlike Booth, who remained at large and whose capture might produce only silence or defiance, Powell was in custody and would eventually talk. His interrogation, conducted over the following days by Baker’s agents and by military officers, would extract details of the conspiracy’s formation, its personnel, its intended targets. Some of these details would be accurate; others, distorted by Powell’s limited perspective or by the pressure of interrogation; still others, invented to satisfy his questioners or to protect those he still considered comrades. But the existence of a cooperative witness transformed the prosecution’s position. It made possible the construction of a narrative that would be presented to the military commission in May, a narrative in which every defendant had a assigned role and every action contributed to a single, criminal purpose.

Mary Surratt’s position in this narrative was ambiguous from the start. She had not been present at Ford’s Theater. She had not struck at Seward. Her only documented actions—delivering packages to Surrattsville, receiving visitors at her boardinghouse, denying knowledge of Powell—were susceptible to multiple interpretations. The prosecution would argue that these actions were essential contributions to the conspiracy, that without her management of the Surrattsville tavern and her hospitality in Washington, the escape route would not have existed and the conspirators would not have had a place to plan. The defense would argue that she had been a victim of her son’s associations, unaware of the purposes to which her property and her name were being put.

The military commission would accept the prosecution’s argument, and Mary Surratt would hang on July 7, 1865, the first woman executed by the federal government. That outcome was not predetermined on the night of April 17. But the conditions that would produce it were established in the raid on 541 H Street: the assumption of collective guilt, the weight given to association over direct action, the emergency authority that suspended normal protections for individuals suspected of threatening the state.

With Mary Surratt and Lewis Powell in custody, a defined set of conspirators was secured. The pressure of constructing a trial case could now begin.
