Chapter 25

Set Primarily Aboard the Ironclad

Across the whole map of a war-worn capital, in the first gray light of a late April morning, the machinery of prosecution lay waiting to grind. Yet on the flat, wide expanse of the Potomac, amidst the hulls and wakes of a winding-down fleet, a more immediate purpose was taking shape. At the Washington Navy Yard along the eastern shore, where granite and timber formed a geometry of military order, that logic was bending toward something stranger. Before charges could be drawn or evidence marshaled, the elemental work of reception and verification began.

The ironclad USS Montauk lay moored near the yard’s center, her low freeboard and armored turret giving her the squat, menacing profile that had made such vessels decisive weapons of coastal warfare. She had fought at Fort Fisher and steamed in blockade squadrons, her hundred-pound Parrott rifles speaking the language of naval bombardment.

Today her deck would serve as examination room. The transfer had been arranged with the secrecy that Edwin Stanton’s War Department applied to all matters touching the assassination. Booth’s body, shrouded in a blanket and tied to the side of an old farm wagon for the trip back to Belle Plain, had made the journey from the Garrett farm through night roads of Virginia to Belle Plain, where the steamer John S. Ide waited.

There, his corpse was taken aboard the ironclad USS Montauk and brought to the Washington Navy Yard for identification and an autopsy. Now, aboard the Montauk, the final stage of that journey began.

The covered wagon bearing the corpse rolled onto the navy yard’s pier shortly after eight in the morning. The body was heavy, waterlogged from immersion in barn straw and wagon-bed damp, stiffening in early stages of post-mortem rigidity. Sailors from the Montauk’s crew, detailed under officers who had received orders directly from the War Department, received the burden and carried it across the gangway. The ironclad’s deck, plated with iron and traversed by heavy turret tracks, offered surface ill-suited to delicate procedure. Yet here the identification would take place, removed from public gaze, contained within the military cordon that Stanton had thrown around every aspect of the manhunt’s conclusion.

The assembly of examiners gathered through morning hours represented parallel authorities whose convergence was itself statement of institutional caution. Dr. John Frederick May, a physician who had known Booth professionally and treated him in the past, came at government summons. The naval officer commanding the Montauk provided martial witness. An army lieutenant detailed from the staff represented the War Department’s direct interest. Colonel Lafayette Baker arrived from his intelligence headquarters, his presence signaling that this examination would generate evidence for prosecutions already taking shape. Other officers and medical men filled out the board, their names recorded in official reports that would be sealed from public view.

The body was carried to the Montauk’s wardroom, a space below deck where officers took their meals and where ironclad armor plate formed walls impervious to both shot and curious eyes. Here the shroud was removed. The corpse presented marks of its final hours: the gunshot wound behind the left ear, the track of bullet visible in matted hair; the broken left leg, splinted and bound in manner Dr. Samuel Mudd had applied it; the hands that had held the derringer at Ford’s Theater now stiff and cooling. The examiners worked methodically, each man contributing particular expertise to composite identification.

Dr. May’s testimony carried special weight. He had treated Booth in 1863 for a neck complaint, had observed the distinctive scar behind the actor’s left ear, had noted the tattoo on his left hand bearing initials “JWB.” These marks, invisible to general public but known to those with reason to verify identity, provided anchor points of certainty. May examined the neck, located the scar, confirmed its correspondence with medical memory. The tattoo, examined by lamplight in ironclad’s gloomy interior, matched his description. The broken leg, subject of so much speculation during manhunt—Booth’s injury variously reported as ankle, shin, or thigh—was confirmed as fracture of fibula some three inches above ankle joint, exactly matching Mudd’s surgical notes.

The procedure extended through hours of careful documentation. Measurements were taken: height five feet seven and three-quarter inches, though body lay in posture making precise determination difficult. The gunshot wound was probed and described, its trajectory noted as entering behind ear and passing toward right eye, consistent with Booth’s position in barn when Boston Corbett fired. The hands were examined for tattoo, for calluses from theatrical sword-work, for any distinguishing marks that might survive transformation of death.

This meticulous process stood in deliberate contrast to violence that had produced corpse. At Garrett farm, twelve hours earlier, barn had burned, Corbett’s pistol had cracked, and Booth had been dragged from flames paralyzed and dying. Cavalrymen had witnessed man expire in agony, had heard final whispered requests, had watched struggle between breath and oblivion. Now, aboard Montauk, that same body became object of clinical scrutiny, its identity established through bureaucratic ritual that stripped away narrative drama in favor of evidentiary certainty.

The contrast was not accidental. Stanton’s War Department required incontrovertible proof that assassin was dead, proof that would satisfy military tribunals, silence rumors of escape, and foreclose political dangers of uncertain fate. Throughout twelve-day manhunt, false reports had proliferated: Booth captured in Baltimore, Booth shot in Pennsylvania, Booth escaped to Canada or England or Mexico. Each rumor had demanded investigation, had consumed resources and attention, had fed public anxiety that Stanton sought to control. The ironclad examination was designed to produce single, authoritative truth, certified by multiple witnesses and sealed within official record.

Yet procedure also revealed limits of that control. The body, however thoroughly examined, remained problem of disposal. Burial at sea, traditional fate of executed criminals and military offenders, would remove remains from any possibility of public veneration or Confederate memorialization. But it would also eliminate physical evidence that prosecutors anticipated needing for trials already in preparation. Military tribunal that would convene in May would want to display instruments of conspiracy, material traces of crime. A body sunk in Potomac could not be produced in court.

The identification board completed its work by mid-afternoon. Their collective testimony, sworn and signed, established that corpse was indeed John Wilkes Booth, that he had died of gunshot wound inflicted by Boston Corbett, that no possibility of mistaken identity survived their examination. Documents were sealed and transmitted to War Department, where they would form part of prosecutorial file against captured conspirators. Physical body remained aboard Montauk, its final disposition deferred by same administrative caution that had governed every stage of its reception.

David Herold, captured at Garrett farm and transported on same steamer that bore Booth’s corpse, was held separately aboard vessel. The pharmacist’s assistant who had accompanied assassin through twelve-day flight, who had surrendered in barn while Booth chose to burn, now faced interrogation by same authorities who had assembled identification board. His testimony, like physical examination of body, would be extracted and preserved as evidence. The parallel processes—one clinical, one coercive—proceeded under Montauk’s armored deck, hidden from public observation.

The ironclad’s presence in navy yard, her guns trained outward toward Potomac’s channel, embodied military character of post-assassination state. Civil courts still functioned in Washington, but decisive actions of manhunt and its aftermath occurred under martial authority. Stanton, as Secretary of War, had assumed powers that blurred constitutional boundaries: suspension of habeas corpus, arrest of civilians by military force, creation of military commission to try conspirators. The Montauk, warship repurposed as examination room and holding cell, was physical expression of that extraordinary authority.

As evening approached on April 27, identification board dispersed, their duty completed. The corpse remained, its disposal unresolved. Colonel Baker, who had directed intelligence apparatus that located Booth at Garrett farm, would later claim responsibility for decision to bury body secretly, to remove it from any site that might become place of pilgrimage or political symbol. But that decision lay in hours ahead, its execution requiring further improvisation within navy yard’s military perimeter.

The Montauk’s crew, ordinary sailors who had expected routine blockade duty, found themselves witnesses to history’s underside. They had not seen assassination, had not pursued fugitive through Virginia’s swamps and pine barrens, had not fired shot that ended chase. But they saw what followed: covered wagon on pier, body’s transfer across their deck, sealed wardroom where doctors and officers performed grim verification. Their testimony, if ever sought, would confirm only physical facts of reception and containment.

The Potomac’s tide turned in late afternoon, current reversing to flow toward Chesapeake and sea. Any vessel departing navy yard after that hour would face uphill passage against river’s strength. The Montauk, anchored and armored, did not move. She remained in place as darkness fell and yard’s lamps were lit, her turret silhouetted against sky, her hold containing certified remains of most wanted man in America.

The administrative ritual completed aboard the ironclad represented a transformation as decisive as any in the twelve days since Ford’s Theater. Booth the fugitive, object of desperate pursuit, became Booth the specimen, subject of documentary record. A state that had failed to prevent his crime, that had struggled to locate him through nearly two weeks of false leads and frustrated searches, now asserted its capacity to fix his identity, certify his death, and control his remains. This was not justice, which would require months of tribunal proceedings and would end with scaffold and rope for those who had aided him. It was something more primitive and more necessary: demonstration that the assassin had been caught, identified, and contained.

Yet containment proved incomplete. The body, officially verified, remained administrative problem. Decision to dispose of it secretly, to bury it in unmarked location within navy yard’s grounds, would be taken in hours that followed, executed by small party of officers and workmen under cover of darkness. That clandestine burial, like so much of assassination’s aftermath, would generate its own rumors, its own contested narratives, its own small literature of speculation and denial. For decades, claims would surface that Booth had escaped, that another body had been substituted, that true assassin lived out his years in Texas or Oklahoma or Indian Territory. The Montauk’s careful identification procedure, designed to foreclose such uncertainty, would become itself subject of doubt.

The parallel authorities who had converged on ironclad dispersed to their separate functions. Dr. May returned to his medical practice, his testimony preserved in War Department files. The Montauk’s commander resumed charge of his vessel, which would soon return to active service in navy’s demobilizing fleet. Colonel Baker turned to interrogation of prisoners and preparation of cases for military tribunal. Each man carried fragment of official narrative, piece of evidence that would be assembled into prosecution of conspiracy.

David Herold, held aboard Montauk through night of April 27, would be transferred to Old Capitol Prison and then to Washington Arsenal, where military tribunal would convene in May. His knowledge of Booth’s movements, of safe houses and Confederate contacts that had sheltered fugitive, made him valuable to prosecutors and dangerous to those who had aided assassination. Body of his companion, verified and certified, would be buried before he stood trial, its physical absence from courtroom requiring identification board’s written testimony to establish chain of conspiracy.

The Washington Navy Yard, in early morning hours of April 28, received final party of workmen who would complete disposal. The grave was dug in obscure corner of military reservation, its location recorded only in secret orders that would be lost and disputed in decades that followed. The body, wrapped in its blanket and weighted with iron, was lowered without ceremony. Earth was replaced, sod tamped down, site left indistinguishable from surroundings. Ironclad that had served as examination room remained at anchor, her crew returned to routine, her wardroom scrubbed of traces of its strange employment.

The state’s final act of transformation followed: fugitive become specimen become secret. The man who had shot Abraham Lincoln in full view of a theater audience, who had leaped to the stage and cried “Sic semper tyrannis,” who had eluded capture for twelve days through landscapes of Confederate sympathy and wartime devastation, ended as an unmarked burial in a military compound. The identification procedure aboard the Montauk had provided the necessary certification, a bureaucratic warrant for this disappearance. The disposal that followed completed the logic of containment that had governed the manhunt’s conclusion.

The pressure that had driven identification board—Stanton’s need for incontrovertible proof, prosecutors’ need for evidence, public’s need for certainty—now shifted to other objects. Military tribunal would require witnesses, documents, testimony of those who had conspired with man whose body lay buried in navy yard. Trial of conspirators, scheduled to begin on May 9, would reconstruct assassination through words and paper, through sworn statements of survivors and captured correspondence of accused. Physical body of assassin, once so urgently sought, would be represented only by records of its examination, measurements and marks preserved in War Department files.

The ironclad scene thus marked pivot in narrative of pursuit and capture. Frantic energy of manhunt, telegraphic urgency that had mobilized thousands of soldiers and civilians across multiple states, gave way to slower procedures of law and punishment. Body that had been object of that energy was now fixed in place, identified and interred, removed from possibility of further flight or disguise. What remained was accounting: trial, verdict, execution of sentence against those who had survived to face judgment.

The Montauk herself would continue in naval service, her armor and guns eventually obsolete in postwar fleet, her name passing into records of Civil War ironclads. Wardroom where Booth’s body had been examined would be restored to ordinary function, deck where sailors had received covered wagon would see countless other loadings and unloadings in years of naval routine. Secret of burial site, known to handful of officers and workmen, would become one of many classified matters of assassination’s aftermath, its revelation gradual and contested.

For men who had assembled identification board, Montauk examination represented professional duty performed under extraordinary circumstances. They had applied ordinary skills—medical observation, military administration, legal documentation—to task that history would remember. Their signatures on identification report testified to moment of verified certainty in narrative otherwise characterized by confusion, rumor, and contested interpretation. That certainty, however, was partial and provisional. It established death of John Wilkes Booth, but it could not establish meaning of his crime, full extent of his conspiracy, or consequences that would flow from his act.

What the ironclad procedure had contained was the body. What it could not contain was the story. The secret burial, executed by torchlight in early hours of April 28, removed physical evidence from possible challenge or display, but it also removed possibility of final, public certainty. The identification board’s documentation would serve in place of corpse, their sworn testimony substituting for physical presence in courtroom. This substitution, necessary for prosecution’s purposes, would also become foundation for decades of doubt. The state’s need to control narrative—preventing martyrdom, denying symbol—had generated its own vulnerability. A verified body, secretly buried, could be claimed as; official certainty, withheld from public view, could be dismissed as official deception.

The pressure that began with covered wagon on navy yard pier, that drove doctors to measure and officers to witness, did not dissipate with completion of ritual. It transformed into something more durable and more dangerous: the burden of proof without proof’s object. The conspirators would be tried before military tribunal that could not exhibit their leader’s corpse. The public would be asked to accept certification by authorities who had hidden what they certified. The body had been officially identified, but its disposal remained unresolved administrative and political problem, creating pressure for its clandestine fate.
