Chapter 10

Its Core Claim

The road to Port Royal ran through a country still waking from its own defeat. On the morning of April 26, 1865, Lieutenant Edward P. Doherty led twenty-six men of the 16th New York Cavalry along that road at a trot, their horses’ hooves throwing dust that the dew had not yet settled. They had ridden through the night from Belle Plain, guided by Willie Jett, the young Confederate courier who had spent the previous evening in the company of John Wilkes Booth and knew exactly which tobacco barn held the man the War Department wanted more than any other living soul. The detachment had steamed 70 miles down the Potomac on the boat John S. Ide, landing at Belle Plain at 10 p.m. the night before, then crossed the Rappahannock River to track Booth and Herold to Richard H. Garrett’s farm, about 2 miles south of Port Royal.

Doherty was thirty years old, Canadian-born, a regular officer who had served through the campaigns of the Army of the Potomac. He understood cavalry tactics: how to deploy skirmishers, how to cut off retreat, how to use terrain. What he did not yet understand, as the first gray light showed the Garrett farm rising from its fields, was whether this operation belonged to him or to the two civilians who rode beside him.

Everton Conger and Luther Baker had come down from Washington with papers from Lafayette Baker’s National Detective Police. Conger was a lieutenant colonel in name, a surgeon turned intelligence officer who had tracked guerrillas in the western theater. Luther Baker was the detective chief’s cousin, a man whose authority rested on kinship and reputation rather than commission. Between the three of them—Doherty with his troops, Conger with his rank, Baker with his connections—there was no clear chain of command for what came next.

The farm appeared as a working concern: a plain frame house, outbuildings, a tobacco barn set back from the main structure. Doherty halted the column in a stand of pines and gave his first order. The men would dismount. They would approach on foot. They would surround the buildings before daylight exposed their numbers.

Conger objected. The colonel wanted to ride straight to the house, to demand entry, to rely on surprise and the weight of federal arms. Baker sided with the cavalryman, or seemed to—the later reports would disagree on who said what, when, and with what emphasis. What is certain is that for several minutes, while the sky paled above the Rappahannock, three men argued in whispers about who had the right to decide how John Wilkes Booth would be taken.

This dispute over authority would matter. It would shape every subsequent decision: how the barn was approached, how the surrender was demanded, how the fire was set, how the shot was fired. The official accounts that Doherty, Conger, and Baker filed in the days afterward contradict each other on essential points—who gave which order, who stood where, who saw what. These contradictions are not merely the confusion of action. They are the trace of a command structure that had never been clarified, a collision between military and civilian authority that no one in Washington had thought to prevent.

Doherty prevailed, or appeared to. The soldiers moved forward on foot, spreading through the farmyard in the half-light. They found the Garrett house dark, its occupants still asleep. John Garrett, the farmer, came to his door in his nightshirt, confronted by armed men who demanded to know where the strangers were hiding. His first answer was evasion. His second, pressed by Conger’s hand on his collar, was the tobacco barn.

The barn was a substantial structure, built for curing leaf tobacco, with a dirt floor and a roof thatched with pine needles. It had no windows, only gaps in the siding, and a single door that faced the house. Booth and Herold had been inside since the previous evening, when Richard Garrett had refused them the house after learning—through whatever combination of suspicion and indiscretion—that his guests were not the Confederate officers they claimed to be. The father had denied them; the son had offered the barn. Now the son stood in his own dooryard, watching federal soldiers take positions around the building where he had placed two men he knew only as Boyd and a companion.

David Herold was twenty-two years old, a pharmacist’s assistant who had followed Booth from Washington because the actor had asked him to and because the adventure had seemed, for a moment, to offer escape from a life of failures. He was slight, nervous, entirely unprepared for what the morning had brought. John Wilkes Booth was twenty-six, an actor of national reputation, a man who had shot the President of the United States and believed, even now, that history would vindicate him. He had a broken left leg, poorly set by Dr. Samuel Mudd twelve days before. He had a carbine and a pistol. He had, according to his own account written in the hours before dawn, a growing sense that his heroic narrative had collapsed into something smaller and meaner than he had intended.

The soldiers completed their encirclement. Doherty placed men at each corner of the barn, with orders to shoot anyone who attempted to escape. Conger and Baker positioned themselves near the door, the colonel taking the lead in what followed. The first demand for surrender came in a shout, directed at the silent building.

The silence held. Then Booth’s voice answered from within, asking who commanded the party and what terms were offered. Conger replied that he commanded, or allowed the impression to stand—another point the later reports would dispute. The terms were unconditional surrender. There would be no negotiation.

Herold wanted to comply. This much is clear from every account. He told Booth that resistance was impossible, that the barn was surrounded, that they had no chance of escape. Booth refused. He would not, he said, be taken alive. He would die in the barn if necessary, and his death would complete the martyrdom that his life had prepared.

The argument between the two fugitives was audible to the soldiers outside. Herold pleaded; Booth threatened. At one point, according to Conger’s report, Herold attempted to open the door and surrender on his own, and Booth pulled him back by force. The young man was weeping. The actor was calculating, or trying to calculate, how long he could prolong the standoff and what advantage delay might bring.

There was no advantage. The sun was rising now, showing the federal positions clearly, revealing the hopelessness of the situation. Yet the standoff continued because no one outside the barn could agree on how to end it. Doherty wanted to wait for full daylight, to reduce the risk of error. Baker favored an immediate assault. Conger proposed setting fire to the barn, smoking the occupants out—a tactic he had seen used against guerrilla hideouts in the west.

The proposal divided the commanders. Doherty opposed it, or said he did later, on the grounds that fire might kill both prisoners before they could be questioned. Baker supported it, or seemed to, though his official report would emphasize his distance from the actual ignition. Conger carried the argument, or assumed he had, and gave orders to prepare combustibles.

Inside the barn, Booth understood what was happening. He could hear the soldiers gathering straw, could smell the pitch. He called out again, demanding to know the terms, offering to fight single combat with any officer who would face him—proposals that mixed the theatrical conventions of his profession with a genuine desperation. No one accepted. The terms remained what they had been: come out with hands raised, or burn.

Herold made his decision. While Booth was positioned at the back of the barn, watching a gap in the siding, the younger man moved to the door and called out that he wished to surrender. Conger instructed him to come out with his hands visible, to walk slowly, to make no sudden movement. Herold complied. He emerged into the morning light disheveled, unarmed, weeping with relief and shame. The soldiers seized him and bound his wrists, and he told them immediately that the man inside was John Wilkes Booth, that he was armed, that he would not surrender.

The removal of Herold simplified the situation without resolving it. Where there had been two prisoners, there was now one. Where there had been the possibility of negotiation, there was now only the choice between fire and assault. Booth understood this. From within the barn, he called out again, his voice carrying a different quality now—still theatrical, still performing the role of the doomed hero, but with an edge of genuine uncertainty. He asked for time. He asked for terms. He received silence, or the silence of men waiting for an order that had not yet been given.

The Garrett family watched from the house. John Garrett, the father, had by now learned enough to understand that his farm had become the stage for an event that would outlast his own memory. His sons stood with him, uncertain whether they were witnesses or suspects, whether their hospitality to strangers would be counted as crime or mere misfortune. The federal soldiers ignored them, their attention fixed on the barn and the man inside it.

Conger gave the order, or gave it implicitly, by directing soldiers to place the prepared combustibles against the barn’s walls. The straw was dry, the pine siding seasoned by years of tobacco curing. A match would transform the structure within minutes, driving any occupant into the open or consuming him where he stood.

Doherty recorded his objection, or recorded it later. The lieutenant’s report emphasized that he had wanted to preserve the prisoner for interrogation, that fire was an unnecessary risk to intelligence that only Booth possessed. This may be true, or it may be the reconstruction of a man who needed to explain his part in what followed. What is certain is that no one countermanded the preparation for fire, and that the soldiers placed their bundles of straw with the methodical care of men who had done this before.

Booth saw the straw through the gaps in the siding. He moved to the front of the barn, positioning himself near the door but not so near that he could be seized if it opened. He had his carbine in his left hand, his pistol in his right. His broken leg made standing painful; he shifted his weight constantly, trying to find a position that would allow him to move if movement became necessary.

The standoff reached its final phase. Outside, the three commanders—Doherty, Conger, Baker—stood in an uneasy triangle near the door, each watching the others as much as the barn. Inside, Booth watched the straw and calculated odds that had become purely personal. He was not thinking, by his own later account, of the cause he had served or the history he had hoped to make. He was thinking of how to die in a manner that would be remembered, that would restore to his death the significance that his life had claimed.

The fire was not yet lit. The soldiers held their positions, their rifles trained on the gaps in the siding, waiting for the order that would end the standoff one way or another. The morning was fully light now, the sun clearing the trees to the east, casting long shadows across the farmyard. The Garrett house stood silent, its occupants withdrawn from windows they had been ordered to close. The tobacco barn waited, its occupant visible only in fragments through the gaps in its walls—a movement here, a gleam of metal there, the sound of a man breathing hard in the darkness he had chosen.

The order came, or the fire was set without formal order, or it caught by accident—this, like everything else in the final minutes, would be disputed in the reports and the testimony that followed. What is certain is that straw and pine needles burn quickly, that smoke began to fill the barn’s interior, that Booth moved toward the door as the heat increased behind him, and that a single shot was fired from the federal line at the moment when the assassin of Abraham Lincoln appeared in the doorway, his hands raised or reaching for his weapons or simply trying to shield his eyes from the smoke and flame.

The bullet struck him in the neck. He fell in the barn’s doorway, his body half in the fire’s light and half in the shadow of the structure that had become his coffin. The soldiers rushed forward, dragging him clear, and found that he was still alive—paralyzed, unable to move his limbs, but conscious and capable of speech. They laid him on the porch of the Garrett house, and for two hours he spoke of his motives, his regrets, his certainty that he had done right, while the surgeons who had been summoned confirmed that no surgeon could save him.

He died as the morning passed into afternoon, asking to be turned on his side so that he could see the face of the sun. Conger refused, or allowed it, or was not present at the moment—the reports disagree. What is certain is that John Wilkes Booth died on the Garrett farm on April 26, 1865, twelve days after he had shot Abraham Lincoln at Ford’s Theater, and that his death resolved nothing of the questions his life had raised.

The capture party returned to Washington with Herold in custody and Booth’s body in a boat, wrapped in a blanket and guarded by men who had been ordered to identify it beyond doubt. The War Department’s telegraph carried the news before nightfall: the assassin was dead, the manhunt concluded, the nation released from the suspense that had held it since Good Friday. But the conclusion was not clean. The command disputes at the barn, the conflicting accounts of who had ordered what, the single shot fired by a soldier whose name would become controversial—all of this would surface in the trials that followed, in the investigations that sought to fix responsibility, in the histories that attempted to make sense of how the pursuit had ended.

David Herold survived to stand trial. He would be convicted, sentenced to death, and hanged on July 7, 1865, alongside three others who had been caught in the net of conspiracy. His role had been minor, his choices constrained by the company he had kept, but the law made no such distinctions in the aftermath of assassination. He died protesting his innocence of the central crime, admitting only that he had accompanied a man he had believed to be a Confederate agent, that he had not understood until too late what Booth intended.

The soldiers of the 16th New York returned to their regiment, their names recorded in the official reports that Doherty, Conger, and Baker filed with the War Department. Each report advanced its author’s claim to credit, minimized his responsibility for the fire and the shooting, and contradicted the others on points of fact that no subsequent investigation could fully resolve. The reward money—$100, 000 offered by the federal government—would be distributed in ways that generated lawsuits and bitterness, another index of how the manhunt’s conclusion failed to produce the clarity that its organizers had sought.

The Garrett farm resumed its obscurity. John Garrett would be arrested, questioned, released, and left to rebuild a reputation damaged by hospitality he had never intended to extend to assassins. The tobacco barn burned to its foundation, or was pulled down, or stood as a ruin that visitors came to see in the years when the assassination was still recent memory. The landscape of the Rappahannock country absorbed the event into its ordinary life, another violence in a region that had known many, another death in a war whose official ending had not yet reached the consciousness of those who had fought it.

The standoff is frozen; the barn is silent in the dawn light, with soldiers outside holding combustible material.
