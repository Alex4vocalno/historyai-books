Chapter 6

The Confederate's Oath After Appomattox

The concrete evidence Mudd produced was specific and actionable. A man with a broken left leg, traveling south toward the Potomac, seeking to cross into Virginia: this description traveled by telegraph to every military station in southern Maryland. It reached the cavalry detachments under Lieutenant Edward P. Doherty at Alexandria, the gunboats patrolling the river, the provost marshals in Port Tobacco and Leonardtown. Yet by the time these orders began to coalesce into cordons, Booth and Herold had already vanished into the Zekiah Swamp, that dense woodland of pine and cypress where the roads dissolved into wagon ruts and the wagon ruts into paths known only to locals. They were not fleeing now. They were waiting.

Thomas Jones found them on the morning of April 16, Easter Sunday, in a thicket of young pines perhaps a mile from his farm. He had been warned to expect them—word passed through the attenuated network of Confederate agents that still clung to life in Charles County, though the war had ended at Appomattox five days before. Jones was fifty-one, a Confederate signal agent who had spent the war running blockade cargoes and military dispatches across the Potomac. He knew the river’s moods, its hidden landings, the schedules of Union patrols.

What he saw in the thicket that morning was not the triumphant avenger Booth imagined himself to be, but a man in pain, his left leg splinted and wrapped in bandages that Mudd had provided, his face drawn and feverish. Herold, the younger man, seemed to Jones like a nervous servant, useful for fetching water and standing watch but lacking any settled purpose of his own.

Jones brought them bread and meat, coffee and newspapers. The newspapers were the fugitives’ only window on the world they had upended, and they carried devastating news. Lincoln had died at 7: 22 on the morning of April 15, across the street from Ford’s Theater in the Petersen boarding house. Secretary of War Edwin Stanton had taken effective control of the government. Washington was in mourning, the theaters closed, the streets draped in black crepe. And there were the reward notices: $50, 000 for Booth, $25, 000 each for Herold and Powell, the would-be assassin of Secretary of State William Seward. The notices described Booth accurately—five feet eight inches, athletic build, jet-black hair, prominent mustache—and added a detail that would narrow every search: a scar on the right side of his neck, about the size of a twenty-five cent piece.

Booth read these papers in the filtered light of the pine thicket, scratching entries in a small memorandum book with a pencil stub. The diary he kept during these days survives as a unique documentary record, though its provenance demands caution. Booth intended it, apparently, as a manifesto for posterity, not a private confession. He wrote with an audience in mind, constructing a narrative of heroic sacrifice rather than recording unvarnished fact. Yet even through this performative haze, something genuine emerges: the psychological toll of immobility, the dawning recognition that his “act” had not produced the Confederate revival he anticipated, the bitter sense of abandonment by the very South he had meant to serve.

After being hunted like a dog, he wrote on April 17, I am here as a bear might be in a hole. The metaphor was apt. The pine thicket offered concealment but no comfort. Rain soaked through the canopy. Mosquitoes rose in clouds from the swamp. Booth’s leg throbbed constantly, the broken fibula—he maintained it had snapped when he leaped from the presidential box to the Ford’s stage—refusing to knit. Herold, for all his devotion, could do little to ease his companion’s suffering. The two men passed the hours in tense proximity, their conversations unrecorded but their circumstances speaking clearly enough. They were dependent now on a single man, Jones, who came and went at his own discretion, bearing food and intelligence and the newspapers that measured their growing notoriety.

Jones’s loyalty was personal and particular, not ideological. He had served the Confederate cause, but the cause was dead. What moved him now was a code of honor among actors in a finished drama, an obligation to men who had placed themselves in his hands. This was the contraction that characterized the post-Appomattox South: not the disappearance of sympathy for the Confederate project, but the rapid narrowing of that sympathy into private channels, into individual relationships too slight to sustain organized resistance. Jones would help these two men because he had given his word, and because they had no one else. He would not raise a company for them, or harbor them indefinitely, or die in their defense. The margin of Confederate solidarity had become exactly the width of one man’s conscience.

The War Department, meanwhile, was learning to convert information into pressure. Stanton’s telegraphic network, the most sophisticated communication system in the world, hummed with orders that narrowed the fugitives’ possibilities by the hour. On April 16, Major General John A. Dix, commanding the Department of the East, directed the commanders at Fortress Monroe and Norfolk to arrest all persons attempting to cross the Potomac from Maryland into Virginia. On April 17, the commander of the Potomac Flotilla, Rear Admiral Samuel P. Lee, ordered gunboats to patrol every landing between Point Lookout and the Aquia Creek. The 16th New York Cavalry, Doherty’s regiment, began systematic sweeps of the river counties, searching barns and woodlots, interrogating ferrymen and fishermen.

These operations were not yet efficient. The federal bureaucracy, for all its resources, struggled to process intelligence from a fractured nation. Descriptions of Booth circulated with maddening variations—some accounts made him tall, others short; some emphasized his theatrical manner, others his ability to disguise it. The $50, 000 reward produced a flood of false sightings: Booth in Baltimore, Booth in Richmond, Booth boarding a ship for Europe. Each report had to be investigated, each investigation consumed men and hours. Yet the net was tightening in ways that Booth, in his thicket, could not fully perceive. Every day of immobility allowed Stanton’s apparatus to grow more effective, to eliminate possible avenues of escape, to compress the geographical space in which a wounded man and his companion might remain concealed.

Booth’s diary entries tracked this compression without acknowledging its cause. Our cause being almost lost, he wrote on April 17, something decisive and great must be done. But what? The great thing had already been done, and it had produced not Confederate revival but universal execration. The Southern newspapers that reached the thicket—those that dared mention the assassination at all—denounced it as a calamity for the defeated South, an act that would justify any severity the victorious North chose to inflict. Booth had imagined himself a Brutus, a liberator; he was learning, too late, that he had become something else entirely, a symbol of lawlessness that united his enemies and estranged his friends.

Herold’s experience of these days is almost entirely absent from the record. He left no diary, gave no extended testimony later, and appears in Booth’s fragmentary notes only as a presence, a helper, a junior partner in an enterprise whose senior partner had lost his bearings. What we know of him suggests a young man of limited imagination who had attached himself to Booth’s charisma and now found himself carried into depths he had not anticipated.

David Edgar Herold, twenty-two years old, had been a pharmacist’s assistant in Washington, familiar with the city’s streets and backrooms, useful for errands and introductions. He had accompanied Booth to Mudd’s farm, had helped secure the weapons at Surratt’s Tavern, had guided the wounded man through the darkened roads to the Zekiah Swamp. But his competence ended at the edge of the cultivated world. In the pine thicket, he was as trapped as Booth, as dependent on Jones’s erratic charity, as subject to the weather and the mosquitoes and the slow terror of waiting.

Jones came to them each day, when he could, with food and newspapers and such river intelligence as he had gathered. The Potomac was the crux of their situation. To reach Virginia, to the Confederate safe houses that Booth still believed awaited him, they must cross that two-mile stretch of water, and the crossing was now fiercely guarded. Union gunboats patrolled the main channel. Informants watched the known landings. The weather, too, conspired against them: a succession of storms in mid-April made the river dangerous for small boats, and Jones, who owned no boat of his own, needed time to arrange passage with the ferrymen and fishermen who still plied the Maryland shore.

This was the mechanism of their entrapment, visible only in retrospect. Booth and Herold had reached the thicket through a chain of individual helpers—Surratt, Mudd, the unnamed men who guided them between—each link forged in the personal loyalty that outlasted organized Confederate resistance. But that loyalty had no institutional depth, no capacity to mobilize resources or control territory. Jones could feed two men in a woodlot; he could not secure them passage across a patrolled river on demand. Every day of delay eroded the network that sustained them, as helpers weighed their risks against a cause that no longer existed and found the balance shifting toward silence.

The War Department telegrams captured this shift in reverse, as growing certainty. On April 18, Stanton wired Major General Henry W. Halleck that the murderer Booth is believed to be in the lower part of Charles County, Maryland, endeavoring to cross the Potomac into Virginia. The belief was accurate; the precision was new. Federal intelligence had begun to coalesce around the right geography, the right description, the right pattern of movement. The cavalry detachments under Doherty and Lieutenant Colonel Everton Conger, the detective Lafayette Baker’s agents, the river patrols—all were converging on the stretch of Maryland shore where Booth and Herold lay hidden.

Booth read of this convergence in the newspapers Jones brought, and his diary entries grew more agitated. I can never repent it, he wrote of the assassination, but the defiance rang hollow against his catalog of grievances: the Confederate leaders who had failed to rise, the Northern tyranny that would now go unchallenged, the personal suffering that consumed his days and nights. He was not, in these entries, a man planning his next move. He was a man explaining himself, constructing an account that might survive him, aware that survival itself was becoming doubtful.

The pine thicket was not a refuge but a trap of a particular kind: one that preserved its victims while their world closed around them. Booth’s leg, untreated beyond Mudd’s splinting, showed no signs of healing. The damp and the enforced immobility promoted infection. Fever came and went. Herold, helpless before this physical decline, could only watch and wait for Jones to bring the means of escape that Jones, for all his good will, could not immediately provide.

On April 19, Lincoln’s funeral train left Washington for Springfield, Illinois, bearing the martyr’s body through a landscape of mourning. Booth learned of this procession from the newspapers, perhaps with some sense of the symmetry: the nation mobilizing its grief while he, its author, lay immobilized in a swamp. The contrast between the public drama and his private suffering seems to have sharpened his sense of historical mission, or historical persecution—the diary entries grow more grandiose, more self-dramatizing, even as their author’s physical condition deteriorated.

I am a hunted man, he wrote, and the statement was true enough, but the hunting was proceeding methodically, through bureaucratic channels that Booth could not see. Stanton’s telegrams on April 19 and 20 directed increased attention to the Potomac crossings, with specific instructions to watch for a man having his left leg injured. The description came from Mudd, filtered through interrogation; it was accurate, and it was narrowing the search. Every ferryman, every fisherman, every farmer with a boat was now a potential informant, every landing a potential trap.

Jones understood these pressures better than his charges. He was not a young man, and he had spent the war navigating exactly this territory of risk and evasion. What he could not control was time. The boat he needed—a small craft capable of slipping across the river at night, undetected by patrols—belonged to others, men whose willingness to help was cooling as the federal rewards and federal penalties came into clearer focus. He visited the thicket on April 19 with no progress to report. He came again on April 20, still empty-handed.

Booth’s diary for these days records desperation masked as resolution. To night I will once more try the river with the intent to cross, he wrote on April 20, but the intent was not matched by capacity. Jones had no boat. The river was high with spring rain, turbulent and cold. The gunboats maintained their patrols. The fugitives were not trying the river; they were waiting for Jones to make it possible, and Jones was struggling against the collapse of the very network that had brought him to them.

This was the crucible the chapter title promises: not a dramatic confrontation but a grinding attrition, physical and psychological, in which the possibilities of action slowly narrowed to a single desperate chance. Booth’s defiance, his theatrical self-conception, his belief in a glorious cause—all were tested against the reality of a pine thicket, a swollen leg, a silent ferryman who could not produce a boat. Herold’s simpler loyalty was tested too, though we have no record of his thoughts. He stayed. That was his testimony.

The War Department’s records for April 20 show the administrative machine reaching full stride. Conger and Doherty, operating under Baker’s direction, had established headquarters at Belle Plain on the Potomac, from which they could coordinate cavalry sweeps and river patrols. The 16th New York Cavalry was reinforced with detectives who knew Booth’s face, his habits, his possible destinations. The reward notices had been translated into Spanish for distribution in Mexico, into French for the Canadian border, into the maritime signal codes for interception at sea. Stanton was not assuming Booth would remain in Maryland. He was assuming nothing, covering possibilities, compressing space.

Yet compression takes time, and time was what the pine thicket provided. Jones, on the evening of April 20, finally secured what he had been seeking: a small boat, hidden in a creek near his farm, available for a night crossing when the river conditions and the patrol schedules aligned. He brought this news to the thicket after dark, finding Booth and Herold in their accustomed places, the wounded man propped against a pine, the younger man keeping watch.

The concrete consequence of this meeting was not immediate escape but its possibility, deferred and conditional. Jones had a boat. He had not yet arranged the precise moment of departure, the landing point in Virginia, the contacts who might receive them on the far shore. The fugitives would spend at least one more night in the thicket, perhaps more, while Jones completed these arrangements. But the stasis was broken. The ferryman had produced his craft. The river, which had been an impassable barrier, became once more a passage—dangerous, patrolled, dependent on darkness and luck, but physically possible in a way it had not been for five days.

Booth’s diary entry for April 20, written after Jones’s departure, struck a note of recovered agency. I have some little faith in the boat, he wrote, and the phrasing suggests how far faith had contracted from the grand Confederate conspiracy he had imagined to this single small vessel, this one man’s reliability. The great thing was now simply to cross a river, to reach the Virginia shore, to disappear into a countryside that might still harbor men willing to hide him. The assassination of Abraham Lincoln had come to this: a broken leg, a borrowed boat, a night crossing planned by a fifty-one-year-old tobacco farmer who was risking his life for strangers because he had given his word in a war that was over.

Thomas Jones had secured a boat for a night crossing, handing off the immediate peril and geographical shift of the fugitives’ next move.
