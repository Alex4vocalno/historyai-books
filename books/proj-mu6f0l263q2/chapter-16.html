Chapter 16

The Boot on the Booth Corpse

On April 26, 1865, the corpses of the executed conspirators would not hang for long; the law required burial, and the Army had prepared the ground. But four years later, in 1869, a work crew digging a cellar near the prison wall would turn up bones that no one could name with certainty, and the War Department would file the report without further inquiry. That future confusion was earned here, in the hours after Boston Corbett’s shot, when the state took possession of John Wilkes Booth’s corpse and began to decide what could be known about it.

—-

Booth died on the porch of Richard Garrett’s farmhouse with the dawn still an hour away, his body cooling in the Virginia air while Union cavalrymen stood in a loose circle and waited for instructions. The bullet had done its work through the night: three cervical vertebrae shattered, the spinal cord partially severed, paralysis spreading downward from the neck while consciousness lingered.

He had asked to be turned on his side. He had asked for water. At the last, according to some accounts, he had asked that his hands be raised to his face so he might see them, though whether this happened depends on which cavalryman was later questioned and whether the interrogator was military or civilian. What is certain is that he stopped breathing around seven o’clock on the morning of April 26, 1865, and that the manhunt which had consumed the War Department for twelve days was suddenly a problem of custody and transport.

Lieutenant Edward Doherty, who had led the detachment of twenty-five men from the 16th New York Cavalry, made the first decisions. The corpse could not remain at the Garrett farm. The barn was still smoldering, the family was frightened, and Confederate guerrillas were rumored to be operating in the area. Doherty ordered a blanket brought from the house. Booth’s body was wrapped in it, tied with rope, and lashed to the side of an old farm wagon for the jolting ride back to Belle Plain on the Potomac. The wagon had no springs. The road was rutted clay. Doherty rode beside it on horseback, and behind him came Lieutenant Colonel Everton Conger, who had directed the encirclement of the barn, and Sergeant Corbett, who had fired the shot. David Herold, captured in the swampy field where he had separated from Booth, traveled under guard in a separate wagon, already talking.

The contrast between the two prisoners, one living and voluble, the other dead and bundled, would frame everything that followed. Herold would survive to testify, to recant, to hang. Booth would enter a chain of custody that transformed him from assassin to evidence, from man to administrative problem. The War Department had not prepared for this transformation. Stanton’s telegrams had envisioned capture, trial, execution. They had not specified who would sign for the body, where it would be stored, or how it would be identified to a public that had already begun to doubt whether Booth had been found at all.

At Belle Plain, the corpse was transferred to the steamer John S. Ide for the run up the Potomac to Washington. The Ide was a side-wheeler, wooden-hulled, typical of the government charter vessels that moved troops and supplies along the river. Doherty placed two sentries over the blanket-wrapped form on the deck, though what danger he anticipated—a rescue attempt, a theft, a desecration—he did not record. The river was calm. The passage took several hours. Booth’s body lay in the open air while the steamer passed the mouth of the Rappahannock and the low shore of St. Mary’s County, where twelve days earlier he had crossed with Herold in a stolen skiff, guided by a Confederate agent named Thomas Jones. Now the current ran against him, and the military bureaucracy waited at the Navy Yard.

—-

The John S. Ide tied up at the Washington Navy Yard on the evening of April 26. Doherty’s report, sent ahead by telegraph, had prepared the commandant for delivery of the remains, but it had not resolved the question of what should be done with them. The War Department wanted identification before announcement. Stanton, who had directed the manhunt from his office on Seventeenth Street, understood that the public would demand proof: the body of the man who had shot Lincoln, displayed and certified, or else the rumors would begin again that Booth had escaped to Mexico, to Canada, to the Confederate trans-Mississippi west where Kirby Smith’s army still held out against surrender.

The ironclad Montauk lay at anchor in the yard, its turret and armored hull an emblem of the new Navy that had blockaded the southern coast and closed the Confederacy’s ports. The vessel was chosen as the site for identification and autopsy because it offered security, because its deck could be cleared of crew, and because its very nature—government property, military space, inaccessible to civilians—served the War Department’s need for controlled procedure. Booth’s body was hoisted aboard and laid on a carpenter’s bench near the after turret. The blanket was removed. The face was exposed to lantern light.

What followed was not a single event but a series of overlapping examinations, each with its own purpose and its own record. The naval surgeon aboard the Montauk, a man named Barnes, conducted the autopsy. He was joined by Army surgeons who had served in the Washington hospitals and who brought their own experience with gunshot wounds to the examination. The bullet’s path was traced: entry through the left side of the neck, trajectory upward and backward, destruction of the third, fourth, and fifth cervical vertebrae, partial severance of the spinal cord. The wound was consistent with a pistol fired at close range, though whether Corbett had been as close as he later claimed—within a few feet—or whether the shot had carried across the barn’s interior remained disputed among the witnesses.

More consequential than the wound itself was the decision to remove the three damaged vertebrae for preservation. Barnes dissected them from the surrounding tissue, wrapped them in paper, and placed them in a small box that would eventually find its way to the Army Medical Museum. Standard procedure for pathological specimens in military medicine: the Civil War had produced thousands of preserved bones, bullets, and tissue samples, catalogued and studied in the hope that surgical knowledge might advance. But applied to the assassin’s corpse, the procedure carried a different weight. The vertebrae were not merely evidence of the fatal shot; they were a physical claim upon Booth’s identity, a fragment of the man that could be displayed, studied, possessed while the remainder of the body was disposed of.

The identification of the corpse proceeded simultaneously with the autopsy. Doherty had brought from the Garrett farm the belongings found on Booth’s person: a diary, a knife, a compass, a pipe, several photographs, and bills of exchange drawn on Confederate banks. These items were catalogued on the Montauk’s deck, their presence intended to establish that the dead man was indeed the actor who had performed at Ford’s Theater and who had shot the President. But objects could be planted, or stolen, or carried by another man. The War Department wanted witnesses who had known Booth in life and could swear to his identity in death.

Dr. John Frederick May, a Washington physician who had treated Booth for a carbuncle on his neck the previous winter, was brought aboard the Montauk on the morning of April 27. May had lanced the swelling in January 1865, leaving a scar that he described as distinctive: a raised, irregular line on the left side of the neck, visible even in the altered condition of the corpse. The identification was macabre in its intimacy. May was asked to locate his own surgical work on a body that had been dead for twenty-four hours, transported by wagon and steamer, and partially dissected by naval surgeons. He found the scar. He signed a statement. The War Department had its medical identification.

Others came to look. Charles Dawson, a clerk at the National Hotel where Booth had kept a room, was escorted aboard to examine the face. William Crowninshield, a former Army officer who had known Booth socially, was brought to view the body. Each was asked to state his certainty, and each, in the official record, supplied the confirmation that Stanton’s subordinates required. Whether their recognition was genuine or prompted by the circumstances—whether they saw what they were expected to see—cannot be determined from the surviving documents. The Montauk was not a courtroom. There was no cross-examination, no alternative hypothesis presented. The witnesses were processed, their statements taken down, their presence noted in the files.

The contrast between the porch at Garrett’s farm and the deck of the Montauk was not merely one of setting. It was a transformation in the meaning of Booth’s body. On the farm he had been a fugitive: wounded, dying, still capable of speech and desire. On the ironclad he was a specimen: stripped, measured, dissected, identified through scars and possessions rather than through recognition by family or friends. The state had taken him apart and put him back together as evidence. The procedure was not hidden; it was documented, photographed, reported to Stanton in detail. But its very openness served a purpose of control. The War Department would decide what could be known about Booth’s death, and it would release that knowledge in forms it could manage.

—-

The autopsy concluded on April 27. The body, now sewn shut and rewrapped, was removed from the Montauk and taken to the Old Arsenal Penitentiary on the Washington waterfront, the same complex where the surviving conspirators were already confined and where the military commission would eventually try them. The choice of storage location was practical—the Arsenal had secure rooms, guards, and proximity to the War Department—but it was also symbolic. Booth would be imprisoned in death as his accomplices were imprisoned in life, held by the same military authority he had tried to strike at.

The specific room assigned to the corpse was a storeroom in the penitentiary’s basement, a space normally used for ammunition and equipment. The body was placed in a pine coffin, unmarked; two sentries were posted at the door and relieved every four hours; and only one key circulated among them—held by the Arsenal commandant—who reported directly to Stanton’s office. This was not a burial but a detention: an extension of manhunt procedures into management of the dead.

The secrecy surrounding the body’s location was immediate and deliberate. Stanton had learned from the mistakes of April 14, when Lincoln’s death was announced before all the conspirators were identified, allowing some to flee. He would not risk a similar leak regarding Booth’s remains. The official announcement of the assassination’s end was made on April 27, but it contained no information about the body’s disposition. The public was told that Booth was dead, that his identity was certain, and that justice would follow. The whereabouts of the corpse were not disclosed.

This secrecy generated its own consequences. Within days, rumors circulated that the body in the Arsenal was not Booth’s, that the assassin had escaped and a substitute had been shot at Garrett’s farm, that the War Department was concealing the truth to cover its own incompetence or complicity. These rumors were not baseless. The identification procedures on the Montauk, however thorough, had excluded the people who knew Booth best: his family, his theatrical colleagues, his intimate friends. Edwin Booth, the assassin’s brother and the most celebrated actor in America, was not asked to view the body. Asia Booth Clarke, his sister, learned of his death from newspapers. The exclusion was deliberate, a judgment by Stanton that family recognition would introduce emotion and uncertainty where the state required certainty and control.

The physical remains posed a problem that outlasted the immediate need for identification. The corpse could not be kept indefinitely in a storeroom. It could not be released to the family for burial, lest the grave become a shrine for Confederate sympathizers. It could not be displayed publicly, lest it become a spectacle that mocked the dignity of the murdered President. Stanton’s solution, implemented in early May, was to move the body to a more permanent secret location within the Arsenal grounds and to inter it without ceremony, without marker, without record that would allow future identification.

The burial was performed at night. A detail of soldiers dug a grave in the penitentiary yard, near the wall where the gallows would later be erected for the execution of the conspirators. The pine coffin was lowered into the earth and covered. The soldiers were sworn to silence. The location was not marked on any map, not entered in any registry, not disclosed to any civilian authority. Stanton’s own papers, examined after his death, contained no reference to the specific site.

This procedure was not merely precaution. It constructed an official narrative by eliminating alternatives—denying Booth martyrdom while asserting state power over his memory through control of his remains.

—-

The objects recovered with Booth’s body followed parallel paths into War Department custody. The diary, a small red leather book with entries dating from the assassination through the flight south, was taken directly to Stanton’s office and examined by the Secretary himself. The knife, the compass, the photographs, and the Confederate currency were catalogued and stored as evidence for the forthcoming trial. The three cervical vertebrae remained at the Army Medical Museum, where they would be displayed to medical students and visiting dignitaries as material proof of the fatal shot.

Each of these items carried a different evidentiary weight. The diary was dangerous because it contained Booth’s own words, his self-justification, his claim to historical significance. Stanton’s decision to withhold it from immediate publication, to treat it as a state secret rather than a public document, extended the control exercised over the body to the control over Booth’s voice. The personal effects established identity and connection to the Confederate underground; they would be produced at trial to demonstrate conspiracy. The vertebrae were the purest form of physical evidence, stripped of personality and intention, available for scientific examination without the complications of interpretation.

This distribution of the assassin’s remains—body in secret grave, bones in museum, words in government safe—represented a comprehensive appropriation. The War Department had not merely captured and killed Booth. It had dismantled him, distributing his physical and textual presence across institutional locations where access could be regulated and meaning controlled. The man who had shot Lincoln in the hope of derailing the federal government had become, in death, an object of that government’s administrative procedures.

The contrast with Lincoln’s own body was stark and, to many contemporaries, disturbing. The President’s corpse had traveled by train through seven states, viewed by hundreds of thousands of citizens in a national funeral that took two weeks. His burial at Springfield was public, marked, attended by the highest officials of the government he had led. The parallel journeys—Lincoln’s corpse north and west in glory, Booth’s south and east in secrecy—mapped the geography of national mourning and national retribution. One body was displayed to confirm the continuity of the state; the other was hidden to prevent the regeneration of the cause it had served.

—-

The locked storeroom at the Old Arsenal, where Booth’s body was first confined, became the template for his subsequent disposition. The secret grave in the penitentiary yard was simply a more permanent version of the same principle: containment without access, possession without acknowledgment. The War Department would maintain this control for four years, until 1869, when a new administration decided that the remains could be released to the Booth family for reburial in Baltimore. Even then, the transfer was conducted at night, without announcement, and the new grave was left unmarked for decades.

The administrative logic governing these decisions belonged less to Stanton personally than to military bureaucracy confronting an unprecedented problem: how to manage a national enemy’s corpse when no established procedure existed—execution display for criminals felt wrong; honorable burial for enemy officers felt wrong; anatomical dissection for unclaimed poor felt wrong—because Booth remained too prominent for obscurity yet too criminal for honor yet too dangerous for science—so suspension became policy: neither fully present nor fully absent—location known only through files Stanton controlled.

This suspension would have consequences that extended far beyond the immediate aftermath of the assassination. The secret burial created the conditions for the rumors that would persist through Reconstruction and beyond: that Booth had escaped, that the body in the Arsenal was another man’s, that the government had concealed the truth. These rumors were not simply errors of information. They were structural products of the War Department’s procedure, the inevitable result of a policy that substituted bureaucratic certainty for public verification. By controlling access to Booth’s body, the state had also controlled the possibility of doubt, and in doing so, it had planted the doubt more deeply than any open procedure could have done.

The locked storeroom at the Old Arsenal, where the pine coffin waited in May 1865, was thus more than a temporary expedient. It was the physical expression of a transformation in the nature of political violence and its aftermath. The assassination of Lincoln had been a theatrical act, performed in public, witnessed by hundreds, immediately reported and universally known. The state’s response was to remove Booth from the theater of public life, to place him in spaces where sight was regulated and knowledge was administered. The manhunt had ended in fire and gunshot on a Virginia farm. The reckoning began in the controlled environment of the Montauk’s deck, and it continued in the silent storage of the corpse, waiting for the trial that would legitimate everything that had been done.
