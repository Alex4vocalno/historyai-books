Chapter 18

The Dauphin Takes Them Aboard

Seen from above, Chapter 18: The Dauphin Takes Them Aboard.

The pressure of these parallel stories, Chase’s boat, Pollard’s boat, Joy’s boat, converges on the reader now, as it converged on Nantucket then. The scattered fragments demand to be recognized as a single catastrophe. But the catastrophe does not end when the survivors are found. It enters a new phase, one that the rescuers did not anticipate and the rescued could not explain.

On the morning of February 23, 1821, the Nantucket whaleship Dauphin sailed in routine pursuit of sperm whales off the coast of Chile, some 2, 400 miles south of the equator. The vessel’s lookout, stationed in the crosstrees seventy feet above the deck, scanned the horizon in the methodical pattern that whalemen practiced: a slow sweep from bow to stern, searching for the telltale spout that meant oil and profit. The Pacific in these latitudes was familiar ground. Captain Zimri Coffin had commanded the Dauphin since its launch in 1815, and his crew of twenty-two knew the seasonal movements of whales, the location of watering stops, the prevailing winds that would carry them home to Nantucket with a full hold. They did not know that they were about to become witnesses to something that had no place in their ledger.

At approximately eleven o’clock, the lookout sighted something unusual. Not a whale spout—a dark object on the water, low and motionless, perhaps three miles to windward. The Dauphin altered course to investigate. As the ship closed the distance, the object resolved into a whaleboat, its sails furled or lost, its hull so low in the water that it seemed to be sinking. Then the deck watch saw figures. Two of them. One appeared to be steering. The other lay collapsed across the thwarts.

Captain Coffin ordered his own whaleboats lowered. Standard procedure for approaching a disabled craft: too many ships had been taken by pirates or run aground on reefs to risk bringing the mother ship close. Six men pulled toward the stranger boat. They carried provisions: a breaker of water, some ship’s bread, a few strips of dried beef. The customary comforts offered to castaways, calculated to sustain without shocking the system. The rescuers expected gratitude, perhaps a story of foundering in a gale, a request for passage to the nearest port. They did not expect what they found.

George Pollard Jr., captain of the whaleship Essex, sat in the stern of his boat holding the tiller. He was twenty-seven years old. Beside him, or rather draped across the center seat in a posture that suggested he had not moved in hours, lay Charles Ramsdell, boatsteerer, aged twenty-one. Both men were naked, their skin blackened by sun and salt to a color that the rescuers initially mistook for the complexions of Pacific islanders. Their eyes were sunk so deep that they seemed to be looking out from skulls. Their limbs had withered to bone and tendon. Pollard’s hands, still gripping the tiller, were claws that could not release their hold without assistance. Ramsdell appeared unconscious, though he would later prove to be alive.

The Dauphin’s men called out. Neither figure responded. The whaleboat itself told part of the story: the thwarts were stained dark, the bottom boards covered with scraps of material that might once have been clothing or sailcloth, the mast stepped but rigging reduced to fragments. In the bow lay bones. Clean bones, some of them cracked open, the marrow extracted. The rescuers recognized the implements of survival: a knife, a piece of iron that might have served as a lever, the remains of a fire that had been made in a wooden box lined with clay. They did not yet understand what they were seeing. The ledger of their understanding had no column for this.

The first mate of the Dauphin reached across and touched Pollard’s shoulder. The captain of the Essex turned his head slowly, as if the movement required enormous effort. His eyes focused with apparent difficulty. When he spoke, his voice was barely audible, a whisper that the rescuers had to lean close to hear. He said: “Essex.” Then, after a pause: “Whale.” Those were the only words he could manage. The effort of speech exhausted him. Ramsdell did not speak at all. He was sucking on something—a piece of bone, the rescuers would later realize—from which he was extracting the last nourishment.

The physical transfer of the survivors to the Dauphin took more than an hour. Pollard could not stand unassisted. When the rescuers tried to lift him from the tiller, his fingers had to be pried loose one by one. Ramsdell was carried like a child, his body so light that the sailor who lifted him expressed shock aloud. The boat itself was searched methodically: the bones collected, the implements examined, the stained clothing bundled for later inspection. Captain Coffin, watching from the deck of the Dauphin through a glass, recorded later that he had seen many distressing sights in twenty years at sea, but none that prepared him for this. The nakedness, the skeletal condition, the apparent indifference of the survivors to their own rescue—these details disturbed him more than he could articulate. He was seeing men who had passed beyond the social contract that made rescue meaningful.

The immediate care of Pollard and Ramsdell followed the protocols that ship’s surgeons had developed for castaways, though those protocols assumed a different scale of deprivation. Fresh water was offered in small quantities—no more than a teacup every fifteen minutes. Ship’s bread, softened in water to a paste, was administered in portions weighing no more than an ounce.

The survivors were washed with fresh water and sea sponges, their blackened skin gradually revealing the sunburn and salt sores beneath. Blankets were wrapped around them, then removed when their bodies proved unable to retain heat, then replaced with layers of dry clothing that had to be cut to fit their wasted frames. Throughout this process, Pollard and Ramsdell displayed a passivity that the Dauphin’s crew found more unsettling than any complaint would have been. They accepted what was given without gratitude, without questions, without the emotional release that the rescuers expected and perhaps needed.

The attempt to feed them revealed the depth of their transformation. Both men vomited the bread almost immediately. Their digestive systems, adapted to weeks of minimal intake and irregular nourishment, rejected the sudden introduction of carbohydrates. The ship’s surgeon tried alternate approaches: fish broth, rice water, small pieces of raw turtle that the Dauphin had taken on its last cruise. Each experiment failed. The survivors’ bodies had become efficient machines for extracting maximum value from minimum input; they could not process abundance. This was the first indication to the rescuers that survival itself had become a medical condition requiring management rather than celebration.

Pollard’s first coherent communication came late that afternoon, after some hours of rest. He asked, specifically and repeatedly, for information about other boats. The Dauphin’s officers understood that he meant other boats from the Essex, though they did not yet know what the Essex was or how many boats it had carried. Pollard wanted to know if the Dauphin had seen a boat commanded by Owen Chase, first mate. He wanted to know about a third boat, commanded by Matthew Joy. The intensity of his questioning, after his earlier silence, suggested that this concern had been the last thread connecting him to consciousness. When the officers told him they had seen no other boats, he relapsed into silence and would not speak again for two days.

The Dauphin’s log for February 23, 1821, records the rescue in the spare formula that whaling captains used for unusual events. The entry gives the position as 33°45’S, 78°00’W—approximately forty miles west of St. Mary’s Island, where the Essex had stopped for provisions fourteen months earlier. The log does not describe the condition of the survivors, the bones in the boat, or the reactions of the crew. These details appear only in later accounts, particularly in statements that Captain Coffin made to other whaling masters and in the narrative that Owen Chase would compile after reaching Nantucket. The official record preserves what could be entered in a ledger. The rest required other forms of testimony.

That night, Pollard and Ramsdell were installed in the Dauphin’s steerage, the small cabin space normally reserved for the captain’s personal storage. The crew donated blankets and clothing. The ship’s cook prepared special provisions that went mostly uneaten. The two survivors lay in their bunks, occasionally attempting to communicate, more often staring at the cabin walls with an intensity that their caretakers found difficult to witness. They were safe. They were fed, clothed, sheltered. They had been returned to the world of ships and schedules, of latitude and longitude, of whales sought and barrels filled. And they could not comprehend it. The vast indifference of the ocean, momentarily punctuated by the Dauphin’s hull, had not prepared them for re-entry.

The Dauphin continued its cruise, because whaling ships did not alter their commercial itineraries for castaways unless those castaways required immediate medical facilities that the ship could not provide. Captain Coffin’s decision to remain on the grounds rather than making immediately for port suggests that he judged Pollard and Ramsdell capable of survival with continued care, or that he was unwilling to sacrifice the season’s profits for their convenience, or that he understood—perhaps from something in Pollard’s fragmentary statements—that other survivors might yet be found. The ship’s log does not record his reasoning. It records only positions, whale sightings, barrels tried out, and the routines of maritime industry that continued around the two figures in the steerage.

Five days later, on February 28, 1821, the Dauphin’s lookout sighted another boat.

This second boat was in better condition than the first, or perhaps its occupants had preserved it better. It carried three men: Owen Chase, first mate of the Essex; Benjamin Lawrence, boatsteerer; and Thomas Nickerson, the cabin boy who had been sixteen years old when the Essex sailed from Nantucket and was now seventeen in a calendar sense and ancient in every other.

They had been rescued once already—by the British merchant ship Indian on February 18, after Lawrence spotted its sail and Chase managed to close the distance despite their weakness. The Indian’s captain, William Crozier, had fed them and carried them for several days before transferring them to the American whaleship Diana, whose master had then transferred them to this whaleboat with provisions and directions toward the Chilean coast. They had been traveling for ten days, eating carefully, managing their strength, following the navigational advice that Chase’s experience made possible. They were, by the standards of Essex survivors, in relatively good condition.

The Dauphin’s approach to Chase’s boat differed from its approach to Pollard’s. The rescuers had been instructed, presumably by Chase himself, in the proper management of castaways. The three men were able to stand, to handle lines, to speak in complete sentences. They asked specific questions: the name of the ship, its home port, whether it had encountered other boats. When Chase learned that Pollard and Ramsdell had been found, his reaction was recorded by one of the Dauphin’s officers as a sudden collapse of bearing, as if the command posture he had maintained for ninety days could finally be released. The confirmation that his captain had survived allowed him to stop.

The transfer of Chase, Lawrence, and Nickerson to the Dauphin took place without the physical drama of Pollard’s rescue. They climbed the ship’s side with assistance, accepted the blankets and food that were offered, and were conducted to the steerage where their former shipmates lay. The meeting between the two groups—Chase and Pollard, Lawrence and Ramsdell, with Nickerson the youngest witness to all of it—occurred without the emotional display that the Dauphin’s crew might have expected. Chase later described it as a scene beyond his power to describe. Nickerson, writing decades afterward, recalled only that Captain Pollard looked at Mr. Chase and said nothing; Mr. Chase looked at him and said nothing; they sat that way for a long time. The silence was not reconciliation but exhaustion of categories: no protocol for what they had done or what they had become.

The Dauphin now carried five survivors of the Essex disaster, representing two of the three boats that had left the sinking ship on November 20, 1820. The third boat, commanded by Second Mate Matthew Joy, had separated from the others in a squall on December 26 and had not been seen since. Its fate—whether it had found land, been rescued by another vessel, or simply vanished into the Pacific’s accounting—remained unknown. The survivors did not speak of it, or of the men who had been in it: Joy himself, who had been ill even before the separation; the boatsteerers Obed Hendricks and Joseph West; the seamen William Bond, Isaiah Sheppard, and the others whose names would be reconstructed later from ship’s records and fading memory. The ledger of survival had not closed. It had only changed its form.

The Dauphin’s log for February 28 records the second rescue with the same formulaic brevity as the first. The entry does not mention that these were the first mate and two of the crew, or that they had already been rescued once, or that their condition was less desperate than that of the captain and Ramsdell. The log’s consistency is itself significant: whether the survivors were skeletal or merely gaunt, whether they arrived in a boat full of bones or in one provisioned by a British merchantman, the entry follows the same pattern. The formal requirements of maritime record-keeping absorbed experiences that the forms were not designed to contain.

The immediate aftermath of the second rescue concentrated the Dauphin’s medical resources in new ways. Chase, despite his relative strength, could not tolerate normal food. His digestive system, like Pollard’s, had adapted to scarcity and rejected abundance. Lawrence and Nickerson, younger and less depleted, were able to keep down small quantities of bread and meat, though they too experienced pain and vomiting when portions exceeded strict limits. The ship’s surgeon developed a protocol: measured amounts at fixed intervals, gradual increase over days, constant monitoring for the signs of refeeding syndrome that he could not name but recognized from trial and error. The survivors became a medical experiment conducted without consent or control group, their bodies teaching lessons that would not be formally described for another century.

Their mental condition presented problems that the surgeon could not address. Chase, when not sleeping, demanded writing materials. He spent hours composing statements that he would not show to anyone, crossing out and rewriting with an intensity that the crew found disturbing.

Pollard lay in his bunk, occasionally asking about the position of the ship, whether they were making progress toward land, whether any other vessels had been sighted. His questions suggested that he had not fully accepted rescue—that some part of him was still navigating, still searching, still responsible for men who were no longer alive.

Ramsdell moved in and out of consciousness, his periods of awareness marked by a single repeated action: he would reach for the bones that were no longer there, feeling the empty space beside his bunk with hands that remembered their work.

Lawrence and Nickerson, the youngest survivors, stayed close together, speaking in low voices that the crew could not overhear, establishing between themselves a private communication that excluded even their rescuers.

Captain Coffin’s reports to other vessels, transmitted when the Dauphin encountered them on the whaling grounds, introduced the Essex disaster into the maritime information network. The whaleship Two Brothers, commanded by George Worth, received the news on March 5 and recorded it in its log. The Hero, commanded by Reuben Joy—brother of the missing Matthew Joy—encountered the Dauphin shortly after and received more detailed information, including the fact that no survivors from the third boat had been found. These reports traveled across the Pacific, reaching other Nantucket ships, British and American consuls, the network of merchants and insurers who would eventually need to account for the loss. Each transmission altered the story, compressing or expanding details according to the teller’s judgment and the listener’s capacity to receive.

On March 5, 1821, the Dauphin spoke the whaleship Constellation, commanded by Thomas Raine, and transferred Chase, Lawrence, and Nickerson to that vessel for the journey to Nantucket. The decision to separate the survivors—Pollard and Ramsdell remaining on the Dauphin, the others going with Raine—reflected practical considerations of speed and routing rather than any judgment about their condition or relationships. Chase protested the separation, according to Nickerson’s later account, insisting that he needed to remain with his captain until both could make formal statements. But maritime logistics overrode this concern. The Constellation was making directly for Nantucket. The Dauphin would continue its cruise, then proceed to Valparaiso with Pollard and Ramsdell, who were still too weak for the longer journey. The survivors were divided by the same commercial calculations that had organized their rescue.

The transfer to the Constellation repeated, in miniature, the experience of rescue. Chase, Lawrence, and Nickerson were required to climb down to a whaleboat, cross to the other ship, and climb again—physical efforts that exhausted them despite their relative improvement. Thomas Raine, receiving them aboard, recorded in his log the same formula that Coffin had used. The Constellation’s crew provided the same gradual care, the same careful feeding, the same observation of behaviors that they could not interpret. The survivors had become a category of maritime experience: men who had been taken up, who required management, whose story would eventually need to be told but who could not yet tell it themselves.

Pollard and Ramsdell remained on the Dauphin for another six weeks, as the ship completed its cruise and made for Valparaiso. Their physical recovery continued slowly, with setbacks that the ship’s surgeon attributed to the depth of their reduction. They were able to take regular food by late March, though in quantities that seemed inadequate for men of their original size. They could walk the deck with assistance, then without it, then for extended periods.

But their interaction with the crew remained minimal, marked by what one observer described as a reserve that seemed not pride but the absence of social faculty. They had spent ninety days in a world where conversation served no purpose, where cooperation was reduced to the distribution of flesh, where the future had collapsed into the present moment of survival. The skills of ordinary human intercourse had atrophied. They would need to be relearned, or replaced by something else.

The Dauphin’s arrival in Valparaiso on April 15, 1821, introduced the survivors to the institutional structures that would manage their story. American consular officials, British merchants, local physicians, and the captains of other whaling ships all demanded information. Pollard, as captain, was expected to provide the formal narrative: the ship’s loss, the conduct of the voyage, the fate of the crew. He attempted this responsibility, producing a statement that the consul forwarded to Nantucket with a covering note about the captain’s evident distress and incapacity. The statement was brief, factual, and silent on the central experience of the ninety days. It mentioned that the crew had been reduced to great extremity without specifying what that extremity had entailed. It noted that some of the crew perished without explaining how. The official record, like the ship’s logs, preserved what could be entered in conventional form.

The physical aftermath of rescue extended for months. Chase, despite his earlier relative strength, suffered a relapse in April that confined him to his house for weeks. Lawrence and Nickerson, younger and more resilient, returned to sea within the year—Lawrence as boatsteerer on another whaleship, Nickerson as ordinary seaman, both resuming the profession that had nearly killed them. Pollard and Ramsdell, arriving from Valparaiso in May on the Two Brothers, were received with the same mixture of private sympathy and official curiosity. The community knew something terrible had happened. It did not yet know, and perhaps did not want to know, the specific form of that terror.

The survivors themselves began, gradually, to construct narratives that could bridge the gap between experience and record. Chase, during his convalescence, worked obsessively on a written account that he would publish later in 1821, a work whose title attempted to name what the narrative itself could only circle around. The book describes the whale attack, the sinking, the voyage in the boats, with increasing specificity as it approaches the events that cannot be directly stated. The drawing of lots. The killing of Owen Coffin. The consumption of the dead. These appear in Chase’s account as facts without interpretation, entries in a ledger that refuses to calculate their meaning.

Nickerson, the youngest survivor, would wait until 1876 to write his own account, dictated to a friend when he was an old man and the other survivors were dead. His narrative contradicts Chase’s on several points of fact: the timing of the separation of the boats, the sequence of deaths, the specific actions of individuals in moments of crisis. These contradictions, which scholars have analyzed without resolving, suggest that the experience of the Essex did not produce a single recoverable truth. Each survivor constructed a story that made survival bearable, that placed their own actions in a framework of necessity rather than choice. The rescue had returned them to a world that required such frameworks. It had not provided them.

The transformation of survivors into witnesses—of physical preservation into narrative obligation—continued through the years following 1821. Pollard, despite his breakdown in Valparaiso, returned to sea in command of the Two Brothers, which wrecked on French Frigate Shoals in 1823 in circumstances that suggested his navigational judgment had never recovered. He finished his career as a night watchman in Nantucket, a figure of local pity and avoidance, the man who had eaten his cousin. Ramsdell, after his recovery, also returned to whaling, then settled into obscurity, his name appearing in no subsequent records until his death decades later. Lawrence and Nickerson lived longer, worked more, left more traces, but never publicly discussed what had happened in the Pacific. The rescue had saved their bodies. The story remained at large.

The specific moment of rescue, when the Dauphin’s boats approached Pollard’s whaleboat on February 23, 1821, contained in miniature this larger failure of integration. The rescuers saw men who had passed beyond the boundaries of recognizable humanity. The survivors saw ships, provisions, the apparatus of rescue, without being able to comprehend their significance. Both parties attempted the gestures of normal maritime encounter—hailing, assistance, transfer, care—and found that the gestures did not communicate what they were intended to convey. The survivors were saved. The experience of salvation remained, for those who could speak of it and those who could not, a wound that would not close.

In the Dauphin’s steerage, in the weeks following the rescue, the physical objects from the whaleboats were stored in a sea chest that Captain Coffin eventually deposited with the ship’s owners in Nantucket. The bones, the knives, the stained clothing—these materials were examined by various officials, then dispersed, then lost. No museum preserved them. No court required their presentation. They became, like the survivors themselves, entries in a ledger that could not balance, debts that could not be discharged. The story of the Essex would be told and retold, each version attempting to provide what the previous versions had lacked: a framework of meaning, a moral accounting, a way of understanding how twenty men became eight, and how those eight became witnesses to something they could not witness.

The rescue was complete. The aftermath had only begun.
