Chapter 10

The Taste of Rain

Owen Chase saw it first, but his mind was still half on the barren rock they had left behind—Henderson Island, that raised atoll without fresh water, where the choice to land or not had been forced upon them under deteriorating conditions. A hand moving beneath a shirt, the quick, furtive motion of a man who believes himself unobserved.

They were three days out from the wreck, November 23, 1820, and the first mate was making his rounds of the whaleboat under his command when he caught the gesture: one of the crew slipping something from the common store into his own clothing, a piece of bread pressed flat against the skin where the salt air would dry it harder but where it would remain his alone. Chase said nothing in that moment. He recorded the act later in the narrative he would write for publication, noting how the discovery affected him—not with anger, he claimed, but with a species of recognition.

The social experiment launched in the immediate aftermath of the sinking, that careful division of men into three boats with equal officers, equal rations, equal prospects, had encountered its first visible crack. The hand beneath the shirt was not merely a theft. It was a vote of no confidence in the future.

The twenty survivors of the Essex had departed the wreck site on November 22, 1820, under the command of Pollard, Chase, and Joy. Their vessels were whaleboats never designed for long voyages, roughly repaired after the chaos of abandonment, leaking constantly. The men had salvaged what they could: bread, water casks, some navigational instruments, a few tools. The provisions were measured, recorded, divided with the precision that Nantucket’s whaling economy demanded. Each man received his portion. Each boat kept its own tally. The system was meant to ensure fairness, to prevent the stronger from taking from the weaker, to maintain the commercial discipline that had governed their lives at sea. What the system could not account for was the gap between measured ration and felt need, between the ledger’s abstract equality and the body’s specific desperation.

In Chase’s boat, the hidden biscuit marked only the most visible symptom. The first mate, twenty-three years old, five feet ten inches, dark-complexioned according to the only physical description that survives of him, had positioned himself as the enforcer of order. Soon after his return to Nantucket, Chase wrote an account of the shipwreck and the attempt to survive, published in 1821 as Narrative of the Most Extraordinary and Distressing Shipwreck of the Whale-Ship Essex. The book presents him as the voice of reason against chaos, the man who maintained discipline when others faltered.

Yet his own account betrays the strain. He notes the hoarding. He notes the accusations. He does not note whether he himself ever felt the temptation to secrete an extra portion, whether his own hands remained entirely clean. The omission is itself a kind of evidence. In the micro-economy of the whaleboat, suspicion was a currency that accumulated interest daily.

Thomas Nickerson, fourteen years old, cabin boy in Captain Pollard’s boat, remembered the atmosphere differently. Where Chase recorded institutional breakdown—the failure of a system—Nickerson recorded personal betrayal. Decades later, when he finally wrote his own account at the prompting of a Nantucket antiquarian, he recalled specific voices, specific grievances. A man accused of taking more than his share of water.

The accusation whispered, then spoken aloud, then shouted across the gunwales. The accused man’s denial, too loud, too insistent, carrying the particular ring of guilt. Nickerson’s memory may have been shaped by time, by the accounts he had read, by the need to make sense of what followed.

But the detail he chooses to preserve—the sip of water disputed, the smallness of the offense magnified by circumstances—suggests an observer who understood early that the crisis would be measured in ounces, in mouthfuls, in the space between one man’s thirst and another’s.

The physical markers of their decline arrived with predictable speed. Saltwater sores opened on skin never designed for constant wetness. The men’s tongues swelled, a condition that would worsen until speech became difficult, then painful, then impossible for some. The bread, already hard when salvaged, grew harder still, requiring effort to break, effort to chew, effort to digest that the men’s bodies could increasingly ill afford.

The water in the casks turned sour, a development Chase noted with the particular anxiety of a man who understood that bad water killed more slowly than no water, but killed nonetheless. They were still in the trade winds, still making what they believed to be easting toward the coast of South America, still operating under the decision made at the wreck site to avoid the western route and its rumored cannibals. The navigation was theoretical, based on dead reckoning from a sunken ship’s last known position, adjusted for winds and currents that the men could feel but not measure with precision. What they could measure was their own diminishment.

The three boats sailed in company, maintaining visual contact through days of empty ocean, signaling by flag or gesture when separation threatened. This proximity was meant to provide mutual aid, shared resources, the comfort of numbers against the vast indifference of the Pacific. In practice, it created a theater of comparison. Each crew could observe the others. Each officer could judge his counterparts. The boats became separate territories of dwindling hope, each guarding its own shrinking store while measuring itself against the others. What one boat consumed could not be consumed by another. What one boat preserved might mean its survival at another’s expense. The mathematics of rescue, if rescue came, would be zero-sum.

Matthew Joy, commanding the third boat, receives less attention in the surviving accounts than Pollard or Chase. He was the second mate, younger than Pollard, less experienced than Chase, positioned awkwardly in the hierarchy of command. His boat appears in the record primarily through its fate: the men who died there, the decisions made there, the slow erosion of its independent existence. But in these first days of the voyage, Joy’s position was theoretically equal, his rations identical, his prospects the same. The parallel structure of the three-boat system required this equality. It could not survive the inequality of events.

The rain came on November 26, or perhaps November 27—the accounts differ on the precise date, agree on the sensation. Chase described it as “a few drops” at first, then more, then enough to wet their shirts and faces, to collect in the canvas they spread, to offer the illusion of abundance. The men opened their mouths. They tilted their heads back. They felt water running down their throats that was not rationed, not measured, not entered in any ledger. For a few minutes, the strict economy of their survival seemed suspended. The ocean itself, which had taken their ship, offered this partial restitution.

The taste of rain is specific. It carries the flavor of the atmosphere it has passed through, the salt of the ocean below, the particular chemistry of cloud and wind. For men whose water casks held liquid turning brackish, whose throats had tightened around measured swallows, the rain must have tasted of possibility, of temporary reprieve, of a nature that might yet be negotiated with rather than merely survived. They collected what they could. They filled what containers remained. They watched the clouds pass and the sun return and felt the familiar dryness settle back over their skin, their clothes, their provisions.

The rain did not solve their problems. It may have worsened them. Wet bread molded faster. Wet clothing chafed more. The temporary relief of thirst created a rebound craving, a body reminded of what it lacked. And the rain created a dangerous precedent, a memory of satisfaction that the rationed system could not replicate. Chase, in his narrative, moves quickly past this episode, as if embarrassed by its sentimentality. Nickerson does not mention it at all in his surviving manuscript. But the rain marked a boundary: before it, the men were sailors conducting an orderly retreat; after it, they were castaways whose survival depended on factors they could not control, gifts they could not predict, a natural world that offered only illusion rather than the sustenance their ledgers required.

The hoarding accelerated. Chase observed more hidden biscuits, more secreted portions, more evidence that the social contract of equal shares had become a fiction maintained for public consumption while private calculation proceeded beneath the surface. The accusations multiplied. Nickerson remembered specific confrontations, specific denials, the particular poison of suspected theft among men who had no privacy and no escape from one another. The boats remained in visual contact, but the space between them had grown psychological as well as physical. Communication became functional rather than friendly. Signals pertained to navigation, to danger, to the bare necessities of coordination. The easy companionship of shipboard life, already strained by the wreck, dissolved into something more primitive: alliance, suspicion, the temporary aggregation of individuals whose interests were aligned only until they diverged.

The officers faced a structural problem they could not acknowledge openly. The ration system assumed a predictable duration of hardship, a calculable rate of consumption, a known destination. None of these conditions obtained. They were sailing for a coast that might be two thousand miles distant or three, through winds that shifted, past currents that their dead reckoning could not precisely locate. The decision to sail east rather than west, made in the first hours after the wreck, had committed them to a longer voyage in exchange for the avoidance of rumored dangers. That decision could not be revisited without admitting error, without consuming the authority that Pollard and his officers required to maintain order. They sailed on, measuring their provisions against an unknown denominator, watching their men watch each other.

The physical symptoms of dehydration advanced in stages that the men could observe and name. The swollen tongue, first. Then the cracked lips that bled when stretched for speech or eating. Then the skin that lost its elasticity, remaining depressed when pinched, signaling the body’s retreat from its surface toward its core. The mental symptoms were harder to catalogue but no less real. Irritability, first. Then the inability to concentrate on tasks requiring sustained attention. Then, for some, a kind of fatalism that manifested as either resignation or recklessness, a willingness to take risks that the situation did not justify. Chase noted these changes without analyzing them, presenting them as moral failures rather than physiological effects. His narrative requires heroes and villains, discipline and its absence. The actual men in the actual boats occupied more ambiguous positions, their choices constrained by bodies they could not fully control.

The bread ran low. The water ran lower. The sourness of the casked liquid became pronounced enough that some men preferred the dry mouth to the nauseated stomach, trading one discomfort for another. The officers reduced the rations officially, matching the reduction to their estimate of remaining distance, their estimate of remaining time. These estimates were guesses, informed by navigation tables and experience but ultimately speculative. The men knew this. They had been sailors long enough to understand that dead reckoning accumulated error, that the Pacific was larger than any chart, that officers could be wrong. The knowledge did not help them. It provided only a vocabulary for grievance, a structure of blame that would grow more elaborate as the voyage continued.

In Pollard’s boat, the cabin boy observed his captain with the particular attention of youth and subordination. George Pollard was twenty-eight years old, on his first command, responsible for men who had seen more of the world than he had, who had survived more than he had yet been tested by.

Nickerson’s later account presents him as humane, reluctant to enforce harsh measures, perhaps too reluctant. This characterization may reflect the cabin boy’s loyalty, his need to believe in the authority he served. It may reflect the truth.

What is certain is that Pollard’s boat experienced the same strains as the others: the same hoarding, the same accusations, the same dissolution of trust beneath the maintenance of formal order. The captain’s “cool and undismayed countenance,” which Chase had praised in the immediate aftermath of the wreck, could not prevent the men beneath him from calculating their individual prospects, from secreting what they could, from preparing for a future in which communal survival might require personal sacrifice they were not yet willing to make.

The nights were hardest. In darkness, the visual contact between boats became theoretical, maintained by lanterns or shouted signals rather than actual observation. Each crew was alone with its own thirst, its own fears, its own whispered conversations. The stars provided navigation but no comfort, their indifferent brightness a reminder of cosmic scale against which human arrangements counted for nothing. The men tried to sleep, huddled in the bottom of boats that shipped water with every wave, waking to bail, to adjust position, to check that companions had not drifted into unconsciousness. Death by exposure, death by drowning, death by the simple failure to wake—these possibilities required constant vigilance, consuming the energy that the reduced rations could not replenish.

Chase, in his narrative, presents himself as maintaining rational calculation throughout this period. He records the positions he estimated, the distances he believed they had covered, the remaining provisions with the precision of a bookkeeper. This presentation serves his larger argument: that the disaster was survivable, that the men who died did so through their own failures rather than through any flaw in the system or its leadership. The account he constructed became, in effect, a legal brief, defending his own conduct and by extension the conduct of his class against the implicit accusation of every shipwreck story, that those who survived did so at the expense of those who did not. But his own details betray him. The hidden biscuit. The accusations over water. The separate territories of dwindling hope. These are not the markers of a functioning community. They are the symptoms of its dissolution.

The rain did not return. The days passed in their measured sequence, each one consuming a fixed portion of their remaining resources, each one bringing them—perhaps—closer to a coast that remained invisible, unmarked, absent from any horizon. The men’s bodies continued their retreat from normal function. Their minds, adapting to circumstances, found new normals: the constant thirst became background rather than foreground, the weakness a condition to be worked around rather than cured. They developed techniques of conservation, of minimal movement, of psychological distance from their own suffering. These adaptations were individually rational and collectively dangerous, permitting survival in the short term while eroding the solidarity that longer survival might require.

The three boats maintained their formation through force of will and the absence of alternatives. Separation meant death for the separated, eventual death for the remainder who would lose the pooling of resources, the distribution of labor, the psychological reinforcement of shared misery. Yet the formation itself became a source of tension. Each boat could observe the others’ condition, compare, judge, suspect. Chase saw hoarding in his own vessel and presumably looked for it in the others. Nickerson, younger and lower in status, heard the accusations in his and wondered about the silence from nearby boats. The parallel structure that was meant to ensure fairness became instead a structure of competitive suffering, each crew locked in its own version of the same ordeal, unable to assist, unwilling to confess need.

By early December, the physical markers of their decline had become unmistakable. The saltwater sores that had opened in the first days had not healed, were not healing, would not heal without fresh water and rest and nutrition that the ocean could not provide. The men’s movements grew slower, more deliberate, calculated to minimize expenditure of energy. Speech diminished, conserving moisture, conserving effort. The boats sailed on, propelled by oars when wind failed, by sails when wind permitted, by the last reserves of strength that discipline and fear could mobilize. The navigation remained theoretical, their position a matter of estimate and hope. They were somewhere in the Pacific, somewhere between the wreck and the coast, somewhere in the space that their ledgers could not fully account for.

The critical insight of this period, visible in retrospect from the accounts both men wrote, is that the ultimate survival decisions—those that would make the Essex famous, that would provide Herman Melville with the ending of Moby-Dick—were made possible by this earlier dissolution. The drawing of lots, the eating of the dead, these acts required not merely desperation but a specific kind of social breakdown, a transformation of crew into collection of individuals whose claims on one another had already been weakened by suspicion, by hoarding, by the private calculation of survival odds. The men who would later cast lots had already, in these first weeks, cast their own lots, made their own private judgments about the value of communal solidarity against individual preservation.

Chase’s account, written for publication, cannot acknowledge this trajectory directly. It must present the later extremities as forced by necessity, as choices made by men who had maintained their humanity until the last possible moment. But the detail he preserves—the hand beneath the shirt, the biscuit pressed against skin—tells a different story, of humanity eroded gradually, choice by choice, in circumstances that made each individual choice comprehensible and each collective consequence inevitable. The ledger mentality that governed Nantucket whaling, reducing all value to quantifiable entries, found its ultimate application in these boats, where the value of a man’s share, a man’s life, a man’s body, could be calculated with increasing precision as the alternatives narrowed.

The rain had offered a false promise. The blank page of the ocean, devoid of human marks, offered only illusions rather than the sustenance the castaways sought from it. They continued to project their hopes onto its surface, to read signs in cloud and wave and bird that might indicate land, might indicate rescue, might indicate that their calculations had been correct and their suffering purposeful. The signs, when they came, were ambiguous. The birds that appeared might mean land nearby or land hundreds of miles distant. The changes in water color might mean shoaling or merely the variation of ocean floor they could not see. Each observation was interpreted through the lens of need, the wish fathering the thought that fathered the action.

By December 5, or thereabouts—the dates in the accounts diverge, converging only on the larger pattern of decline—the men in all three boats had entered a new phase of their ordeal. The initial discipline, the careful rationing, the maintenance of formal equality, had given way to something more provisional, more individual, more desperate. The officers still commanded, still navigated, still maintained the shell of order. But the substance had changed. The men they commanded had become, in effect, separate sovereignties, each guarding his own diminishing resources, each calculating his own prospects, each preparing for the moment when collective arrangements might fail entirely and individual survival become the only possible goal.

This was the condition in which they approached Henderson Island, though they did not know it was Henderson Island, did not know that any island existed in this stretch of ocean, had no reason to believe that their navigation had brought them within reach of land. The island appeared as a darkening of the horizon, a variation in cloud formation, a suspicion that hardened into hope and then into certainty as the boats drew closer. For men who had sailed three thousand miles of open water, who had watched their provisions dwindle and their companions weaken, who had felt the social bonds of their company dissolve into suspicion and private hoarding, the sight of land—any land, even barren rock—offered a termination to the ordeal that the open ocean could not provide.

They did not know what they would find. They knew only that the blank page had finally offered something they could read, something that might break the logic of their diminishing returns, something that made the following movement feel necessary without any promise of what that movement would bring.
