Chapter 6

The Sound of the Ship Breaking (20 November 1820, Afternoon)

The hammering had just stopped. Owen Chase and the men in his boat were alongside the Essex’s starboard bow, plugging with oakum and pine splinters a leak where a whale had grazed the hull an hour earlier—routine carpentry, the ordinary maintenance of a wooden ship—when the sound reached them. Not the blow of a fluke or the splash of a sounding whale, but a solid, structural crack, the noise of timber failing under impossible stress. It traveled through the water and up the hull planks and into the soles of their boots before they saw anything.

Then the sight. Chase, who would soon after his return to Nantucket write an account of this disaster in Narrative of the Most Extraordinary and Distressing Shipwreck, recorded that he was struck with astonishment. The whale—eighty-five feet of scarred sperm whale—had turned and come back. It rammed the ship again, head-on, with a velocity that seemed to defy the animal’s bulk. The Essex shuddered along her entire length. Oak timbers splintered. The men in Chase’s boat felt the concussion through their hands where they gripped the gunwales.

Within seconds, the geometry of their world inverted. The Essex had been a containing vessel, a walled fortress of Nantucket industry floating two thousand nautical miles from the nearest coast; now she became a wound. Water entered the bow with a rush that drowned out the shouts of the lookouts. The ship settled forward, her bowsprit dipping toward the Pacific, increasing pressure on the damaged planking and accelerating the intake. Chase saw the waterline climb the forechains, darkening the white paint that had been scrubbed that same morning.

A mile distant, George Pollard Jr., the twenty-eight-year-old captain, was returning in his whaleboat with a dead whale alongside when he saw the Essex cant forward at an angle that no carpenter’s brace could correct. The sun stood high in the November sky—early afternoon, perhaps one o’clock—but the horizon had tilted. Pollard would later return to sea in early 1822 to captain the Two Brothers, only to wreck her on the French Frigate Shoals northwest of the Sandwich Islands, sealing his reputation among Nantucket owners as a Jonah whom no vessel could safely carry; but in this moment he was simply a commander watching his command die. He ordered his men to cast loose from their prize and pull for the ship with every ounce of strength.

Matthew Joy, the second mate, was closer, returning in the third boat from a different quarter of the hunting ground. He saw the masts sway against the sky like reeds in a current before he understood that the current was the ship herself, listing to starboard as tons of Pacific water displaced the air in her hold. His boat crew pulled harder, but the distance seemed to stretch.

Men were already in the water. Some had been below decks when the second impact came—cook and cabin boy and green hands—and they surfaced gasping through the copper taste of bilge mixed with salt. Thomas Nickerson, the fourteen-year-old cabin boy who would decades later write his own account of this afternoon with details colored by memory’s longer perspective, remembered the cold shock of the water and the sudden disappearance of familiar walls. In Chase’s immediate record, there is only action: men swimming, debris floating, the ship’s boats cutting through chop toward the wreckage.

The Essex settled by the bow with a terrible deliberation. Her forecastle went under, then her foremast step, then the tryworks where hours ago they had rendered blubber into gold-standard oil. The sound of the ship breaking was not a single cataclysmic explosion but a sequence of structural failures: stocks parting, beams splintering against beams, the hull working itself apart against the pressure of the deep. The ocean, which had been a blank page upon which the Essex had presumed to write her course in oil and profit, now violently reasserted itself by erasing those marks entirely. No oil slick marked her grave yet; only bubbles and the white turbulence of displaced water.

Then came the scramble. As the ship’s waist submerged to the level of the hatches, men swarmed up from below with whatever their hands could seize. They threw bread casks onto the deck—hard bread in wooden barrels that would float if secured—and lowered them over the side into Chase’s boat and Joy’s boat as these pulled alongside. Water casks followed, heavy and rolling, some splitting their hoops against the gunwales in the haste. The carpenter’s tools went over: adzes and augers that might be needed to repair the whaleboats when ocean swell worked their seams loose. Navigation instruments—the sextant, the chronometer, the charts that marked the blank spaces of the Pacific—were passed hand to hand across the sloping deck.

Chase’s narrative lists what they saved with the precision of a manifest: bread, water, navigational tools, some line and harpoons, a few clothes. Nickerson, recalling the same hour years later, emphasized what they lost—the security of walls, the familiar creak of timbers that had been home since leaving Nantucket in August 1819. The difference between the two accounts is instructive: one records assets, the other absence.

By two o’clock in the afternoon, the Essex had settled so deep that only her stern remained above water, the quarterdeck rail awash, the tiller useless. The three whaleboats—Chase’s, Pollard’s, and Joy’s—circled like flies around a dying animal, collecting the last floating casks and men who clung to spars. Twenty men were accounted for, spread now among three open boats designed for pursuit of whales, not ocean passages. Each boat was twenty-five feet long, built of cedar and oak, capable of holding perhaps seven or eight men in moderate weather for short durations. They were about to be asked to carry twenty men across thousands of miles of open sea.

The Ledger Mentality manifested immediately in the inventory. Chase and Pollard did not pray first; they counted. Hard bread: perhaps sixty-five pounds per boat if equally divided, enough for perhaps two weeks at full ration. Water: two casks per boat, twenty gallons each, barely a mouthful per man per day for a long voyage. No fresh meat save what little remained from breakfast. No shelter from sun or squall but canvas sails now being unstepped from their tryworks and rerigged as weather protection. The value of every item was calculated not in dollars but in days of life: one bread cask equaled three days; one water cask equaled five; a hatchet equaled the ability to cut firewood on whatever desolate shore they might find.

The Blank Page lay before them in all directions. To the west, perhaps twelve hundred miles distant, lay the Marquesas and Society Islands—green archipelagos of breadfruit and fresh water that could sustain them indefinitely. But these islands were marked on Nantucket charts as dangerous blanks filled with imagined cannibals. To the east lay the coast of South America—three thousand miles of open ocean away, but downwind and downcurrent in the trade winds, and populated by Christians who spoke Spanish and traded with whalers. To the south lay nothing but Antarctic ice and screaming westerlies.

Pollard’s boat had joined the circle now, and the three craft wallowed in the swell alongside the dying ship. The captain’s face showed the shock of loss, but his voice remained steady. Nickerson would later recall that “the cool and undismayed countenance of the captain soon brought all to their sober senses.” Pollard looked at his first mate and his second mate, looked at the empty horizon where no sail showed, and made the calculation that would cost twelve men their lives. They would not sail west toward the imagined cannibals of the Marquesas. They would sail east-southeast, toward South America, into the teeth of the prevailing winds if necessary, but toward the known.

The decision was not debated for hours; it emerged from the geography of fear and the instinctive preservation of maritime discipline. West meant uncertain natives; east meant Chile and Peru, ports where American whalers put in for repairs, places where ledger entries could be reconciled and debts paid. They would sail south first to pick up the variable winds below the trades, then beat eastward along the latitude of desolation.

The water rising through the Essex’s hold did not discriminate between cargo and survival gear. As the forecastle disappeared beneath the chop, men who had been stowing barrels of sperm oil only hours earlier now found themselves diving into flooded compartments, groping through black water for items that might mean the difference between rescue and oblivion. The ship’s own architecture worked against them: her holds were designed to keep whale oil contained, not to facilitate rapid evacuation.

Men had to swim down through hatches already nearly submerged, fighting the buoyancy of their own bodies to reach the lower tiers where spare canvas and additional water casks had been stored. They surfaced gasping, blood streaming from splinter wounds incurred against the combings of hatchways, clutching hatchets or spare oarlocks or the heavy iron try-pots that had rendered their fortune into liquid gold. Some items sank immediately—the dense oak of spare spars, the pig iron ballast that stabilized the ship’s ride, the very try-pots that had seemed so precious when hauled up from the deep now dragged men down if held too long.

Other items refused to sink at all: the casks of hard bread, sealed with oakum and iron hoops, bobbed like corks; bolts of canvas floated in spreading white rafts; personal sea chests containing letters from Nantucket wives and the accumulated small treasures of two years’ voyage drifted away on the current, their owners unable to secure them while struggling to keep their own heads above water.

The shock of immersion in sixty-degree Pacific water produced a physiological crisis that maritime discipline could only partially mask. Within minutes of entering the water, the men’s bodies began diverting blood from extremities to core organs, inducing a clumsiness that made simple tasks—climbing over a gunwale, securing a line—require exhausting concentration. Thomas Nickerson, fourteen years old and still growing, suffered disproportionately; his slight frame lacked the insulating fat of seasoned whalemen, and his teeth chattered so violently he could not at first answer when Pollard called the roll.

Yet the hierarchy of the ship held even as the ship herself dissolved beneath them. Pollard directed the distribution of men into boats with the same authority he had used to assign whaleboat crews during the hunt: Chase in the first mate’s boat kept his usual crew plus two additional hands pulled from the water; Joy took his regular complement plus the ship’s steward and the cooper; Pollard’s own boat absorbed the remainder, including Nickerson and the green hands who had never before sat on an oar for more than a few hours.

This was not kindness but calculation—experienced oarsmen needed to be distributed evenly so that no single boat would lag behind when they began their passage. The injured—those who had broken ribs against falling spars or gashed scalps on splintered decking—were placed amidships where they would not interfere with the rowing, their wounds bound with strips torn from shirts that would themselves be needed for warmth before nightfall.

To call these whaleboats lifeboats misrepresents their design and their danger. Each vessel measured roughly twenty-five feet in length with a beam of less than six feet, built light and double-ended so that they could be spun quickly after a harpooned whale. They possessed no keel to speak of, only a shallow centerboard that provided minimal lateral resistance to leeway; they were meant to be pulled, not sailed, and certainly not sailed against prevailing winds for thousands of miles. Now they were asked to serve as arks.

The mathematics of their inadequacy became apparent as soon as the men attempted to stow provisions. Twenty gallons of water in a wooden cask occupied cubic feet that had previously held nothing but air and the occasional lunch of hardtack; when divided among three boats, the casks filled the footwells where men’s feet needed to rest during rowing, forcing them to sit with knees drawn up to their chins or to straddle the barrels like awkward jockeys.

The bread casks, similarly distributed, raised the center of gravity dangerously high, making each boat tender and prone to rolling gunwale-under in any swell larger than a moderate chop. And there was no shelter: the sails, stepped on short masts designed for pursuit of whales in calm waters, provided shade from a tropical sun but would offer scant protection when the Pacific winter storms arrived, as they surely would before any landfall could be made.

The decision to steer east-southeast rather than west toward the Marquesas emerged from a cartography of anxiety that Nantucket navigators had been engraving for decades. The Society Islands and Marquesas, though only twelve hundred miles distant—less than half the distance to South America—existed in the Nantucket imagination as places where civilization’s contract dissolved. Recent accounts from missionaries and escaped sailors had filtered back to Massachusetts with tales of cannibalism, tribal warfare, and shipwrecked crews being kept as slaves or food. These were economic liabilities as much as stories; to fetch up on those shores meant, in the ledger mentality of the Quaker owners, a total loss of the ship, the men’s labor value, and the social order that made whaling profitable.

South America, by contrast, offered ports—Valparaiso, Lima, Coquimbo—where American consuls resided, where bills of exchange could be drawn against Nantucket firms, where the men could book passage home on merchant vessels and where Pollard could explain the loss to underwriters who understood that a whale might sink a ship but who would never understand a captain who chose to maroon his crew among “savages.” The oil they had lost—nearly two thousand barrels rendered from two years’ hunting—already represented a financial catastrophe; to compound it by vanishing into the western islands would brand Pollard and Chase as men who had chosen dishonor over death. They sailed toward Chile because it was accountable.

Pollard announced the course with the same voice he had used to order the cutting-in of whales: flat, certain, brooking no discussion. Chase, who would later write that west would have been the wiser choice, said nothing in dissent; maritime law and nautical custom vested absolute authority in the captain during crisis, and Chase’s own boat was already shipping water through seams loosened by the day’s heat. Joy, the second mate, merely nodded, his face gray with exhaustion.

There was no vote, no council of war, no weighing of alternatives against the fading light. Instead, there was immediate action: the distribution of the compasses—one per boat, with Chase keeping the ship’s best instrument in the first mate’s boat—the allocation of the tiller arms, the division of the few available blankets. They checked their ankle lines—the leather thongs that connected them to the boats should they capsize—and tested the fit of thole pins in the oarlocks, discovering that some had warped in the tropical sun and would need to be shaved down with the salvaged carpenter’s tools before they could row effectively. Each boat received one firearm, a musket with powder and ball kept dry in a sealskin bag, not for hunting but for signaling should they sight a sail on the horizon.

As the Essex’s stern rose higher against the afternoon sky—her counter lifting until her rudder post cleared the water, creating a temporary island of oak and copper where men had lately walked—the final accounting took place. Pollard did not consult charts; he held in his mind the great circle routes, the trade wind belts, the known positions of islands that appeared on no chart he possessed. He looked at Chase, and Chase looked back, and between them passed the silent acknowledgment that they were about to attempt something unprecedented in the annals of Nantucket whaling: an open-boat passage of three thousand miles against the weather gage.

The men spoke little; the cold and the shock had compressed their world to the immediate circumference of wood and water. They were no longer a ship’s company but a drifting archipelago of castaways, bound together only by line of sight and the unspoken agreement that to separate was to die alone. The ship’s bell, which had marked the watches for two years, tolled once as a wave struck the hanging clapper, then fell silent as the belfry submerged, leaving only the sound of water against wood and the breathing of twenty men who had just begun to understand the measure of their isolation.

As the sun declined toward the western horizon—now unobstructed by any mast or sail—the men transferred the last casks and themselves into the boats. The Essex’s stern lifted once, settled deeper by the head, and disappeared. She left no mark on the surface, no spreading slick of whale oil or scatter of lumber to indicate where she had gone down. The waves washed over the spot where she had been, as indifferent as they had been to the whale that destroyed her.

The three boats rigged their canvas—those small triangular sails meant for chasing cetaceans in calm waters, not for beating against trade winds—and pointed their prows away from the empty circle of sea that had swallowed their world. The decision was made; the boats turned their bows east-southeast, leaving the wreckage behind, committing to a voyage across thousands of miles of open ocean.
