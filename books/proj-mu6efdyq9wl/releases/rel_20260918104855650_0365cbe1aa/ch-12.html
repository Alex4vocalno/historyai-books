Chapter 12

The Parting of the Boats

The men who sailed from Henderson Island on December 27, 1820, were not the same men who had watched the Essex sink thirty-seven days before. Owen Chase, first mate of the Essex, measured this transformation in pounds of flesh and ounces of bread. In his account written after his return to Nantucket, he would remember the calculations precisely—the daily ration reduced to half a biscuit, then to none at all—because precision was what remained when hope dissolved. The twenty survivors had become seventeen. Three men had chosen to stay behind on the island: William Wright, Seth Weeks, and Thomas Chappel, the English sailor who had first spotted the green flash of land. They would be found alive eight months later by a whaler, having survived on fish, birds, and the stubborn refusal to believe that rescue was impossible.

The boats that pushed off from Henderson’s reef that morning carried a different cargo: the certainty that staying meant death, and the equally certain knowledge that leaving probably meant the same. Chase’s boat held six men. Captain George Pollard’s boat held five. The third boat, commanded by second mate Matthew Joy, held six more. They had been sailing in company for a day, keeping visual contact, when the first fracture appeared.

Chase records the reason without sentiment: Pollard’s boat was slower. The captain’s whaleboat had taken on water during the landing at Henderson, its seams loosened by the pounding surf, and despite constant bailing it sat lower in the water than the others. In a flat calm, this meant little. But the southeast trade wind, which had carried them toward the island, now pressed them onward with a steady insistence that turned every difference in speed into a widening gap. Chase and Joy could shorten sail and wait, burning precious time and morale. Or they could sail on, abandoning their captain to the pace of his leaking craft.

The choice was presented as mutual agreement. In the language of survival, this meant it was presented at all.

—-

The boats had sailed together through Christmas Day, a date noted by Thomas Nickerson, the cabin boy in Chase’s boat, with the particular accuracy of the very young recording their first experience of extremity. Nickerson, born in Harwich, Massachusetts, orphaned before his second birthday and raised by his grandfather on Nantucket, had turned fourteen during the voyage out. He would live to write his own account of the disaster, decades after Chase’s narrative had fixed the public story, and his later recollections would sometimes diverge from the first mate’s in matters of sequence and emphasis. But on the question of the parting, both men agreed on the essential fact: the three boats separated because they could not agree on how to stay together.

The specific point of disagreement was directional. Pollard, consulting his Bowditch and the surviving navigational instruments, argued for continuing east-southeast, holding as close to the latitude of the South American coast as the variable winds permitted. This course offered the shortest distance to inhabited land—Chile or Peru, some 2, 300 nautical miles distant—but it crossed the widest stretch of open ocean, where no ships were expected to patrol and where the Humboldt Current would set them northward, away from their target, for hundreds of miles before they felt the coastal counter-currents.

Chase favored a more northerly route. His reasoning was meteorological: the trade winds blew most reliably from the southeast, and by beating northward they could reach the waters where the Pacific whaling fleet worked the Offshore Ground, that newly discovered longitude where sperm whales gathered in numbers that justified the three-thousand-mile sail from Peru. Ships would be there. Rescue, or at least provisions, was a calculable probability.

Joy, in the third boat, seems to have offered no independent position. Second mates learned early that initiative above their station was dangerous. His boat would follow whichever course the other two agreed upon, or it would follow neither, as circumstances determined.

What neither Chase nor Nickerson records, and what Pollard never spoke of in the surviving fragments of his testimony, is the conversation itself. We know it happened because the separation happened. We know it involved practical considerations of speed and course because these are the considerations that survive in both narratives. What we do not know—what the sources refuse to yield—is whether anyone proposed what must have been obvious: that three boats sailing in company had better odds than three boats sailing alone, and that the temporary inconvenience of a slower companion was a price worth paying for the permanent advantage of numbers.

The silence suggests its own answer. By December 27, or perhaps December 28—the exact date is disputed between Chase’s immediate account and Nickerson’s later recollection—the social fabric of the Essex company had worn through. The men who had shared a ship, shared a sinking, shared an island, could no longer share a course. The ledger mentality that had governed Nantucket whaling—every barrel of oil accounted for, every man-hour converted to profit or loss—had been translated into a more desperate arithmetic. Each boat now represented a separate account. The question was which account would show a surplus of survivors.

—-

The distribution of provisions was conducted with formal precision. Each boat received its share of the remaining hard bread, the salvaged Galapagos tortoise meat, the water in the breakers. The amounts were small enough to be counted: Chase records that his boat left Henderson with sixty days’ provisions at half allowance, which meant thirty days at full ration, which meant, in the reality they all understood, perhaps fifteen days before the first man weakened beyond recovery.

The water was the critical constraint. Henderson’s brackish spring, exposed only at half tide, had yielded enough to fill their casks but not enough to sustain confidence. They had supplemented it with the blood of birds, with the moisture in fish, with the disciplined refusal to imagine thirst. Now each boat carried its own finite quantity, measurable in days of survival, unshareable across the widening water.

Chase describes the final moments of contact without dialogue. “We parted company,” he writes, and the passive construction is itself significant—no single hand on the tiller, no decisive voice. The boats simply diverged, as objects diverge in current and wind, their sails adjusted to different angles, their hulls settling into different rhythms. Pollard’s boat fell behind almost immediately, its leaking seams demanding constant attention. Joy’s boat held with Chase’s for a time, then followed its own assessment of the wind.

The last words across the water went unrecorded. Perhaps there were none. Perhaps the captains exchanged formal good wishes, or practical information about rendezvous points that both knew were fictions. Perhaps the men simply watched each other diminish, each boat becoming first a hull, then a sail, then a speck, then a vacancy in the blue.

Nickerson, writing fifty years later, would remember the silence more vividly than any speech. The boy who had watched Nantucket disappear over the horizon in August 1819 now watched his last connection to that world dissolve into the Pacific’s empty compass. The blank page of the ocean—devoid of marks, refusing annotation—had swallowed the community that might have sustained him.

—-

The immediate aftermath of the separation was, for Chase’s boat, a strange relief. Six men in a whaleboat designed for six to eight: this was manageable space. The discipline of sailing, of adjusting sail to wind and wave, of maintaining the rhythm of bailing and steering and watching, filled the hours that might otherwise have been devoted to dread. Chase was in command. His authority, challenged implicitly by Pollard’s continued captaincy of the overall enterprise, now operated without check. The boat was his. The men were his responsibility. The outcome would be his to record.

Benjamin Lawrence, a Nantucket boatsteerer, pulled an oar. Thomas Nickerson tended the sail. The other three—a black sailor from New York; another Nantucketer; and the fourth man, whose name the records give variously—occupied the remaining positions in the rotation. They were young, mostly. Whaling crews were young. The work killed the old or drove them ashore.

For two days they sailed north-northeast, holding to Chase’s chosen course. The wind remained steady from the southeast, allowing them to lay close-hauled without excessive leeway. On the third day, the wind shifted to the east, then died altogether, leaving them becalmed on a glassy swell that reflected the sun with an intensity that burned skin and parched throats. The water in their breakers, already rationed, became the subject of hourly calculation. Chase reduced the allowance again: a gill per man per day, then half a gill, then “a small taste,” as he would later write, the phrase itself a measure of how far quantification had collapsed into approximation.

The calm lasted four days. When the wind returned, it came from the north, directly opposed to their desired course, forcing them to beat to windward with agonizing slowness or run southward toward waters they had already traversed. Chase chose to beat, tacking repeatedly through the night, the men hauling on the sheets until their hands blistered and cracked. Each tack gained them perhaps a mile of northing. Each tack consumed energy that could not be replaced.

By January 10, 1821, they had made perhaps 300 miles from Henderson. They had sighted no ships. The tortoise meat was gone. The hard bread was reduced to weevilly crumbs. The water was a daily anxiety that consumed all other thought.

—-

Pollard’s boat, sailing more slowly, may have covered half that distance. The captain’s narrative of these weeks—preserved only in fragments, in the testimony he gave to the captains who rescued him, in the secondhand reports that reached Nantucket before his own return—suggests a different rhythm of despair. Five men in a leaking boat: this was closer to the margin of survival. The bailing never stopped. The sail area had to be reduced to prevent strain on the weakened hull, which reduced speed, which extended the voyage, which increased the daily consumption of provisions relative to distance gained.

Charles Ramsdale, the only Nantucket mate to sail with Pollard, shared the captain’s boat. So did a black sailor; Owen Coffin, the captain’s eighteen-year-old cousin; and another of the Essex’s crew. The fifth man’s identity remains uncertain in the records—a confusion that testifies to how quickly individual identities dissolved in the common experience of suffering.

Pollard’s later testimony, filtered through the reports of the captains who found him, emphasized his continued commitment to the eastward course. He had rejected Chase’s northern strategy. He would make his landfall on South America or die in the attempt. The determination reads, in the surviving fragments, as stubbornness or as loyalty to his original calculation—perhaps both. What it does not read as is flexibility. The captain who had followed his first mate’s advice at Henderson, agreeing to the landing that had cost them three men and three weeks, would not follow it again. The separation had freed him to pursue his own judgment. The cost of that freedom would become apparent only gradually, then all at once.

—-

Joy’s boat, the third vessel in the dispersed fleet, presents the greatest documentary silence. Matthew Joy died on January 10 or 11, 1821—the exact date is uncertain, and the manner of his death is variously reported as starvation, dehydration, or a combination of both with the general collapse that the survivors called “giving up.” His boat continued under the command of a boatsteerer who had served under Joy’s authority without ever quite possessing it.

The six men in Joy’s boat included the new commander; a sailor with the same surname as Chase’s boatsteerer but no relation; and two black sailors. The confusion of names in the records is significant. These were not men who kept their own records. Their experience survives only in references from the other boats, in the testimony of the two survivors eventually rescued from their craft, and in the negative space of Chase’s narrative, which mentions them only when their paths crossed his own.

What is known is that Joy’s boat attempted to follow Chase’s northern course, then diverged southeastward when the winds made northing impossible. What is known is that the new commander, in command, made decisions that his men sometimes questioned and sometimes accepted. What is known is that this boat, like the others, reduced its rations, counted its water, and watched its crew weaken day by day until the weakest could no longer pull an oar or adjust a sail.

The separation had created three experiments in survival, each with its own variables of leadership, morale, and luck. The results would not be comparable, because the conditions were never identical. But the men in each boat knew, or suspected, that their fates were being measured against the others. This was the cruelty of the parting: it transformed companions into competitors, each boat’s continued existence a reproach to the others’ failures, each death a datum in a calculation no one had chosen to make.

—-

The mechanics of isolation operated with physical immediacy. A whaleboat in the open Pacific was visible from another whaleboat at a distance of perhaps five miles in clear weather, less in haze or swell. Once separated by more than this distance, the boats existed in different worlds, connected only by the abstract knowledge of shared origin and approximate position. The men in Chase’s boat could calculate that Pollard’s boat was somewhere to their south, Joy’s somewhere to their east or west depending on the wind, but calculation was not contact. They could not see the other sails. They could not signal. They could not know whether their companions were alive or dead, progressing or foundering, until some future convergence that both hoped for and dreaded—hoped for because rescue meant rescue, dreaded because it would force comparison of their respective conditions.

The blank page of the ocean amplified this isolation. There were no landmarks to share, no common reference points. Each boat navigated by its own sun shots, its own dead reckoning, its own interpretation of the wind’s shifts. The coordinates in their logbooks—Chase kept one, Pollard presumably kept one, Joy’s was lost with his boat—would show diverging tracks, lines of position that spread across the chart like the branches of a tree that would never reconverge.

On January 15, 1821, Chase recorded a position of approximately 28° south, 112° west. This was still more than 1, 800 miles from the South American coast, more than 1, 000 miles from the nearest whaling grounds. The calculation was approximate. The sextant was a delicate instrument, difficult to use from a small boat in a seaway, and the chronometer—if they still had one functioning—required steady conditions for its comparison of local and Greenwich time. Chase’s navigation was good enough to reach land eventually. Whether it was good enough to reach land before his men died was a question that could not be answered by instruments.

—-

The psychological fracture that accompanied the physical separation deepened with time. In the first days, the men in each boat must have looked back frequently, scanning the horizon for the sails they had lost. By the second week, this looking became ritual rather than expectation. By the third, it ceased entirely. The other boats had become hypothetical, existing in memory and in the future tense of possible rescue, but not in the present reality of wind and water and the immediate demands of survival.

Chase’s narrative, written in the immediate aftermath of his rescue, preserves this progression without quite acknowledging it. He describes the daily routine—the sail adjustments, the bailing, the distribution of food and water—with the precision of a man who has learned that precision is the only defense against chaos. He notes the first death in his boat—the black sailor from New York, on January 20—without extended commentary, as if the fact were itself sufficient. He notes the second—the other sailor, three days later—with the same restraint. The narrative is not cold, but it is controlled, and the control is itself a response to experiences that could not be fully expressed without threatening the writer’s composure.

What Chase does not describe, because he could not have witnessed it directly, is the parallel collapse in the other boats. Pollard’s boat, slower and more crowded relative to its seaworthiness, must have experienced similar losses at similar intervals. Joy’s boat, under its new commander’s uncertain authority, may have fared worse. The absence of information became, for each group, a kind of negative knowledge: they knew that others were suffering, and they knew that they could do nothing to relieve that suffering, and they knew that their own suffering would receive the same indifferent attention from the same impossible distance.

—-

The separation was never formally acknowledged as final. In their various accounts, the survivors would maintain the fiction that the boats had simply lost contact, that they would rejoin if conditions permitted, that the dispersion was temporary and tactical rather than permanent and structural. This fiction served psychological needs that the men could not afford to examine too closely. To admit that the parting was irrevocable was to admit that they had abandoned their companions to independent fates, that the community of the Essex had dissolved into three isolated struggles for existence in which the success of one group offered no assistance to the others.

The fiction was sustainable only while the boats remained at sea. Once rescue began—once Chase’s boat encountered a ship on February 18, 1821, and Pollard’s boat another vessel four days later—the truth became unavoidable. The men who had sailed from Henderson Island together had experienced radically different ordeals. Chase’s boat, with three survivors from six, had lost half its company. Pollard’s boat, with two survivors from five, had lost more than half. Joy’s boat, with two survivors from six, had lost two-thirds, including its commander and its replacement commander, who died before rescue reached them.

The differential mortality was not random. It reflected the specific conditions of each boat: its initial state of repair, its leadership, its course, its luck with wind and current. But it also reflected something less tangible: the quality of decision-making under conditions of extremity, the capacity to maintain morale and discipline as bodies weakened, the willingness to make choices that previous experience had not prepared anyone to face.

The parting of the boats had been presented as a practical response to divergent sailing characteristics. Its consequence was to transform a collective disaster into three separate trials, each with its own verdict. The survivors who reached Chile in February 1821 carried with them not only the memory of their own suffering but the knowledge of alternatives that had been tried and had failed. They had become, without choosing the role, witnesses to the cost of separation.

—-

The physical separation of the boats into three isolated entities creates the narrative necessity of tracing each group’s distinct and harrowing path forward. Chase’s account will carry us through the northern route, the encounter with rescue, the salvation that seems like deliverance until it reveals its own complications. Pollard’s testimony, fragmentary but essential, will document the eastern course and its terrible conclusion. The third boat, Joy’s and then its successor’s command, survives only in the reports of those who found its survivors and in the negative space of what was not recorded. Each path leads to the same oceanic wilderness, but the wilderness receives each group differently, measuring their resources against its own indifferent scale. The ledger is still open. The entries continue.
