Chapter 28

The Sea Has No Need of Memory

Nantucket, May 1822 — thirty years before the inquiry that would close the counting-houses, and the town on its sandbank still keeps its perfect geometry: cobbled streets radiating toward the harbor, counting-houses along Main Street, shipyards where new whaleships rise on the stocks. The ledgers record prosperity, vessels departing weekly for the Pacific grounds, their cargoes insured, their crews signed from distant shores. The system functions, absorbing the loss of one ship, one season’s profit, one captain’s reputation. What it cannot absorb is the knowledge that walks among its streets.

George Pollard, thirty-two years old, master of the Essex, survivor of ninety days in an open boat, has come home to this. He has been examined by the American consul at Valparaíso, found blameless in the wreck of his vessel, cleared of any suspicion of misconduct. The formal record is clean. Yet when he applies for new commands, something in the interviews goes wrong. Owners study his face too long. They ask after his health, his sleep, whether he has recovered his appetite. The questions are kind. The appointments do not follow.

In early 1822, the ship registers record a change. Pollard is named master of the Two Brothers, a whaleship of 480 tons, newly fitted and bound for the Pacific. The appointment represents either charity or faith, depending on which owner one asks. The Two Brothers sails from Nantucket on November 26, 1821, according to the port clearance records, carrying a crew that includes two survivors from the Essex: Thomas Nickerson, now seventeen, signed as ordinary seaman, and Charles Ramsdell, twenty-one, boatsteerer. Neither boy has found work ashore. The sea is the only employment they know.

The voyage proceeds normally through its first year. The Two Brothers rounds Cape Horn, hunts the Offshore Ground, fills her hold with oil. In February 1823, the ship is working near the French Frigate Shoals, northwest of the Sandwich Islands, searching for another whaleship, the Martha, which has separated from the fleet in a gale. Pollard navigates by dead reckoning through heavy weather. The latitude he calculates places him clear of danger. The reef that tears the bottom out of the Two Brothers proves him wrong.

The crew takes to two whaleboats and a broken pinnace. This time there is no ninety-day drift. A passing vessel, the Martha herself, rescues them after hours, not months. But the pattern is established. Pollard has lost a second ship. The formal inquiry, held in Honolulu, again clears him of blame—navigational error in difficult conditions, a common enough verdict. What the inquiry cannot measure is the look in the owners’ eyes when he returns to Nantucket in the fall of 1823, traveling as a passenger on a merchant ship, arriving without command, without prospects, without the confidence of the men who finance voyages.

The ship registers tell the rest. Pollard never commands another vessel. By 1825, he is working as a night watchman for the town, walking the dark streets with a lantern and a staff, checking the locks of counting-houses that once considered his credit good. The job is not dishonorable. Many retired seamen take such work. But Pollard is not retired. He is thirty-five years old. The ledgers that record his rounds—all secure at the Pacific Bank, oil warehouses checked at two in the morning—are kept in the same offices where his earlier voyages were planned, where the profits from the Essex were projected before the whale struck.

He continues in this employment for decades. Census records list him as watchman in 1830, 1840, 1850. He marries, loses his wife, marries again. The town grows around him, its wealth increasing even as its whaling supremacy faces competition from New Bedford and the new steamships. Pollard attends the reunions of Essex survivors when they occur, speaks when called upon, tells the story of the whale with the flatness of a man reciting a lesson rather than a man remembering his life. Visitors who seek him out find a courteous, guarded man who answers questions about the cannibalism with a single sentence that functions as a wall. He says they did what they had to do, and says nothing more.

He dies in 1870, aged seventy-eight, having outlived most of his crew, most of his judges, most of the system that made and unmade him. The Nantucket Inquirer notes his passing in a paragraph that mentions his remarkable escapes at sea without specifying what he escaped, or what pursued him afterward.

—-

Owen Chase’s path diverges, then converges toward the same silence. Where Pollard’s career collapses publicly, Chase’s continues with apparent success. He commands whaleships through the 1820s and 1830s, accumulating the record of a man who has conquered his experience. The ship registers show him master of the Winslow in 1825, the Charles Carroll in 1830, the Napoleon in 1836. His narratives of these voyages, published in the Nantucket newspapers, describe the ordinary hazards of the fishery: storms, mutinous crews, the mechanical difficulties of processing whales at sea. They do not mention the Essex. They do not need to. Everyone knows.

Chase’s first account of the disaster, published in 1821 with the help of a ghostwriter, had established the template: the whale as agent of destruction, the crew as victims of extraordinary misfortune, the captain as steady amid chaos. The narrative sold well, was pirated in England, translated into French and German. It made Chase briefly famous, the authority on whale attacks, consulted by insurance underwriters and naval architects. The fame did not last. What persisted was the private knowledge that the narrative had not captured, the experience that continued in his body after the book was closed.

Later in his life, according to his son’s later recollection, Chase began hiding food in the attic of his house on Orange Street. The behavior had no practical purpose. The house was provisioned, the town surrounded by farms and fishing boats. He would take bread, salt pork, ship’s biscuit, and secrete them in the rafters, in boxes, behind the chimney. When questioned, he offered no explanation. The action was not rational enough to be called fear, not systematic enough to be called madness. It was simply the persistence of a need that the Pacific had installed in him, the recognition that stores could be exhausted, that rescue was uncertain, that the body must prepare against the return of hunger.

He suffered headaches of increasing severity through his forties and fifties. The medical records, such as they are, suggest neuralgia, possibly related to the head injuries he sustained in the whaleboat—Chase records in his narrative that he was struck by a flailing oar during the initial attack, was stunned for some moments. The headaches came with visions, his second wife reported, though she would not specify their content. Nightmares, certainly. The whale appearing not as the instrument of Providence he described in print, but as something more personal, more patient, more knowing.

Chase married four times. The first marriage, to Peggy Gardner in 1820, produced a daughter and ended with her death in 1828. The second, to Eliza Worth, ended in divorce in 1841—a rare and socially damaging event on Nantucket, suggesting behavior that could not be accommodated within the ordinary forms of marital difficulty. The third marriage, to Susan Coffin, lasted two years before her death. The fourth, to another Susan Coffin, a cousin of the third, began two months after the divorce was finalized and continued until his death. He never sailed again after 1840, though he was only fifty-one. The official reason was declining health. The unofficial reason, preserved in family memory, was that he had begun weeping without warning on the deck of the Napoleon, watching the boats lowered for a whale, unable to explain why.

He died in 1869, a year before Pollard, having outlived his literary reputation. The Narrative of the Most Extraordinary and Distressing Shipwreck remained in print in various forms, but increasingly as a curiosity, a specimen of maritime disaster rather than a living text. Chase’s annotations to his personal copy, preserved by his descendants, show a man arguing with his own published words, adding marginal notes that contradict the main text, questioning his own memory, finally abandoning the project of revision entirely. The last pages are uncut.

—-

The other survivors scatter across the record with less density, their fates harder to trace but following similar patterns of reduction and constraint. Benjamin Lawrence, the twenty-year-old boatsteerer who had drawn the lot that spared him on Pollard’s boat, returned to sea and continued whaling through the 1820s. He appears in the crew lists of the Loper in 1823, the Columbus in 1826, the Peru in 1829. By 1835, he is listed as a cooper—a barrel-maker, shore-based, working in the oil-processing yards rather than the ships. The job used his skills, paid adequately, removed him from the grounds where he had eaten human flesh. He died in 1844, aged forty-three, cause unrecorded.

Charles Ramsdell, who had drawn the short straw with Owen Coffin and been spared by the latter’s sacrifice, made one more voyage with Pollard on the Two Brothers, survived its wreck, and signed on with the Martha for her return to Nantucket. After 1823, he disappears from the ship registers for five years. When he reappears in 1828, it is as a crewman on a coastal schooner running between Nantucket and New York, never again venturing beyond sight of land. He died in 1836, aged thirty-four, in the Nantucket poorhouse—a destination that required not merely poverty but the failure of family and community support, the final exhaustion of social credit.

Obed Hendricks, the boatsteerer who had commanded the third whaleboat and been separated from the others during the drift, never returned to Nantucket. Rescued by the Australian whaleship Surry in December 1820, he made his way to Sydney, then to London, working as a common sailor on merchant vessels. The British consular records show him applying for relief in 1824, destitute and unwell, claiming American citizenship but unable to prove it. What became of him afterward is unknown. The Australian archives contain no further record.

Thomas Chappel, the seaman who had chosen to remain on Henderson Island rather than continue in the boats, was rescued with Hendricks by the Surry. Unlike Hendricks, he returned to Nantucket, where he worked as a laborer and occasional preacher, having experienced during his solitary weeks on the island what he described as a religious conversion. His accounts of the Essex, when he gave them, emphasized divine providence rather than human agency, the whale as instrument of God’s will rather than natural hazard. He died in 1834, aged thirty-one, leaving no written narrative.

William Bond, the only African American survivor, disappears from the record entirely after the consular inquiry in Valparaíso. The crew lists of subsequent Nantucket voyages contain no name that matches his age and description. He may have changed his name, left the island, died unrecorded. The systematic omission of Black seamen from the archives of this period makes certainty impossible. What is certain is that he received no share of the profits from Chase’s narrative, no recognition in the subsequent literature, no place in the mythology that formed around the disaster.

Only Thomas Nickerson, the youngest survivor, achieved a form of resolution. Thomas Gibson Nickerson, born March 20, 1805, died February 7, 1883, served as cabin boy on the Essex at fourteen, survived the three months at sea, the cannibalism, the rescue. He continued whaling through the 1820s and 1830s, rising to the rank of boatsteerer, then mate, finally captain of the whaleship Mechanic in 1837. The ship registers show him commanding vessels through the 1840s, accumulating the ordinary record of a successful whaling master. He married, raised children, built a house on Pleasant Street.

In 1876, at the age of seventy-one, he wrote out his own account of the Essex at the request of a former shipmate’s daughter, producing a narrative that contradicted Chase’s on crucial points: the behavior of the whale before the attack, the distribution of provisions in the boats, the circumstances of Owen Coffin’s death. Nickerson’s manuscript remained unpublished in his lifetime. He died in 1883, aged seventy-seven, having seen Melville’s novel appear, decline into obscurity, and begin its slow resurrection in critical esteem. The manuscript passed to his family, was stored in an attic, was discovered and published only in 1984. Its existence changes nothing in the official record. It adds only the testimony of a man who was fourteen when the whale struck, who grew old in the shadow of another man’s narrative, who finally spoke and was not heard.

—-

The system that produced the Essex disaster continued to function. Nantucket’s whaling fleet reached its peak in the 1840s, with more than eighty vessels employed in the Pacific grounds. The technology improved: the try-works grew more efficient, the harpoons more lethal, the ships larger and more heavily armed against the very creatures they hunted. The profits increased. The insurance markets in London and Boston developed more sophisticated instruments for spreading risk across multiple voyages, multiple vessels, multiple seasons. What could not be insured against—the psychological cost to the men who returned, the erosion of judgment in captains who had seen too much, the slow accumulation of fatal decisions—remained off the books.

The whale that sank the Essex was never identified. Sperm whales continued to attack ships occasionally through the nineteenth century, though never with the same decisive result. The behavior was rare enough to remain anomalous, common enough to sustain the fear that Chase’s narrative had installed in the maritime imagination. Melville, researching Moby-Dick in 1850, read Chase’s account in a New York bookstore, annotated it heavily, borrowed its central image of the vengeful whale, its ending of the sole survivor, its meditation on the whiteness of the animal and the blankness of the oceanic wilderness. What he added was meaning: the whale as symbol, the hunt as metaphysics, the destruction as fate rather than accident.

The meaning was not available to the men who lived it. Pollard walking his rounds with a lantern, Chase hiding food in his attic, Lawrence making barrels on the waterfront, Ramsdell dying in the poorhouse—they experienced the disaster as pure event, without the consolation of symbolism. The ocean that had nearly killed them remained indifferent to their survival. It had no memory of the ninety days, the drawing of lots, the eating of the dead. It received their testimony as it receives all testimony: as surface disturbance, soon smoothed.

This is the final reckoning. The system that sent the Essex into the Pacific, that calculated the profits from oil and the risks of navigation, that cleared Pollard of blame and promoted Chase to new commands, that published narratives and forgot the men who wrote them—this system absorbed the disaster and continued. The wilderness that produced the whale, that furnished the blank space into which the boats drifted, that killed the men who could not adapt to its terms—this wilderness remained unchanged. Only the individuals in the middle, the men who carried the system’s orders into the wilderness and brought the wilderness back in their bodies, were permanently altered.

The collision was not, finally, between the whale and the ship. It was between two incompatible ways of measuring value: the ledger, which reduces everything to quantifiable profit and loss, and the ocean, which recognizes no currency but survival. The men who lived were those who learned, temporarily, to translate between these systems: who understood that the stores could be exhausted, that the rescue was uncertain, that the body could be made into food. This knowledge could not be carried back into ordinary life. It produced behaviors that looked like madness—hiding food, weeping at whales, walking dark streets with a lantern—and were in fact the persistence of a truth that the ordinary world had no place for.

The sea has no need of memory. It does not keep the records that Nantucket kept, does not publish the narratives, does not preserve the testimony of survivors. What remains is the silence that settled over the eight men who returned, the unanswerable gap between what they knew and what they could say, the gradual reduction of their lives to the dimensions that their community could accommodate. Pollard’s lantern, sweeping across the locked doors of counting-houses. Chase’s attic, stocked against a hunger that would not come. Nickerson’s manuscript, unread for a century. These are the true monuments to the Essex: not the narratives that tried to explain the disaster, but the behaviors that proved it could never be explained, only survived.

The system and the wilderness continue their collision in other forms, other vessels, other bodies. The whale remains in the water, patient, watching, requiring no justification for its violence. The ledger remains in the counting-house, balanced, requiring no acknowledgment of what it cannot account for. Between them, the human being continues to translate, to suffer, to be reduced. The sea has no need of memory. It has, instead, the power to make memory unnecessary, to absorb all testimony into its blank and continuing presence, to demonstrate that the final reckoning is not a verdict but a silence.
