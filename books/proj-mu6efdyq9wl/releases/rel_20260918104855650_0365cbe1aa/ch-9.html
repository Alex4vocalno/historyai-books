Chapter 9

The Historical Content

They were twenty men in three open boats, provisioned for perhaps a month, facing a voyage of three months or more. The first morning after the wreck, November 21, 1820, opened with a ritual that would become their religion: the distribution of water. Captain George Pollard Jr. held the tin measure, a gill per man, while Owen Chase watched the level with the same attention he had once given to the try-pots of the Essex. The water was warm, slightly brackish, drawn from casks that had floated free of the wreck. Each man received his portion in a tin cup or scooped shell. They drank it slowly, or all at once, according to temperament. Then the measure was wiped dry and stowed, and the day began.

The boats lay in formation on a sea that gave no sign of the violence beneath. The Pacific at this latitude, some 1, 800 nautical miles west of South America and 1, 200 south of the Galápagos, presented a surface of such mechanical regularity that the men could measure progress only by sun and stars. Chase, in his 1821 narrative, would reconstruct their positions with the precision of a man who spent his remaining life trying to fix the coordinates of disaster. Nickerson, the cabin boy, would remember the same days differently: not the numbers but the sensations, the particular quality of thirst, the way the boards seemed to shrink and swell with heat. The discrepancy between their accounts begins here, in these first ten days, when hope and routine still made memory selective.

Pollard’s boat, the largest, carried the captain, two Black sailors, and six others including sixteen-year-old Nickerson. Chase commanded the second boat with six men. Second Mate Matthew Joy took the third with five. They had rigged small sails from salvaged canvas and set course south-southeast, angling toward islands that existed on their charts at approximately 24° south, 128° west: Ducie’s Island, Henderson Island, the Duke of Clarence’s Island. Believed to lie some 1, 200 miles from the wreck site, the calculation was reasonable by 1820 standards. It was also wrong.

The first days brought no immediate crisis. The men established watches, two hours on and two off, to bail constant seepage through the seams and scan the horizon. They ate their bread—one pound per man daily, hard biscuit resistant to moisture, durable enough to survive immersion. Men with sound teeth broke off pieces and worked them with their jaws; those with dental problems softened fragments in their water ration before swallowing. The ledger was open. The first entries had been made.

The physical regime established itself with speed that suggests how thoroughly the Nantucket system had prepared these men for reduced circumstances. Whaling voyages were themselves exercises in rationed existence: months of short provisions, cramped quarters, deferred gratification. What the Essex survivors faced was not qualitatively different from ordinary experience, only quantitatively extended. They knew how to measure. They knew how to wait. The problem was that the measurements were wrong and the waiting had no certain terminus.

By November 25, the fourth day, first symptoms appeared. The water supply, calculated at six gallons per boat, was already showing effects of evaporation and imprecise salvage. Chase noted that men began suffering from excessive thirst, a condition he distinguished from ordinary desire by its persistence and physical marks: cracked lips, swollen tongues, white coating on the roof of the mouth. The sun at this latitude, approaching the southern summer solstice, rose early and set late, its angle sufficiently direct to strike through thin canvas clothing. They had no adequate hats. Some tied cloth strips around their foreheads; others simply suffered. Salt spray, thrown up by the boat’s motion, dried on skin and left residue that itched and burned. At night, when temperature dropped, the same spray chilled them. The rhythm was established: heat, thirst, brief shivering sleep, then heat again.

The boats remained in sight of each other, a discipline requiring constant adjustment of sail and steering. Pollard had determined they would not separate, and this decision, maintained through these first days, represented the last effective exercise of his command. The three boats, each about twenty-five feet long, were visible to each other at distances up to several miles in clear weather. They communicated by signal flags improvised from clothing, or by waving oars.

When squalls approached—and the Pacific trade wind zone generates them with mechanical regularity—they closed formation to avoid losing each other in rain and reduced visibility. The squalls brought fresh water, caught in sails spread for the purpose, but also danger of swamping. Each boat carried a single iron pump of the type used for clearing whaleboats in pursuit, but the volume a squall could deliver exceeded the pump’s capacity. Men bailed with hands, with cups, with any container available.

Nickerson, in the account he wrote decades later, remembered these days as marked by curious combination of routine and apprehension. The young cabin boy had been trained to obedience, to acceptance of authority as natural condition. What he observed in Pollard’s boat was maintenance of formal structures: prayers morning and evening, captain’s consultation with officers, distribution of rations at appointed hours. The prayers were particularly notable. Pollard was a Quaker, as were most Nantucket men, and the form of worship required no priest, no liturgy, only gathered silence of believers and spoken testimony of any moved to speak. In the whaleboat, this translated to periods of quiet broken by extemporaneous prayer. The content, as Nickerson recalled, focused on deliverance, on hope of finding the islands, on preservation of the company. They did not, at this stage, address possibility of failure.

Chase’s boat presented different atmosphere. The first mate was not a man of formal religious practice; his narrative makes no mention of prayer in these first days. What he recorded instead was constant labor of navigation: taking sights with the sextant salvaged from the Essex, calculation of position, comparison of actual drift with expected set of currents. The discrepancy worried him. The boats were making less southing than charts predicted, and eastward progress was complicated by westward set he could not fully account for. Navigation became psychological as much as technical. Every calculation offered possibility of confirmation, of islands appearing on horizon at computed moment. Every failed prediction extended the ordeal.

The food held. Hard biscuit was consumed by established ration, supplemented by single gallon of Galápagos tortoise meat and small quantities of salvaged vegetables. But the water was another matter. By November 28, the seventh day, Chase recorded that men were suffering excessive thirst and hunger, a pairing indicating beginning of physiological crisis. The human body, deprived of water, cannot process food efficiently; kidneys require fluid to eliminate nitrogenous wastes of protein metabolism. The men were eating their pound of bread and deriving less benefit than caloric content suggested. Their bodies were beginning to consume themselves, breaking down tissue to obtain water bound in cells.

The skin changes came first. Saltwater exposure, prolonged beyond occasional immersion of normal whaling, produced sores at points of friction: buttocks, where men sat on hard thwarts; hands, where they gripped oars and rigging; feet, where wet leather boots rubbed against softened skin. The sores wept, then crusted, then cracked open again with movement. There was no way to keep them dry. The boats were wet through, bilge water rising and falling with sea’s motion, and men’s clothing—woolen trousers, cotton shirts, oilskin jackets—never dried completely. At night they huddled together for warmth, sharing body heat in configuration requiring continual adjustment as men on outside edges became cold and demanded rotation toward center.

The search for islands continued. Each morning at dawn, each evening at sunset, men scanned southern horizon for dark line indicating land. The charts showed Ducie’s Island as low coral atoll, Henderson as raised limestone platform with sufficient elevation to be visible at twenty miles or more. On clear days, with trade wind blowing steadily from southeast, visibility extended to theoretical limit. They saw nothing. The horizon remained unbroken circle of sea and sky.

The failure to find islands disoriented them more than it disappointed them. The men of the Essex were navigators by profession, trained in measurement of angles and calculation of position. The discrepancy between their dead reckoning and actual location of land represented fundamental challenge to their understanding of world. Chase, in his narrative, would later defend accuracy of his navigation, suggesting islands were incorrectly charted. He was correct. Henderson Island actually lies at 24°22′S, 128°19′W, some distance from positions given in standard Pacific sailing directions of the period. But this knowledge, unavailable to survivors, could not console them. Each day of empty horizon eroded confidence that was their most necessary resource.

By November 30, the ninth day, the water situation had become critical. Chase recorded that they were reducing ration to very small quantity, though he did not specify exact measure. The original calculation of six gallons per boat, intended to last thirty days at a gill per man daily, was proving optimistic. Evaporation, spillage, and impossibility of measuring precisely in moving boat had reduced actual supply. The men were now receiving less than physiological minimum required to maintain kidney function. Their urine, when they produced it, was dark and scant. Their faces, observed by Nickerson, began showing characteristic hollows of dehydration: sunken cheeks, prominent orbital bones, tightening of skin across forehead.

The boats remained together, but strain of coordination was beginning to tell. Joy’s boat, with only five men, was lighter and faster than others; it tended to draw ahead in favorable winds, requiring constant attention to avoid separation. Pollard’s boat, overloaded with nine men, sat lower in water and shipped more spray. Different rates of consumption—some men drank water quickly, others saved it—created inequalities that formal ration system could not fully address. Nickerson remembered complaints about fairness of distribution. Chase noted silences indicating resentment unexpressed.

On December 1, the tenth day, they altered course to east. The decision was Pollard’s, made in consultation with Chase and Joy; it abandoned southern search for islands that had been missed or misplaced on charts. To continue south meant higher latitudes with colder temperatures and stronger winds without certainty of land; turning east committed them to longer voyage toward coast of South America some 2, 000 miles distant but along established tracks where other ships might sail or wreckage might drift ashore if they did not survive to tell it themselves—a rational choice made desperate by circumstance.

The physical symptoms accelerated with course change. The men had been conditioned to expect land; its continued absence produced psychological reaction that Chase described as despondency and Nickerson as kind of stupor. The distinction matters. Chase observed his men from perspective of command, noting their utility, their capacity for work. Nickerson experienced condition from within, recording sensation of time slowing, of boat’s motion becoming hypnotic, of impulse to surrender to heat and thirst rather than resist them. Both descriptions point to same phenomenon: beginning of neurological changes produced by dehydration and caloric restriction, brain’s adaptation to circumstances it cannot alter.

The ration system continued, but its formality now masked fundamental inadequacy. They were consuming stores at calculated rate, and calculation was proving insufficient. The bread, which had seemed substantial provision, now appeared as what it was: dry mass requiring water to digest, water that was itself rationed. The tortoise meat, consumed in small pieces over several days, offered protein without hydration. The vegetables, already limited, were gone. The men were entering state of negative water balance that no discipline could correct, only delay.

The nights brought temporary relief from sun but introduced other hardships. Temperature drop, while welcome to overheated bodies, produced shivering that consumed energy and prevented restorative sleep. The men lay in bottom of boats, packed together for warmth, their wet clothing drawing heat from skin. The sky, clear of clouds in trade wind zone, displayed stars of intensity unknown in northern hemisphere, unfamiliar constellations that offered navigation but no comfort. Some men slept; others lay awake, listening to water against hull, calculating remaining days, remembering warmth of Essex’s forecastle and certainty of Nantucket.

December 2 and 3 brought no change in horizon. The boats sailed on, making perhaps fifty miles daily in favorable winds, less when wind failed and they were forced to row. The oars, heavy pine shafts salvaged from wreck, required effort that weakened men could not sustain for long. Chase recorded that they rowed as much as their strength permitted, a phrase admitting its own limitation. The strength was diminishing. Calorie deficit, combined with dehydration, was producing characteristic weakness of starvation: reduced cardiac output, lowered blood pressure, impaired thermoregulation. Men who had pulled oars through hours of whale pursuit now found thirty minutes of rowing exhausting.

The interpersonal tensions, suppressed in first days by discipline of survival, began to surface. Nickerson remembered argument about distribution of small quantity of fish that one boat had managed to catch by handline. The fish, few pounds of bonito or similar species, represented supplement to ration, but its division raised questions of equity: did boat that caught it keep it, or did it enter common store? The resolution, as Nickerson recalled, favored common store, but discussion left residue. Chase recorded that some men began to show signs of discontent, a formulation suggesting both fact and his reluctance to specify it.

The navigational problem continued to preoccupy Chase. He was attempting to calculate position by dead reckoning—course and distance traveled—while correcting for observed drift. The trade wind, steady from southeast, pushed them northwest when they wished to go east; they had to sail close-hauled, tacking back and forth across wind, making progress in zigzag fashion. Each tack consumed time and distance. The sextant sights, taken at noon when sun reached maximum altitude, gave them latitude with reasonable accuracy, but longitude required comparison of local time with known standard, calculation dependent on chronometer that had gone down with the Essex. They were navigating by estimation, and estimation was subject to cumulative error.

By December 4, the fourteenth day, water ration had been reduced to level Chase described as barely sufficient to sustain life. The men were now receiving, by his account, approximately half a gill daily—two ounces, a small cup. Physiological effects were visible to all: skin had taken on grayish tinge, eyes were sunken and bright with fever, movements had become slow and deliberate, conserving energy. Speech was reduced to necessary communication; stories and songs that had occupied first days were abandoned. The boats sailed in silence, each man enclosed in his own consciousness of thirst.

The decision to keep boats together, maintained through these difficult days, represented Pollard’s last successful exercise of authority. Formation sailing required continual attention—adjustment of sail, signals, closing of distance when visibility threatened separation—but it preserved possibility of mutual assistance and psychological benefit of numbers. To be alone in open boat, as they all knew, was to confront Pacific without consolation of shared witness. They remained together, though strain of coordination added to their exhaustion.

December 5 brought change in weather. The steady trade wind, their constant companion since wreck, began to falter and shift. Squalls approached from unpredictable quarters, requiring shortening of sail and bailing that accompanied heavy rain. The rain itself was welcome—they caught what they could in sails and canvas—but disruption of eastward progress was costly. They were now approximately 800 miles from South American coast, by Chase’s calculation, with supplies for perhaps fifteen days at current ration. The arithmetic was becoming inescapable.

The first death occurred in this period, though sources disagree about date and circumstances. The Essex’s steward, a Black sailor in Pollard’s boat, was observed to be failing faster than others. Nickerson remembered him as fine, hearty man who had begun to waste visibly after first week. Chase recorded no specific death in these early days, but his narrative was written for publication and may have suppressed details reflecting badly on survivors’ conduct. The discrepancy—whether this man died early or survived longer—is significant not for its resolution but for what it reveals: uncertainty that already surrounded most basic facts of their experience, way memory and interest shaped recollection even among witnesses.

What is certain is that by December 6, the sixteenth day, condition of company had deteriorated to point making continued progress doubtful. The men were rowing less, sailing more conservatively, accepting drift of current when wind failed. Their bodies were consuming muscle tissue to maintain essential functions; protein deficiency was producing edema, swelling of extremities that made oars harder to grip. Mental changes were equally marked: irritability alternating with lethargy, difficulty concentrating, obsessive thoughts of water and food. The ledger mentality, Nantucket training in measurement and control, was encountering resistance of biology.

The search for islands had not been formally abandoned. They continued scanning horizon, particularly at dawn and sunset when atmospheric conditions might reveal land below visible horizon. But expectation was fading. The charts had failed them; navigation that should have brought them to fresh water and food had delivered only more ocean. The institutional confidence of Nantucket whaling—belief that world was knowable, measurable, subject to calculation—was being tested against Pacific that refused to conform to its representations.

On December 7, the seventeenth day, they sighted birds. The birds were boobies or tropicbirds, species that range far from land but do not venture beyond few hundred miles of coast or island. Their appearance produced moment of renewed hope: perhaps land was near, perhaps charts had been correct after all, perhaps navigation had erred in direction of safety. The men watched birds through day, noting direction of flight, attempting to follow. The birds led them nowhere. They were fishing, not traveling to roost, and their random movements offered no guidance. The hope lasted a day, then subsided into previous despondency.

The water situation was now critical in all three boats. Chase recorded that they were reduced to last extremity, a phrase admitting multiple meanings: extremity of thirst, of hunger, of hope. The men were drinking their urine, a measure providing minimal relief and psychological degradation. Some refused; others accepted necessity. The practice was not discussed in formal record, but Nickerson’s later account mentioned it as turning point, moment when boundary between civilized conduct and desperate survival was first crossed. They had not yet eaten human flesh, but they had begun to consume what body rejected, to turn waste back into sustenance.

The boats continued east, making what progress they could in variable winds. Formation had loosened; they were now often out of sight of each other for hours at a time, reconnecting by prearranged signals at sunset. Discipline of togetherness was yielding to practical necessity of individual survival. Each boat, each group of men, was becoming isolated unit, responsible for its own navigation, its own rations, its own fate. Pollard’s authority, never fully established after wreck, was eroding in face of geographical separation.

December 8 and 9 brought no improvement. The men were now entering third week of their ordeal, duration exceeding any previous experience of open-boat survival in Pacific whaling. Their bodies had adapted to calorie deficit by reducing metabolic rate, by prioritizing essential functions, by consuming stored fat and then muscle. Physical changes were visible to each man in his companions: prominent ribs, hollow cheeks, skin hanging loose where subcutaneous fat had been metabolized. They were becoming different creatures, adapted to different environment, stripped of padding that civilization provided.

Mental changes were equally profound. Chase noted that men were much debilitated and their spirits depressed, restrained description of what Nickerson remembered as condition approaching stupor. The will to act, to bail, to row, to scan horizon, was becoming harder to summon. The heat, the thirst, constant motion of boat—these produced kind of hypnotic state in which time lost its structure, in which past and future collapsed into eternal present of discomfort. The men who had been sailors, technicians, calculators, were becoming something else: survivors, stripped to physiological minimum, their consciousness reduced to immediate demands of body.

On December 10, the twentieth day, they altered course again. The decision, made in consultation between three boats when they managed to rendezvous, was to steer more directly east, accepting northward drift that would carry them toward lower latitudes and possibility of rain. The change represented abandonment of southern search, acknowledgment that islands were lost to them, commitment to longer voyage toward South America. They were now sailing into different blank space, one measured in thousands rather than hundreds of miles, with no certainty of landfall before provisions failed completely.

Physical condition of men made this commitment desperate. They were weaker than at start, more vulnerable to effects of exposure, less capable of sustained effort that long-distance navigation required. The boats themselves were deteriorating: seams opening, caulking working loose, constant bailing becoming more necessary and more exhausting. Equipment salvaged from the Essex—sails, oars, pumps, navigation instruments—was showing effects of salt water and continuous use. Material world was failing along with biological.

Yet routine continued. Water was distributed, measured with same care as on first day, though quantity was now less than half original ration. Bread was divided, pieces smaller, harder to swallow without adequate moisture. Prayers continued in Pollard’s boat, though Nickerson remembered them as shorter, more mechanical, silences between words longer. The ledger was still open. Entries were still being made. But account was running into deficit, and creditors were gathering.

December 11 and 12 brought variable winds and little progress. Boats were scattered, separated by squalls and difficulty of maintaining formation in deteriorating conditions. Chase recorded that they were much distressed, phrase covering physical suffering, mental anguish, and breakdown of social order. The men in his boat were, by his account, still responsive to command, still capable of coordinated effort. But margin was narrowing. Each day of exposure, each night of shivering, each ration of inadequate water was consuming reserves that made civilized conduct possible.

The birds had disappeared. The sea was empty, horizon unbroken, sky dome of blue offering no promise of rain. They were now approximately 600 miles from South American coast, by Chase’s calculation, with water for perhaps ten days at current ration. The arithmetic was simple and terrible: they would die of thirst before reaching land, unless wind changed, unless it rained, unless some unforeseen deliverance occurred. The blank page of ocean, space without marks or meaning, was revealing its true nature: not wilderness to be conquered, but void that absorbed human intention without trace.

On December 13, the twenty-third day, they sighted land.

It appeared first as darkening horizon to east visible shortly after dawn to man on watch in Chase’s boat—confirmed by others as boats closed so men could stand in thwarts for better view—low rocky without green vegetation at this distance; not island sought nor any island on charts but land nonetheless offering possibility water food rest from constant motion.

Identification came slowly. Chase, consulting fragmentary charts they had salvaged, believed it to be Ducie’s Island, same land they had sought and missed to south. The latitude, estimated by sextant sight, was approximately 24° south, consistent with charted position. But longitude was wrong, impossibly far east. The discrepancy did not matter. What mattered was solid fact of rock and earth, interruption of ocean’s monotony, promise of end to voyage in open boats.

They approached with caution of desperate men. The island, as they drew nearer, revealed itself as raised coral platform, steep-sided, with fringe of breaking reef that made landing difficult. There was no visible harbor, no beach of sand or shingle, only vertical walls of limestone and white water of surf. They sailed along coast, searching for entrance, boats pitching in swell that reflected from cliffs. Sun was high, heat intense, men’s thirst now sharpened by proximity of land and effort of approach.

The landing when they found it was small cove on island’s western side protected from prevailing wind by projecting point rock; they brought boats through gap reef men leaping overboard pull through shallow water feet touching sand first time twenty-three days. Henderson Island though they did not know it raised atoll uninhabited without permanent fresh water vegetation limited scrub stunted trees charts placed elsewhere or not at all barren rock forcing new concrete choice land or not under deteriorating physical conditions.
