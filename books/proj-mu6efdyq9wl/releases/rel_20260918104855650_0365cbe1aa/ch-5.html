Chapter 5

The Chase

Seven bells in the morning watch brought no warning. The Essex lay hove-to in the offshore grounds, her sails drawing just enough to hold position against the trades, when the cry erupted from seventy feet above the deck. “There she blows!” The shout traveled down to Captain George Pollard, who stood near the taffrail speaking with his second mate, Matthew Joy. The date was November 20, 1820. The ship had been prowling these waters, some one thousand miles west of the Galápagos Islands, for days, finding nothing but empty ocean. Now the system stirred into motion.

The deck became a theater of coordinated violence. Men ran to their stations without spoken command, muscle memory guiding them through an evolution practiced a hundred times in fifteen months at sea. Harpooners gathered their irons; boat-steerers checked the lines that would attach their craft to the mother ship before casting them loose for the chase. Three whaleboats hung in their davits along the Essex’s sides, each twenty-five feet long, clinker-built of cedar and oak, light enough to be rowed at racing speed yet sturdy enough to withstand the shock of a thrashing whale. A fourth boat, the captain’s spare, remained inverted on spare spars above the main hatch, a reserve against the losses that every voyage expected.

Pollard’s boat was first away, as protocol demanded. The captain took the steering oar himself, his boat-steerer handling the forward oars while four oarsmen bent their backs to gain distance from the mother ship. Second mate Joy commanded the second boat with his own crew. The third mate took the third. Owen Chase, the twenty-three-year-old first mate, remained aboard with his crew, not from any failure of opportunity but from mechanical necessity. His boat had shipped water during the previous day’s hunt, and Chase had determined to repair a leak in its bow before lowering again. This distribution of forces, three boats in pursuit and one alongside for repair, would prove decisive, though at the moment it registered only as ordinary friction, the small delays that every hunt accommodated.

Chase set to work with the impatience of a man who understood that every whale sighted was oil uncollected, profit unrealized. The leak was minor, a seam opened in the cedar planking perhaps from the shock of a previous strike, perhaps from ordinary working in heavy seas. He supervised while men passed tools between the two hulls—oakum, pitch, caulking irons—working to make the vessel tight again while opportunity swam away across the water.

From his position alongside, Chase could observe the dispersion of forces. The three boats pulled away from the Essex’s hull, spreading across the water in a formation born of experience. Sperm whales do not travel alone; where one spout appeared, others usually followed. The boats fanned out to intercept, each captain reading the whale’s behavior, the direction of its spout, the angle of its flukes when it sounded, the interval between dives, to anticipate where it would surface again. This was the art that Nantucket had refined into an industrial science: the reading of signs in an element that offered no purchase, the projection of human intention onto creatures that weighed sixty tons and could remain submerged for ninety minutes.

The pod numbered, by Chase’s later estimate, some twenty to twenty-five whales. They were large bulls, mature males traveling in loose association across the offshore grounds. Pollard’s boat drew ahead, his oarsmen pulling strongly, steering by the intermittent plume of vapor marking his quarry’s breathing. Joy and the third mate followed other spouts, their boats becoming smaller against the ocean’s plane until they were visible only as dark shapes, then as points against the horizon, then not at all.

The morning advanced. Chase’s repair neared completion. The Essex lay alone on the water, a small island of human purpose in an ocean measuring three thousand miles to the nearest continent. Below decks, the try-pots sat cold and black, filmed with residue from boils months past. The cooper had checked his casks and found them tight, empty, waiting. Thomas Nickerson, the fourteen-year-old cabin boy, moved about his duties—coiling lines, fetching coffee for the officers—performing the routines that had become muscle memory over fifteen months.

Then the whale appeared.

It surfaced at some distance, Chase would estimate two hundred yards, moving with what he described as a strange sort of velocity. The direction was toward the Essex, not away. This was itself unusual; hunted whales typically fled, sounding deep and running fast. A whale approaching a vessel violated the expected pattern of predator and prey. Chase observed this from the deck, his boat still alongside, repair nearly complete. The whale’s movement was deliberate, its massive head, one-third of its total length, containing the spermaceti organ that made it valuable, pointed directly at the ship’s starboard bow.

Chase had stood in whaleboat bows while sixty tons of living flesh passed within arm’s reach. He understood that these creatures were capable of violence; flukes could shatter planking, jaws could crush bone. But such violence was defensive, reactive—the thrashing of escape. What approached now was different. The whale built speed as it closed the distance, its scarred back dark above the waterline.

The Essex displaced 238 tons burden, her hull sheathed in copper below the waterline, oak and pine above, strengthened for ice she had never reached. She was not frail; whaleships were built to endure grinding ice and heavy seas. But she had not been built to withstand what approached.

The whale struck at full speed. The impact jarred the vessel with tremendous violence, nearly throwing them all onto their faces. The Essex shuddered from stem to stern, timbers groaning, masts whipping in their partners. Men below decks were thrown against bulkheads, against the try-pots and casks that filled the hold. The whale’s head had struck just forward of the starboard chains; copper sheathing tore like paper, oak planking splintered inward, shock traveling through the vessel’s fabric as a wave travels through water.

Chase recovered his feet. The whale lay alongside now, stunned or recovering, its flank heaving against the hull. For a moment there was only the creak of straining timber, the lap of water against damaged planking. Then Chase called for axes, for lances that might drive off the creature before it could strike again. Men ran to obey.

The whale recovered. It moved forward along the ship’s side, then turned—Chase would describe its motion as apparently convulsed—and swam rapidly away to leeward. It traveled perhaps two hundred yards in seconds despite its bulk, then turned again.

Chase watched it come.

The second strike was more terrible than the first. The whale had gathered speed; its angle was calculated for maximum damage. It struck just aft of the fore-chains, where the foremast stepped through the deck into the keel. The impact drove the vessel backward, stopped her forward motion entirely, sent a shock through her fabric that her builders had never imagined she would need to absorb.

Then the whale was gone. It sounded immediately, flukes rising once above the surface before disappearing into the depths. The water closed over it as if it had never been.

The Essex began to settle.

Chase did not need to sound the well to know the damage was mortal. The jar of the second impact, the angle of the strike, the position of holes below the waterline—all told him what inspection would confirm. Water entered with oceanic pressure, filling spaces between cargo and ballast, rising toward lower decks. The ship’s buoyancy was compromised beyond any pump’s remedy.

He called the crew to quarters—automatic response to crisis: all hands on deck, boats prepared for lowering, damage assessed. But as men ran up from below—Nickerson among them, suddenly witness to events that would shape his remaining life—Chase understood that normal protocols were insufficient. The Essex was sinking. The three whaleboats representing their only hope were miles distant, scattered across hunting grounds, their crews unaware that their world had changed.

The ship settled by the bow. The angle increased as water flooded forward, pressing damaged planking against external pressure. Chase ordered pumps manned, though he knew they could not save her. He ordered signal guns fired, reports carrying across water in hope that Pollard or Joy might hear and understand. He ordered what he could order, because the alternative was standing idle while the vessel died.

Men worked in silence or curses according to their natures. The carpenter examined damage and reported what Chase already knew: two holes in the starboard bow, each large enough to admit a horse, timbers around them splintered and sprung. Water rose steadily in the hold. The ship’s remaining boats—the captain’s spare and Chase’s repaired craft—were made ready for lowering. Provisions were gathered for what all now understood would be long exposure: six hundred pounds of hard bread, two casks of water, Galápagos tortoises still living in the hold, a musket with powder and ball, navigational instruments that might guide them toward land.

From the deck of the settling vessel, Chase could see nothing of the hunting boats. They had pursued their whales over the horizon; signal guns might reach them or might not, depending on wind and distance and the preoccupation of men focused on immediate tasks. The Essex was alone in her extremity.

The three hunting boats appeared at last, pulling toward their mother ship with exhausted slowness. Pollard’s face, when he understood what had happened, would become part of lore Nickerson would carry for sixty years. The captain’s boat was empty of oil; now it returned to find that the larger hunt had been destroyed by an agency no lookout could spot, no harpoon reach.

The boats gathered around the dying ship. Transfer of men and materials continued while the Essex settled deeper, her bow submerged, stern rising until rudder lifted clear. Try-pots showed above surface like black monuments. The spare whaleboat floated free from lashings, recovered by desperate work. Masts stood against sky until sinking angle became too steep and they went overboard.

By afternoon she was gone. Her final plunge was witnessed by twenty men in three small boats on a circle of empty ocean. The place was approximately 0°40′ south latitude, 119° west longitude—established later by calculation, not by any fixed mark in that unmarked space. Nearest land was Henderson Island, one hundred miles distant, limestone without water. Nearest inhabited land was the Marquesas, twelve hundred miles west. The coast of South America lay two thousand miles east.

The boats held six or seven men each, crowded beyond designed capacity. They held provisions for days that must stretch across unknown duration. They held compasses, sextants, chronometers whose accurate use might mean life or death. They held men whose knowledge of one another’s capacities would now be tested in ways no training could anticipate.

Chase commanded one boat by rank; Pollard another by prerogative; the third fell to the boat-steerer promoted by necessity. They held brief council on water, gunwales touching while they discussed their situation. The decision they reached—to sail east for South America rather than west for islands—would be argued for decades afterward. At that moment it represented only the choice seeming least dangerous to men who feared cannibals more than starvation.

The leak in Chase’s boat was neither unusual nor negligible, a separation of cedar planks where the bow met the waterline, probably sprung during the previous day’s hard pulling when the boat had shipped green water over the gunwales during a fast chase. Such injuries accumulated silently throughout a voyage, the cost of driving light craft through heavy seas at pursuit speeds. Chase worked with his own hands, not from democratic impulse but from urgency; he knew that every minute spent passing oakum into seams with a caulking iron represented barrels of oil swimming away across the Pacific.

The cedar planks, barely half an inch thick between ribs of white oak, flexed with each swell, testing the waterproof integrity of pine pitch and hemp fiber. This was the material reality of Nantucket’s industry: not the romantic image of sailors against whales, but men kneeling in bilge water with knives and mallets, repairing disposable craft that were sacrificed by design to the violence of the hunt.

That morning, Chase’s impatience had a specific texture, the knowledge that the Essex’s hold remained largely empty after weeks of cruising, that the owners in Massachusetts would calculate this voyage’s profitability down to the gallon, and that his own one-twentieth lay as first mate depended entirely on the barrels stowed below. The repair required twenty minutes of concentrated labor; twenty minutes that would separate him from the catastrophe about to unfold, placing him on deck as witness rather than in the water as victim.

The whale that surfaced carried its history written across its flank. Chase noted later that its back appeared “scarred,” a detail that carries significance beyond description in the taxonomy of sperm whale behavior. Such scarring indicated previous encounters with the iron, harpoon points that had torn flesh and healed into pale ridges of collagen, perhaps from Spanish try-pots in Peruvian waters or from American ships working these same grounds. A scarred bull was not a naive animal; it had survived the hunt before, possibly learned the connection between the splash of oars and the sting of steel. In the economy of the offshore grounds, where mature males traveled in bachelor pods distant from breeding grounds, such veterans represented both the highest value, eighty barrels of oil in a large bull, and the highest risk.

Most sperm whales fled from vessels, diving deep at the first scent of trouble, following evolutionary programming that favored escape over confrontation. But this individual had apparently unlearned or rejected that programming, carrying instead a conditioned aggression that would prove catastrophic. The scars suggested not random injury but a biography of violence exchanged between species, a history that made this particular whale an active participant in its own narrative rather than mere prey.

The Essex had sailed for these offshore grounds, waters west of the Galapagos that Nantucket navigators called the “Offshore Ground” as distinct from the “Onshore Ground” along the coast of Peru and Chile, because the easier pickings closer to land had been exhausted by fifteen years of intensive American whaling. The ship’s owners, Gideon Folger and Sons, had outfitted her for a voyage expected to last two and a half years and return with two thousand barrels of sperm oil, the highest-grade illuminant then available for domestic lamps and lighthouse beacons. Each empty day in these waters represented compound interest lost on capital invested in provisions, wages advanced, and insurance underwritten.

The hold below Chase’s feet as he worked contained not treasure but potential: empty copper-lined casks waiting in the dark, Galapagos tortoises crawling over each other in the longboat lashed amidships, serving as living fresh meat against scurvy. The try-pots sat cold because the crew had not yet found the concentration of whales that usually marked these latitudes in November. This was the pressure that drove Pollard to spread his boats wide, that kept Chase working with frozen fingers even as opportunity swam away, that made them all extend their radius of pursuit beyond prudence into the realm of statistical necessity.

When Pollard’s boat pulled away from the ship’s side, the maneuver executed with precision that belied its danger. Four men bent to oars of second-growth ash, their stroke rate calibrated to the captain’s command, long pulls for distance, short choppy strokes when closing for the kill. The boat-steerer stood in the bow, his bare feet gripping cedar planking worn smooth by countless such hunts, holding the iron, a forged steel harpoon attached to a whale-line of Manilla hemp coiled carefully in a tub amidships so it would run freely when struck.

This was the choreography of death as practiced by Nantucket’s fleet: the approach from astern to avoid the flukes, the dart of the iron into the thick blubber behind the head, the immediate transfer of command from officer to boat-steerer as the whale sounded and ran, taking line at speeds that could smoke against the gunwale. Chase watched this familiar ballet from his disadvantage, knowing that his crew should be participating, that his own lay was diminishing with each spout that went unstruck.

The normalcy of the scene, the coordinated dip of oars, the distant cry of “Stern all!” as a whale sounded suddenly, masked the essential absurdity of men in twenty-five-foot boats attacking sixty-ton mammals capable of crushing them with a casual swipe. Yet this absurdity had been normalized through repetition until it appeared as routine as mending sails or scraping decks.

After the first impact, Chase descended into the hold long enough to confirm what his feet had told him through the deck planking. The carpenter, Henry De Witt, held a lantern against the starboard bow where copper sheathing had peeled back like a fruit rind, revealing splintered oak beneath. Water pressed through gaps with rhythmic insistence, each pulse of the ship’s motion admitting another gallon.

The damage was structural, the whale’s forehead, a battering ram of gristle and bone backed by tons of momentum, had struck at precisely the angle calculated to spring timbers rather than merely dent them. Chase understood immediately that standard damage control, fothering a sail over the breach, manning pumps continuously, would prove insufficient because the holes were below the waterline and large enough to pass a barrel through. The physics of displacement worked against them: as water flooded forward, the bow settled deeper, increasing pressure on damaged planking and accelerating the intake.

Sun declined toward western horizon. Boats spread small sails—spritsails and jibs designed for pursuit of whales, not ocean passages—and took departure from where their ship had died. The Essex left no mark on surface, no oil slick or debris to indicate where she had gone down. Ocean closed over her as it had closed over the whale that destroyed her.

The hunt is at its peak, boats in pursuit, the Essex waiting—a tableau of human order moments before the whale’s charge rewrites everything.
