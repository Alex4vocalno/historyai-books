Chapter 27

The Mark in the Margin

Seen from above, the literary landscape of the early nineteenth century was one where narratives of harrowing events were carefully shaped into acceptable public documents. The book that emerged from Nantucket in 1821, a memoir of a shipwreck and survival, was one such document, its raw testimony honed into a form that performed the work of literature. This structural irony defined its legacy: a claim of authenticity that was itself a kind of fiction, a selection and arrangement that rendered an ordeal into a civilized account. It would take another writer, three decades later, to recognize what its author, Owen Chase, had actually made, and to complete that transformation.

From above, the Berkshire hills of western Massachusetts appear as a rumpled manuscript, valleys folding into ridges like sentences into paragraphs, the whole region waiting for a reader. In 1850 this landscape held a single farmhouse that would become, for one winter, the most consequential writing room in American literature. Arrowhead sat on a hillside outside Pittsfield, its rooms cold, its windows overlooking fields that would soon enough bury themselves in snow. Here Herman Melville, thirty-one years old, father of a growing family, beneficiary of his father-in-law’s judicial salary, was attempting something that his previous five books had not prepared him for: a work large enough to contain the Pacific Ocean, the whale fishery, and the metaphysics of American expansion.

The desk he built himself, assembled from boards and barrels, spread across one room like a raft cobbled from wreckage.

On it lay the chaos of his ambition—notes, charts, volumes of natural history, and among them, almost certainly, a slim octavo volume published thirty years before: Owen Chase’s Narrative of the Most Extraordinary and Distressing Shipwreck of the Whale-Ship Essex.

Melville had not purchased this book new. Whaling vessels carried small libraries, books that passed from hand to hand, from ship to ship, their pages soft with handling, their bindings strained by the humidity of the Pacific trade. He likely encountered Chase’s account through a shipmate’s collection, or through one of the Nantucket connections that his Berkshire isolation had not severed.

The date of acquisition is lost. What remains is the physical record of encounter: a copy now in the New York Public Library, its pages bearing pencil marks that trace the movements of a reader hunting for something he could not quite name.

The annotations begin with attention to the whale itself. Chase had written of the creature’s apparent intention, and Melville marked this quality—Chase’s insistence that the sperm whale had acted with purpose, had struck the Essex not in panic or confusion but with something like design. The first mate had described the whale surfacing, spouting, turning, then swimming toward the ship at speed, striking with force enough to stagger the men at their stations. Melville copied phrases about the whale’s apparent rage, its apparent understanding that the wooden hull was the source of the harpoons that had wounded it. These were not the observations of a naturalist. They were the testimony of a man who had seen something that violated his categories, who had witnessed an animal behave like an avenging intelligence.

Chase’s narrative offered Melville something his own experience had not: a story in which the whale won. In 1841, shipping as a green hand on the whaler Acushnet, Melville had heard whales described as floating capital, as resources to be extracted, as the passive objects of human industry. The Essex disaster inverted this relation. Here was the industrial instrument destroyed by its intended victim, the factory ship sunk by the raw material it sought to process.

The annotations track this inversion closely. Where Chase described the ship’s boats pulling away from the wreck, Melville marked the detail. Where Chase calculated the provisions, the distances, the diminishing chances, Melville followed the calculation.

The first mate’s narrative was a ledger of loss—barrels of bread, gallons of water, miles of ocean, men converted into numbers that grew smaller with each page. Melville read this as a critic of the system that had produced it, seeing in Chase’s careful accounting the madness of a commerce that measured everything except its own cost.

The physical book shows the pressure of this reading. The pencil marks grow heavier in the sections describing the open-boat ordeal. Chase’s account of the separation of the boats, the drawing of lots, the eating of Owen Coffin—Melville annotated these with a density that suggests recognition, or perhaps resistance. He was not simply collecting material. He was arguing with his source, testing its claims against his own experience, asking whether Chase’s version of events could bear the weight that Melville intended to place upon it. The Narrative was a survivor’s testimony, constrained by the need to appear respectable, to maintain the fiction that the Essex men had remained civilized even in their extremity. Melville needed to break that constraint, to show what civilization looked like when its supports were removed, when men floated on an ocean that answered neither to prayer nor to profit.

The transformation began in the margins and continued in the manuscript. Melville’s working notes for Moby-Dick survive in fragments, but the published novel shows the archaeology of his reading. The Essex became the Pequod, Chase’s whale became Moby Dick, the first mate’s careful distribution of credit and blame became Ahab’s monomaniacal pursuit. But the borrowing was never simple theft. Melville extracted from Chase’s account not its facts but its structure of feeling—the sense that the encounter between whale and ship was something more than an accident, that it participated in a larger pattern of meaning that Chase himself had only glimpsed.

Consider the problem of the whale’s intention. Chase had insisted upon it, had used language that strained the boundaries of natural description. This was the testimony of a witness who had seen something that should not have been possible, who had watched a whale behave like a moral agent. Melville took this impossibility and made it the center of his book.

Ahab’s pursuit of Moby Dick is not simply revenge for a lost leg; it is the attempt to force the universe to declare its meaning, to make the white whale speak the truth that Ahab suspects but cannot prove—that the world is governed by malice, that suffering is not accidental but intended, that the power behind appearances can be confronted and destroyed. The Essex whale had struck once and disappeared. Melville’s whale must be hunted, must be made to answer, must destroy in turn the ship and the man who sought to read divinity in its whiteness.

The annotations in Melville’s copy trace this expansion. Where Chase described the Essex sinking, Melville marked the moment of impact, the physical shock that had traveled through the hull. In the novel, this becomes the chapter “The Chase—First Day,” where Ahab’s boat is stove by the whale, where the captain is nearly drowned, where the pursuit begins in earnest. Where Chase calculated the survivors’ diminishing chances, Melville marked the arithmetic of despair. In the novel, this becomes the chapter “The Dying Whale,” where the creature’s blood turns the sea to crimson, where the industrial process of rendering is revealed in its full horror. The Narrative had presented these scenes as discrete events, linked by chronology and the necessities of survival. Melville read them as episodes in a single drama, the drama of human limitation confronting natural power.

The question of sources becomes urgent here. Melville had other accounts of the Essex disaster available to him. He knew, certainly, that Captain Pollard had survived, had returned to Nantucket, had sunk a second ship and ended his days as a night watchman. He may have heard versions of the story that contradicted Chase’s—versions in which the decision to sail east rather than west appeared less rational, in which the cannibalism seemed less like a necessary evil and more like the collapse of moral order. But it was Chase’s book that he owned, that he marked, that he carried with him into the composition of Moby-Dick. The first mate’s narrative provided the armature upon which Melville constructed his larger argument, the documentary base that allowed the novel to claim the authority of fact even as it pursued the freedom of symbol.

This was the specific genius of Melville’s reading: he saw that Chase’s account was already, despite itself, a work of imagination. The first mate had shaped his materials, had selected and arranged, had imposed a pattern of meaning upon events that might otherwise have appeared merely random. Melville recognized this shaping because he practiced it himself, because he understood that all narrative is a form of interpretation. The annotations show him working through Chase’s interpretations, testing their sufficiency, finding the places where they failed to account for the horror they described. The whale’s apparent intention was one such place. The collapse of civil order was another. The conversion of men into meat was a third. In each case, Melville found Chase reaching toward a meaning that his own constraints—respectability, piety, the need to appear as a reliable witness—prevented him from fully articulating.

The Berkshire winter of 1850–51 enclosed Melville in a solitude that matched the Pacific void. Arrowhead was cold, isolated, expensive to maintain. His friendship with Nathaniel Hawthorne, struck up that summer, provided the only intellectual companionship that could sustain the weight of his ambition.

The two writers walked the hills together, talked of books and metaphysics, recognized in each other a common desperation to make American literature something other than what it had been. Hawthorne had his own darkness, his own sense of the Puritan inheritance as a wound that would not close. Melville brought to their conversations the specific darkness of the whale fishery, the knowledge that men went out in small boats to kill creatures that could kill them in return, that this exchange was not sport or adventure but industry, that the industry was built upon a violence that could not be acknowledged without threatening the civilization it sustained.

Into this conversation, Chase’s Narrative entered as a document that required translation. The first mate had written for an audience that wanted to believe in the possibility of orderly disaster, in the capacity of men to maintain their humanity even when reduced to their elements. Melville wrote for an audience that he suspected did not exist, or that existed only in the future—readers who could bear the knowledge that humanity was a local effect, sustainable only within certain limits, likely to dissolve when those limits were exceeded.

The annotations show him preparing this translation, marking the places where Chase’s account approached truths that Chase himself could not speak. The whale’s apparent rage was such a truth. The sufferings of the survivors was another. The fact that eight men had lived while twelve died, and that the living had eaten the dead—this was the fact that Chase’s narrative circled without ever fully confronting, that Melville’s novel would approach through indirection, through the carefully staged catastrophe of the Pequod’s sinking.

The mechanism of this transformation can be traced in the specific borrowings. Chapter 45 of Moby-Dick, “The Affidavit,” contains Melville’s most explicit acknowledgment of his source. Here the narrator cites the printed account of the capture of the great sperm whale that destroyed the Essex, and declares that this whale is not only a specimen of the largest magnitude, but in other respects a model of the most valuable importance. The language is deliberately ambiguous—model suggesting both pattern and miniature, both the thing itself and its representation. Melville has collapsed the distinction between the Essex disaster and his own fiction, making the historical event serve as evidence for the novel’s claims while admitting that the novel’s claims exceed anything that evidence could support.

This is the work that the annotations prepared. Melville read Chase’s Narrative not as a fixed record but as a prompt to further invention, a set of constraints within which his own imagination could operate.

The whale that sank the Essex became, in his reading, a whale that could be hunted, that could be wounded, that could be made to symbolize the forces that Ahab opposed. The men who died in the open boats became, in his transformation, the crew of the Pequod, diverse in their origins, united in their destruction. The specific horror of the Essex—that the survivors had eaten their own—was displaced onto the general horror of the whale fishery, the systematic destruction of intelligent creatures for the profit of distant markets.

Melville did not need to reproduce Chase’s most shocking details. He had found a way to make the whole industry shocking, to make the Pequod’s voyage appear as a single extended act of consumption that would finally consume itself.

The evidence of this reading survives in the marked book, in the working notes, in the published novel itself. But the full measure of Melville’s engagement can only be understood by considering what he refused to take from Chase.

The first mate’s narrative is, at its core, a story of survival and return, of men who endured the impossible and came back to tell of it. Melville’s novel is a story of destruction without return, of a ship that sinks with all hands, of a narrator who alone escapes to bear witness. This is not a difference of detail but a difference of kind.

Chase wrote to reassure his readers that the human could survive the inhuman, that the forms of civilized life could be maintained even in extremity. Melville wrote to deny this reassurance, to show that the pursuit of meaning through violence must end in violence, that the white whale would not be made to speak, that the only truth available was the truth of the abyss.

The annotations approach this denial without quite reaching it. Melville marked Chase’s description of the survivors’ rescue, their arrival in Valparaiso, their eventual return to Nantucket. But these marks are lighter than those that accompany the destruction, the open boats, the despair. The return to civilization interested him less than the collapse of civilization, the moment when the Essex went down and the men were left with nothing but water and sky and the diminishing hope of land. This was the moment he would expand, the moment he would make the occasion for his novel’s largest claims. The Pacific Ocean that Chase had described as a theater of suffering became, in Melville’s reading, the void itself, the blank page upon which human meaning could be projected and erased, written and rewritten, until the writing finally ceased.

The winter progressed. Melville wrote with a speed that suggests possession, twelve to eighteen hours a day, the manuscript growing by accretion and revision, the simple whaling narrative that he had first conceived expanding to include cetology, philosophy, drama, poetry. The annotations in Chase’s book provided anchor points in this expansion, moments when the historical record could be cited to authorize the fictional invention.

But the expansion itself was driven by something that the annotations only partially reveal: Melville’s recognition that the Essex disaster was not unique but typical, not an accident but a revelation of the system’s inherent violence. The whale ship was a floating factory; the factory required the destruction of whales; the destruction of whales required men to risk their lives in small boats; the risk was justified by profit; the profit was measured in barrels of oil; the oil was consumed by lamps that made possible the civilization that sent the ships to sea.

Chase had described one break in this chain, one moment when the whale refused its assigned role and struck back. Melville would describe the chain itself, would make the Pequod’s voyage an allegory of industrial modernity, would show that the whale’s revenge was only the most visible form of a violence that ran through the entire enterprise.

The Berkshire spring came slowly. Melville finished his manuscript in the summer of 1851, delivered it to his publisher in the fall, saw it published in November to reviews that ranged from baffled to hostile. The book did not sell. The reading public that had embraced his earlier South Sea narratives found Moby-Dick unreadable, excessive, mad. Melville had written beyond his moment, had produced a work that required readers capable of following his annotations into territory that Chase himself had not explored.

The Essex disaster remained, for most of his contemporaries, simply a disaster—a terrible event, certainly, but an event with no larger meaning, no claim upon the imagination of those who had not lived through it. Melville had made it into something else, had transformed Chase’s narrative into the foundation of a myth that would outlast the industry it described, the civilization it sustained, the very language in which it was written.

The physical book that made this transformation possible survives in the New York Public Library, its pages brittle with age, its pencil marks faded but still legible. Scholars have studied these marks, have catalogued them, have argued about their significance. But the essential fact is simple and visible: Melville read Chase’s Narrative with the attention of a writer looking for what he needed, and he found it. He found the whale that could not be explained, the ocean that could not be crossed, the men who could not be saved. He found, in Chase’s careful prose, the materials for his own wilder, more desperate utterance. And he left his marks in the margin—underlinings, vertical lines, question marks—the traces of a reading that became a rewriting, a rewriting that became a founding document of American literature.

The cost of this transformation fell upon Melville himself. Moby-Dick ended his career as a popular writer, began his long descent into obscurity, the twenty years of silence that would end only with his death in 1891 and the slow recognition that followed.

The Essex survivors had paid a different cost, measured in bodies and in the conscience of those who survived. Chase had paid in the labor of turning experience into narrative, of making the intolerable appear merely extraordinary. The system that had produced the disaster continued, sending ships to the Pacific, processing whales into commodities, converting the living ocean into the machinery of illumination.

Melville’s annotations did not change this system. They only made it visible, made it possible to see what the system required its participants to forget: that the whale was not oil but intelligence, that the ocean was not highway but wilderness, that the pursuit of profit could end only in the destruction of the pursuer.

George Pollard Jr., captain of the Essex, survived to sink another ship, the Two Brothers, in 1823. He returned to Nantucket, became a night watchman, lived until 1870. Owen Chase, first mate and author, returned to sea, suffered mental collapse, locked himself in his room to fast, died in 1869. Thomas Nickerson, cabin boy, wrote his own account late in life, unpublished until the twentieth century. The eight survivors of the open-boat ordeal lived various lengths, died various deaths, were forgotten by the industry they had served. Only Melville’s marks in the margin preserved their story from the general oblivion that waits upon maritime disaster, the forgetting that is the ocean’s deepest response to human suffering.

The annotations have permanently etched the Essex into American literature; the disaster is no longer a historical incident but a myth that explains the system’s fatal collision with the wild.
