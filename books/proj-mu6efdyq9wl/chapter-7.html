Chapter 7

The Captain’s Decision (Afternoon, 20 November 1820)

The three boats rigged their canvas, small triangular sails meant for chasing cetaceans in calm waters, not for beating against trade winds, and pointed their prows away from the vortex where the Essex had foundered, leaving twenty men assembled on a plane of water that offered no distinction between the cardinal points. Afternoon on the twentieth of November, 1820. The sun stood past its zenith somewhere west of the Galápagos Islands, and the wreckage of the ship still wallowed in the long swells, her broken spars and empty try-pots drifting in a loose constellation that marked the place where the vessel had died. The survivors now found themselves commanders of three open boats twenty-three feet long, each burdened with more men than design allowed and provisions that could be counted by the hour.

George Pollard Jr., master of the lost ship, sat in the stern of his own whaleboat, his hands still smeared with the tar he had used hours earlier to seal a sprung plank. He was twenty-eight years old, young for a captain but already carrying the authority of Nantucket’s hierarchical system, where a man’s rank was measured by the oil he could render and the discipline he could maintain across years of isolation.

Beside him sat his second mate, Matthew Joy, a thin man whose recent illness had left him gaunt, and across the thwarts were seamen who had watched their floating factory dissolve into splinters. In a second boat, Owen Chase commanded his own crew, the first mate’s jaw still swollen from the accident that had nearly killed him weeks before. A third boat held the remaining sailors and boys, including Thomas Nickerson, the cabin boy who had turned fourteen during the voyage and who would later remember these hours with a different cadence than his superiors.

The immediate crisis of survival, of keeping the boats afloat and the men from drinking salt water, had passed with the settling of the wreckage. Now a slower, more terrible pressure asserted itself.

The wreck of the Essex still floated, her hull submerged but her upper works breaking the surface, a sodden island of splintered oak and iron that drifted with the current. Upon this ruin, or alongside it in the gently bobbing boats, the officers convened.

Chase would later write that Pollard gathered him and Joy with the gravity of a commander assessing battle damage, while Nickerson’s later account suggested a more frantic assembly, born of the realization that the sun was descending and no rescue ship would appear over the horizon. Where Chase remembered deliberation, Nickerson recalled desperation; where Chase described a chart spread across a cask and weighted with a compass, Nickerson remembered only the captain’s voice cutting through the wind. Both agreed on the essential fact: before dark, a decision had to be made that would fix their course across two thousand miles of ocean.

Pollard produced the navigational instruments they had managed to salvage. There was a compass, its card still trembling on its pivot, and a chart that showed the eastern Pacific as a field of white interrupted by occasional ink marks. The chart was not blank in the manner of untouched paper; it was annotated with the names of islands that existed in rumor more than verification—Ducie’s Rock, Henderson’s Island, the scattered reefs that whalers spoke of in Nantucket’s taprooms but which few had visited with instruments precise enough to fix their longitude. These marks represented hope. Between them and the coast of South America lay a vast expanse that the chart left largely empty, save for the rhumb lines indicating the prevailing southeast trade winds that blew steadily toward the west, pushing against any vessel that might try to beat toward Peru or Chile.

The alternative lay behind them. To the west, perhaps twelve hundred miles distant, lay the Marquesas Islands; further still, the Society Islands. These were known coordinates, marked on charts with the confidence of repeated visitation. They offered fresh water, coconuts, and the possibility of repair or rescue.

But they also represented a different kind of void. The cognitive framework that governed Nantucket’s whaling industry, where every barrel of oil was entered in a log and every hour of daylight accounted for as profit or loss, had its corollary in the navigation manuals and sailors’ lore that filled the blank spaces of the Pacific with projection.

Among the officers and crew of the Essex, the western islands had become associated with reports of native hostility, of cannibals who greeted shipwrecked mariners with clubs rather than breadfruit. In fact, both the Marquesas and Society Islands would have been safe destinations, as the inhabitants were at that time friendly towards mariners, but Pollard accepted the suggestion of Chase and Joy.

Chase’s account emphasized the rationality of their fear. He described how he laid out the distances with his finger on the chart, calculating that beating against the trades to reach the Marquesas would require weeks of laborious tacking, consuming their limited stores before they sighted land. Nickerson remembered the discussion differently, recalling a silence that fell over the officers when the western course was mentioned, a silence filled with stories they had heard in port—stories that belonged to the mythology of the Pacific rather than its surveyed reality. The difference between their memories is instructive: Chase, writing for publication within months of his rescue, constructed a narrative of cool calculation, while Nickerson, looking back from old age, retained the texture of dread that had settled over the boats that afternoon.

What is certain is that Pollard declared the damage was so extensive that they should sail back to Nantucket for repairs, but Chase and Joy persuaded him otherwise. The captain’s initial impulse, revealed in Chase’s narrative, was to attempt the longer return to their home port, a voyage that would have taken them eastward around Cape Horn against the prevailing westerlies of higher latitudes. This was perhaps the instinct of a man who saw his command shattered and sought restoration in the familiar.

Yet the first mate’s composure soon brought all to their sober senses. Chase argued, and Joy concurred, that they lacked the provisions for such a passage.

They had salvaged approximately six hundred pounds of hard bread, some gallons of water in breakable casks, two Galapagos tortoises, and a few tools. The math was inexorable: divided among twenty men, the food would last sixty days at most, and a voyage to Nantucket would require four times that duration.

Instead, they turned to the southern quadrant of the chart. There, sketched with less certainty than the mapped archipelagos of the west but with more optimism than the empty eastern reaches, lay the suggestion of land. The officers knew of Henderson Island, Nickerson would later recall its name being spoken with particular emphasis, because British captains had reported it in recent years. On March 2, 1819, Captain Henry King of the Elizabeth had landed there to find the King’s colors already flying, his crew scratching their ship’s name into a tree. This was concrete intelligence, a fixed point in the ocean that offered the possibility of resupply before the longer run to the coast of Chile. Beyond Henderson lay Ducie Island, and beyond that, if the winds permitted, the long reach toward Valparaiso or Concepción.

The decision was not made in panic. It was made with the particular confidence of men trained to read the ocean as a text of wind and current. They believed the southeast trades would carry them down toward the latitude of the forties, where they might pick up westerlies to push them toward South America. They believed Henderson Island existed where the chart placed it. They believed that sailing west into the teeth of the trades would exhaust their water before they reached the Marquesas.

These beliefs were wrong in crucial respects, the trades would not serve them as they hoped, and Henderson would prove a prison rather than a refuge, but they were not irrational. They were the product of maritime knowledge accumulated in the age of sail, filtered through the anxieties of Nantucket masters who viewed the Pacific not as a wilderness to be inhabited but as a ledger to be balanced.

Pollard did not force the decision; he accepted it. In Chase’s telling, the first mate presented the navigational logic with such force that agreement became inevitable. In Nickerson’s memory, the captain seemed to withdraw slightly, allowing the younger officers to steer the consensus toward the southern course.

What both accounts obscure—but what the physical evidence of their subsequent voyage reveals—is that the choice to avoid the western islands was also a choice to avoid confronting the unknown populations that inhabited them. The oceanic wilderness of the western Pacific had been inscribed with fear: cannibals waiting on beaches, savages with no interest in the commercial reciprocity that governed Nantucket’s relations with the world.

Whether Pollard shared this fear or merely bowed to the unanimous counsel of his officers remains unclear. What is clear is that by late afternoon, with the sun beginning its descent toward the horizon they had rejected, the three boats had been assigned their stations and their course had been fixed.

The men who were not officers watched this deliberation from their places in the boats, bailing with their boots or holding onto the gunwales as the swells passed beneath them. They could not hear the discussion over the wind, but they saw the gestures toward the chart and the pointing toward the south. They knew that a decision had been made when Pollard stood in his boat and gave the order to cast off from the wreckage. The Essex was still floating, her broken hull a temporary raft that might have supported them for days, but the officers feared that staying near the wreck would breed despair or that the men would plunder the provision casks without measure. Better to leave the scene of the disaster and commit to the open ocean.

As the boats separated from the wreck, the practical consequences of the abstract decision began to assert themselves. Each boat had to be trimmed for a voyage of unknown duration. The sails—those small triangles of canvas designed for short sprints after whales—had to be reefed and adjusted for running before the wind or beating into it. The compasses had to be secured where they could be read without being jostled by panicked movements. And the stores—those six hundred pounds of bread, those precious gallons of water—had to be inventoried and distributed with the precision of clerks closing an account.

Chase took charge of the provisioning in his boat with the same intensity he had brought to repairing the ship’s pumps weeks earlier. He counted the bread by the pound, breaking the hard tack into daily portions that he wrapped in cloth to keep dry. The water casks were sounded with a dipper, and the inches of liquid were translated into days of survival. This was the accounting mentality made flesh: every ounce of food entered into a mental account against the nautical miles that separated them from Chile. The men watched these calculations with eyes that had already begun to sink into hollow sockets. They understood that they had ceased to be whalemen and had become entries in a desperate balance sheet.

In Pollard’s boat, the atmosphere was different. The captain kept his own counsel, his face set in the expression that Chase described as composed and steady, but which Nickerson later recalled as mask-like, hiding a shock that had not yet fully registered. Joy, weakened by illness, lay in the bottom of the boat between thwarts, wrapped in a piece of canvas. The sailors assigned to Pollard’s command looked to their captain for some sign of encouragement, but he offered only orders: secure the mast, check the lashings on the water breaker, prepare the lines for towing if one boat should fall behind.

The Nantucket system that had placed Pollard in command was not designed for moments when the ledger showed only loss. Whaling masters were selected for their ability to render profit from darkness—to find oil where others saw only empty ocean—and this commercial imperative had trained Pollard to read the Pacific as a grid of economic potential rather than a field of hydrological reality.

The same instincts that had led him to trust Chase’s repair of the pumps now compelled him to trust the first mate’s reading of the chart, for in Nantucket’s hierarchy, navigational competence was currency more valuable than formal rank. Yet the choice to sail south represented a rupture in that system: they were abandoning the whaling grounds without oil, without a ship, and without the prospect of returning home as successful men.

The southern course offered no commercial redemption, only survival, and this distinction pressed upon Pollard with a weight that Chase—younger, more robust—did not yet feel.

The chart itself, likely a copy of Blunt’s American Coast Pilot supplemented by whalers’ penciled annotations, revealed the epistemological poverty of their position. The islands marked in the western Pacific, Nuku Hiva, Tahiti, appeared with the solid confidence of repeated survey visits, their coordinates verified by chronometer and sextant.

But the southern quadrant showed a different geography: Henderson Island appeared as a speck with a question mark, Ducie as a mere rumor, and the space between them and South America remained stubbornly white. These were not blank spaces in the sense of untouched wilderness; they were negative spaces defined by absence, by the lack of confirming witnesses.

The officers were betting their lives on the hope that absence of evidence did not constitute evidence of absence, that the ink marks representing Henderson were as reliable as those representing the Marquesas. They were not, but the officers had no way of knowing that the British reports they trusted had themselves been based on fleeting sightings and dead-reckoning estimates made in bad weather.

Time itself had become an antagonist as the afternoon wore on. The sun’s descent toward the western horizon—a direction they had now psychologically abandoned—created a literal deadline. Navigation by compass alone was possible but treacherous in the dark, and the officers knew that once night fell, the boats would drift apart or collide, or the men would succumb to paralysis.

Chase later wrote that the decision was reached by “unanimous consent,” but Nickerson’s memory of the captain’s voice “cutting through the wind” suggests a moment when deliberation hardened into command.

Pollard had to choose before the light failed, and this temporal pressure compressed the institutional deference that normally governed shipboard relations. Where a captain might ordinarily retire to his cabin to consult his private journals, Pollard had no cabin, no privacy, and no time to seek counsel from Nantucket’s absent merchants. He had to decide in the open air, with twenty pairs of eyes watching the chart as if it were scripture whose meaning might shift with the shadows.

The mechanics of implementing the decision revealed the subtle transfer of authority that had occurred. Chase did not wait for Pollard to issue detailed orders regarding the provisioning; he simply began the inventory, his hands moving with the automatic precision of a man who had spent years measuring whale oil by the barrel.

This usurpation of the captain’s administrative function was accepted without comment because it was necessary: Pollard’s expertise lay in command, but Chase’s lay in the practical mathematics of survival.

Joy remained largely silent during these hours, his illness having reduced him to a passenger in the very boat he nominally commanded as second mate. His weakness created a vacuum in the chain of command that Chase filled instinctively, leaving Pollard to manage the psychological welfare of the men while Chase managed their material existence. It was an efficient division of labor, but one that established patterns of dependency that would harden into hierarchy over the coming weeks.

The physical condition of the crew imposed its own logic on the decision, constraining the officers’ options regardless of their preferences. The men had not eaten since morning, many had swallowed salt water during the wreck, and several were already showing signs of dehydration where the equatorial sun reflected off the water with blinding intensity. They could not have rowed westward against the trades even if the officers had ordered it; their muscles lacked the reserves for sustained exertion, and their mental acuity was already diminishing. The southern course, running before the wind with sails set, required less physical labor than beating to windward, and this biological reality—though unspoken in the officers’ accounts—must have weighed in the calculation.

By late afternoon, the three boats had formed into a loose formation, connected by lines that allowed them to communicate signals but separated enough to avoid collision in the swells. They set a course south-southeast, aiming for a latitude where they hoped to find Henderson Island and then catch the winds toward South America. The wreck of the Essex drifted away astern, becoming smaller against the horizon until it was indistinguishable from the whitecaps. The men did not look back for long. They were facing forward now, toward the empty water that stretched toward the pole.

The charts had been folded and stowed. The compasses were set. The decision was made; the crew now faced the concrete, logistical consequence of implementing it, moving from abstract chart to the physical weight of salvaged stores.

As dusk approached on that first day, Chase distributed the first evening ration: a single piece of hard bread and a measured swallow of water for each man. The bread was weevil-ridden and hard as oak, but it was entered into the ledger of survival as credit against the days ahead.

The men ate slowly, chewing the dry crusts while watching the sun sink toward the west—the direction they had not chosen—turning the ocean to a sheet of copper that reflected their own diminished forms back at them. When darkness came, they shipped their oars and lay in the bilge water, listening to the creak of the rigging and the slap of waves against hulls that were now their only world.
