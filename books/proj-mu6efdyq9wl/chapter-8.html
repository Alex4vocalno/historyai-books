Chapter 8

The Meticulous and Grimly Practical

The copper dipper struck the rim of the water cask with a sound like a cracked bell, and Owen Chase marked the level with his thumbnail: four inches, perhaps five, of brown Pacific water that had been meant for boiling whale blubber, not for drinking. It was just past noon on November 20, 1820. The Essex lay two thousand nautical miles west of the Galápagos, her stern settled so deep that waves washed across the deck where Chase now worked.

The first mate had sent men below with every bucket, jar, and breaker they could salvage. They came up with water black with bilge, stinking of oakum and tar, liquid that would keep them alive if it did not first make them sick. Chase measured each haul against the gunwale, converting gallons to days, days to miles, miles to the uncertain coast of South America that Captain Pollard had chosen over the nearer islands to the west.

The wreck was still settling. Every hour brought some new collapse: a spar breaking loose, the mizzenmast tearing free, the deck tilting another degree toward the water below. Yet the men worked with the methodical patience of their trade, as though they were stripping a whale rather than abandoning their home. This was the ledger mentality of Nantucket made visible—not despair, but inventory; not prayer, but calculation. Chase had kept the ship’s books. He knew to the gallon how much oil they had taken, to the pound how much bread they had consumed. Now he applied the same precision to ruin.

They had three whaleboats. Each would carry seven men, more than such craft were built to bear. Each would need water, bread, navigational instruments, and some means of making fire. The boats were twenty-three feet long, clinker-built of cedar and oak, designed to be launched from a moving ship and recovered at day’s end. They were not designed for an ocean voyage of three thousand miles. Chase knew this as he ran his hand along the gunwale of his own boat, feeling the give of the thwarts. The Essex had carried these boats across two oceans. Now the boats would have to carry the Essex’s men.

The inventory emerged piece by piece from the sinking hull. Hard bread—flour and water baked to the density of wood, weevil-ridden but durable. The hardtack had been stowed in the Essex’s bread room for months, absorbing moisture that made it harder rather than softer, until each biscuit had the consistency of seasoned oak. Weevils had tunneled through the flour, leaving tracks that were themselves edible protein, though men would pretend not to notice when they bit down on the bodies.

Chase broke one biscuit against the gunwale to test it; it rang like a ceramic tile. He counted the bags: sixty-five pounds per bag, eight bags in total, each divided three ways, each man’s portion weighed on a makeshift scale rigged from a handspike and line. The scale trembled with the wreck’s motion. Chase watched the pointer settle, then marked the amount in his mind. No paper would survive where they were going.

Water came next. The Essex had sailed with casks of varying capacity—fifteen-gallon breakers, forty-gallon casks, one sixty-gallon hogshead intended for the try-pots. The staves were oak, hooped with iron that already showed rust from the salt spray. Chase checked each bung for tightness, knowing that evaporation and leakage would consume their stores as surely as drinking. He calculated the surface area of water exposed to air, the rate of loss per day in tropical heat, subtracted this from the total, and arrived at a figure that allowed no margin for error. He directed men to roll the smaller casks up from the hold, bailing out what they could not save, hammering in bungs with any tool that came to hand. Each boat received one fifteen-gallon breaker and portions of the larger casks, measured by Chase’s dipper and thumbnail, entered into the mental ledger that would govern their survival.

Thomas Nickerson, the fourteen-year-old cabin boy, watched from the deck. In the account he would write fifty years later, Nickerson remembered particular desperation—reaching into the ship’s stores while the vessel died beneath them, men snatching at familiar objects as though theymight carry the whole of their former lives into twenty-three feet of cedar and oak. Nickerson would recall that they saved two Galápagos tortoises, creatures taken on at Hood’s Island for fresh meat, now pressed into service as living larders. Chase mentioned no tortoises in his official narrative; the discrepancy would linger in the record, a small shadow across the ledger.

The navigational instruments required more care than bread or water. Pollard gathered the sextant, the quadrant, the chronometers himself, distributing them among the three boats so that no single accident could rob them of all means of finding position. Each boat received a compass mounted in a wooden box with a glass face, and either a quadrant or sextant wrapped in oilcloth. The charts—those sheets marked with soundings and coastlines—were divided with the same care as the hardtack, each captain receiving copies of the relevant latitudes, the coast of South America inked in where the blank page of the ocean stretched otherwise uninterrupted.

George Pollard, twenty-eight years old, moved among his men with a deliberation that masked his fear. He had made the decision that morning: east, not west. Now came its implementation, the translation of choice into kilograms and liters, into the distribution of men among three fragile craft. He assigned himself to the second boat. Matthew Joy, the second mate, took the third. Chase, as first mate, commanded the first. The hierarchy held—captain, first mate, second mate—each with his boat, his men, his portion of the collective resources. This was the institutional reflex of Nantucket whaling, the discipline that had made the island’s fleet the most efficient in the world. It persisted now in extremis, as though the forms of order could somehow contain the chaos they were meant to govern.

The men were assigned with the same care as the provisions. Chase’s boat took six besides himself: Benjamin Lawrence, the twenty-three-year-old boatsteerer; Thomas Nickerson; and four others whose names appear in both accounts with slight variations in spelling. Pollard’s boat took six: Obed Hendricks, the boatsteerer; Owen Coffin, the captain’s cousin; and four more. Joy’s boat took the remaining six, including Charles Ramsdell and Samuel Reed. The distribution balanced experience against youth, strength against skill. Whether it succeeded would not be known for weeks.

The final items were the most telling. Chase recorded lines and hooks, harpoons repurposed as weapons against sharks, and two small pistols with powder and ball for signaling—or for whatever other purpose desperation might require. What he did not record was the weight of these objects in the boats now riding low in the water, gunwales within inches of the surface, or the silence that fell when the men realized they would not be going back aboard.

The wreck was still settling. Her stern had lifted slightly as the bow went down, and the deck tilted toward the starless water below. The men worked with the methodical patience of their trade, as though they were stripping a whale rather than abandoning their home. This was the ledger mentality made visible—not despair, but inventory; not prayer, but calculation.

By mid-afternoon, the loading was complete. Each boat carried approximately two hundred pounds of hard bread, divided into daily portions of about one pound per man. Each carried water sufficient for perhaps ten days at strict ration—half a pint per man per day, though Nickerson’s memory of the amounts varied slightly. Each carried its compass, its quadrant, its charts, its weapons, its captain. The total displacement pressed the freeboard to a hand’s breadth. A moderate sea would swamp them.

They cast off with something approaching ceremony. Chase’s narrative describes the moment with the restraint of a man still trying to comprehend it: they took leave of their unfortunate ship with as much feeling as men were capable of who had been preserved from death by a miraculous escape. The three boats lay on the water, oars shipped, while Pollard consulted with his officers one final time. The decision to sail east had been made. Now came its first material consequence: they must row clear of the wreck before the wind could carry them back upon it, must find separation from the dying ship that had been their only shelter in two thousand miles of ocean.

Chase took his bearing from the sun, noting the position in his memory—0°40’ south latitude, 119°0’ west longitude—a point where no chart showed land for a thousand miles in any direction. They formed a line, Chase leading, Pollard center, Joy trailing. The oars rose and fell in rough unison. The Essex settled deeper as they watched, her hull finally filling, her cargo dissolving into the Pacific that had sustained and destroyed her.

The blank page surrounded them. The Pacific stretched to every horizon, its surface disturbed only by the long swell from the southeast and the occasional dark fin of a shark. The sky was clear, the sun hot, the wind light. These were conditions for whaling, not for an open-boat voyage of three thousand miles against wind and current.

They rowed until dusk, putting perhaps twelve miles between themselves and the wreck. When Chase called a halt, they found themselves alone on the water, the Essex’s hull now visible only as a dark absence against the stars, marked by phosphorescence. They hove to, boats lying side by side in the swell, men taking turns at the oars to keep their bows into the wind.

Chase issued the first ration. He measured the water with his thumbnail against the dipper: half a pint per man. The tin cup passed from hand to hand. Then the bread: one piece of hardtack per man, counted out by Chase himself to ensure equality. The biscuit rang like ceramic when broken. Men held the pieces in their mouths to soften them with saliva before attempting to chew.

Nickerson would remember the sound of the bread breaking, the taste of the water flat and metallic with the cask’s lining. These details escaped Chase’s narrative, which moved from the fact of rationing to its result without dwelling on the sensation. The difference between the two accounts—Chase’s official record against Nickerson’s remembered experience—would become important later, when the story was told and retold, when Herman Melville read Chase’s narrative and imagined his own.

The makeshift scale Chase had rigged—a handspike balanced across a fulcrum of splintered spar, with canvas bags filled with shot as counterweights—trembled not only with the rhythmic settling of the wreck but with the mathematical cruelty of the calculation itself. Each division of hardtack required translation from weight to time: sixty-five pounds per bag divided by three boats, then by seven men per boat, then by the estimated days to Easter Island or the coast of Chile, whichever catastrophe arrived first. Chase performed these operations silently, his lips moving slightly as he converted avoirdupois into survival, subtracting leakage and evaporation and human error from totals that already permitted no margin.

When the scale tipped toward Lawrence’s boat by the weight of a single biscuit, Chase removed it, broke it precisely in half, and distributed the fragments with the impartiality of a clock mechanism. The Nantucket method was refined across generations here—the belief that equity could be weighed, measured, and recorded, that survival was fundamentally a problem of accounting rather than divine intervention.

Yet the numbers refused to resolve into comfort: at one pound per man per day, the hardtack would last twenty days, perhaps twenty-five if reduced to three-quarters rations, by which time they would need to have covered nearly a thousand miles to reach the southeast trade winds that might carry them toward salvation. The mathematics allowed no haste and no delay; the ledger demanded a velocity that human arms could not sustain, a perfect efficiency that flesh could not achieve.

Nickerson’s memory of the tortoises—two Galápagos creatures with shells like ancient shields and eyes that regarded the chaos with reptilian patience—introduced a spectral variable into Chase’s otherwise immaculate record. In Nickerson’s recollection, written half a century later when the taste of salt had returned to his dreams with hallucinatory clarity, these animals represented a crucial reserve, walking meat that could be slaughtered when the hardtack failed, their blood a source of moisture when the water turned brackish, their fat a means of making fire when the matches succumbed to humidity. Chase, writing his narrative for the ship’s owners and the maritime insurers of Nantucket, omitted them entirely, perhaps because living cargo did not fit the rigid categories of his manifest, or perhaps because acknowledging them would require acknowledging a contingency beyond his mathematical control.

The discrepancy haunted the historical record: two tortoises that either swam away when the boats cast off, or were never there, or were consumed so early in the voyage that they left no trace in the official memory. Their presence or absence became a test of credibility—Nickerson’s emotional truth against Chase’s institutional reportage—revealing how even the most meticulous inventory could not capture the full weight of what was being abandoned to the Pacific.

The distribution of the navigational instruments required a ceremony approaching religious reverence, each object representing a specific hope of converting chaos back into order. Pollard unwrapped the sextant himself, its brass limbs folded like the legs of some delicate crustacean, the mirrors still bright despite the humidity that had warped the Essex’s timbers. He assigned the chronometers—those pocket-sized mechanisms of longitude that ticked with the authority of Greenwich itself—to Chase’s boat and his own, keeping Joy’s vessel dependent on dead reckoning should disaster strike the senior officers.

Each quadrant was swaddled in oilcloth against the salt spray that would soon crust every surface with white rime; each compass was tested against the magnetic variation Chase had noted in his log during the Pacific crossing, verified against the sun’s azimuth while they still had deck beneath their feet. These instruments represented the last bridge between their frail cedar shells and the civilization that had produced them: without the sextant to measure the angle of the sun above the horizon, without the chronometer to calculate the hour angle against celestial time, they would become mere driftwood, subject to currents they could not measure and winds they could not predict.

The charts, copied by Chase from the Essex’s atlas onto scraps of canvas with charcoal and fish oil, showed the coast of Chile as a tentative line drawn against blank paper, the soundings marked with the confidence of explorers who had never imagined twenty men approaching in boats built for day-trips.

The harpoons presented a darker algebra that Chase recorded without commentary. Originally forged for the industrial murder of whales, their iron blades now served as deterrents against the sharks that circled the wreck with increasing boldness, drawn by the blood of crushed barnacles and the scent of human fear.

Chase distributed them with the awareness that they might serve other purposes before the voyage ended—uses that violated every code of the fishery but might yet preserve the lives of the users from worse fates.

The two pistols, wrapped in wool against moisture and packed in tin boxes, carried twelve charges each, powder and ball measured out with the same precision as the hardtack, each grain accounted for in Chase’s mental ledger. Officially they were signaling devices, meant to alert separated boats or passing ships; unofficially, they represented a final arbitration, a means of ending suffering or enforcing discipline when the institutional bonds began to fray under pressure of starvation. Lawrence checked the flints while Hendricks counted the balls, each man understanding that these weapons shifted the balance of power in ways that no manifest could record, introducing a potential for violence that lay dormant beneath the surface of their careful preparations.

Pollard’s assignment of men revealed the invisible calculus of sacrifice and protection that lay beneath the surface of maritime fairness. He placed his cousin Owen Coffin in his own boat—a decision that appeared nepotistic but was strategically defensive, keeping the eighteen-year-old under his direct authority rather than risking him to another captain’s judgment in moments of crisis. Chase received Benjamin Lawrence, whose skill with the steering oar might prove decisive in heavy seas, along with Nickerson, whose youth represented an investment in endurance despite his lack of experience.

Joy, the second mate, took Charles Ramsdell and Samuel Reed, men whose physical strength Pollard calculated against the boat’s displacement limits with the care of a shipwright loading cargo. Each distribution balanced seven bodies against the provisions: approximately twenty-one stone of human weight plus two hundred pounds of bread plus water at eight pounds per gallon plus iron and canvas and the intangible weight of hope. Chase estimated the freeboard—the distance from waterline to gunwale—at less than six inches when fully loaded, a margin that mathematics could not stretch but might, with sufficient seamanship and fortune, suffice to keep the ocean outside the boat.

The boats rocked in the darkness. Twenty men lay in their bottoms, wrapped in whatever canvas they had salvaged, listening to the water lap against cedar strakes that had never been meant to bear them across an ocean. The institutional reflex had produced an orderly division that belied the desperation of their situation. The ledger was open. The first entries had been made: water consumed, bread consumed, distance covered against distance remaining.

They were twenty men in three open boats, provisioned for perhaps a month, facing a voyage of three months or more. The meticulous preparation was complete. The grimly practical logic of their situation now governed them completely. They had converted ship into boats, abundance into scarcity, certainty into calculation. The boats were loaded and cast off.
