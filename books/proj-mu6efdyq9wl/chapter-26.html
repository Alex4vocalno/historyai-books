Chapter 26

The Unquiet Pen

Seen from above, the island of Manhattan in 1821 reveals itself as a vast, unfinished engine for the conversion of paper into profit. Its fixed grid, radiating outward from the harbor, is a logic of leases, lots, and mortgages. The financial currents flow from the Battery, channeling past the hotels and theaters into the dense cluster of booksellers and printers at Broadway and Pearl. Here, the pulse of the trade is measured in the urgent intervals between a ship’s arrival and a pamphlet’s printing, between a scandal’s emergence and a narrative’s sale. In this city, where speed is its own revenue, a book’s necessity is weighed in profit.

Down from this aerial view, through the layers of the trade—compositors setting type by candlelight, pressmen working the lever, binders stitching signatures—descends a single object: a fresh copy of Narrative of the Most Extraordinary and Distressing Shipwreck of the Whale-Ship Essex, its title page bearing the name Owen Chase. The book is small, octavo, printed on ordinary paper, priced at thirty-seven and a half cents. It carries the date 1821 and the imprint of W.B. Gilley, New York. The author is identified as a first mate of Nantucket origin. The title promises extraordinariness and distress. The preface, dated November 1821, explains that Chase wrote the account soon after his return and that publication was delayed only by his absence at sea. The delay was brief. Chase returned to Nantucket in June 1821. By November, the book exists.

The publication of Owen Chase’s Narrative of the Most Extraordinary and Distressing Shipwreck of the Whale-Ship Essex in New York in 1821 introduced a new and critical piece of evidence into the narrative chain. The process by which the account was written, edited, and published reveals its tone, its strategic omissions and emphases—particularly regarding Chase’s own role versus Pollard’s—and its intended audience.

The book is a mechanism. Its inner workings reveal a survivor’s calculation: how to transform eighty-nine days of starvation, cannibalism, and moral collapse into a coherent public story. Chase’s narrative operates in a different register from his sworn testimony before the Port Wardens of Nantucket. The testimony was procedural, constrained by questions and the need for legal caution; the book is dramatic, even theatrical. The whale that rammed the Essex is not merely described; it is characterized. Chase writes of the malignity and vengeance of the animal, of its deliberate attack, of its “fury.” These are not the words of a witness but of a storyteller who understands that readers require emotion to accompany information.

The mechanism begins with the author himself. Owen Chase, twenty-four years old in 1821, five feet ten inches tall, dark complexion, had already shipped out again by the time the book appeared. In December 1821 he signed as first mate on the whaler Florida, sailing from New Bedford on the twentieth of that month. The crew list provides the only extant physical description: a young man still, despite what he had seen and done. The book was written in the interval between return and departure, perhaps dictated to a collaborator—Chase’s education was limited, and the prose shows the polish of professional assistance—perhaps drafted in his own hand and then revised for the press. The preface claims the narrative was written by himself, but the trade of 1821 made no sharp distinction between author and amanuensis. What mattered was the name on the title page, the guarantee of authenticity.

The strategic omissions begin with the captain. George Pollard, Jr., master of the Essex, appears in Chase’s narrative as a diminished figure. The decision to sail east for South America rather than west for the Marquesas or Society Islands—a decision that condemned the crew to ninety days in open boats—is attributed to Pollard’s authority. Chase records his own dissent, his preference for steering westward. But he does not press the point. The narrative preserves the forms of deference while allowing the reader to perceive the first mate’s superior judgment. Pollard’s later testimony, his own account of the voyage, would tell a different story. But Pollard published nothing in 1821. Chase’s version, printed and distributed, became the first fixed shape of the disaster.

The cannibalism is handled with similar care. Chase describes the drawing of lots on his own boat, the eating of Owen Coffin’s body, the survival of the four men who remained. He does not linger on the physical particulars, the ceremonies of partition. These details would emerge only in the later, unofficial accounts. What Chase provides is moral justification: the lots were drawn fairly, the sacrifice was accepted, the survivors were driven by necessity beyond the reach of ordinary law. The narrative transforms an act that might destroy a reputation into an episode of stoic endurance. The first mate emerges not as a murderer but as a man who did what was required.

The book’s intended audience is suggested by its price and its publisher. Thirty-seven and a half cents placed it within reach of artisans and clerks, not merely the wealthy. W.B. Gilley specialized in American productions, in the rapid capitalization of local news. The Essex disaster had already been reported in the newspapers; Chase’s narrative offered the authoritative inside account, the survivor’s own words. The book was a marketable commodity, a personal testament, and an act of professional insurance. The first mate who had lost his ship, who had eaten his cousin, who had survived when others died, required a public identity that would secure his future employment. The book made him an author, a figure of known qualities, rather than a name on a crew list with a suspicious gap.

The consequences for each party were immediate and unequal. Chase’s reputation was established. He would sail again, and again, eventually commanding his own vessels. The narrative had done its work: it presented him as competent, decisive, and morally intact. Pollard, who had no book, suffered a different fate. He was given another command, the Two Brothers, and lost that ship as well, wrecked on a reef in the Pacific in 1823. The double disaster ended his career. Whether Chase’s narrative contributed to this eclipse is impossible to determine with certainty. But the absence of Pollard’s version, the silence where his book should have been, allowed Chase’s account to fill the space of public memory.

The contrast with Thomas Nickerson’s manuscript illuminates what Chase achieved. Nickerson, the cabin boy, also wrote an account—decades later, in retirement, for no audience but himself. His manuscript remained unknown until the twentieth century. Chase’s narrative is polished, shaped for effect; Nickerson’s is rough, episodic, sometimes contradictory. Nickerson remembers Chase as a man of fortitude and resolution, but he also records details that Chase suppressed: the quarrels between the boats, the suspicion that Chase’s boat had taken more than its share of provisions, the particular horror of eating a shipmate whose face was still recognizable. These are not the materials of public consumption. Nickerson wrote for himself; Chase wrote for the market. The market chose its text.

The reception of Chase’s book can be traced in the newspapers and in the records of the trade. It was reviewed, favorably, as a document of extraordinary interest. It was reprinted, in whole or in extract, in periodicals on both sides of the Atlantic. It entered the circulating libraries, those engines of middle-class reading. By 1822, the Narrative was established as the authoritative source, the book to consult for the true history of the Essex. The raw events of 1820–1821 became history and myth within months of their conclusion, driven by a survivor’s agency rather than by fate or accident.

The act of writing itself was a tool for control and legacy. Chase, sitting with his manuscript in the months after his return, was not merely recording but arguing. Each line, each date, each characterization of the whale’s malignity was a choice in the construction of a public self. The first mate who had already begun to write his second life—in the sense of a life after the disaster, and perhaps in the sense of a second draft—understood that paper outlasted memory, that the printed word could fix a version of events against the erosion of time and rival testimony.

The book’s influence extended beyond its immediate readers. It shaped the early press reports, which quoted or paraphrased its account. It solidified certain episodes in the popular imagination: the whale’s deliberate attack, the malignity of its purpose, the almost human intelligence of its vengeance. These were Chase’s constructions, not necessary inferences from the event. A whale rams a ship; the ship sinks. The interpretation of this as deliberate, as vengeful, required a narrator willing to project intention onto the animal. Chase provided that narrator, and his projection would persist.

The transformation from testimony to narrative required more than mere transcription; it demanded a theory of causation that the courtroom had not required. In his sworn statement before the Port Wardens, Chase had answered questions about longitude and latitude, about the stowage of casks and the timing of signals. The book, by contrast, needed to explain why the Essex had been destroyed, and Chase found his explanation in the character of the whale itself.

The animal that struck the ship was, in his account, no ordinary sperm whale—not the lumbering, blubber-laden target that Nantucket’s fleet pursued by the thousands, but something singular, almost demonic. Chase describes its approach with the deliberation of a predator, its withdrawal and return, the “fury” of its final assault. This was not zoology; it was dramaturgy.

The whale became an actor with motives, capable of intention, and its intention was vengeful. The effect was to relocate the disaster’s origin from human error or natural hazard to a confrontation between man and a hostile intelligence. Chase thus positioned himself not as the victim of a maritime accident but as the survivor of an encounter with something monstrous and purposeful.

The construction of this whale served multiple functions for its architect. It answered the question that every reader would eventually ask: how could a wooden ship be destroyed by an animal? The answer—because this was no ordinary animal—preserved the dignity of the Essex’s crew and the reputation of Nantucket’s seamanship. A ship sunk by accident could be a ship poorly commanded; a ship assaulted by a monster was a ship overwhelmed by the exceptional.

Chase’s whale also satisfied the appetite of the reading public for the marvelous, that taste for natural wonders and prodigies that had sustained the sales of captivity narratives and earthquake accounts since the previous century. The Narrative thus operated in two registers simultaneously: as a document of authentic suffering, verified by the survivor’s name on the title page, and as a tale of the extraordinary, promising readers an experience beyond the ordinary bounds of maritime life. This dual character was essential to its commercial success and to its survival as a text.

The process of publication itself left traces in the physical book. The octavo format, the ordinary paper, the absence of illustrations or ornamental typography—all suggest haste and economy, the determination to reach market before the news grew stale.

Yet certain features indicate deliberation. The preface, with its careful dating and its explanation of delay, anticipates skepticism and answers it. The table of contents, unusually detailed for so brief a work, guides the reader through the narrative’s phases: the departure, the accident, the separation of the boats, the sufferings, the rescue. This architecture, invisible to the casual reader, reveals a constructed experience, a journey through controlled revelations.

The cannibalism, when it arrives, has been prepared for by accounts of hunger and despair; it emerges as the inevitable term of a sequence rather than as a sensational interruption. Chase or his collaborators understood that the book’s most dangerous material required the most careful staging.

The comparison between Chase’s published narrative and his earlier testimony illuminates the work of revision. Where the Port Wardens’ record is sparse, almost telegraphic, the book expands each episode into scene and consequence. The moment of the whale’s attack, described in the testimony in a few sentences of damage assessment, becomes in the narrative a set piece of suspense: the animal’s first appearance, its disappearance, the crew’s false relief, the catastrophic return. Time dilates; perspective narrows to the first mate’s own consciousness. This is not deception but art, the art of making experience communicable. Yet the art serves interest. Chase’s own actions in the critical hours—his management of the boats, his preservation of discipline, his calculations of distance and provision—receive emphasis that the testimony did not require. The book constructs a heroism that the legal record merely assumed.

The silence surrounding the book’s actual composition suggests the social conditions of its production. Chase was not a gentleman author; he was a working sailor with, at most, a common-school education. The prose of the Narrative, with its balanced periods and its controlled emotional vocabulary, bears the marks of assistance—whether from a professional writer, from a clergyman, or from one of the amanuenses who served the trade.

The preface’s insistence that the account was “written by himself” may be technically true, in the sense that Chase supplied the substance, but it obscures the collaborative nature of early nineteenth-century publication. What matters for the argument is not the precise division of labor but the authorization that Chase’s name provided. The survivor’s guarantee, the promise that these events were witnessed and endured, was the commodity that W.B. Gilley purchased and sold. The book’s authenticity was a function of its attribution, not of its unmediated transmission.

The strategic diminishment of Pollard operated through a grammar of indirect statement. Chase records his own advice to steer west; he records Pollard’s decision to steer east; he does not explicitly judge between them. The narrative’s power lies in this restraint, in the confidence that readers will draw the proper inference. The captain appears as well-meaning but mistaken, committed to a course that the first mate had foreseen as fatal. This construction preserved the formal hierarchy of shipboard command while subverting its substance. Pollard, reading the book, would have recognized the technique; his later testimony, when it finally emerged, would attempt the same reversal, presenting his own decisions as considered and Chase’s dissent as impulsive. But Pollard’s testimony would never achieve print in his lifetime. The asymmetry of publication—Chase’s voice amplified, Pollard’s suppressed—determined the shape of the disaster’s memory.

The handling of Owen Coffin’s death reveals the moral calculus that governed Chase’s selections. The cabin boy, Pollard’s cousin, had drawn the fatal lot on the captain’s boat; his execution and consumption were the episode most likely to excite horror and condemnation. Chase describes the event with a spareness that approaches the liturgical: the lots were drawn, the sacrifice was made, the survivors were sustained. He does not describe Coffin’s face, his words, his compliance or resistance. These absences are themselves eloquent, the marks of a writer who knew where description would damage and where it might console.

Nickerson’s later manuscript, by contrast, preserves the particularity that Chase suppressed: the recognition of the victim, the familiarity of his features, the specific horror of eating one who had been a shipmate and a friend. Nickerson wrote without the pressure of publication; his account could accommodate contradiction and pain without resolution.

Chase’s narrative, shaped for strangers, required the appearance of resolution, the transformation of atrocity into necessity.

The book’s price point—thirty-seven and a half cents—placed it in a specific segment of the market, below the three-dollar volumes of history and biography, above the penny broadsides of ephemeral news. This was the territory of the “useful and entertaining,” the literature of improvement and sensation that filled the shelves of the circulating libraries. The Narrative thus entered into competition with tales of Indian captivity, accounts of shipwreck and piracy, the narratives of sailors who had visited exotic lands.

Its distinction was its immediacy: the disaster had occurred months, not years, before publication; the author was not a retired veteran but a young man still in the trade. This contemporaneity justified the book’s claim to authentic instruction, its promise that readers would learn something essential about the dangers of whaling and the resources of human endurance.

The instruction was moral as well as practical: the book demonstrated that even in extremity, order could be preserved, discipline maintained, civilization sustained against the pressure of nature and appetite.

The geographical circulation of the Narrative extended its authority beyond its initial readership. Extracts appeared in newspapers from Boston to Charleston, often without attribution, as if the facts spoke for themselves. The book was reviewed in the North American Review and the American Journal of Science, publications that conferred intellectual respectability. British editions followed, adapting Chase’s American prose for transatlantic consumption. Each reprinting, each quotation, each casual reference reinforced the narrative’s status as the source, the account to which all others must refer. By 1822, a writer seeking to describe the Essex disaster would naturally turn to Chase; alternative versions, if they existed, had no comparable accessibility. The mechanism of reproduction had produced a canon.

The persistence of certain phrases from Chase’s text in later accounts demonstrates the depth of its penetration.

The structural irony is that Chase’s narrative, for all its claims of authenticity, was itself a kind of fiction: a selection, an arrangement, a heightening. The accounting mentality of Nantucket whaling—its reduction of all value to quantifiable entries—finds its literary equivalent in Chase’s careful distribution of credit and blame. The void of the Pacific, that space empty of human marks, begins to be overwritten by his traumatic testimony. These patterns emerge from the facts: the price of the book, the name on the title page, the contrast with Nickerson’s suppressed manuscript, the fate of Pollard’s unanswered voice.

By 1822, the mechanism has completed its work. Chase’s book is now the public canon of the disaster, a settled text that will soon fall into the hands of a sailor-turned-writer who will read it not for facts but for truth.
