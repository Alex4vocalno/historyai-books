Chapter 19

The Sickness in the Salt

In February 1821, the rescue was complete, though the aftermath had only begun. It was not fifty years later, when the last survivor would die in his bed on Cape Cod, but now: the men who had eaten their shipmates to stay alive lay in clean bunks aboard the whaleship Dauphin, and their bodies were trying to kill them.

Owen Chase, first mate of the sunken Essex, had kept seventeen men alive through eighty-nine days of open-boat navigation. He had rationed bread, calculated currents, buried the dead, and watched his own cousin draw the black spot that meant he must die. Now, in a cabin that smelled of whale oil and tar instead of salt and human decay, Chase could not keep down a mouthful of ship’s biscuit. The swelling had started in his ankles. By the third day, his legs had ballooned with fluid, the skin shiny and taut, pressing against the wool blanket a Nantucket sailor had tucked around him. He was twenty-three years old. His body had learned to consume itself, and it would not stop.

The Dauphin’s log, kept by her master Captain Zimri Coffin, records the rescue on February 23, 1821, in position 33°45’S, 81°03’W. The entry notes the sighting of a boat and the recovery of men who proved to be part of the crew of the ship Essex, stove and sunk by a whale. What the log does not record, because ship’s logs do not measure such things, is what happened when the rescued men tried to eat.

Captain George Pollard, who had commanded the Essex and watched his own nephew Owen Coffin executed by lot, could not swallow solid food. His throat had closed against it. The Dauphin’s crew, experienced in the ways of the sea, knew what to do with men pulled from wreckage. They forced milk down their throats. They administered gruel. They watched the survivors vomit it back up, or worse, retain it in swollen guts that had forgotten how to process nourishment.

Thomas Nickerson, the cabin boy who had been fourteen when the whale struck, later wrote that the survivors suffered from a dropsical complaint that affected them all to varying degrees. Chase’s account, published that same year in New York soon after his return to Nantucket, describes the edema more clinically: the swelling of limbs, the distension of abdomen, the urine that came scanty and high-colored. The two men agree on the symptoms but diverge on their severity. Chase, writing for a public that would judge his command decisions, emphasizes his own rapid recovery and his efforts to assist the others. Nickerson, writing decades later for a private audience, remembers Chase as equally afflicted, lying in his bunk while the Dauphin bore east toward the coast of Chile.

The discrepancy matters because both men were constructing evidence. Chase’s 1821 narrative, rushed into print by W.B. Gilley while the story still held the public’s horrified attention, needed to establish its author as competent, resilient, a man who could suffer and still lead. Nickerson’s 1876 memoir, discovered in an attic and published by the Nantucket Historical Association more than a century later, had no such burden. It could afford to remember weakness. The truth, as it usually does, likely sits between them: Chase was both functional and broken, managing the navigation while his body betrayed him, calculating the Dauphin’s position while his mind replayed the drawing of lots on February 6, when his cousin Owen Coffin had accepted the black spot with what Chase described as great fortitude and submission.

The Dauphin was not a hospital ship. She was a Nantucket whaleship, three months out, with 1, 200 barrels of oil in her hold and a crew that needed to keep hunting. Captain Zimri Coffin—no relation to the dead Owen Coffin, though the name must have struck the survivors like a bell—had responsibilities that did not pause for eight wrecked men. The whaleship’s routine continued: the lookouts at the mastheads, the boats lowered when spout was sighted, the tryworks burning through the night to render blubber into gold. Against this industrial rhythm, the Essex survivors lay in their bunks like specimens of another species, their bodies marked by experiences that the Dauphin’s crew could imagine but not share.

What happened to a man who had consumed human flesh? The question hung in the cabin air, unspoken but present. The Dauphin’s sailors had heard rumors of such things. Every whaleship carried stories of the South Seas, of savages, of shipwrecks and desperate measures. But here were men they knew, or knew of: Pollard from a respectable Nantucket family, Chase with his reputation for seamanship, the cabin boy Nickerson who should have been learning his trade instead of surviving atrocity. The survivors were evidence of something the whaling industry preferred not to examine too closely. They were proof that the ledger mentality—that reduction of all value to quantifiable entries—had limits that broke against human flesh.

The swelling worsened. Chase recorded that several of the men became so swollen that they were unable to put on their clothes. Their bodies, having consumed muscle and organ tissue to stay alive, now retained fluid in rebellion against rehydration. The medical understanding of the day, filtered through shipboard practice, recognized the condition as dropsy but could not explain its mechanism. We now know that prolonged starvation depletes the body of protein, reducing the osmotic pressure that keeps fluid in blood vessels; when food returns, the fluid leaks into tissues, causing edema. The survivors were drowning from the inside, their cells awash in water they could not process.

Captain Pollard suffered additional injuries that the records note but do not fully explain. Nickerson mentions that Pollard’s feet had been much frozen during the open-boat ordeal, and that the frostbite wounds reopened and festered in the warmth of the cabin. The captain who had navigated 4, 500 miles of Pacific Ocean, who had kept his boat alive while others died, could not walk without pain. He lay in his bunk and listened to the Dauphin’s crew work the ship, the familiar commands passing overhead in a language he still understood but could not join.

The psychological symptoms were harder to name than the physical ones. Chase writes of a great debility of body and mind that afflicted them all, but his narrative quickly moves past this to the practical business of recovery. Nickerson is more expansive, remembering a state of mental depression that was worse than any bodily suffering. The cabin boy had watched three men draw lots on Pollard’s boat, had seen Owen Coffin shot by his own crewmates, had helped consume the body. Now, in the relative safety of the Dauphin, he could not sleep without seeing those faces. The ocean’s blank page, that void which had refused to be written upon with their suffering, had inscribed itself on his mind.

The Dauphin’s surgeon, if she carried one—the records do not name him—would have recognized some of what he saw. Ship’s medicine in 1821 had experience with scurvy, with dysentery, with the injuries of the whale fishery. But no manual prepared a man for the Essex survivors. Their condition was not in the books because their ordeal was not supposed to happen. Nantucket whaling had built an elaborate system for extracting value from the ocean: the ships, the tryworks, the cooperages, the counting houses on Main Street. That system assumed that men could be replaced, that losses could be insured, that risk could be calculated and distributed. It did not account for what happened when the calculation failed, when the whale struck back, when the open boats became floating laboratories of human extremity.

The forced feeding continued. The Dauphin’s crew, following the best practice they knew, administered light nutritious diet in small quantities, gradually increasing as the patients could tolerate it. Chase records that this approach eventually succeeded, that by degrees they recovered strength and were enabled to take food with some appetite. But the phrase conceals as much as it reveals. How many days? How many nights of waking to the sensation of drowning in one’s own skin? How many moments when the mind, released from the immediate struggle for survival, turned to inventory what it had done to survive?

The Dauphin was sailing for Valparaíso, the Chilean port where Nantucket whaleships reprovisioned and where American consuls could arrange passage home. The voyage would take two weeks, perhaps more, depending on wind and current. For the Essex survivors, each day brought new humiliations of the flesh. Chase, ever the navigator, asked for paper and calculated their position, finding comfort in the mathematics that had kept him alive. Pollard lay in silence, his frostbitten feet wrapped in bandages that needed changing. Nickerson, youngest of the survivors, walked the deck when he could stand, trying to reacquaint his legs with solid planks after months of thwarts and gunwales.

Other survivors from other boats were elsewhere on other ships. Benjamin Lawrence, second mate, had been rescued by the Dromo on February 26, three days after Chase and Pollard. Charles Ramsdell, who had drawn lots with Pollard and lived, was with him on the Dromo. Thomas Chappel, Seth Weeks, and William Wright—the three men who had chosen to stay on Henderson Island rather than continue in the boats—had been found alive by the Australian ship Surry and were even now working as crew members on their rescue vessel, making their slow way toward England and eventual dispersal. The Essex company, twenty men who had sailed together from Nantucket in August 1819, was scattered across five thousand miles of Pacific Ocean, bound for ports that would receive them not as heroes but as problems.

The Dauphin’s log continues its impersonal record: February 24, employed getting ready for sea; February 25, light winds and pleasant weather; February 26, saw a sail bearing NW. The rescue of Lawrence and Ramsdell appears on February 27, entered in the same hand, with the same economy: at 11 AM spoke ship Dromo and found she had on board two men more of the Essex crew. The Dauphin and Dromo would meet again in Valparaíso, their captains comparing notes, their surgeons comparing patients. The system was beginning to reassert itself, to gather information, to prepare the reports that would eventually reach Nantucket and New London and the insurance offices on State Street.

But the survivors could not yet be absorbed into that system. Their bodies resisted. Chase records that even as the swelling subsided, a languor and feebleness continued long after they had apparently recovered. This was not merely physical. The languor was the cost of consciousness itself, the weight of memory that no amount of ship’s biscuit could restore. The men had done things that Nantucket’s ledger mentality could not account for. They had reduced human life to its most literal commodity, had consumed their shipmates to extend their own days, had made the calculations that survival required. Now they must learn to live with those calculations, to carry them into a world that would ask uncomfortable questions about how they had been made.

The Dauphin crossed the Tropic of Capricorn on March 3, 1821. The log notes fine weather and fresh breezes. Below deck, Owen Chase was finally keeping down solid food, though his ankles still showed the marks of edema, pale indentations that would remain for months. Captain Pollard had managed to stand, leaning on a sailor’s arm, to look at the horizon that had nearly killed him. Thomas Nickerson had begun to write in a journal, practicing the letters that would eventually record his testimony. The three tracks of their recovery—physical, psychological, documentary—were moving at different speeds, and would not synchronize for years.

What did they talk about? The records do not say. Chase’s narrative, published within months, maintains the decorum of disaster literature: the facts of navigation, the statistics of suffering, the thanks to rescuers. Nickerson’s memoir, written in old age, remembers conversations about home, about the families who did not yet know if they were alive, about whether they would ever go to sea again. The silence between these accounts is itself evidence. There were things that could not be spoken in 1821, when the story was still being contested, and things that could only be spoken in 1876, when all the principals were dead.

The Dauphin made her landfall on March 8, entering the harbor of Valparaíso with her strange cargo: eight men who were simultaneously survivors and witnesses, patients and evidence. The American consul, William Henry Allen, would receive them. The local physicians would examine them. The captains of other Nantucket ships, reprovisioning in port, would visit their bunks and carry news home. The machinery of reception was beginning to turn, to process the Essex disaster into the forms that institutions required: depositions, insurance claims, newspaper accounts, cautionary tales.

But first, there were the bodies. Chase writes that even upon arrival in Valparaíso, they remained very weak and emaciated. Nickerson remembers being carried ashore on sailors’ backs, his legs unable to support him after the short boat ride from ship to wharf. The city that greeted them—Spanish colonial architecture climbing hills above a natural harbor, the Pacific’s great port before the rise of San Francisco—must have seemed hallucinatory after months of empty horizon. Streets. Buildings. Women in dresses. Food that did not need to be rationed. The shock of return was as disorienting as the shock of wreck.

The medical examination found what it expected to find: malnutrition, dehydration, the sequelae of exposure. What it could not find, because no stethoscope or thermometer could measure it, was the transformation that had occurred in the survivors’ relationship to the world. They had seen the ocean’s indifference made concrete. They had acted upon that indifference, making choices that no training had prepared them for. They had become, in effect, a new kind of human being: the kind who knows what he will do to survive, and must live with that knowledge.

The Dauphin’s crew watched them go. These sailors who had pulled men from a whaleboat in the empty Pacific, who had forced milk down swollen throats and changed bandages on frostbitten feet, returned to their proper work. The tryworks were lit again. The boats were lowered again. The Dauphin would hunt whales for another year before turning for home, her hold full of oil, her log full of positions and weather and sightings. The rescue would be one entry among hundreds, notable only because of what followed.

Chase would publish his narrative. Pollard would return to sea, commanding another ship that would also wreck, confirming in Nantucket minds that he was cursed or incompetent or both. Nickerson would disappear into the ordinary life of whalemen, rising to boatsteerer, then mate, eventually captain, carrying his secret memoir through decades of ordinary disasters. The three Henderson Island survivors—Chappel, Weeks, and Wright—would make their way to England, then disperse: Chappel to die of plague fever in Timor, Wright to be lost in a West Indies hurricane about 1834, Weeks to retire to Cape Cod and outlive them all by decades, dying on September 12, 1887.

But that was later. In March 1821, they were simply sick men in a strange port, their bodies stabilized but their minds still adrift in the Pacific. The Dauphin had carried them across the ocean’s blank page, had delivered them to the institutions that would try to make sense of what had happened. But the page was not blank anymore. It was inscribed with their decay, their choices, their survival at costs that would compound across years. They had been rescued from the sea. They had not been rescued from what the sea had made them.
