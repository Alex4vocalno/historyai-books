Chapter 23

The Long Voyage Home

Seen from above, the great commercial circuit of the Southern Ocean is a fourteen-thousand-mile belt of wind and current, its shipping lanes arching from Valparaíso toward Cape Horn like a conveyor for merchant vessels. In May of 1821, this system processes copper, hides, and silver alongside the occasional human cargo of broken sailors. Against the Pacific gray, the brig Eagle appears as a speck of new canvas, her hold stuffed with ordinary freight but her decks carrying something volatile: four survivors of the whaleship Essex, stowed like fragile cargo. The remaining survivors travel on later vessels, each group sealed in a liminal passage between catastrophe and a reckoning waiting at forty-one degrees north.

Below decks, among the sea chests that contain their only salvage from the wreck, rests the Essex’s compass: a brass instrument no larger than a dinner plate, its glass spider-webbed with cracks, its needle still trembling toward magnetic north. Owen Chase wrapped it in oilcloth before the first whaleboat separated from the others; he carried it through ninety days of open-boat navigation, through the drawing of lots and the consumption of the dead, and now it sits beside his ink and paper like a witness to failed command.

The compass is joined by three rusty spades, a coil of line, and a water cask patched with lead—objects that constitute the entire physical residue of a two-hundred-and-thirty-eight-ton whaleship and her twenty crew. Together they form a portable archive of disaster, a ledger of what was lost rendered in metal and wood, the only tangible proof that the Essex ever existed beyond the memories of eight men.

Chase writes. He occupies a narrow berth near the steerage, his knee serving as a desk, scratching his narrative into a leather-bound book with the methodical precision of a man settling accounts. He records latitudes and longitudes, the dimensions of the whale that stove the ship, the sequence of deaths with dates attached. He does not write of dreams or of the taste of salt pork turned rancid; he writes of currents, of sail adjustments, of the exact moment when Barzillai Ray expired in the second boat.

The writing is a form of armor, a way of converting the chaotic violence of the past months into navigable prose. While other survivors lie in their hammocks staring at the deck beams above them, Chase fills page after page with the practical determination that will see him captain another vessel within sixteen months, departing again in August for a three-and-a-half-year voyage while his new wife remains ashore.

The other sailors aboard the Eagle regard the four survivors with a mixture of horrified fascination and superstitious dread. In the fo’c’sle, seamen gather to gamble and speak in low voices of cannibalism, watching to see if Chase flinches at the sight of salt beef or if Thomas Nickerson—the youngest survivor at fifteen—still wakes screaming. The eight are quarantined by reputation if not by bulkhead, bound together by an experience that makes them incomprehensible to the uninitiated.

When the cook serves pea soup, thick and green, two sailors push their plates away and glance toward Chase’s table; Chase eats steadily, measuring his rations with the same calculation he used when dividing ounces of hardtack among dying men. He has become an object lesson in the cost of Pacific whaling, a walking demonstration of what happens when the industrial hunger for oil collides with the oceanic wilderness it seeks to dominate.

George Pollard travels on a different ship, departing Valparaíso two weeks later. Where Chase bends over his manuscript, Pollard sits motionless against the rail, watching the wake dissolve behind the stern without recording a word. He has not touched pen to paper since signing his testimony at the consular inquiry aboard the Bachelor’s Delight. He carries no salvage from the boats, no relics of command; his hands are empty, folded in his lap as if holding something invisible that grows heavier with each nautical mile northward.

The crew of this second vessel give him wide berth, whispering when he passes that this is the Jonah who lost his ship to a whale and then drew lots to eat his own cousin. They are wrong about the relationship—Henry De Witt was crew, not kin—but correct about the curse. Pollard does not correct them. He eats little, speaks less, and stands his watch in silence, a captain without a ship practicing the posture of authority over an empty quarterdeck.

Between these two vessels stretches the gulf of the Pacific, and beneath their keels lies the haunted seascape they have crossed twice: once outbound in search of oil, once drifting eastward in boats that grew lighter as men died. Now they traverse it a third time, not as conquerors of the wilderness but as survivors carrying its mark. The Eagle rounds Cape Horn in late May, beating against westerlies that scream through the rigging with the same voice that filled their whaleboats during the worst of the starvation.

Chase seals his manuscript in oilcloth and writes an address: to be published in New York by W.B. Gilley. He includes no dedication, no plea for understanding, only the facts as he has recorded them, beginning with his departure from Nantucket on August 12, 1819, and ending with his rescue by the Dauphin on February 18, 1821. The narrative is already a commodity, a product to be exchanged for money and reputation even before he has seen his daughter.

He drafts letters to the families of the deceased—careful, formal paragraphs explaining that lots were drawn fairly, that the deaths were necessary, that duty was performed. He does not mention that he tasted human flesh, though every reader will infer it. He signs his name with a flourish that betrays no tremor, addressing envelopes to widows who have already received the news via faster packets that outran the sailing ships. These letters precede the men by weeks, carrying confirmation of rumors that have circulated through Nantucket’s coffee houses and insurance offices since April. Nancy Hussey receives word that her husband has survived; Peggy Pollard learns that her brother Owen Coffin drew the short straw; mothers discover which sons became sustenance for others. The town holds its breath, calculating grief as it calculates interest, preparing for the arrival of men who have been dead for months yet still walk.

In Nantucket town, the infrastructure of reception prepares itself with the cold efficiency of the insurance market. The ship’s owners, Gideon Folger and Sons, have already tallied their losses: the Essex valued at complete loss, the oil cargo never recovered, the wages paid to men now dead. They wait not for husbands but for data, for the logbooks that might prove negligence or validate claims. In the houses on Center Street and Orange Street, widows pace floors that will never again be crossed by Matthew Joy or Seth Weeks or Samuel Reed. The Pacific Club, where owners discuss premiums over Madeira, buzzes with speculation about whether Pollard’s decision to sail east rather than west toward the Marquesas constituted actionable poor judgment, or whether the entire disaster falls under the category of unavoidable peril.

The Eagle makes her landfall on June 11, 1821. A large crowd has gathered at the wharf despite the early hour, drawn by newspaper accounts that have preceded the ship up the coast. They watch the survivors descend the gangplank with the stiff gait of men who have forgotten how to walk on stationary ground.

Chase steps ashore first, carrying his sea chest in one hand and his manuscript in the other. He is met not by his wife—he married Eunice Chadwick on April 5, before departing Valparaíso—but by a nurse holding a fourteen-month-old girl named Phoebe whom he has never seen. He looks at the child with the same measuring gaze he applies to navigation, then takes her in his arms with a competence that surprises the onlookers.

The crowd presses forward, eager for details, for horror, for the confirmation of rumors they have heard in taverns. Chase gives them nothing but his presence, moving through the throng toward a waiting carriage while behind him Nickerson and Ramsdell and Lawrence receive their own receptions, smaller and more muted.

Pollard arrives weeks later, stepping onto the same wharf to a silence that feels like accusation. He has no child to meet him, no wife—his fiancée released him from their engagement after news of the cannibalism reached Nantucket through early dispatches. He carries only his sea bag and the weight of command that ended in open boats. The crowd that greeted Chase with morbid curiosity parts for Pollard with something like fear, as if disaster were contagious and might spread from his clothes into the cobblestones. He walks alone up Main Street toward his father’s house, passing the chandlers who once outfitted his command, past the shipyards where new whaleships stand ready for Pacific voyages. He does not stop to speak with anyone. He has become a doomed captain, a Jonah whom no owner will trust with a vessel, marked by the same superstition that governs sailors’ luck.

Through July and August, the two men move through Nantucket like ghosts haunting their own lives. Chase marries formally in a ceremony that serves as both wedding and homecoming celebration, then immediately begins outfitting for another voyage. He visits the shipyard daily, inspecting provisions with the same attention he once gave to water casks in a whaleboat. He has calculated that he can support his new family only by returning to sea immediately; there is no land-based trade that pays wages sufficient to offset the loss of his Essex shares. On August 12—the anniversary of his original departure—he signs articles for a three-and-a-half-year cruise to the Pacific grounds. He leaves before autumn turns the harbor cold, chasing the oil that nearly killed him with the same pragmatic determination that carried him through the cannibalism.

Pollard remains. He takes rooms above his father’s counting house and sits by the window watching ships depart, counting the days until November 20, when he will fast in memory of those he could not save. He does not apply for another whaling command; he knows already that he has become a walking symbol of maritime ill-fortune. When he walks the streets, children follow at a distance, chanting rhymes about whales and cannibals that have sprung up in his absence. He answers no letters, accepts no invitations, speaks only to his sister and to Thomas Nickerson, who visits occasionally to sit in silence beside him.

The eight survivors form a closed circuit of memory. They meet rarely in public but send messages through intermediaries, sharing information about pension claims and insurance disputes. They know things about each other—who refused to eat until forced, who suggested the lots, who prayed—that cannot be spoken to outsiders. When they pass on the street, they nod with the minimal gesture of men who have shared a prison from which there is no release except death. Nickerson, still a boy in appearance but aged in his eyes, works briefly as a clerk before shipping out again, unable to tolerate the stillness of land. Ramsdell takes employment as a baker, handling flour instead of flesh. Lawrence disappears into the Carolina trade. They scatter like debris from a wreck, each carrying his own fragment of the story.

The handling of the meager salvage becomes a ritual of contested ownership among the survivors.

The three spades, rusted and pitted from salt exposure, represent the only tools that served both to dig graves in the sand of Henderson Island and to lever open the shell of turtles; Chase claims them as his by right of continuous possession, yet Thomas Nickerson quietly argues that one spade belongs to the boat he shared with Pollard, and therefore to the captain’s eventual disposition. They compromise without speaking of it, Chase retaining two while the third disappears into Nickerson’s sea chest, where it will remain for sixty years until discovered by his grandchildren.

The water cask, patched with lead beaten thin by Chase’s own hands during the worst of the dehydration, develops a slow leak during the Atlantic crossing; rather than repair it, Chase allows it to drain completely, then burns the staves in the Eagle’s galley fire, watching the smoke rise with an expression that the ship’s cook interprets as satisfaction but Nickerson recognizes as something closer to cremation.

The drafting of letters to bereaved families consumes Chase’s evenings through the tropics, each composition a negotiation between the requirements of truth and the preservation of dignity.

He writes to the widow of Barzillai Ray with particular care, knowing that she has already received conflicting accounts of her husband’s death—some suggesting cowardice, others heroism. Chase settles on a formula that acknowledges Ray’s final weakness without dishonoring his earlier service, describing how the man “failed gradually, as we all expected to fail, and was comforted as we were able.” He does not mention that Ray’s body provided sustenance for three days, nor that his own hands helped prepare the flesh.

To the parents of Owen Coffin, he sends a separate letter, longer and more anguished, in which he permits himself a single sentence of emotion: “Your son met his fate with a firmness that I have never seen surpassed, and I pray that his example may sustain you as it has sustained me.”

He drafts and redrafts this sentence seventeen times, the paper growing soft with handling, before he allows it to stand. The original survives in the Coffin family papers, the ink blurred at one corner where seawater reached it during a storm in the Horse Latitudes.

The unspoken preparation for home takes place in the silences between these tasks, in the hours when the survivors lie in their hammocks listening to the ordinary sounds of a merchant vessel—the watch bell, the creak of yards, the snoring of men who have never drawn lots to eat their companions.

Nickerson develops the habit of waking at the precise hour when Henderson Island first appeared on the horizon, his body retaining the circadian rhythm of starvation even as his stomach learns to accept ship’s biscuit. He does not speak of these wakings to Chase, who pretends sleep through the same hours, both men maintaining the pretense that their nights are untroubled. The isolation of their experience deepens precisely because they cannot acknowledge its persistence; they are prisoners of a courtesy that demands they perform recovery for the benefit of sailors whose curiosity they simultaneously resent and require.

When a young apprentice asks Nickerson, with the directness of youth, whether human flesh tastes like pork, the boy receives a blow to the jaw from a boatswain who has been instructed by the captain to prevent such inquiries; Nickerson thanks neither the boatswain nor the captain, understanding that the protection itself constitutes a form of display.

The divergence between Pollard and Chase manifests in their relationship to time itself. Chase measures the passage of days with renewed urgency, calculating how many pages he can complete before landfall, how many letters dispatched, how quickly he can return to sea and begin earning again. He treats the voyage home as an intermission, a necessary delay between acts of productive labor.

Pollard, traveling on the slower vessel Two Brothers, experiences time as viscosity, each day stretching and thickening like the grease that coats the rigging of a whaleship in cold weather. He stands his watches without consulting the chronometer, allowing the officer of the deck to correct his estimates; he has lost faith in his own navigation, not because he doubts his technical competence but because he no longer believes that arrival at any destination constitutes progress.

Where Chase writes to prepare for the future, Pollard sits with the past, replaying the moment of the whale’s impact until it loses all narrative sequence and becomes pure sensation—the splintering of oak, the rush of water, the cry of his cousin Owen that might have been surprise or might have been recognition.

The institutional pressures awaiting them in Nantucket begin to make themselves felt through the mail packets that overtake the sailing ships.

Chase receives a letter from Gideon Folger, senior partner in the Essex’s ownership syndicate, inquiring whether the captain’s decision to sail east rather than west might be attributed to “advice received from persons experienced in the navigation of those seas”—a carefully phrased probe for evidence that would transfer liability from the owners to the deceased Matthew Joy or to Pollard himself.

Chase drafts a reply that protects Pollard while establishing his own foresight, noting that he “urged repeatedly upon the captain the advantages of the western course” without specifying whether these urgings occurred before or after the separation of the boats. The letter travels in the packet’s hold like a seed of future litigation, its sentences already being parsed by insurers who will determine whether widows receive full compensation or the reduced settlement awarded in cases of “willful deviation from customary routes.”

The physical experience of the voyage home compounds their psychological displacement. The Eagle carries them through latitudes they crossed in open boats, the same currents and winds now encountered from the security of a deck they do not command. Chase finds himself unable to sleep when the ship rolls to starboard, his body remembering the tilt of the whaleboat in heavy seas, the constant calculation of whether the next wave would swamp them.

As summer turns to fall, the last of the survivors completes the journey home. The vessel carrying him beats up against westerlies in Nantucket Sound, her sails reefed against an autumn gale. On deck, George Pollard stands alone at the rail, though he is passenger now, not master. He has been offered a berth on a merchantman sailing to Charleston, a chance to escape the island’s scrutiny, but he has chosen instead to face what waits ashore. The wind shifts; the captain orders sails trimmed; the island rises from the horizon like a judgment.

The sight of Nantucket’s coastline from the deck presents the survivors with the daunting exposure of homecoming.
