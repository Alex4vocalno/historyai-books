Chapter 15

The Second Ship

The boat sails on, its course unchanged, but its world now defined by the grim, proteinaceous reality in its bottom.

Yet back in August 1819, when the Essex first cleared Nantucket harbor, no soul aboard could have imagined that eighty-nine days of open-boat horror would end not with divine deliverance but with the routine, almost bureaucratic intervention of another working vessel—the whaleship Dauphin, Captain Zimri Coffin, whose log for February 5, 1821, records nothing more remarkable than wind from the east-southeast and sail trim adjusted for the passage. The entry sits in the vessel’s official record like thousands of others: latitude 33°45’ S, longitude 81°03’ W, the ship making steady progress through grounds well known to Nantucket’s fleet.

The Dauphin had departed her home port in November 1819, a month after the Essex, and had been cruising the Pacific whaling grounds for fourteen months without incident. Her hold contained 1, 200 barrels of sperm oil. Her crew of twenty-six men expected another year at sea before turning for home. The ordinariness of their labor—that patient, methodical patrolling of latitudes where whales had been found before—constitutes the first half of a story whose second half would test whether any human institution could absorb what the Essex had become.

On that same February 5, in a boat now more coffin than craft, Captain George Pollard Jr. Drifted at the mercy of currents he could no longer fight. The sun rose on a vessel reduced to four living men and the memory of two dead ones. Owen Coffin, the cabin boy, had drawn the short straw on January 25; his body had been consumed by his shipmates, including his own cousin, the captain. Samuel Reed, the sailor from Nantucket who had drawn the second lot, had followed on February 1. The survivors—Pollard, Charles Ramsdell, Barzillai Ray, and Owen Coffin’s own brother-in-law, the sailor who had shot him—now existed in a state that Chase’s later narrative would sanitize and Nickerson’s would not fully recover.

They had been ninety-one days without proper food. Their boat, originally twenty-five feet long, had been lightened by throwing overboard everything not essential to immediate survival, then lightened again by the disappearance of human weight. The sails, patched from spare clothing, hung in rags. The water casks had been empty for twenty days. The men chewed their own leather boots, sucked the bones of their former companions, and waited for death with the mechanical patience of those who have seen every alternative exhausted.

The contrast between these two maritime realities—one recorded in the confident script of a ship’s log, the other existing in the silence between such documents—defines what happened when they finally collided. For the Dauphin was not searching for survivors. She was searching for whales, following the same commercial logic that had brought the Essex to these waters and would bring hundreds more vessels after her. The rescue, when it came, would be not miracle but miscalculation: a routine professional act that suddenly encountered the limits of professional comprehension.

—-

The Dauphin’s voyage had proceeded according to the established rhythms of Nantucket’s whaling industry. Built in 1819 at Rochester, Massachusetts, she was a new vessel, 87 feet long, 239 tons burden—smaller than the Essex but similarly rigged for the long Pacific cruise. Captain Zimri Coffin was forty-three years old, a veteran of the Brazil and Pacific grounds, respected among the island’s whaling masters for steady judgment and consistent returns. His crew included his own nephew, a boatsteerer named Obed Coffin, and several other Nantucket men who had shipped with him before. The vessel had rounded Cape Horn in March 1820, worked the Chilean coast through the southern winter, and followed the sperm whale migration westward toward the offshore grounds where the Essex would meet her catastrophe.

The Nantucket ship register for 1819 lists the Dauphin’s owners as a consortium of island merchants: William Rotch Jr., Charles W. Morgan, and others who held shares in dozens of vessels simultaneously. Their investment strategy depended upon diversification and volume: not every voyage would succeed, but the aggregate return across the fleet would justify the risk. The Dauphin represented one thread in this larger fabric, her movements tracked through the informal intelligence network of whaling masters who exchanged positions and sightings whenever their paths crossed at sea. When Captain Coffin made his February 5 entry recording light breezes from the east-southeast and the ship steering west-northwest with all well, he was participating in a system of knowledge production that had made Nantucket the center of American whaling. The coordinates he recorded would be copied into letters, reported to consuls, filed with underwriters. The ocean was not empty; it was annotated.

This system had its limits. The same logs that recorded whale sightings and weather patterns could not record what happened to vessels that disappeared. The Essex, sunk on November 20, 1820, had left no trace in the documentary record until survivors appeared to tell their tale. For nearly three months, she existed only as a growing silence: overdue at Valparaiso, unreported by any consul, absent from the intelligence network that was supposed to track such movements. Her owners, Gideon Folger and Paul Macy, would not learn of her loss until May 1821, when Chase’s boat reached Nantucket with the first news. In the interval, the industry continued its operations without adjustment, as industries do. The Dauphin’s routine patrol through the grounds where the Essex had been destroyed was not a search mission; it was standard practice in waters where whales were known to congregate.

The professional diligence that brought Coffin to 33°45’ S, 81°03’ W on February 5 had no provision for what his lookout spotted that morning. The log entry for February 6, made in Coffin’s hand, records the event with the same compression that characterized all his entries: at 11 AM he saw a boat ahead with sails made of shirts and other improvised materials. He hove to and took them in. He found them to be the remainder of the crew of the ship Essex of Nantucket which was upset by a whale and sunk on November 20 last.

The compression is significant. The boat ahead with sails made of shirts—this detail, preserved in the official record, captures the visible manifestation of a catastrophe that the log could not otherwise describe. The Dauphin’s crew, trained to assess weather and whale behavior, found themselves confronting an artifact that fell outside any professional category. A whaleboat was supposed to carry canvas sails, not improvised rigging of clothing. It was supposed to move with purpose, not drift. The men inside it were supposed to be recognizable as sailors, not as the spectral figures that Chase’s narrative would later struggle to render in conventional language.

The approach took hours. In the whaling industry, a strange boat could mean many things: deserters, castaways from a merchant vessel, survivors of some unreported wreck. Captain Coffin would have approached with caution, preparing for the possibility of hostile or deranged occupants. What he found instead was George Pollard, who had once commanded a vessel like his own, now reduced to a condition that the log could only record through its silences. The remainder of the crew numbered four. The ship Essex of Nantucket had been upset by a whale and sunk—a formulation that omitted the eighty-nine days of open-boat ordeal. The separation of the three boats went unmentioned. So did the landing on Henderson Island, the drawing of lots, the consumption of the dead. The log recorded what it could verify: a date, a position, a vessel name. The rest would require human testimony.

—-

The collision of these two worlds—routine commerce and catastrophic survival—produced moments that the documentary record preserves only in fragments. We know that Captain Coffin learned, in the first hours after the rescue, that his young cousin Owen Coffin had been among the Essex crew. We know that the boy had drawn the fatal lot and been shot by his shipmates, his body then consumed. The family connection, unremarked in the official log, must have transformed the professional encounter into something else: a master mariner suddenly confronted with the specific fate of a named relation, the abstract catastrophe of his industry made personal and particular.

Nickerson’s later account, composed decades after the event, would restore some of this human texture. He described the Dauphin’s crew as overcome with horror and pity at the condition of the survivors, their professional composure shattered by the physical evidence of prolonged starvation. The rescued men were scarcely able to stand, their bodies reduced to skeletal frames, their faces marked by a wildness of expression that spoke of experiences beyond ordinary maritime disaster. The Dauphin’s cook prepared food immediately, but the survivors could not digest it; their stomachs had shrunk past the capacity for normal nourishment. They required gradual feeding, medical attention, the restoration of bodily function through methods that the whaling vessel was not equipped to provide.

The contrast extended to language itself. Pollard, when questioned, told his story in the technical vocabulary of his profession: positions, dates, the behavior of the whale that had destroyed his ship. But this vocabulary could not accommodate what had happened in the boats. The phrase “we drew lots” appeared in his account as a bare statement, without the procedural detail that Chase would later supply. The consumption of the dead was mentioned, if at all, as a necessity that required no elaboration. The Dauphin’s crew, listening to this testimony, found themselves struggling to reconcile the speaker with his speech: a captain who retained the forms of command while describing the collapse of every command structure, a Nantucket sailor who had survived through acts that Nantucket could not acknowledge.

The rescue itself required practical decisions that the log records without commentary. The Dauphin was a working vessel with a hold full of oil and a cruise still in progress. Captain Coffin could not abandon his commercial mission to conduct a humanitarian expedition. Yet the survivors required immediate transport to a port where they could receive proper care; Valparaiso, the nearest significant settlement, lay some three weeks’ sail to the east. The solution, recorded in the log for February 7, was to transfer the four men to another vessel. Coffin spoke the ship Hero of Nantucket, Captain Reuben Joy, at latitude 33°30’ S, longitude 80°15’ W. He put the four men on board of her for Valparaiso.

This second transfer, less famous than the rescue itself, completed the reintegration of the Essex survivors into the institutional structures from which they had been expelled. The Hero was also a Nantucket vessel, her captain the brother of Matthew Joy, the Essex’s second mate who had died in the boats on January 10. The network of family and professional connection that defined Nantucket whaling thus enclosed the catastrophe within its ordinary operations: a cousin’s ship rescuing a cousin’s crew, a brother’s vessel completing the delivery. The horror was passed from hand to hand like any other maritime transaction, its specific content muted by the forms of commercial reciprocity.

—-

The Dauphin continued her cruise. The log for February 8 returns to its established pattern: winds, weather, sail changes, the routine work of whaling. Captain Coffin had performed his professional duty; the surviving Essex crew were now the responsibility of Captain Joy, of the Valparaiso consul, eventually of the owners and underwriters who would calculate the financial cost of the disaster. The encounter that had briefly opened a window into extremity closed again, leaving only the brief entries in two vessels’ logs to mark its occurrence.

Yet something remained. The Dauphin’s crew had seen what the industry preferred not to see: the physical consequence of its own operations, the human cost of a system that sent young men into dangerous waters in pursuit of marketable oil. Owen Coffin’s fate was not merely personal tragedy; it was the structural outcome of an industry that recruited boys into hazardous labor, that isolated its workers from institutional support, that treated the ocean as a space of extraction without adequate provision for extraction’s failures. The cousin who learned of his death, the uncle who would read of it in Nantucket newspapers, the owners who would calculate their insurance claims, all participated in a system that had produced this result through its ordinary functioning.

The rescue of February 6, 1821, thus stands as a moment of collision between two incompatible truths. The Dauphin’s log records a routine commercial encounter: a boat ahead sighted, survivors taken in, four men transferred. The survivors’ bodies recorded something else: eighty-nine days of exposure, starvation, and the deliberate killing of companions. The institutional forms of whaling, its logs, its transfers, its professional protocols, were adequate to the first truth but not the second. The gap between them would define what happened when the survivors finally returned to Nantucket, when their testimony encountered the community’s need to believe that its industry was essentially sound.

For the present, the immediate crisis passed into documentation. The Dauphin’s log, preserved in the Nantucket Historical Association’s collections, provides the checkable record: position, date, action taken. Captain Coffin’s handwriting, steady throughout, betrays no emotional response to what he had witnessed. The Hero’s log, similarly preserved, records the receipt of four men in a most distressing condition and their subsequent delivery to Valparaiso on February 25. The consular records there, copied to Nantucket and Boston, initiated the official process of inquiry and insurance claim that would eventually produce Chase’s published narrative. Each layer of documentation refined the raw experience into forms that could be administered: depositions, damage assessments, actuarial calculations.

The survivors themselves underwent this process as objects rather than subjects. Pollard, Ramsdell, Ray, and the fourth man—whose name appears variously in the records as “the sailor who shot Coffin” and “Owen Coffin’s brother-in-law,” his individual identity subsumed by his functional role in the catastrophe—were fed, clothed, and transported according to established procedures for distressed seamen. Their testimony was taken, their physical condition noted, their future employment discussed. The system that had failed to protect them now assumed responsibility for their rehabilitation, not through any acknowledgment of failure but through the automatic extension of its ordinary operations. A sailor who had consumed human flesh was, for administrative purposes, indistinguishable from a sailor who had suffered any other maritime accident.

This institutional response, simultaneously adequate and inadequate, defines the meaning of the Dauphin’s intervention. The rescue was not miraculous; it was the predictable result of persistent patrolling in known grounds, the professional diligence that Nantucket’s industry required. Yet this same diligence could not comprehend what it had found. The log’s description of a boat ahead with sails made of shirts captures the visible sign of a catastrophe that the log’s categories could not contain. The transfer to the Hero, the delivery to Valparaiso, the consular processing, all continued the work of normalization that would eventually produce Chase’s narrative as the official account, its most disturbing elements carefully managed for public consumption.

The rescue is logged: a date, a position, a headcount. The open-boat ordeal is over, but the reckoning with society has just begun.
