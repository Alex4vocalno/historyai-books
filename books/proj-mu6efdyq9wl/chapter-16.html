Chapter 16

The Captain’s Reckoning

The log of the whaleship Dauphin records for the morning of February 6, 1821, a position of 34°22′S, 84°20′W: the vessel beating west-northwest in light winds, the lookouts aloft, the captain on deck with glass in hand scanning a quadrant of empty water. At that same hour, by the later reckoning of Charles Ramsdell, the whaleboat of Captain George Pollard drifted somewhere between 35° and 36° south latitude, perhaps four hundred miles to the east-southeast, though Ramsdell would admit no confidence in any figure. The Dauphin’s crew numbered thirty-four men, provisioned for two years, her hold empty of oil, her mission now one of search and rescue. Pollard’s crew numbered two: the captain himself, twenty-eight years old, and Ramsdell, a boatsteerer aged twenty or twenty-one. Between them they possessed three ounces of bread, the sinews of a dead man, and the certain knowledge that they could no longer work the boat.

The difference between the two vessels is categorical, not numerical. The Dauphin embodies the institutional response of Nantucket’s whaling economy to its own catastrophe: owners, agents, and underwriters mobilizing capital, men, and time to recover what can be recovered. Pollard’s boat marks the terminal point of that same system’s logic, the reduction of human beings to biological units consuming one another in a space the system cannot reach. By late January 1821, the whaleboats had become experiments in the limits of social organization. Chase’s boat, rescued five days before, demonstrated that the limit is approximately eighty-nine days: three men pulled from the water on February 18, alive but incapacitated, having maintained through sheer mechanical discipline some residue of navigational purpose. Pollard’s boat would demonstrate that the limit, for some, extends further, but that extension purchases nothing recognizable as survival.

Owen Chase, in the narrative he published within months of his return, devotes fewer than four hundred words to the period between the drawing of lots that killed Owen Coffin on February 6 and his own rescue on February 18. He speaks of the most deplorable and affecting condition, of the most distressing spectacle, and passes quickly to his own deliverance. The omission is structural. Chase’s account is organized around the demonstration of his own competence—his navigation, his discipline, his survival—and the final phase of Pollard’s ordeal offers no material for that demonstration. It offers only failure: the failure of command, of navigation, of the social contract itself. For this, Chase relies on hearsay, and the hearsay he records is filtered through the only man who could provide it. Charles Ramsdell, rescued with Pollard on February 23, becomes the sole witness to the final dissolution of the Essex company.

What Ramsdell testified to, in statements taken aboard the Dauphin and later in Nantucket, was a process of systematic unwinding. The death of Barzillai Ray, the Nantucket seaman who had drawn the lot that spared him on February 6, occurred within days of Coffin’s execution. Ramsdell could not fix the date with precision, his own calendar having collapsed, but his sequence is clear. Ray, already weakened by the ordeal of the open boat, succumbed to the combined effects of starvation, dehydration, and exposure. His body, like Coffin’s before it, became provisions.

The act of consumption, which in Chase’s boat had been surrounded with ritual, the drawing of lots, the appeal to divine will, the formal assignment of the executioner, occurred in Pollard’s boat without ceremony. Ramsdell described simply that they made use of him. The phrase is notable for its grammatical passivity. In Chase’s account, men act: they draw, they decide, they perform. In Ramsdell’s, something is done to them, or they find themselves doing what necessity demands without the mediation of collective deliberation.

The first resource to exhaust itself was the social technology of justification. Nantucket whaling had developed elaborate procedures for distributing risk and reward: shares, lays, the formal hierarchy of officers and crew, the contractual obligations that bound men to ships and ships to owners. These procedures depended on the possibility of recording, of entering transactions in ledgers that could be audited and disputed. The open boat offered no such possibility. The drawing of lots for Owen Coffin had represented a final, desperate adaptation of this technology: the random procedure, witnessed by all, producing a result that no individual could be held accountable for. With Ray’s death, even this adaptation failed. There were no longer enough men to constitute a collective witness. Two individuals cannot draw lots. Two individuals can only decide, or succumb.

The second resource to fail was navigational purpose. Chase’s boat, even in extremity, maintained some orientation toward the South American coast: erratic, miscalculated, but deliberate. Pollard’s boat, by Ramsdell’s account, ceased to be steered at all. The men lacked the strength to work the oars with effect. The sail, improvised from shirts and fragments of canvas, remained set but unadjusted, carrying them wherever the wind and current directed. This was no strategy of drift, no calculated preservation of energy. Navigation had ceased to be a meaningful activity. The compass, the quadrant, the daily observation of sun and stars, all the apparatus of position-finding that defined the whaleman’s professional identity, became objects without function. Ramsdell recalled that they let the boat go. The phrase suggests both passivity and release: the abandonment of effort, the surrender of control to forces indifferent to human intention.

The geographical consequence is difficult to establish. The Dauphin’s search pattern, as recorded by her master Zimri Coffin, covered the waters between 33° and 37°S, from 81° to 89°W, a rectangle of some two hundred thousand square miles. The rescue of Chase’s boat on February 18 occurred at approximately 33°45′S, 81°W—far to the north and east of the Dauphin’s position on February 6, suggesting that Chase had maintained a more effective westerly course than the search vessel itself.

Pollard’s boat, drifting without steerage, seems to have moved south and east, into the higher latitudes where the westerlies blow more consistently and the cold intensifies. Ramsdell’s estimate of their position at rescue—35° to 36°S—places them perhaps three hundred miles from Chase’s boat at the time of his rescue, and four hundred miles from the Dauphin’s track. They were, in effect, invisible to the organized search. Their rescue would depend not on the institutional response of Nantucket whaling but on chance: the passage of another vessel, on other business, through waters no one had designated as search territory.

The third resource to collapse was language itself. Ramsdell testified that he and Pollard, in the final days before rescue, ceased to speak. This was not the silence of men conserving energy, nor the silence of hostility or despair.

By Ramsdell’s description, it was something more radical: a condition in which communication had become impossible because there was nothing left to communicate. They had no plans to discuss, no observations to share, no memories to recall that could sustain the present. The stories that men tell in extremity, of home, of previous voyages, of imagined futures, require a listener capable of responding, of maintaining the dialogic structure that makes narrative meaningful. Two men, equally depleted, equally without prospect, cannot perform this function for each other. The silence that descended on Pollard’s boat was the silence of subjects who had become, for each other, mere objects: bodies occupying space, consuming the last residues of their common resource, waiting for biological termination.

This silence has no parallel in the other accounts. Thomas Nickerson, the cabin boy in Chase’s boat, recorded in his later narrative a continuous stream of talk: prayers, recollections, the reading of the daily ration, through the final days. The three men in Chase’s boat maintained, even in delirium, some social fabric. The two men in Pollard’s boat unraveled it.

The difference is structural, and it bears on the question of command. Pollard, as captain, had presided over the drawing of lots that killed his cousin Owen Coffin. He had, by the account Ramsdell confirmed, participated in the consumption of both Coffin and Ray. The authority that had once derived from his position, from the owners’ commission and the formal hierarchy of the ship, had been converted by these acts into something else: a burden of responsibility that could not be acknowledged and could not be shared. Ramsdell, as subordinate, could not address him as captain. Pollard, as survivor of his own decisions, could not address Ramsdell as crew. They were left with the fact of their mutual presence, stripped of the relations that would make that presence meaningful.

The physical symptoms of their condition, as Ramsdell described them, followed from this social collapse. They did not, in the final days, attempt to fish or to collect rainwater. They lay in the bottom of the boat, unmoving, while the sun passed overhead and the water cask—long empty—rolled beside them. Their skin, burned and cracked by exposure, had lost the capacity to heal. Their eyes, affected by ophthalmia, could no longer tolerate light. Ramsdell recalled that they kept their faces covered, not from the sun alone but from each other: a mutual aversion that was also a mutual recognition. What they had become was visible, and the visibility was unbearable.

The date of their rescue, February 23, 1821, places their total time in the open boat at ninety-four days, assuming the sinking of the Essex on November 20 as the starting point. This is five days longer than Chase’s ordeal, and it produced a different kind of survivor.

Chase, pulled from his boat by the crew of the British merchantman Indian, was able to walk, to speak coherently, to participate in the narrative construction of his own rescue. Pollard and Ramsdell, found by the Dauphin on the afternoon of February 23, had to be lifted from their boat like cargo. The Dauphin’s log records the position: 35°30′S, 92°W, west of the search area, south of Chase’s track, in waters that Zimri Coffin had reached only by extending his voyage beyond its planned term.

The find was accidental, the product of persistence rather than calculation. The Dauphin had been at sea since January 6, her holds still empty, her owners’ investment producing no return but the recovery of men who could no longer work.

The contrast between the condition of the two survivors, as reported by the Dauphin’s crew, measures the distance they had traveled beyond recognizable humanity. Ramsdell, younger and perhaps more resilient, responded gradually to nourishment and care. Pollard did not.

The captain of the Essex remained, by all accounts, in a state of profound withdrawal through the remainder of the voyage to Valparaiso and beyond. He would speak when spoken to, perform necessary actions when directed, but initiate nothing. The quality of his silence was different from the silence of the open boat: it was the silence of a man who had seen the limits of his own competence and found them to coincide with the destruction of everything he had been responsible for. The ledger of command, that systematic accounting of authority and outcome that structured Nantucket’s whaling enterprises, now showed only loss: twenty of twenty-one crew members dead, the ship destroyed, the voyage a catastrophe without mitigation. Pollard had kept the books. He knew the figures.

The institutional response to this catastrophe was already forming, even as the Dauphin carried her survivors toward Chile. The Nantucket ship registers record the Essex as a total loss: 238 tons burden, valued at $12, 000, insured for $9, 000, the underwriters—Coffin, Swain, and others—facing claims that would be paid, with varying degrees of resistance, over the following months. The human loss was not similarly quantifiable. The owners, Gideon Folger and Paul Macy, who had appointed Pollard to his command in April 1819, would face questions not about their financial exposure but about their judgment in selecting a captain who had now presided over the most destructive whaling disaster in the island’s history. These questions would be asked, initially, in private: in the letters between Nantucket and Valparaiso, in the reports of American consular officials, in the informal networks of information that connected the dispersed fleet of Pacific whalers.

The formal inquiry would come later, and its shape would be determined by the narratives that the survivors produced. Chase’s account, written rapidly after his return and published in 1821, established the first authoritative version: the whale’s attack as unprecedented, the captain’s decision to sail east as unfortunate but understandable given the intelligence about conditions in the Marquesas, the open-boat ordeal as a demonstration of seamanship and endurance. Pollard’s narrative, recorded by the London Missionary Society member George Bennett during the captain’s stay in England in 1822-23, would offer a different emphasis: the personal responsibility of the captain, the failure of leadership, the horror of the final days in a tone less triumphant than Chase’s. The two accounts, read together, constitute different strategies for survival in the aftermath rather than different perspectives on the same events: Chase’s asserting professional competence, Pollard’s accepting moral burden.

The deterioration of Ray’s body, once it became available as resource, followed a pattern that Ramsdell described with the same flatness he applied to all the final transactions. The flesh, what remained of it after the initial taking, was divided without measurement. There was no need for the formal equity of shares that governed the distribution of oil on a whaleship; two men could simply eat until the material was exhausted or until their bodies rejected it. The latter occurred with increasing frequency.

Ramsdell noted that they vomited repeatedly, their digestive systems having lost the capacity to process protein after weeks of near-total abstinence. The vomiting itself became part of the routine, a mechanical rejection that neither man commented upon. They would eat, vomit, and lie still until the cycle could be repeated. The bread—three ounces, carefully preserved—was not touched. It had become, by some unspoken agreement, a reserve against some future contingency that neither man could articulate but neither would abandon.

The weather during this period compounded their immobility. The westerlies that had carried them south brought with them a succession of low-pressure systems, gray skies and rain that fell too lightly to be collected in any quantity. The boat’s canvas, already inadequate for sailing, became waterlogged and heavy. Ramsdell recalled that they made no attempt to bail, letting the accumulated water slosh in the bottom of the boat around their bodies. The cold, which would have been lethal to men in their condition, was somehow not. Their metabolism had slowed to a point where external temperature registered as a distant sensation, like the pressure of the water against their skin or the movement of the boat beneath them. They existed in a state of suspended animation, neither fully alive nor actively dying, their hearts continuing to beat because they had not yet stopped.

The delirium that overtook them in the final week before rescue took different forms. Ramsdell experienced periods of visual hallucination—he would see islands on the horizon, ships approaching, birds that he recognized as albatrosses but could not name. These hallucinations were not frightening; they were simply present, as real as the actual horizon, and he made no effort to distinguish them from perception. Pollard, by Ramsdell’s account, did not hallucinate.

He became instead fixated on objects within the boat: a piece of twine, a wooden cleat, the empty water cask. He would stare at these objects for hours, his finger tracing their outlines without purpose. Ramsdell could not determine whether this represented concentration or its complete absence, whether Pollard was engaged in some private calculation or had simply lost the capacity to redirect his attention. He did not ask. The question of what occupied Pollard’s mind had become, like so much else, unaskable.

The institutional machinery that would eventually process their experience was, during these final days, invisible and unimaginable. The Dauphin’s search, systematic and funded, proceeded according to schedules and positions that meant nothing to men who had lost the ability to locate themselves. The possibility of rescue had become, for Pollard and Ramsdell, a category without content. They did not hope for it; they did not despair of it. They had passed beyond the temporal structure that would make such attitudes meaningful. The future, like the past, had collapsed into an eternal present of physical sensation: hunger that was no longer painful but simply present, thirst that was indistinguishable from the self, the weight of their own bodies against the boat’s planks.

What neither account could address directly was the condition of the final days in Pollard’s boat, the period that Chase had glossed and Pollard could only approach through Ramsdell’s testimony. This silence at the center of the record—the absence of Pollard’s own voice from the description of his own extremity—produces a particular effect on the reader, then and now. We are left with the image that Ramsdell provided: two men, incapable of speech, incapable of movement, waiting in a space that had ceased to be navigable or even locatable. The Dauphin’s log gives us the date and position of their rescue. Ramsdell gives us the condition that made rescue necessary. Between these two sources, a gap opens that no additional testimony can close.

This gap is the true subject of the chapter: not the suffering itself, which can be described but not conveyed, but the collapse of the systems that would make suffering meaningful. The Dauphin represents the restoration of those systems—the reconnection of the survivors to the institutional networks of whaling, commerce, and law that would process their experience into narrative, compensation, and historical record. But the photograph, as it were, has already been taken. The image of Pollard and Ramsdell, catatonic and adrift, hands off the stark reality of what the rescuers are about to find.
