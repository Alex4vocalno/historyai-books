Chapter 15

The Player Who Became a Politician

The candidacy declaration was posted under the thread title ‘CSM Candidacy – Mittani (TNT, Goonswarm)’. Its text used numbered points. The third point addressed sovereignty warfare mechanics.

It argued for a reduction in the reinforcement timer for control towers in null-security space from twenty-four hours to a shorter, undefined window. This proposed change favored organized offensive fleets over passive defenders who relied on the clock. The document listed the candidate’s in-game name, his corporation, and his alliance beneath the manifesto.

This forum post, published in early 2009, was not a guild charter or a corporate policy. It was a campaign promise.

The offices created by written constitutions, like the Auditory Office and legislative roles forged in the Ascendant Frontier scandal, were no longer empty. Players now sought them—individuals who understood that authority would now be measured against a written standard, and that securing it required a new kind of work. CCP Games had conceived the Council of Stellar Management in 2008. Its first council of nine elected players was meant to act as a formal advisory body. The elections for the second council, CSM 2, began in March 2009, following the constitutional crises that had forced virtual societies to codify their laws.

This was not a frontier. The power vacuum that followed the constitutional crises of 2007 and 2008 had created a demand for legitimate governance that extended beyond any single alliance’s borders.

The society had matured to the point where it required a professional political class. The first true virtual politicians were not the architects of the systems. They were the operators who learned to campaign within them.

The candidate known as The Mittani was one archetype. His credibility was martial. He was a strategic director of the Goonswarm Federation, a coalition notorious for its large-scale warfare. His platform reflected that pedigree. The plank on sovereignty timers was a policy designed to benefit an entity skilled at mounting sudden, overwhelming force.

His campaign materials did not appeal to universal fairness. They appealed to a constituency that valued decisive military advantage.

His political rhetoric was an extension of his in-game persona. He campaigned on voice chat servers, holding ‘town halls’ where he fielded questions from pilots across different alliances. The questions were often technical. The answers were framed as deliverables.

A vote for The Mittani was not a vote for a person but for a set of mechanical changes.

A second candidate, running in that same election cycle, presented a different archetype. This player, whose handle was Two Step, was a director of the Band of Brothers alliance. His public reputation was also tied to warfare, but his campaign emphasized diplomacy and structural reform. His manifesto spoke of bridging communication gaps between null-security space power blocs and the game’s developers. He promised to advocate for changes that would encourage conflict rather than entrenchment. His language was less about tactical advantage for his own group and more about systemic health. His platform acknowledged the existence of rival coalitions as legitimate political entities rather than mere enemies.

This was a subtle but significant shift. The guild leader managed internal morale and external threats. The politician had to acknowledge an electorate that included those threats.

A third line emerged from the economic sphere. Candidates appeared who built their credibility not on fleet command but on market mastery or industrial logistics.

Their platforms focused on mineral scarcity, manufacturing bottlenecks, and the economic impact of proposed warfare changes. They appealed to a silent majority of players who did not fly in supercapital fleets but who built the ships and mined the ore. Their campaigns were quieter, conducted more through detailed forum posts than raucous voice chat events. They parsed an electorate that was anonymous and diffuse.

These candidates offered a different translation of in-game credibility. A trader’s reputation for fair deals became a promise of economic stewardship. The political class was forming along familiar fault lines.

The mechanics of the election itself imposed novel challenges. The electorate was the entire subscriber base, spread across thousands of solar systems. There were no physical precincts, no demographic data beyond what could be inferred from a character’s employment history. Campaigning meant navigating a landscape where identity was performative and allegiance was volatile. A candidate’s past actions were logged forever in killboard statistics and old forum flame wars. Political opponents dredged up years-old comments.

The campaign trail was a digital archive, and every candidate was haunted by their own logged history. The ‘meet-and-greets’ on third-party voice servers became the analogue of the stump speech. Candidates would join a channel hosted by a neutral party. Dozens of players would listen in. They typed questions into text chat. The candidate would answer aloud, their voice the only tangible, human signifier in a campaign otherwise conducted through text.

The tone of that voice mattered. A flat monotone could suggest incompetence. Too much bluster could read as hollow propaganda. The best performers blended technical knowledge with a sense of relatable conviction. They had to sound like they understood the game’s intricate systems while also caring about the player on the other end of the microphone.

The voting process was a lesson in scaled democracy. Each active account received one vote. Large alliances could organize bloc voting, instructing their members to support a chosen candidate. This practice turned alliances into de facto political parties. It also highlighted the tension at the core of the experiment.

The council was meant to represent the player base, but the player base was a collection of pre-existing, often autocratic, power structures. A politician needed the backing of one of these structures to have any chance of winning. Their mandate was dual. They were ambassadors of their bloc to the developers, and representatives of the whole community to their bloc.

The pressure to reconcile those roles began the moment the election results were announced. The results of the CSM 2 election in April 2009 confirmed the rise of the martial archetype. The Mittani topped the poll. Two Step and other candidates with strong alliance backing filled most of the remaining seats. The council that convened was dominated by figures from the largest null-security space coalitions. Their platforms had been tested and their constituencies had delivered. They had translated in-game charisma and managerial credibility into electoral mandates. The transition from guild leader to politician was, in one sense, complete. These individuals now held a formal, recognized office within the game’s social architecture. They had a direct line to the developers.

They could claim a democratic legitimacy that no guild master could independently assert. The constraints of that office became apparent almost immediately. The Council of Stellar Management was an advisory body. It had no legislative power. It could not write code or deploy patches. Its influence was persuasive, contingent on the willingness of CCP Games’ designers to listen and act.

The politicians quickly discovered that their hard-won mandates collided with commercial and design realities over which they had no control. A candidate might have promised a change to sovereignty mechanics. Implementing that change required developer man-hours and balancing against other features. The politician’s new role involved both advocating for their platform and managing their constituents’ expectations when those promises met the slow reality of software development.

This dynamic created a new kind of burnout. Guild leadership burnout came from endless logistical planning. Political burnout came from the gap between expectation and agency. A council member would spend hours compiling feedback from their constituency and presenting it to developers. They might argue passionately for a change.

They might then return to their electorate and report that the change was being considered, or that it was rejected. Their credibility was now tied to a process they did not control. They became mediators between an impatient society and a distant sovereign.

Parallel developments in other virtual worlds echoed this emergence, though the forms differed. In Minecraft, Notch added multiplayer on May 31, 2009, in update 0.15 during the Classic phase of the game. The first server mods quickly began to arise around this time. On September 3, 2010, work began on hMod, a notable mod which implemented an API for plugins that allowed server administrators to create custom rules and management tools. This technical foundation gave rise to thousands of independent, player-run servers. On these servers, political authority often resided with the server owner and their appointed moderators. Yet even here, successful administrators learned to behave like politicians. They published server rules as constitutions. They adjudicated disputes between players. They translated technical control into social legitimacy, becoming the first true virtual politicians for their small worlds.

In EverQuest, the release of the fifteenth expansion, Seeds of Destruction, on October 21, 2008, continued a trend of providing content for high-level players. Within the fictional storyline, Zebuxoruk, an insane fallen god, was the only person who could aid players in preventing the destruction of Norrath.

This narrative played out against a social backdrop where guild leadership had long been a form of intense managerial politics. Raid targets were scarce resources. Distributing loot required complex systems of merit. Maintaining a roster for nightly campaigns was an exercise in personnel management. The most successful guild leaders were already politicians in everything but name. Their state was the guild itself.

The virtual politician’s ultimate test was not winning an election, but wielding the influence that election granted. Success changed the actor in return. A fleet commander elected to the CSM had to learn to speak in terms of game health and broad incentives. A logistics tycoon had to consider how a mineral redistribution would affect new players. The office demanded a widening of perspective.

These campaigns, however, were not waged in a vacuum. They unfolded within a society that had, in a remarkably short span, internalized the rituals of formal politics. The forum threads where candidates posted their manifestos became digital town squares, with every line scrutinized, every past action cited as precedent or indictment. This electorate, though anonymous, was fiercely literate in the game’s own history.

A candidate’s killboard—a public record of every ship destroyed and lost—could be read not just as a resume of martial prowess, but as a political ledger. A history of attacking new player corporations could be framed as ruthless pragmatism or as a liability proving a lack of concern for the broader community’s health. This constant audit forced a new discipline.

The offhand, provocative comment that once fueled forum drama now carried the risk of alienating a potential voting bloc. The politician-in-training had to learn to speak in a calibrated register, blending the technical jargon of game mechanics with the inclusive rhetoric of public service, a far cry from the focused, adversarial communication of alliance diplomacy.

The very structure of the election process shaped the nature of representation. With one vote per subscribed account, the system was mathematically democratic, yet its practical outcome was the consolidation of power into alliance-sized blocs. This created a paradox of virtual representation. To secure a seat, a candidate needed the organized support of a major coalition, effectively making them that coalition’s delegate.

Yet once elected, their remit was to represent the entire player base, including the rivals of their own backers. This dual mandate placed the first politicians in a constant state of negotiation. They had to persuade their own alliance members to accept compromises that benefited the wider game ecology, while simultaneously convincing the broader electorate that they were not merely a puppet for their home coalition.

This delicate dance was the core work of the new political class, a daily practice in translating parochial interests into arguments for universal benefit, a skill never required of a pure guild leader whose authority stemmed from serving a single group’s exclusive advantage.

The advisory nature of the Council of Stellar Management further refined this political sensibility. Without the power to directly implement change, influence became currency. A council member’s effectiveness was measured by their ability to build consensus within the council itself, to package player feedback into coherent, persuasive briefs for the developers, and to manage the flow of information back to a restless constituency. This turned politics into a craft of mediation and expectation management.

The most successful early politicians were those who understood that their role was less about issuing demands and more about framing problems in a language the developers at CCP could recognize as aligned with the game’s long-term health. They learned to trade the immediate, visceral satisfaction of a campaign promise for the slower, more nuanced gratification of seeing a community concern formally acknowledged and placed on a development roadmap, a subtle but critical evolution from advocate to statesman.

This professionalization of politics also created new social strata within the virtual society. The elected council member gained a form of recognized status that transcended their in-game corporation or alliance affiliation. Developer blogs quoted them, third-party news sites solicited their opinions, and their public appearances on voice chats drew larger crowds. This celebrity came with intensified scrutiny and a novel kind of risk. Their real-world identity, once carefully partitioned from their in-game persona, now faced greater exposure. A political misstep could ignite controversies that spilled beyond the game’s forums, attracting attention from wider gaming media and forcing a level of personal accountability that was entirely new. The politician’s avatar was no longer just a character; it had become a public office, with all the attendant pressures and vulnerabilities that entailed, blurring the line between player and persona in a way that mere guild leadership never had.

The emergence of this political class in EVE Online was mirrored, in less formalized but equally telling ways, across other virtual frontiers. In the burgeoning world of Minecraft servers following the addition of multiplayer in 2009, administrators who installed mods like hMod were not just technicians setting permissions; they were foundational lawmakers. By choosing which plugins to enable—ones that protected player builds, managed economies, or instituted systems of justice—these server owners were writing a de facto constitution for their small world. Their campaigns were quieter, but the translation of managerial credibility into a mandate for rule-making was the same fundamental handoff from player to politician.

It also created a new kind of celebrity and a new kind of vulnerability. Every forum post became part of their permanent political record. They were now accountable to a history they were helping to write. The election results and the constrained power of the new politicians laid bare the gap between the society’s demand for self-governance and the commercial reality of the server’s ultimate owners. The players had built constitutions, held elections, and mandated representatives. They had generated a political class from their own ranks.

Yet the final authority always rested with a company whose primary duty was to its shareholders. The politicians were intermediaries in a negotiation where one side held all the code. Their legitimacy was real within the society they represented, but it was perpetually contingent on the goodwill of a power outside their political universe. They had become governors of a country that could have its fundamental laws changed by a distant landlord. This was the concrete consequence of institutionalized power.

It created a professional class to operate the machinery of state, only to show them the limits of the machine’s design. The power vacuum left by scandal had been filled not by charismatic warlords but by spreadsheet-literate candidates. Their campaigns had generated a political class from the society’s own ranks, a class now tasked with the bureaucratic administration of virtual sovereignty. The offices were no longer empty, and the individuals who filled them understood their authority was measured against a written standard. The next step was to see how that authority would be exercised when the state itself became a sprawling, password-protected spreadsheet to be updated every Sunday evening.
