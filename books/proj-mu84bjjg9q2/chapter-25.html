Chapter 25

The Phantom Fleet at Hunter’s Point

Seen from above, the bay stretched beneath the October sky, a sheet of hammered steel where the morning fog burned off the Oakland hills and drifted in wisps across the water. The port functioned as ports always had, its channels marked by buoys, its wharves busy with cargo, its skyline unchanged since the boom years of the previous decade. From three thousand feet, San Francisco appeared much as it always had, the Golden Gate Bridge catching the early sun, the cable cars grinding up Powell Street, the ferries threading between Alcatraz and Angel Island. The war had been over for four years.

Closer in, descending toward the southern reach of the bay, the silhouette of a ship emerged from the haze. She rode at anchor off Hunter’s Point, her flight deck stripped of aircraft, her island structure bare of radar and antennae. Paint peeled in long sheets, revealing gray primer beneath, and where the paint had flaked away entirely, the steel showed the dull orange of surface rust. She had the lines of a light carrier, converted from a cruiser hull, her narrow beam giving her a speed that the big fleet carriers could not match. But she would not move again under her own power. The USS Independence, CVL-22, lay at anchor as a ghost, her wartime service concluded, her atomic trial survived, her final purpose still being determined by men in laboratories on shore.

The Naval Radiological Defense Laboratory had claimed her. Teams in white coveralls approached by launch, climbing the accommodation ladder that hung from her starboard side, their boots clanging on steel that still registered above background on the geiger counters they carried in canvas cases. They were not there to salvage her. They were there to study what the Baker shot had done to her hull, her compartments, her ventilation systems, her wiring. The Independence had been through the base surge, that wall of radioactive mist that had rolled across the lagoon at Bikini on the morning of July 25, 1946. She had been washed, scrubbed, scraped, and washed again. The readings had fallen but never reached zero. Now she served as a floating classroom, a source of samples, a three-dimensional textbook on the persistence of fission products in steel and paint and confined spaces.

The Joint Task Force that had assembled at Bikini had represented the largest military operation of the postwar period, forty-two thousand personnel deployed to an isolated atoll in the Marshall Islands to witness two atomic detonations and assess their effects on a fleet of target vessels. The health and safety of those personnel had fallen to medical officers and radiation specialists operating under protocols that were still being written as the tests proceeded.

The specialized job of dealing with contaminated vessels had fallen to a small cadre of experts whose assumptions about what could and could not be cleaned had been overturned by the behavior of the base surge. The Independence carried that lesson in her very metal. The scientists who boarded her each morning understood that they were walking into a laboratory that could not be replicated on land. The contamination was not uniform. It pooled in unexpected places, collected in bilges, clung to the underside of pipes, settled in the crevices where bulkhead met deck. The hoses had washed the surfaces, but the surfaces were only the beginning.

The laboratory at Hunter’s Point had grown around the problem. What had begun as a small research unit assigned to study the effects of the atomic bomb on naval materiel had expanded into a permanent installation, its buildings spreading along the waterfront, its piers filling with vessels that had no other destination. The Independence was the largest, but she was not alone. Other ships from the target array had made the journey across the Pacific, towed through the canal or around the Horn, their holds still carrying the invisible residue of the lagoon. They arrived at Hunter’s Point and were met by men in protective clothing, men who had learned to read the geiger counter as a sailor reads the sea, understanding its language of clicks and pulses, its sudden accelerations and gradual declines.

The process by which a contaminated warship became a data set was neither dramatic nor swift. The Independence had been towed from Kwajalein in the spring of 1949, her engines silent, her crew a skeleton detachment of specialists and handlers. The Navy had announced that she would be used for further weapons tests, a phrase that suggested something purposeful and controlled. In reality, the purpose had already shifted. The weapons tests would not happen. What remained was the slower work of understanding what the Baker shot had left behind.

The teams from the laboratory worked in shifts. They descended into the carrier’s compartments with flashlights and survey meters, mapping the contamination, taking scrapings from bulkheads, cutting samples from pipes and ventilation ducts. Each sample went into a labeled container, each container into a lead-lined box, each box into a truck that drove to the laboratory buildings on shore. There, behind thick concrete walls, other scientists subjected the samples to analysis, measuring the beta and gamma emissions, identifying the isotopes, calculating half-lives and decay rates. The work was meticulous, repetitive, and profoundly unglamorous. No photographs appeared in the newspapers. No headlines announced the latest findings. The Independence sat in the bay, and the scientists came and went, and the files in the laboratory’s archives grew thicker month by month.

Twelve target ships had been so lightly contaminated that they were remanned and sailed back to the United States by their crews. The Navy had made much of this fact in the immediate aftermath of the tests, pointing to those twelve vessels as proof that atomic weapons, while terrible, did not render a fleet permanently unusable. But the Independence was not among those twelve. She belonged to the larger category of ships that could not be restored, ships whose contamination had penetrated too deeply, ships that could be studied but not sailed. Nine target ships in total were scrapped rather than scuttled. The rest had gone to the bottom, sunk by gunfire or aerial torpedoes or their own deteriorating hulls, their radioactive cargo buried under thousands of feet of water. The Independence had escaped that fate, but only because she had become useful as a specimen.

The German cruiser Prinz Eugen had not been so fortunate. Seized at Bremerhaven as a prize of war, she had been assigned to the target array at Bikini, her sleek hull painted with the same orange aiming markers that marked the Nevada. She had survived both Able and Baker, damaged but afloat, her German crew replaced by American sailors who struggled with her unfamiliar systems. Towed to Kwajalein, she had anchored in the lagoon where so many of the target ships had gathered in the months after the tests.

There she waited, her fate uncertain, her bilge pumps requiring constant attention. All ships leak, and without regular pumping, they sink. The Prinz Eugen’s pumps failed on December 22, 1946. She listed, settled, and went down by the stern, her propellers still visible above the water for weeks afterward, a monument to the war she had survived and the peace that had destroyed her.

The Independence would not sink, not yet. She was too valuable as a source of information, too carefully monitored, too closely watched by the men who had staked their careers on understanding what the atomic age meant for naval warfare. The laboratory at Hunter’s Point existed because the Navy had recognized, however grudgingly, that the old assumptions about damage and repair no longer applied. A ship hit by a conventional bomb could be patched and returned to service. A ship hit by an atomic bomb might survive the blast and the heat, only to be rendered uninhabitable by an invisible poison that no hose could wash away.

The radiological safety officers who had argued with Admiral Blandy’s staff during those frantic days after the Baker shot had been proven right. Stafford Warren had warned that the contamination would persist, that the base surge had deposited fission products throughout the target ships, that no amount of scrubbing would restore them to safety. The Navy had resisted that conclusion, had wanted to believe that the fleet could be salvaged, that the tests had demonstrated the survivability of ships as well as their vulnerability. The truth had emerged more slowly, in the readings that refused to fall, in the sailors who developed skin lesions, in the ships that had to be abandoned one by one. Now, at Hunter’s Point, that truth was being codified into manuals and procedures, turned into the bureaucratic language of safety protocols and exposure limits.

The men who boarded the Independence each morning did not think in terms of dramatic revelations. They thought in terms of samples and measurements, in the practical details of how to cut a piece of contaminated pipe without spreading the dust, how to store a radioactive filter without exposing themselves to dangerous levels of gamma radiation, how to document what they found so that someone else could make sense of it. The work was dangerous, but the danger had become routine. They wore their dosimeters, they logged their exposure times, they submitted to regular blood counts. The laboratory had its own medical department, its own protocols for dealing with overexposure. No one had died, not yet. The longer-term effects remained theoretical, a problem for the future.

The Independence had served during World War II, her air group striking Japanese positions across the Pacific, her hull damaged by a torpedo at Tarawa and repaired at Pearl Harbor. She had earned eight battle stars. Now she earned nothing, produced nothing, carried nothing but the accumulated evidence of what an atomic bomb did to a ship that survived the initial detonation. The laboratory teams treated her with a kind of respect, not the reverence due a warship but the careful attention due a specimen. She was a Fleet of Intended Ruin reduced to a single vessel, the last representative of a category that had once numbered ninety.

The public knew nothing of this. The Independence sat in the bay, visible to anyone who cared to look, but her purpose was not advertised. The laboratory operated behind fences, its reports classified, its findings shared only within the narrow community of military and scientific experts who had clearance to receive them. The lessons of Bikini were being learned, but they were being learned in secret, translated into technical language that meant nothing to the taxpayers who had paid for the tests and the congressmen who had authorized them. The Navy had moved on, its attention turning to new classes of ships, new weapons systems, new strategic doctrines. The contaminated hulk at Hunter’s Point was a relic of a problem already considered solved.

But the problem was not solved. It had merely been contained, isolated, turned into a set of procedures that assumed the next atomic war would allow time for such careful study. The scientists at Hunter’s Point knew better, or suspected better. The data they gathered from the Independence told a story of persistence, of contamination that could not be eliminated, only managed. The base surge had deposited its poison throughout the ship, in places that no hose could reach, in concentrations that no scrubbing could reduce. The ship was a lesson in what it meant to survive an atomic attack, and the lesson was that survival was only the beginning of a longer ordeal.

The work continued through the winter of 1949 and into the spring of 1950. The teams came and went, the samples accumulated, the reports multiplied. The Independence rode at anchor, her hull gradually accumulating barnacles and algae, her interior gradually being stripped of anything that might be useful. The laboratory removed pumps and valves, electrical components and ventilation fans, anything that could be studied for signs of radiation damage or residual contamination. The ship became a shell, her compartments echoing with the footsteps of men in protective suits, her bulkheads marked with the chalk codes of the sampling teams.

Other vessels shared her fate. The attack transport Fallon had been towed to Hunter’s Point the previous year, her hull too contaminated for return to service but not so contaminated that she could not be studied. She lay at a different pier, smaller than the Independence, less heavily instrumented, but subject to the same routine of sampling and analysis. The laboratory had established a protocol for handling these ships, a set of procedures that had been refined through repetition. Each vessel was surveyed, mapped, sampled, and gradually stripped. The process could take months or years, depending on the level of interest and the availability of personnel.

The men who did the work were not the same men who had been at Bikini. The original radiological safety officers had moved on to other assignments, their expertise dispersed across the growing bureaucracy of the atomic establishment. Stafford Warren had gone to the Atomic Energy Commission, where he continued to advocate for the safety protocols he had developed during the tests. The teams at Hunter’s Point were a new generation, trained in the procedures that Warren and his colleagues had improvised under pressure, inheriting a body of knowledge that had been paid for in exposure and uncertainty.

The Independence did not look dangerous. She looked like what she was: a warship at the end of her service life, stripped and waiting. The danger was invisible, contained in the isotopes that clung to her steel, in the particles that had worked their way into the microscopic crevices of her hull. The base surge had deposited its burden three and a half years ago, and the burden remained. Half-lives measured in decades or centuries meant that the contamination would outlast the ship itself, would outlast the men who studied it, would outlast the laboratory that had been built to understand it.

The Navy had learned something from Bikini. The protocols developed for handling contaminated ships, the procedures for decontamination and disposal, the very concept of a radiological safety officer permanently assigned to naval operations—all of these had emerged from the tests and their aftermath. But the learning had come at a cost, and the cost was still being calculated. The ships at Hunter’s Point were evidence of that cost, floating monuments to a problem that could not be commanded away.

The laboratory’s reports made their way through channels, eventually reaching the desks of officers and officials who had never set foot on a contaminated vessel. The reports were technical, dry, filled with numbers and charts. They did not convey the experience of walking through a ship that registered above background on every surface, of seeing the geiger counter respond to contamination that no eye could detect. They reduced the reality of Bikini to data, and the data was filed and forgotten, or filed and ignored, or filed and misunderstood by men who had other priorities.

The Independence would not survive much longer. The laboratory had extracted what it needed, and the Navy had no use for a carrier that could not put to sea. She would be sunk, eventually, towed out beyond the continental shelf and sent to the bottom with whatever ceremony the Navy deemed appropriate. But she had served one last purpose. She had been a classroom, a textbook, a source of samples and data and procedures. She had taught the Navy what it meant to survive an atomic attack, and the Navy had incorporated that teaching into its manuals and moved on.

The Bikinians had been moved too, displaced from their atoll to make way for the tests, their islands rendered uninhabitable by the same forces that had contaminated the Independence. They waited on Rongerik, then on Kwajalein, then on Kili, their displacement extending year after year while the Navy and the Atomic Energy Commission debated responsibility. The ships at Hunter’s Point and the islanders in exile were connected by the same event, the same explosion, the same invisible poison that had entered their respective worlds and refused to leave. The laboratory studied the ships. No one studied the islanders with the same systematic attention.

The sun rose over San Francisco Bay, burning off the last of the fog, illuminating the Independence where she rode at anchor. The men from the laboratory arrived at the pier, collected their equipment, boarded the launch that would take them to the ship. They climbed the accommodation ladder, their boots clanging on the steel, their dosimeters clicking softly in the morning quiet. They had work to do. There were samples to take, measurements to record, reports to file. The work was routine, and the routine was the point. The danger had been domesticated, turned into procedure, rendered manageable by the bureaucratic processes that the modern military had perfected.

A sailor had been assigned to the Independence’s skeleton crew during the tow from Kwajalein. He had spent three months aboard the contaminated carrier, standing watch in compartments that still registered above safe limits, eating his meals in a mess deck that had been declared habitable only after extensive scrubbing. He had worn his dosimeter, had logged his exposure, had been told that the levels were acceptable, that the risk was minimal, that the experts knew what they were doing.

Now he was back at Hunter’s Point, assigned to the shore facility, and he had begun to notice things. The fatigue that did not go away. The bruises that appeared without cause. The blood in his urine that he had not yet reported to the medical officer. He told himself it was nothing, that the doctors would have said something if there was a problem, that the Navy knew what it was doing. He had signed the papers, accepted the assignment, done his duty. The Independence sat in the bay, and he walked the pier, and somewhere in the files of the Naval Radiological Defense Laboratory, his name appeared on a roster with a number beside it, a measurement of exposure that would follow him for the rest of his life, however long that might be.
