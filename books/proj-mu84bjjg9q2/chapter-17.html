Chapter 17

The Scuttling of the Nagato

Seen from above, across the whole Pacific, the battleship Nagato had once been the most feared warship in the Pacific. From her bridge, the commander of the Japanese Combined Fleet had directed the attack on Pearl Harbor. Her sixteen-inch guns had fired the opening salvos of a war that reshaped the world. Now she sat at anchor in Kwajalein lagoon, listed in a disposal memo as “Target Ship Number 31,” her hull too contaminated with fission products to be repaired, her fate sealed by a radiological report no one had expected to write. The material necessity for disposal had been established. The Nagato’s turn had come.

The contrast could not have been starker. This ship had symbolized Japanese naval power at its zenith, the prize American sailors had yearned to capture when they first steamed into Tokyo Bay in August 1945. They had found her damaged, listing, her crew sullen and starving, but intact enough to serve as a trophy. Now, eighteen months after the surrender ceremonies in which she had played a ghostly, silent role, she was something far more dangerous than a vanquished enemy: a vessel whose every compartment held the invisible residue of the Baker shot, the underwater detonation that had coated the target fleet with a radioactive spray no hose could wash away.

The decision to scuttle her had come slowly, reluctantly, through a chain of deliberations that revealed more in their silences than in their written conclusions. The Joint Chiefs had wanted to preserve the Nagato as a war prize, a tangible symbol of victory to be displayed or perhaps opened to the public. The Bureau of Ships had drawn up estimates for repair, calculations that assumed the contamination could be scrubbed, sealed, or otherwise neutralized. But the radiological officers who had monitored the ship since July knew what the bureaus refused to accept: the Nagato was poisoned at a depth no sandblaster could reach.

Stafford Warren’s team compiled evidence methodically: readings from every accessible compartment, decay curves tracked over months, data compared against safety thresholds set for sailors who might work aboard her. The numbers told an unambiguous story. The Baker shot deposited fission products throughout interior spaces—bilges and voids between hull plates and labyrinthine passages designed for battle rather than easy cleaning—and left behind residue no amount of seawater or soap could remove.

Some 42, 000 personnel had been deployed to Bikini Atoll as part of Admiral William H.P. Blandy’s Joint Task Force One to conduct the tests. Their health and safety was the responsibility of Colonel Stafford Warren, the chief radiological safety officer, but the specialized job of monitoring contamination fell to teams equipped with Geiger counters and film badges, men who had trained for this work at Los Alamos and Oak Ridge. They had watched the Baker shot from the observation ships, seen the base surge rise from the lagoon like a wall of mist, recorded the readings as the cloud passed over the target fleet. They knew what they were seeing. They knew what it meant.

The Independence had provided the proof that the contamination was permanent, that the ships of the target fleet could not be reclaimed for service. But the Nagato presented a different problem. She was not an American carrier, a ship whose loss the navy might mourn or whose fate might prompt congressional questions. She was a Japanese battleship, a prize of war whose disposal could be framed as a routine administrative decision. No American jobs depended on her preservation. No American port had lobbied to receive her as a museum. She could be sunk without ceremony, and the navy could present the act as the final disposition of captured enemy materiel.

The internal memoranda that crossed desks in Washington and Pearl Harbor during the winter of 1946-47 betrayed no awareness of the symbolism. They spoke of “disposal options” and “economical methods” and “radiological constraints.” They weighed the cost of towing against the cost of scrapping, the depth of the ocean floor against the depth of the contamination. Nowhere did they acknowledge that the sinking of the Nagato would mark the first major material outcome of the radiological safety officers’ victory in cancelling Test Charlie. Nowhere did they admit that the scuttling was an admission of defeat.

The orders arrived at Kwajalein in late January 1947. The Nagato would be towed to deep water and sunk. The operation was to be conducted with minimal personnel, all of whom would wear protective equipment and submit to monitoring before and after exposure. The designated site lay approximately two hundred miles northeast of Kwajalein, where the seafloor dropped to depths exceeding four thousand fathoms. At such depths, the hull would be crushed by pressure long before it reached the bottom. Whatever contamination remained within her would be entombed in darkness, beyond the reach of divers, salvagers, or future generations who might wonder what lay beneath the waves.

The preparations began in early February. A skeleton crew of American sailors, none of whom had served aboard the ship during her operational life, went aboard to prepare her for the final voyage. They opened sea cocks, inspected bulkheads, rigged demolition charges that would accelerate the flooding once the order was given. They worked in protective suits, their movements clumsy in the tropical heat, their faces hidden behind masks that filtered the air but could not filter the knowledge of what they were breathing.

The radiological officers accompanied them, taking readings at regular intervals, marking the compartments that remained hazardous and those that had decayed to acceptable levels. The distinction was academic. The ship would sink regardless of what the Geiger counters registered. But the protocols demanded documentation, and the documentation demanded numbers, and so the officers compiled their reports with the same meticulous attention they had brought to every phase of Operation Crossroads.

The ship had been selected to participate as a target vessel in the atomic tests, part of the collection of obsolete and captured warships assembled in Bikini lagoon. In mid-March 1946, Nagato had departed Yokosuka for Eniwetok under the command of Captain W.J. Whipple, with an American crew of about 180 men. Her hull had not been repaired from the underwater damage sustained during the attack on 18 July 1945, and she leaked enough that her pumps could not keep up. Her consort, the light cruiser Sakawa, had broken down on 28 March, and Nagato had taken her under tow, a crippled vessel dragging another crippled vessel across the Pacific toward a destination neither would survive.

The Sakawa had foundered during the preparations for Test Able, sinking in the lagoon before the first bomb fell. The Nagato had endured both tests, riding out the aerial detonation of July 1 and the underwater blast of July 25. The Baker shot had been the more damaging of the two, not because of physical force but because of what it left behind. The radioactive sea spray had coated every surface, penetrated every opening, transformed the ship into a floating monument to a new kind of warfare.

The second test was Baker. The bomb was known as Helen of Bikini and was detonated 90 feet underwater on July 25, 1946. Radioactive sea spray caused extensive contamination. A third deep-water test named Charlie was planned for 1947 but was canceled primarily because the navy could not guarantee the safety of the personnel who would have to re-enter the lagoon after the shot. The cancellation had been a victory for Warren and his officers, a recognition that the contamination problem was beyond the navy’s capacity to manage. But the victory had come at a cost. The ships remained. The fission products remained. The question of what to do with them remained.

The towing operation began on the morning of February 18, 1947. The fleet tug USS Current took the Nagato in tow, paying out a steel cable that stretched between the two vessels like a fragile lifeline. The battleship’s engines had been silent since the surrender, her boilers cold, her screws still. She moved now only because another vessel pulled her, a dead weight displacing thousands of tons of water as she passed through the channel and into the open sea.

The weather held fair, the trade winds blowing steady from the east, the seas running three to four feet. The Current’s crew maintained a speed of approximately eight knots, slow enough to prevent the tow cable from snapping, fast enough to reach the designated site before the fuel ran low. The Nagato followed in their wake, her superstructure silhouetted against the horizon, her listing hull a reminder of the damage she had sustained long before the atomic tests.

A small party of radiological officers and sailors remained aboard the battleship during the tow, monitoring the flooding, ensuring the demolition charges remained secure, ready to accelerate the sinking if the tow cable parted or the weather turned. They lived on the upper decks, avoiding the confined spaces below where the contamination lingered. They ate rations from sealed containers, drank water brought over from the tug, slept on cots lashed to the deck where the night breeze could reach them.

The voyage took three days. On the morning of February 21, the Current reached the designated coordinates, a point in the Pacific Ocean where the water turned from turquoise to indigo, where the depth sounder registered bottom at more than four thousand fathoms. The tug’s crew paid out the last of the tow cable, bringing the Nagato to a stop, letting her drift in the gentle swell while the final preparations were made.

The sinking was scheduled for the afternoon. The Current’s crew sent a signal to Pearl Harbor, confirming their position and requesting final authorization. The authorization came back within the hour: proceed as planned. The radiological officers aboard the Nagato made their last rounds, taking final readings, recording the data that would be filed away in archives few would ever read. Then they boarded a small boat that had accompanied them from the tug, transferring their equipment and themselves to safety before the charges were detonated.

The demolition team waited until the small boat was clear, until the tug had taken up the slack in the tow cable and begun to pull away, until the Nagato sat alone in the vast emptiness of the Pacific. Then they sent the signal that ignited the charges.

The explosions were muffled by the water, the sound traveling through the hull before reaching the air. The Nagato shuddered, her decks buckling at predetermined points, her hull opening to the sea. Water rushed in through the breaches, filling the compartments that had once housed Japanese sailors, American occupiers, and finally the contaminated remnants of the atomic age. The ship began to settle by the stern, her list increasing as the water found its level.

The Current’s crew watched from the bridge, recording the scene with cameras and logbooks, documenting the end of a vessel whose career had spanned the entire Pacific War. The Nagato’s sixteen-inch guns, which had bombarded American positions at Guadalcanal and Leyte, pointed skyward at angles that grew more acute as the ship tilted. Her pagoda mast, the distinctive superstructure that had made her silhouette instantly recognizable to American pilots, caught the afternoon light as the hull slid beneath the surface.

The sinking took less than an hour. By 1600, the Nagato was gone, her stern disappearing first, her bow rising briefly before sliding down into the depths. The water closed over her with a sound like a sigh, leaving only a spreading circle of foam and debris to mark the place where she had been. The Current’s crew waited until the surface settled, until the debris dispersed, until the ocean resumed its eternal indifference to the vessels that passed above it.

The radiological officers recorded the final entry in their logs: Target Ship Number 31 disposed of as ordered. The coordinates were noted, the time confirmed, the operation declared complete. The Nagato now lay beneath more than twenty-four thousand feet of water, her hull crushed by pressure, her contamination sealed within a tomb of steel and darkness.

The Current turned toward Kwajalein, her engines thrumming, her wake stretching behind her in a line that would fade long before she reached port. The crew had performed their duty, following orders, executing a plan, completing a task. The officers would file their reports, the photographers would develop their film, the logbooks would be stored in filing cabinets and eventually archives. The Nagato would become a footnote, a statistic, a ship that had once existed and now did not.

But the sinking of the Nagato was not a clean resolution to the contamination problem. It was a tactical retreat, an admission that the navy had no solution for the radioactive ships in its care, no method for rendering them safe, no technology for undoing what the Baker shot had done. The decision to sink her had been presented as practical, economical, routine. It was none of those things. It was a choice made because no other choice remained, a burial at sea for a vessel that could not be saved.

The symbolism was inescapable, even if the officers who planned the operation refused to acknowledge it. The Nagato had been the flagship of the Combined Fleet, the command vessel from which the Japanese high command had directed the attack that brought the United States into the war. Her capture had been a prize, her presence at the surrender ceremonies a tangible sign of victory. Her sinking should have been a triumph, the final chapter in a story of conquest and redemption.

Instead, her end was a warning. The atomic tests had transformed her from a trophy into a liability, from a symbol of victory into evidence of a new kind of danger. The contamination that had killed her was not the result of enemy action but of American experimentation. The bomb that had coated her decks with fission products had been detonated by American scientists, dropped by American bombers, authorized by American commanders. The Nagato had been a target, not a victim, and her fate had been planned as carefully as any battle.

The fleet assembled for the tests had been designed to absorb damage, a collection of vessels whose sacrifice would provide data on the effects of atomic weapons on naval architecture. The Nagato had been part of that fleet, a prize of war whose presence among the targets had added a layer of symbolism to the operation. Her survival of both tests had seemed like evidence of her construction, a reminder that even the atomic bomb could not easily sink a battleship. But survival had proven to be a different kind of death. The contamination had killed her as surely as any torpedo, just more slowly, more invisibly, more completely.

The Current arrived back at Kwajalein on February 24, 1947. The crew disembarked, the reports were filed, the operation was declared complete. The Nagato was gone, sunk in water so deep that no diver could reach her, no salvager could raise her, no future generation could reclaim her. The contamination she carried was entombed, sealed away, removed from the world of human affairs.

But the reports could not address the larger question that the Nagato’s sinking raised. If a Japanese battleship could be disposed of by scuttling, what about the American ships? If the contamination could not be removed, only buried, what did that mean for the vessels that had survived the tests but could not be returned to service? The Independence waited at her mooring, her flight deck awash, her hangar bays holding the residue that no hose could clear. The New York had been towed back to Pearl Harbor, her hull still holding the residues of Baker. The Pennsylvania, the Salt Lake City, the dozens of other vessels that had ridden at anchor in Bikini lagoon—they all carried the same invisible burden.

The reports filed in Washington and Pearl Harbor spoke of disposal and decontamination, of protocols followed and safety thresholds maintained. They did not speak of failure. They did not acknowledge that the sinking of the Nagato was an admission that the cleanup had not worked, that the hoses and the scrubbing parties and the monitoring teams had accomplished nothing of lasting value. The ship had been dirty. The ship had been sunk. The problem had been solved.

But the problem had not been solved. It had been hidden. The Nagato lay on the bottom of the Pacific, her hull crushed, her contamination sealed within steel plates that would eventually rust and release their contents into the water column. The fission products would decay over years and decades and centuries, their half-lives marking time in a way that human calendars could not measure. The ship was gone, but the contamination remained. It had simply been moved from one place to another, from a lagoon where it threatened sailors to a depth where it threatened no one, at least no one who could be seen or counted.

The navy had learned something from the Nagato. It had learned that disposal by sinking was possible, that a contaminated ship could be towed to deep water and sent to the bottom without incident. It had learned that the public would accept such operations without protest, that the sinking of a Japanese battleship would generate no headlines, no congressional inquiries, no demands for explanation. It had learned that the contamination problem could be managed, if by management one meant the removal of contaminated vessels from the sight of those who might be troubled by them.

What the navy refused to learn was more significant. It refused to learn that the contamination was permanent, that the fission products deposited by the Baker shot could not be washed away or sealed over or otherwise neutralized. It refused to learn that the ships of the target fleet were not merely dirty but fundamentally changed, their steel infused with radioactivity at a molecular level that no scrubbing could reach. It refused to learn that the atomic age had created a new category of hazard, one that did not respond to the traditional methods of naval damage control.

The Nagato was the first to go. She would not be the last.
