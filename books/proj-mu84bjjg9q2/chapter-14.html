Chapter 14

The Long Tow to Kwajalein

Kwajalein Atoll, September 1946. The anchorage lay spread under a hard Pacific sun, its waters crowded with the machinery of victory and occupation. Support ships rode at their moorings in ordered rows, their decks busy with the work of a navy still mobilized for a war that had ended a year before. Men moved across the steel surfaces with the purposeful rhythm of a functioning military port, loading supplies, repairing engines, maintaining the vast infrastructure that projected American power across the Pacific. The atoll had been taken from the Japanese in February 1944, its defenders killed to the last man in a battle that lasted four days. Now it served as the forward headquarters for the Marshall Islands, a pristine base of operations where the Pacific Fleet could refuel, rearm, and rest. The war had moved on, leaving behind a working machine.

Two hundred miles to the east, a different fleet crept westward through the open ocean.

The submarine Apogon lay dead in the water, her hull awash, under tow from a navy tug whose crew worked in shifts around the clock. The attack transport Crittenden followed behind, her own engines silent, her decks empty of the marines who had once trained to storm beaches from her holds. Ahead of them both, the captured Japanese battleship Nagato rode low in the swells, her list visible from a thousand yards, under tow from another tug whose winches strained against the weight of a vessel that had once been the pride of the Imperial Navy.

These were ghost ships—stripped of their crews, their weapons disabled, their holds filled with seawater that no pump could clear. They moved across the Pacific not under their own power but as cargo, hauled toward a destination that promised containment but delivered only displacement.

The contrast between the two scenes held the central problem of Operation Crossroads in miniature. Kwajalein represented the navy as it understood itself: organized, functional, capable of moving men and materiel across thousands of miles of ocean. The slow convoy approaching from the east represented what the atomic age had made of that navy: a custodian of hazards it could not neutralize, forced to move its problems from one anchorage to another in the hope that distance might accomplish what decontamination had failed to achieve.

The decision to move the most heavily contaminated ships from Bikini to Kwajalein had emerged from the collision of institutional momentum and radiological reality. By late August, Colonel Stafford Warren and his radiological safety officers had concluded that the target fleet at Bikini could not be safely reboarded. The base surge from Test Baker had deposited fission products across the ships’ decks and into their ventilation systems, creating contamination that hoses could not remove and scrubbing could not abate. Warren had recommended that the ships be sunk where they lay, their radioactive materials entombed in the lagoon’s sediments.

The navy rejected this recommendation. The ships represented data—platforms for studying atomic effects that might prove valuable for future weapons development. They represented money—vessels that could be scrapped for steel if their contamination could be reduced to manageable levels. They represented a problem that the navy refused to believe was insoluble.

The compromise was to move them: Kwajalein offered a larger anchorage, better support facilities, and a location far enough from Bikini that any contamination released during transit would be diluted across the open Pacific. The reasoning made sense on a map; it failed to account for what the ships themselves had become.

The Nagato had once been the most powerful battleship in the Japanese Navy. Commissioned in 1920, she had served as the flagship for the Combined Fleet during the attack on Pearl Harbor, her bridge the vantage point from which Admiral Yamamoto had watched his planes return from their strike on Battleship Row. She had survived the war’s great naval battles, though her later years had been marked by inactivity and decay. By the time she arrived at Bikini for the atomic tests, her engines were failing, her crew was demoralized, and her hull had not seen a proper dockyard in years. The Americans had taken her as a prize, sailed her to the Marshall Islands under a prize crew, and anchored her in the lagoon as a target. Test Able had missed her. Test Baker had drenched her in radioactive spray.

Now she rode under tow, her list increasing with each passing day. The tug’s crew had been briefed on radiological safety procedures: maintain distance from the hull, monitor exposure with Geiger counters, limit time spent on the tow line. The procedures assumed a level of understanding that many of the sailors did not possess. Geiger counters were new instruments, their readings open to interpretation, their significance unclear to men who had spent the war handling depth charges and anti-aircraft guns rather than scientific equipment. The tug’s skipper had his orders, and his orders were to deliver the Nagato to Kwajalein. The contamination aboard the battleship was someone else’s problem.

The tow itself became an exercise in controlled desperation. The Nagato’s hull leaked from damage sustained during the tests and from years of neglect before them. Her bilge pumps could not operate without power, and her power plant had been disabled. Water entered the hull faster than it could be bailed, and the tug’s crew found themselves managing a slow-motion sinking. They adjusted the tow speed, changed headings to reduce the stress on the tow line, and watched the battleship settle lower in the water with each passing hour. The Pacific’s swells worked against them, driving water into the hull’s compromised sections and threatening to break the tow line that was the only thing keeping the Nagato from slipping beneath the surface.

The submarine Apogon faced her own difficulties. Submarines were not designed to be towed. Their hulls, optimized for underwater running, presented poor profiles for surface transit. The tow line chafed against the hull’s fairweather, threatening to cut through the steel cable that connected her to the tug. Her ballast tanks, damaged by the Baker detonation, could not be properly trimmed, and she rode the swells at an angle that made the tow line’s strain uneven and unpredictable. The crew of the tug maintained a constant watch, adjusting the line’s tension by hand, fighting to keep the submarine moving in the right direction.

The attack transport Crittenden had her own story. During the war, convoy escort duty in the Atlantic had been her first assignment, followed by fire-support missions during five major amphibious invasions—Attu, Normandy, Southern France, Iwo Jima, and Okinawa. When Japan surrendered, the navy selected her for Operation Crossroads, transforming her combat service into a final role as a target vessel. Now she moved westward under tow, her war record irrelevant to the atomic contamination that coated her decks and filled her holds. The men who towed her knew nothing of her history. They knew only that she was radioactive and that their orders required them to deliver her to Kwajalein.

The journey across two hundred miles of open ocean transformed the Pacific sea lanes into a vector of potential contamination. The ships leaked more than water. As they moved through the swells, their hulls released radioactive particles into the water around them, creating a moving plume of contamination that dispersed into the Pacific’s vastness. Dispersion was the point—or at least, it was the justification for the move.

Dilution was the navy’s preferred solution to contamination, the assumption being that the ocean’s volume would render the radioactivity harmless. The assumption remained untested. No one had measured the effect of releasing fission products into the open sea. No one knew whether the dispersed contamination would accumulate in marine life, whether it would travel with currents to distant shores, whether it would persist in forms that could enter the food chain. The navy moved the ships first and asked questions later—if it asked them at all.

Stafford Warren’s team monitored the convoy’s progress with increasing alarm. The radiological safety officers had argued against the move from the beginning. They had seen the contamination firsthand, had watched their Geiger counters climb as they walked the ships’ decks, had documented the readings that showed fission products embedded in paint and metal and wood. Moving the ships would not remove the contamination but merely relocate it, creating new hotspots wherever the ships were anchored. The men who crewed the tugs were being exposed to radiation levels that exceeded the safety limits Warren had established. The navy’s schedule—the drive to clear Bikini, to move the problem elsewhere, to declare the operation complete—was overriding the precautions that radiological science demanded.

The reports that reached Warren’s desk told a story of institutional momentum colliding with radiological reality. The tug crews had been given exposure limits, but those limits were being exceeded. The ships they towed were unstable, their hulls leaking, their lists increasing. Contamination aboard the target vessels had not been reduced by the move; it had simply been carried across the Pacific, invisible and persistent. The unwashable mist that had coated the fleet at Bikini now rode with the convoy, spreading its contamination across the sea lanes and into the anchorage at Kwajalein.

The Nagato’s condition deteriorated as the convoy approached Kwajalein. Her list had increased to fifteen degrees, and water continued to enter her hull through cracks and seams that the atomic blast had opened. The tug’s crew worked to keep her afloat, but their efforts were temporary measures against a problem that had no permanent solution. The battleship was sinking slowly, her rate of descent measured in inches per day rather than feet per minute. The question was not whether she would sink but when, and where, and what would happen to her contamination when she finally went under.

The convoy’s arrival at Kwajalein marked the transformation of the atoll from a support base into a contamination site. The anchorage that had received the ships of victory now received the ships of the atomic age—vessels that could not be used, could not be safely boarded, could not be scrapped or salvaged without exposing workers to radiation levels that exceeded any standard of safety. Kwajalein had been claimed by the German Empire along with the rest of the Marshall Islands in 1885. After World War I, the island came under the South Seas Mandate of the Empire of Japan, though the island itself was uninhabited. During World War II, the atoll became part of the vast US Naval Base Marshalls, its lagoon transformed into an anchorage that could accommodate the Pacific Fleet. Now that anchorage would accommodate the fleet’s radioactive remnants.

The vessels entering Kwajalein lagoon were not the same ships that had arrived at Bikini three months before. Exposure had transformed them: steel and wood and paint now carried an invisible hazard no hose could wash away. Men working at Kwajalein—stevedores, mechanics, support personnel—watched the convoy arrive with curiosity that gave way to unease as rumors spread. These were the ships from Bikini; these were the ships that had been at ground zero; these were the ships no one could safely board.

The navy’s response to this unease was to control information. Radiological hazards aboard the target ships were classified, their details restricted to those with a need to know. Workers at Kwajalein were told that the ships were contaminated but not that the contamination was dangerous. They were told to maintain distance, to limit exposure, to follow procedures that no one fully understood. The gap between official reassurances and Geiger counter readings created a space where fear grew, fed by rumor and speculation and the sight of vessels that had once been proud warships now reduced to radioactive hulks.

The disposal calculus that had governed Operation Crossroads from its beginning now governed the move to Kwajalein. The ships were valuable as data, as scrap, as symbols of American power over the atomic age. Their contamination was a problem to be managed, a cost to be weighed against the benefits of their preservation. The men who towed them, the men who would work on them at Kwajalein, were costs too—individual exposures to be tracked, health records to be maintained, risks to be distributed across a population that had not been consulted about the dangers they faced. The calculus did not include the Marshall Islanders, whose atolls had been transformed into testing grounds and whose waters now received the radioactive remnants of experiments they had not chosen to host.

The Nagato’s arrival at Kwajalein marked the end of her journey but not the end of her problems. She anchored in the lagoon’s deeper water, her list still visible, her hull still leaking. Japanese sailors who had once crewed her were gone, repatriated to a homeland they barely recognized. The American prize crew that had brought her to Bikini had been reassigned, their service records noting their exposure to atomic detonations but offering no context for what that exposure might mean. The battleship sat alone, a monument to a defeated empire and a victorious nation’s atomic ambitions, her decks empty, her guns silent, her hull filled with water that no pump could clear.

The other ships joined her in the anchorage, their hulls forming a loose formation that mirrored the target array at Bikini. The Apogon lay nearby, her submarine hull awkward on the surface, her ballast tanks unable to maintain proper trim. The Crittenden rode at anchor a few hundred yards away, her transport decks empty of the marines who had once practiced amphibious landings from her holds. Support ships that had serviced the fleet at Bikini now serviced the fleet at Kwajalein, their crews aware that something had changed but unsure what it meant.

Radiological safety officers who had opposed the move found their concerns vindicated by the convoy’s arrival. Contamination that had plagued the ships at Bikini had traveled with them, as persistent as the navy’s determination to preserve its atomic test data. Readings taken at Kwajalein matched readings taken at Bikini, Geiger counters responding to fission products that had not decayed, had not dispersed, had not responded to any decontamination methods the navy had attempted. The problem had not been solved. It had been moved.

The move itself had created new problems. The tow had exposed tug crews to radiation levels that exceeded the safety limits Warren had established. The ships had leaked contaminated water into the Pacific, creating a plume of radioactivity whose effects remained unknown. The anchorage at Kwajalein now contained a concentration of radioactive vessels that posed risks to support personnel working there, risks that had not been present before the convoy’s arrival. The navy had succeeded in clearing Bikini, but it had failed to contain the contamination that the atomic tests had created.

The institutional response to this failure was to proceed as if success had been achieved. The ships were at Kwajalein. The lagoon at Bikini was clear. The tests were over. The data had been collected. Contamination was someone else’s problem now—a phrase that would echo through the navy’s handling of atomic waste for decades to come. Men who had been exposed, waters that had been contaminated, ships that could not be used: all of these were costs to be absorbed, risks to be distributed, problems to be managed.

Management of those problems would fall to the same men who had created them. Stafford Warren remained responsible for radiological safety, his authority constrained by the navy’s determination to preserve its ships and its schedule. Admiral William H.P. Blandy, who had commanded the Joint Task Force that conducted the tests, remained responsible for the fleet, his authority constrained by the contamination that rendered his ships unusable. The collision between their imperatives—safety on one side, operations on the other—would shape the navy’s response to the atomic age in ways that neither man fully understood.

The ships at Kwajalein were not the end of the problem. They were its beginning. Contamination coating their decks would persist for years, decaying slowly, releasing particles into the water and air around them. The vessels themselves would decay faster, their hulls corroding, their structures weakening, their ability to stay afloat diminishing with each passing month. The navy would face the same question that had confronted it at Bikini: what to do with vessels that could not be used and could not be safely destroyed. The question would recur, unanswered, as the atomic age progressed.

The Prinz Eugen, the German heavy cruiser that had been transferred to American custody after the war, had also been present at Bikini. She had been towed to Kwajalein along with the other contaminated ships, her own hull leaking, her own systems failing. She would sink in the Kwajalein lagoon in December 1946, four months after the Baker test, her bilge pumps unable to keep pace with water entering her hull. Her sinking would be the first of many, a preview of what awaited the other vessels that now rode at anchor in the atoll’s contaminated waters.

Men who had crewed the tugs, who had walked the decks of the target ships, who had operated the Geiger counters and monitored exposure levels, returned to their regular duties with service records that noted their participation in Operation Crossroads. Those records did not explain what participation might mean for their health, their futures, their children. Exposure limits established for the tests were based on assumptions about radiation’s effects that would prove optimistic. Men who had trusted those limits would discover, years later, that the trust had been misplaced.

The Pacific that had received the convoy’s contamination was vast, its waters capable of diluting radioactive particles to levels that instruments could barely detect. But dilution was not disappearance. Particles entering the water would persist, their half-lives measured in years and decades, their effects on marine life and the humans who depended on it unknown and largely uninvestigated. The ocean that had absorbed the navy’s atomic waste would keep that waste, moving it through currents and food chains, concentrating it in organisms and sediments, returning it to human populations in forms that no one had anticipated.

The anchorage at Kwajalein, crowded with contaminated ships, presented a consolidated problem that demanded a high-level decision. The navy could not simply leave the vessels where they were, decaying and leaking, posing risks to personnel working at the base. But it could not easily move them again, could not scrap them without exposing workers to radiation, could not sink them without releasing their contamination into the lagoon’s waters. The problem that had been moved from Bikini had not been solved by the move. It had been concentrated, made visible, made impossible to ignore.

Warren would look at the ships in the anchorage and see evidence of his worst fears realized. Contamination he had fought to contain had spread, carried by the very ships he had recommended sinking. Exposure limits he had established had been exceeded, men who crewed the tugs added to the roster of atomic test participants whose health would need monitoring. Institutional momentum that had overridden his recommendations had created a new crisis, one that would require a new response.

Blandy would look at the same ships and see a challenge to be managed. The navy had moved its atomic test problem from Bikini to Kwajalein, had preserved its data platforms, had maintained its capacity to study the effects of atomic weapons on naval vessels. Contamination was a complication, not a catastrophe. The ships could be studied, their radiation levels monitored, their eventual disposal planned. The navy had faced challenges before and had overcome them. This challenge would be no different.

The difference in their perspectives would shape the conflict to come. Warren saw contamination that could not be removed, risks that could not be managed, a problem that could not be solved by the navy’s traditional methods. Blandy saw a logistical challenge, a complication to be worked around, a problem that would yield to sufficient effort and ingenuity. The collision between these views would determine the fate of the ships, the safety of men who worked on them, and the navy’s understanding of what atomic weapons had made of its fleet.

The lagoon at Kwajalein lay still under the Pacific sun, its waters holding the reflections of ships that had once been instruments of war and were now instruments of a different kind of history. Contamination they carried was invisible, its presence marked only by Geiger counters that monitored their decks and exposure records that accumulated in personnel files. Men working around them went about their duties, aware that something had changed but unsure what it meant for their futures. The ships themselves waited, their hulls slowly filling with water, their structures slowly weakening, their contamination slowly spreading. The anchored, contaminated fleet at Kwajalein presented a consolidated problem that demanded a high-level decision, handing off the pressure to Stafford Warren and Admiral Blandy.
