Chapter 3

The Unwilling Pioneers of Rongerik

The fleet of ghosts waited for the bomb. Ninety vessels rode at anchor in Bikini’s lagoon, their hulls painted in orange and gray, their decks stripped for sacrifice. The water lay calm around them, turquoise shading to indigo where the drop-off fell away into the Pacific deep. On the beach, a different kind of preparation was under way. The 167 Bikinians were being moved to Rongerik, a relocation that would begin a permanent exile.

February 1946. Bikini Atoll, Marshall Islands.

The landing craft grounded on the coral sand with a grinding hiss. The Seabees had come to take the church apart. King Juda stood watching as men with hammers and crowbars climbed the steps of the structure his people had built with their own hands, the planks brought from distant supply ships, the nails purchased with copra saved over years. The white-painted walls that had sheltered hymns on Sunday mornings now came apart board by board.

The American officer in charge had explained the plan. The church would be reassembled at the new location. Every plank numbered, every beam marked. Nothing lost. The Bikinians would have their house of worship on Rongerik, just as they had here.

Juda said nothing. The decision had been made in distant rooms where his voice carried no weight. The Americans had asked for his atoll, and he had given it. Now he watched his community’s heart being dismantled and stacked in a landing craft like so much surplus lumber.

March 6, 1946. The camera crew had arrived to film the departure. Commodore Ben Wyatt, the military governor of the Marshall Islands, wanted a record of the moment. He arranged the Bikinians on the beach, positioning them for the lens. He asked Juda to speak, to say again what he had said on February 10, when the Americans had first come with their request.

Juda stood before the camera. Wind moved the palm fronds behind him. The Seabees waited with their tools. His people waited with their bundles. Wyatt called for action.

“We are willing,” Juda said.

Wyatt stopped the camera. He asked for another take. The king needed to say more, needed to repeat the words that had made history, the words that had given away an atoll to the atomic age. The film would be shown to presidents and admirals, to the world watching this experiment. The king needed to speak as a leader should speak.

Seven times the camera rolled. Seven takes. Each time, Juda stood before the lens and offered the same phrase, nothing more. The film recorded his face, his eyes, the silence between his words. The crew packed up. The moment captured, though not in the form Wyatt had imagined.

The Bikinians had agreed on February 10. Commodore Ben H. Wyatt, the military governor of the Marshall Islands, had come with the proposition: the United States needed their atoll for a test that would determine the future of warfare. Scientists needed to understand what the atomic bomb could do to ships, to harbors, to the machines of modern navies. Bikini, with its protected lagoon and remote location, served the purpose perfectly.

Juda had listened to the translator. The Americans spoke of temporary relocation, of return when the tests were complete. Compensation, protection, partnership in the great work of science. God’s will and man’s destiny.

The king turned to his people. The eleven family heads, the alaps, who held authority over the land and its use. Nine chose Rongerik as their new home. The atoll lay 120 miles to the east, close enough to reach by ship, far enough to be safe from whatever the bomb might bring. Uninhabited, empty, waiting. No one lived on Rongerik because it had an inadequate water and food supply and due to deep-rooted traditional beliefs that the island was haunted by the Demon Girls of Ujae.

The Americans nodded. Rongerik would do. The Seabees would help with the move. The Navy would provide transport. The Bikinians would be relocated before the tests began, returned when the tests were done. A temporary displacement, a small price for the advancement of human knowledge.

What the Americans did not say, what the Bikinians knew but could not make the translators understand, was that Rongerik was not empty. Home of Lōmōr, the demon goddess who walked the beaches at night. The place where spirits gathered, where the living should not go. The atoll had been uninhabited for generations because the Marshall Islanders knew what waited there.

But the Americans had maps and charts. Surveys and reports. The authority of conquerors who had defeated Japan and now administered these islands as trust territories. They needed the Bikinians moved. Rongerik was available. The logic of power did not include demons in its calculations.

The loading continued. The Bikinians carried their belongings to the landing craft: cooking pots and fishing gear, woven mats and wooden tools, the things that could be transported. The things that mattered. The things that could not be left behind. The coconut palms that had fed them for generations stayed rooted in the soil of Bikini. The breadfruit trees that had shaded their meetings. The wells that had given them sweet water. The graves of their ancestors.

Some things cannot be carried.

The LST pulled away from the beach. Islanders watched their home recede. Palm trees stood in rows along the shoreline, fronds waving in the trade winds. Thatched roofs of their houses showed above the vegetation. The church was gone, but the foundation remained, a rectangle of coral rubble where children had been baptized, where marriages had been blessed, where dead had been mourned.

Bikini Atoll. Twenty-three islands surrounding a lagoon of 229 square miles. The name meant “coconut place” in their language. Home for longer than memory could reach. Now it belonged to the bomb.

The crossing to Rongerik took hours. The landing craft rolled in the swells, spray breaking over the bow. Bikinians huddled on the deck, bundles around them, eyes on the horizon where home had disappeared. Children slept against their mothers. Old men sat with faces to the wind.

Rongerik emerged from the sea as a low line of vegetation, seventeen islands on a coral ring surrounding a lagoon far smaller than Bikini’s. Total land area: 1.68 square kilometers, less than a third of what they had left. Palm trees stood sparse on the narrow strips of sand. Vegetation looked stunted, struggling. The lagoon enclosed 144 square kilometers, but the land could not sustain them.

The landing craft grounded on the beach. Bikinians stepped onto sand that felt different beneath their feet. Air smelled different. Light fell differently through the trees. Not their place. Somewhere else, somewhere that belonged to other beings, other powers.

Seabees had prepared a camp. Tents stood in rows on cleared ground. Canned food and water had been stored in supply sheds. The Americans had done what they could to make the place habitable, or at least to make it look habitable. The relocation was complete. The Bikinians were now the unwilling pioneers of Rongerik.

Navy vessels departed. Seabees packed their tools. Cameras stopped rolling. The experiment could proceed.

March turned to April. Bikinians explored their new home with growing understanding of what they had lost. The lagoon at Rongerik was small, its fisheries limited. Coconut palms bore fewer fruits, the trees thinner and more widely spaced. Breadfruit trees that had provided starch for daily meals were absent entirely. Wells yielded water, but it was brackish, tainted by salt.

Fishing parties went out in their canoes. Reef fish were smaller here, harder to catch. Shellfish beds that had supplemented their diet at Bikini did not exist. Ocean currents that brought nutrients to Bikini’s lagoon did not reach this smaller atoll with the same abundance.

They were 167 people on an island chain that could not support them.

Americans had left supplies. Canned meat, powdered milk, rice, flour. Rations that fed armies could feed islanders too, for a time. But the Bikinians were not soldiers. Fishermen and farmers, people who had lived from their land and sea for generations. Canned food was foreign to their taste, foreign to their ways. They ate it because there was nothing else.

April turned to May. Supply ships that were supposed to return did not come. Navy was busy with other matters. Fleet was assembling at Bikini. Scientists were preparing instruments. Congressmen and journalists were booking passage on observation ships. World’s attention focused on the coming spectacle.

On Rongerik, the spectacle was hunger.

Bikinians sent messages. Asked for more food, more water, more of the supplies they had been promised. Messages went to the military governor, to Navy commanders, to anyone who would listen. Responses were slow in coming. Logistics of feeding 167 people on a remote atoll were complicated. Supply runs had to be scheduled around test preparations. Priorities of Operation Crossroads came first.

Juda watched his people grow thinner. Children did not run and play as they had on Bikini. Old men sat in shade and conserved strength. Women cooked meager rations and tried to make them stretch. Fishing parties went further from shore, stayed longer, returned with less.

Americans had called this a temporary relocation. Spoken of return. Promised that everything would be restored, that tests would be safe, that Bikinians would come back to their atoll when scientists were done.

Bikinians had believed them. They had said they would go believing that everything is in the the hands of God. The phrase had been recorded, translated, presented to the world as evidence of willing cooperation. Americans had gotten what they needed: consent, or something that could be presented as consent.

What the Bikinians had actually said was more complicated. Everything is in God’s hands. Not an affirmation of the American plan but a recognition of their own powerlessness. They could not stop the bomb. Could not refuse the request. Could only place their fate in powers larger than the Navy, larger than atomic scientists, larger than political calculations that had selected their home for destruction.

Now they waited on Rongerik and discovered what God’s hands held for them.

May turned to June. Supply ship finally arrived. Navy had not forgotten them entirely. But supplies were inadequate, a fraction of what was needed. Officers in charge had other concerns. Test date was approaching. Fleet was ready. World was watching.

On Bikini, preparations continued. Target ships rode at their moorings, waiting for the bomb. USS Nevada, painted orange for visibility, held the aiming point. Japanese battleship Nagato, captured prize of the Pacific War, waited nearby. German cruiser Prinz Eugen, taken from the defeated Kriegsmarine, floated in the lagoon. Aircraft carrier Saratoga, veteran of a hundred Pacific operations, stood ready to receive the blast. Independence and other vessels of the ghost fleet waited for their fate.

Scientists placed instruments on every deck. Photographers mounted cameras on every vantage point. Radiological safety officers prepared equipment. Navy established its command structure. Admiral William H.P. Blandy oversaw the Joint Task Force One, some forty-two thousand men assembled for the greatest experiment in naval history. The specialised job of radiological safety was handled by the Manhattan Project, with Stafford Warren as Chief of the Radiological Safety Section.

On Rongerik, 167 people tried to survive.

Cultural dislocation compounded physical hardship. Bikinians had been a community of fishermen and farmers, people who knew their place in the order of things. Chiefs and family heads, traditions and ceremonies, a way of life that had sustained them for generations. That way of life had been tied to their atoll, to specific geography of Bikini, to reefs and channels and islands they knew as intimately as they knew their own bodies.

Rongerik was not their place. Knowledge that had guided them at Bikini meant nothing here. Fishing grounds were different. Weather patterns were different. Seasons and tides and movements of fish through the lagoon followed patterns they did not know.

Beginners in their own lives, apprentices to survival in a land that did not want them.

Demons of Rongerik were not just superstition. Embodiment of a truth Americans had not understood: that people belong to places, that relationship between community and land cannot be transferred like cargo from one vessel to another. Bikinians had been removed not just from homes but from web of relationships that made those homes meaningful.

Americans had seen atolls as interchangeable, dots on a map that could be swapped according to military necessity. Bikini was needed for the test. Rongerik was available. Logic was simple, calculation straightforward. Human costs were externalities, details to be managed, problems for someone else.

Pattern was already clear, though few recognized it at the time. Disposability calculus that would mark the entire operation was visible in the removal of the Bikinians. Their welfare ranked below the test schedule. Their needs ranked below scientific requirements. Their lives ranked below data that would be gathered when bombs fell.

Same calculus would appear again when sailors scrubbed radioactive ships with brooms and soap. Same calculus would drive conflict between Stafford Warren’s radiological safety officers and Navy’s operational schedule. Same calculus would determine what was recorded and what was forgotten, what was studied and what was ignored.

But that was still to come. In June 1946, immediate concern was survival.

Bikinians had been on Rongerik for three months. Situation was deteriorating. Food supplies running low. Fishing inadequate. Water barely drinkable. Children showed signs of malnutrition. Old people grew weaker.

Juda sent another message. People needed help. Needed more supplies, better supplies, regular deliveries. Needed Americans to honor promises that had been made.

Response came through channels. Test was imminent. Navy was occupied. Supply runs would continue when possible.

Test was imminent. That was what mattered. Operation that had displaced 167 people from their ancestral home was approaching its climax. World was watching. Scientists were ready. Ships were waiting.

On Rongerik, Bikinians waited too. Food that might not come. Water that tasted of salt. News of when they might return to the coconut place they had given to the atomic age.

Navy had assembled its fleet with meticulous precision. Ninety ships positioned at carefully calculated distances from aiming point. Instruments deployed to measure blast and heat and radiation. Cameras ready to record moment of detonation. Every detail planned, every contingency considered.

Relocation of Bikinians had been handled with none of that precision. A few meetings, a few promises, a few supply drops. People who had lived on Bikini for generations transported to an atoll that could not sustain them, left to survive as best they could while world’s attention focused on spectacle to come.

Contrast revealed priorities. Ships mattered. Data mattered. Demonstration of American power in atomic age mattered. The 167 people who had been displaced to make demonstration possible did not matter, or mattered only enough to be managed, contained, kept from interfering with main event.

June gave way to final preparations. Target fleet was ready. Observation ships were taking on passengers: congressmen and journalists, scientists and military observers, witnesses who would see atomic bomb’s effect on naval power. Countdown had begun.

Test Able was scheduled for July 1. First atomic detonation since Nagasaki would airburst over the fleet, testing what new weapon could do to warships at sea. Scientists would gather data. Navy would learn whether its vessels could survive blast. World would see what atomic power meant for future of warfare.

On Rongerik, Bikinians knew nothing of these preparations. They knew only that they were hungry, that children were thin, that promises of return seemed increasingly distant. Given their atoll to Americans for an experiment they did not understand. Trusted in promises made by men who had already moved on to other concerns.

Church that had been dismantled and transported had been reassembled on Rongerik’s shore. Planks were the same, nails were the same, shape was the same. But light fell differently through windows. Sound of waves came from different direction. Hymns they sang echoed against unfamiliar walls.

A church is more than planks and nails. A home is more than a house on a beach. A people are more than bodies to be transported from one dot on a map to another.

Americans had taken what they needed. Bikinians had given what they had been forced to give. Experiment would proceed. Bombs would fall. Data would be gathered and analyzed and presented to world as evidence of American power and scientific achievement.

But first cost had already been paid, and it had been paid by people who had no stake in outcome, no share in glory, no voice in decisions that had changed their lives forever.

The 167 Bikinians were now stranded on inadequate Rongerik, their crisis deepening as the calendar moved inexorably toward July 1st and Test Able.

While the Bikinians struggled, the immense machinery of Operation Crossroads moved forward. The paint smelled of chemicals and distance. A boatswain’s mate knelt on the teak deck of the USS Nevada with a five-gallon bucket and a wide brush, working the bright orange pigment into the gray wood. Around him, the lagoon filled with silent ships.
