Chapter 22

The Skeletons in the Kwajalein Lagoon

The press release dated January 1949 spoke in the clipped, confident tones of naval public relations. The USS Independence, a light aircraft carrier that had served with distinction in the Pacific War, would depart Kwajalein Atoll for the United States, there to participate in further tests related to the nation’s defense preparations. The ship would be used as a target, the announcement explained, contributing to the Navy’s ongoing research into the effects of modern weapons on naval vessels. Photographers documented her departure. The accompanying captions noted her wartime service and her role in the Bikini tests. Now she would serve one final time, a proud vessel giving her all for the fleet’s future.

The radiological survey log from the same week told a different story. Deck plates aboard the Independence still registered readings that required protective clothing and time limits for exposure. The hangar deck, scrubbed and rescrubbed since July 1946, remained hot. The engineering spaces, where salt water had circulated through the Baker detonation’s radioactive base surge, showed contamination levels that no amount of washing had reduced. The ship was not departing for tests. She was departing because she could not stay.

Human and environmental costs remained officially unaddressed, awaiting their long-term reckoning. The Board of Inquiry had finished its work. The target fleet remained.

—-

Over the first half of 1949, the navy’s initial salvage ambition collapsed entirely under the unyielding reality of radioactive persistence. The ships that had survived the Baker detonation, ninety vessels anchored in the lagoon at Bikini and then towed to Kwajalein for study and disposition, had become something else entirely. Not a fleet. Not a resource. A problem. Ultimately, only nine target ships were able to be scrapped rather than scuttled. The remaining target ships were scuttled off Bikini or Kwajalein Atolls, or near the Hawaiian Islands or the California coast during 1946–1948. This final dispersal, the last act of the target fleet’s physical existence, was the unacknowledged consequence of the Baker test’s first, concentrated radioactive fallout.

The Independence had been the centerpiece of the radiological study program. She was a carrier, large enough to absorb contamination in multiple compartments, complex enough to test whether a major warship could survive atomic attack and remain operational. The Army-Navy Joint Task Force had designated her for intensive examination. Technicians had gone aboard with Geiger counters and film badges. They had mapped the contamination. They had tried to remove it.

The ship had absorbed the Baker test’s radioactive water through her hull, her intakes, her ventilation ducts. The base surge had washed over her flight deck and drained through her hangar bay, leaving fission products embedded in the steel. Salt spray had carried radioactive particles into every corner. The decontamination teams had scrubbed. Soap, acid, steam, sandblasting, each method had been tried in turn. Contaminated paint was stripped and replaced. Pipes were flushed, valves swapped out.

None of it had worked. The readings remained.

—-

The scientists had understood this would happen. Stafford Warren, the chief radiological safety officer for Operation Crossroads, had warned that fission products bound to metal and coral in ways that ordinary cleaning could not reverse. The Baker test had detonated underwater, and the water had become radioactive. That water had touched every surface. It had dried there. The particles had lodged in the microscopic irregularities of steel, in the pores of paint, in the fibers of rope and canvas. No hose could wash them away because no hose could reach them.

The navy had not wanted to believe this. The ships represented enormous value. A battleship, an aircraft carrier, submarines, destroyers, landing craft, the target fleet contained vessels worth millions of dollars, vessels that could still sail, still fight, still serve. The notion that atomic contamination rendered them permanently unusable contradicted every instinct of naval management. Ships were built to be repaired. Damage was meant to be fixed.

But the contamination was not damage in any sense the navy understood. It was a condition. It could not be repaired because it was not broken. It was poisoned.

—-

The Independence had been towed from Bikini to Kwajalein in the months after Crossroads. She was too radioactive to crew. Tugs hauled her through the lagoon channels and out into the open sea, then west to the vast anchorage at Kwajalein Atoll. There she joined the other survivors of the target fleet. Some floated at anchor, listing slightly, their decks empty. Others had been beached on the lagoon’s edge, their hulls settling into the sand.

Kwajalein had become a graveyard. The atoll’s lagoon, one of the largest in the Pacific, could hold the fleet with room to spare. The navy had established a maintenance detachment there, a skeleton crew tasked with monitoring the ships, pumping them out when they flooded, keeping them afloat long enough for study. The detachment kept logs. The logs recorded readings.

The readings did not fall.

—-

This chapter chronicles the final, grisly phase of the target fleet’s disposition. The ships at Kwajalein had to go somewhere. Staying where they were, leaking contamination into the lagoon, requiring constant attention, serving no purpose, was not an option. Returning to service was impossible. Scrapping them in ordinary shipyards, where workers would encounter the same invisible hazard that had defeated the decontamination teams, was unthinkable. They had to be destroyed.

The navy had hoped to learn from them first. The Independence had been the primary subject. Technicians had gone aboard in protective gear, working limited shifts, taking measurements, cutting samples. Contaminated machinery was removed for laboratory analysis. Everything was documented. The data would inform future ship design, future damage control procedures, future war.

But the data had limits. The ship could not be made clean. Every attempt to reduce the radiation had failed. The Independence was a floating laboratory that could not leave the laboratory, a study subject that could not be released.

—-

In January 1949, the decision came down. The Independence would be scuttled. Not at Kwajalein, where her contaminated hull would rest on the lagoon floor, potentially leaching fission products into the water for decades. Not in some foreign port where local authorities might object. She would be towed to the United States, to a location off the California coast, and there she would be sunk as a target.

The announcement framed this as a test. The navy would gain valuable data on ship vulnerability. The public would see a spectacle. The Independence would serve her final purpose.

The reality was simpler. The ship had to be disposed of. Sinking her at sea removed her from the navy’s inventory without requiring admission that she was too hot to keep.

—-

The towing operation began in early 1949. A fleet tug took the Independence in tow and began the long journey across the Pacific. The carrier’s hull plowed through the waves, dead weight behind the tug. No crew walked her decks. No lights showed from her bridge. She moved like a corpse, inert and empty.

The route took her to Pearl Harbor first. There, in the naval base where the Pacific Fleet had risen from the attack that began the war, the Independence lay at anchor while technicians conducted further surveys. The readings confirmed what the Kwajalein logs had shown. The contamination remained. The ship was still hot.

From Pearl Harbor, she continued east. The tug brought her to San Francisco, to the naval shipyard at Hunters Point. There she lay for months while the navy decided what to do with her. The further tests mentioned in the press release continued. Technicians went aboard. Measurements were taken. The data accumulated.

The data did not change the outcome.

—-

On January 29, 1951, the USS Independence met her end. The navy had selected a site near the Farallon Islands, a chain of rocky outcrops off the California coast. The islands had long been a dumping ground. The Independence was finally scuttled near the Farallon Islands off the coast of California on 29 January 1951, by packing two torpedoes in her hull. The explosions tore through the carrier’s bottom. Water rushed in. The Independence settled by the stern, her flight deck tilting upward toward the sky, and then she slid beneath the surface. The Pacific closed over her.

The sinking was deliberate and efficient. Two torpedoes had been packed into her hull. At the designated time, they detonated. The explosions tore through the carrier’s bottom. Water rushed in. The Independence settled by the stern, her flight deck tilting upward toward the sky, and then she slid beneath the surface. The Pacific closed over her.

She rests there still, in water nearly three thousand feet deep, her radioactive hull a monument to the Baker test’s persistence. Controversy has subsequently arisen about the sinking of Independence, as it is claimed that she was loaded with barrels of radioactive waste at the time of her sinking.

—-

The Independence was the largest vessel to meet such an end, but she was not alone. The grisly phase of the target fleet’s disposition involved dozens of ships, each requiring its own solution, each presenting its own hazard. The navy had to clear Kwajalein. The lagoon could not serve as a permanent repository.

The submarines posed particular problems. The Japanese submarine I-19, captured at the end of the war and designated a target vessel, had ridden out the Baker test partially submerged. Her hull had absorbed contamination. Returning her to service was impossible. Studying her indefinitely was impractical. Leaving her to rot was unacceptable. The navy sank her in deep water, far from any shipping lane, far from any fishing ground.

The landing craft were easier. They were smaller, simpler, expendable. The navy had gathered them at Kwajalein, lining the beaches with their flat-bottomed hulls. Many had been contaminated by the base surge. Others had taken on radioactive water through their ramps and bilges. The navy disposed of them methodically. Some were towed to sea and scuttled. Others were broken apart in a dedicated graving dock, their metal cut into pieces small enough to transport for burial.

The graving dock at Kwajalein became a disassembly line. Workers in protective gear cut contaminated vessels into sections. The sections were loaded onto barges and taken to sea. The barges dumped their loads in deep water. The process repeated.

—-

The Japanese battleship Nagato had already met her end. She had sunk at Bikini five days after the Baker test, her hull ruptured by the underwater shock wave. She rested on the lagoon floor, her superstructure still visible above the surface during low tide. She was too radioactive to approach. She remains there still, a tomb for the Imperial Japanese Navy’s pride, her steel slowly corroding in the warm Pacific water.

Other vessels had sunk during the tests themselves or in the days following. The attack transport Fall River had gone down. The submarine Apogon had been sunk by the Baker shock wave. The landing ship LST-133 had succumbed to damage. These ships rested on the bottom of Bikini lagoon, their contamination contained by depth, their hulls accessible only to divers willing to accept the risk.

The ships at Kwajalein were different. They floated. They required attention. Ignoring them was not an option.

—-

Twelve target ships had been lightly contaminated enough to reman and sail back to the United States and Hawaii for radiological inspection. Their crews had taken them in hand, fired their boilers, and brought them home under their own power. These were the lucky ones. Their contamination was superficial. Scrubbing and time had reduced the readings to levels the navy considered acceptable. They returned to service or were scrapped in ordinary yards. Twelve target ships were so lightly contaminated that they were remanned and sailed back to the United States by their crews. They were the exception that proved the rule: for the vast majority of the fleet, the contamination was permanent.

But twelve ships out of ninety was not a success rate. It was a remnant. The other seventy-eight vessels could not be saved. They were too hot, too damaged, too expensive to decontaminate. The navy had spent two years trying. The effort had produced data, reports, recommendations. It had not produced clean ships.

—-

The decision to scuttle rather than preserve represented an admission. The navy had wanted to believe that atomic contamination could be managed like any other form of damage. The Baker test had proven otherwise. The fission products from the underwater detonation had bonded with the ships in ways that defied ordinary maintenance. The base surge had left its mark permanently. The Unwashable Mist had become a physical reality embedded in steel.

The scuttling program cleared the physical evidence. The ships went to the bottom. The lagoon at Kwajalein emptied. The maintenance detachment could stand down. The problem, visibly, had been solved.

But the solution was burial, not cleaning. The contamination had not been removed. It had been relocated. The Independence resting off the Farallon Islands still contained the same radioactive particles that had defeated the decontamination teams at Kwajalein. The submarines in the deep Pacific still emitted their invisible energy. The landing craft cut apart in the graving dock had been dispersed, not neutralized.

The navy had learned that atomic contamination could not be washed away. The navy had not yet learned what that meant.

—-

The Fleet of Intended Ruin had served its purpose. The ships had been assembled to be damaged, to demonstrate what atomic weapons could do to naval power. They had been damaged. They had demonstrated. The demonstration had shown more than the navy expected.

The Baker test had shown that an underwater atomic detonation could contaminate a fleet so thoroughly that the ships became unusable. This was a new kind of destruction. The ships had not been sunk outright. They had been poisoned. They floated, they looked intact, they could theoretically be repaired. But they could not be used. The contamination made them hazardous to their own crews.

This was the lesson that the navy had resisted. The Board of Inquiry had documented it. The scuttling program had confirmed it. The ships had to be destroyed because they could not be cleaned.

—-

The process took months. Over the first half of 1949, the Kwajalein anchorage emptied. Tugs towed vessels to sea. Scuttling parties set charges. The ships sank. The lagoon grew quieter.

The workers who performed this work accepted risks that the navy only partially acknowledged. The protective gear available in 1949 was rudimentary. The understanding of long-term radiation exposure was incomplete. The men who went aboard contaminated vessels, who cut them apart, who handled their radioactive components, did so under assumptions about safe exposure levels that later research would revise.

The navy kept records. The records showed readings, times, dates. They did not follow the men home.

—-

Nine target ships were ultimately scrapped rather than scuttled. These were the vessels whose contamination had faded enough, or whose value justified enough effort, to allow them to be broken up in shipyards. The scrapers cut them apart. The metal entered the commercial stream. Some of it may have been made into new ships, new buildings, new products. The navy did not track where the scrap went. Ultimately, only nine target ships were able to be scrapped rather than scuttled. This minuscule salvage rate stood as the final, quantitative verdict on the navy’s decontamination efforts.

The scuttled ships were easier to account for. They rested on the bottom. Their locations were charted. Their depths were recorded. They were out of sight, out of mind, out of the navy’s inventory.

—-

The Independence had been the last major vessel to leave Kwajalein. Her departure marked the end of the target fleet as a physical presence in the Pacific. The ships that had gathered at Bikini in July 1946, ninety vessels anchored in the lagoon while the world watched, were gone. Some rested at Bikini. Some rested off California. Some rested in deep water scattered across the Pacific. The fleet that had been assembled to test the atomic bomb had been consumed by the test.

The navy had gathered data. The data filled reports. The reports filled archives. The lessons were codified, distributed, incorporated into doctrine. The navy had learned that ships could survive atomic blast. The navy had learned that ships could not survive atomic contamination.

The question of what this meant for naval warfare remained open. If a single underwater detonation could render a fleet unusable, what did that mean for the future of sea power? The navy did not want to answer this question. The navy wanted to believe that ships could be protected, that countermeasures could be developed, that the problem could be solved.

The scuttled fleet suggested otherwise.

—-

The physical labor of disposal fell to men whose names appear in no headlines. The tugs that towed the contaminated vessels had crews. The scuttling parties that set the charges had hands and faces. The workers in the graving dock who cut apart the landing craft breathed air that still carried particles of the Baker test’s residue.

Kwajalein in 1949 was not a place of glory. It was a place of work. The war had ended years before. The atomic tests had become history. The target fleet had become a problem to be solved, and solving it required men willing to do the work.

The navy provided those men. They did what was asked. The ships went down.

—-

The contamination that had defeated the decontamination teams did not disappear when the ships sank. It persisted. The radioactive particles embedded in the Independence’s hull continued to emit their energy. The fission products in the scuttled submarines continued their decay. The process would continue for decades, for centuries, for time scales that exceeded any naval schedule.

The navy had acted on the assumption that the problem could be managed. The physical reality had proven otherwise. The ships could not be cleaned. They could only be hidden.

—-

The public story and the private reality diverged. The press releases spoke of tests, of targets, of valuable data. The internal documents spoke of contamination, of hazard, of disposal. The gap between these narratives represented the gap between what the navy wanted to believe and what the physics demanded.

Operation Crossroads had been presented as a triumph of organization and science. Admiral Blandy had received accolades. The Joint Task Force had been disbanded with honors. The tests had demonstrated American power and technological prowess.

The scuttling of the target fleet demonstrated something else. It demonstrated that the power had costs that could not be managed. The atomic bomb had created a new kind of problem. The navy had no solution for it.

—-

The ships at Kwajalein had been assembled as targets. They were meant to be damaged. They were meant to be studied. They were meant to demonstrate what atomic weapons could do.

All of that had been accomplished. But the demonstration had also shown something the navy had not intended. The atomic age created hazards that could not be washed away, could not be repaired, could not be ignored. The contamination was permanent. The ships could only be buried.

—-

The lagoon at Kwajalein emptied. The anchorage that had held the survivors of the Baker test returned to its ordinary rhythms. The maintenance detachment packed its equipment and departed. The logs were archived. The readings were filed.

The ships were gone. The problem they represented had been moved, not solved.

—-

The Independence resting off the Farallon Islands contains the same contamination that defeated the decontamination teams. The submarines in the deep Pacific carry their radioactive burden in eternal silence. The landing craft cut apart in the graving dock were dispersed into waters whose depths exceed any diver’s reach.

The navy had learned that atomic contamination could not be managed. The navy had not yet learned what that meant for the future. The lesson was clear, but the navy was not ready to accept it.

The physical stage of the primary evidence, the contaminated fleet, was now cleared, leaving only the human and policy legacies to be resolved.
