Chapter 23

The Last Report from Rongerik

Seen from above, the Marshall Islands scattered across the equatorial Pacific like a constellation, their coral atolls arranged in two parallel chains. On a map flattened across a desk, they appeared as green dots ringed by white breakers, connected by a dotted supply route. One speck, barely visible at this scale, was distinguished only by a marginal notation.

Three years had passed since that notation was first inscribed. The file on Rongerik had accumulated additional notations in the interval: supply requests, inspection reports, the occasional medical bulletin. But the file had not been a priority. The file had been, in the language of naval administration, a closed matter. The Trust Territory officer who now examined the map could see the problem in its starkest cartographic form. Rongerik was smaller than Bikini. Its lagoon was shallower. Its land area was insufficient to support the coconut groves that had sustained the Bikinian community for generations. The map showed the facts. What the map could not show was what those facts had produced: the 167 Bikinians moved there prior to Operation Crossroads were unable to gather sufficient food or catch enough fish and shellfish to feed themselves.

In April 1949, John F. Rubeli stepped from a naval landing craft onto the beach at Rongerik and encountered the silence that had descended over the atoll. The children who emerged from the palm-frond shelters moved with a lethargy that contrasted sharply with the energetic greetings Rubeli had encountered on other atolls. Near the water’s edge stood a girl of perhaps seven years, her abdomen swollen in the pattern that medical officers recognized immediately. The distension was not the sign of a well-fed child; it was the signature of protein deficiency, the body’s desperate attempt to compensate for nutrients it could not obtain.

Behind the village, the coconut groves stretched across the largest islet, but the ground beneath the trees was bare. Fallen nuts should have littered the earth at this season. Instead, the Bikinians had gathered every mature coconut and found the younger trees producing at a rate far below what the community required. They had tried to supplement their diet with fish. The fish had made them ill. The reef fish of Rongerik, they explained to Rubeli through an interpreter, were poisonous. When people ate them, their extremities went numb. Their arms and legs “fell asleep,” as they described it, and the weakness would persist for days. The Bikinians had learned to avoid the fish that caused the worst symptoms, but this meant avoiding most of the fish they could catch. The atoll’s lagoon was too shallow to support the deeper-water species that had populated Bikini’s fishing grounds.

Rubeli had come to Rongerik to conduct a routine assessment. What he found was not routine. The community was in the advanced stages of deprivation. The children showed the most visible signs, but the adults were malnourished as well. They had been on Rongerik for three years. In that time, they had consumed the mature coconuts and watched the younger trees fail to mature at the rate the community required. They had attempted to fish and had been thwarted by the toxic properties of the reef species. They had waited for supply ships that arrived sporadically and with insufficient cargo. They had, in short, been placed on an atoll that could not support human life and then left to survive as best they could.

Rubeli’s report traveled from Rongerik to Majuro and then to the Trust Territory headquarters in Honolulu. The language was clinical, but the facts were clear. The Bikinians were starving. The atoll’s resources were inadequate. The situation had been inadequate from the beginning, and three years of neglect had transformed a hardship into a crisis.

The response from the naval administration was not immediate action. The navy had transferred administrative responsibility for the Marshall Islands to the Trust Territory in July 1947, but the legacy of the Bikinian relocation remained caught between jurisdictions. The removal had been a naval operation, conducted under the authority of Admiral William H.P. Blandy’s Joint Task Force One. The promises made to the Bikinians had been naval promises. But the navy’s attention had moved on. The contaminated ships from Operation Crossroads were still being dealt with at Kwajalein. The lessons of the atomic tests were still being debated in Washington. The Bikinians on Rongerik were a matter for the Trust Territory now. The file had been transferred. The responsibility, in the navy’s view, had been transferred with it.

In June 1949, a medical officer from the Trust Territory arrived at Rongerik to conduct a formal health assessment. His examination confirmed what Rubeli had observed and added the precise terminology of clinical diagnosis. The community was suffering from protein-calorie malnutrition. The children exhibited the distended abdomens and thin limbs characteristic of the condition. The adults showed signs of chronic undernutrition. The food supplies that had been delivered over the previous three years had been insufficient from the beginning and had grown more irregular as time passed. The atoll’s own resources had proved inadequate from the moment of arrival. The navy had left provisions for a few weeks in March 1946 and had then failed to return with anything approaching the regularity or quantity required.

The medical officer’s report followed Rubeli’s through the administrative channels. It landed on desks in Guam and Honolulu and generated memoranda requesting authorization for emergency supplies. The Trust Territory did not have unlimited resources. Supplying an atoll that could not support itself required ships and fuel and personnel, all of which cost money that had not been allocated for this purpose. The Bikinians had been moved for the atomic tests. The atomic tests were over. Operation Crossroads had been declared a success. The file on Rongerik was supposed to reflect a resolved situation.

But the reports kept arriving.

In July 1949, an anthropologist named Leonard Mason reached Rongerik as part of a broader survey of Trust Territory conditions. Mason brought training in Pacific cultures and experience with atoll environments. He walked the islets with the Bikinians and listened to their accounts of the preceding three years. He examined their food caches and found them empty. He watched them fish and observed what they caught. He recorded their testimony in their own words.

The fish of Rongerik were not fit to eat, the Bikinians explained. They were poisonous because of what they consumed on the reef. After eating them, people’s arms and legs would fall asleep, a numbness spreading through their extremities. They could not move properly. They suffered weakness and dizziness. They had learned which species caused the worst symptoms, but avoiding those species meant avoiding most of the available catch.

Mason’s report carried the weight of professional observation. He did not minimize what he saw. He described a community placed on an atoll incapable of sustaining it and then abandoned to survive as best it could. The coconut trees were dying faster than they were being replaced. The fishing grounds were contaminated by toxic reef species or simply inadequate to the community’s needs. The people were hungry. The children were malnourished. The situation was not improving with time; it was deteriorating.

The reports from Rubeli, the medical officer, and Mason converged on the desk of the Trust Territory’s high official. They described a humanitarian crisis that had been three years in the making. The navy had removed the Bikinians from their home with assurances that they would be cared for. The navy had then proceeded with the atomic tests and the subsequent decontamination efforts and the disposal of the target fleet, and somewhere in that sequence of operations, the Bikinians had been forgotten. Not entirely forgotten, perhaps, but forgotten enough. Remembered when convenient, ignored when not.

The Trust Territory administration faced a decision. It could continue to send emergency supplies to Rongerik, treating the situation as a temporary problem that would somehow resolve itself. Or it could acknowledge that Rongerik was uninhabitable and that the Bikinians needed to be relocated again. Neither option was attractive. Emergency supplies were expensive and did not address the underlying problem. A second relocation would be even more expensive and would constitute an admission that the first relocation had been a failure.

But the evidence was accumulating. Mason’s report in particular made it clear that Rongerik could never support the Bikinian community. The atoll was too small, its resources too limited, its ecosystem too fragile. The navy had known this in 1946, or should have known it. The Bikinians had certainly known it. They had protested the move from the beginning. They had been assured that Rongerik would be adequate. They had believed the assurances because they had no choice.

Now, three years later, the consequences of that forced belief were visible in the distended bellies of children and the hollow cheeks of adults who had once been healthy.

The administrative machinery began to turn. A request went to Washington for authorization to relocate the Bikinians again. The request had to pass through naval channels, because the navy still held certain responsibilities for the Marshalls. The navy’s response was not enthusiastic. The Bikinians had already been moved once. Moving them again would cost money and would draw attention to the fact that the first move had been inadequate. It would raise questions about naval competence in matters of island administration. It would create a precedent for future complaints.

The correspondence between the Trust Territory and the naval administration revealed the conflict. On one side were the reports from the field, documenting starvation and desperation. On the other side were the bureaucratic imperatives of an institution that had already declared success and moved on. The gap between these two realities was the gap between a child with a distended belly and a file folder in a cabinet in Washington.

In August 1949, the situation reached a breaking point. The Trust Territory administration informed the navy that the Bikinians could not remain on Rongerik through another season. The food supplies were exhausted. The coconut groves were failing. The fishing remained inadequate. The people were starving. If the navy would not authorize a second relocation, the Trust Territory would have to act on its own authority, and the consequences would fall on the navy’s reputation.

The navy’s position had been that the Bikinians were adequately cared for. Supplies had been left in 1946. Arrangements had been made for periodic deliveries. Obligations had been fulfilled. That the Trust Territory had failed to maintain those arrangements was not a naval problem. The file had been closed.

But the reports from Rongerik made it impossible to maintain that position. The evidence was too clear, too specific, too damning. A medical officer had documented the malnutrition. An anthropologist had recorded the testimony. A Trust Territory administrator had witnessed the conditions firsthand. The facts were on paper, signed and dated, and they could not be explained away by bureaucratic correspondence.

The decision came in early September 1949. The Bikinians would be moved from Rongerik. The navy would provide transport. The Trust Territory would identify a suitable location. The relocation would be conducted properly this time, with adequate preparation and ongoing support.

The question of where to move them remained unanswered. The Marshall Islands were not overburdened with uninhabited atolls that could support a community of 167 people. Bikini was radioactive. Rongerik had proved inadequate. Other atolls were already occupied. The Trust Territory began a survey of potential sites, knowing that the search itself would take time and that the Bikinians did not have time.

Emergency supplies were dispatched to Rongerik in the interim. Rice and flour and canned goods, enough to keep the community alive while the administration searched for a permanent solution. The supplies arrived on a naval vessel, the same navy that had moved the Bikinians three years earlier and then forgotten them. The sailors who unloaded the crates did not know the history. They knew only that they were delivering food to hungry people on a remote atoll.

The Bikinians received the supplies with the quiet dignity of people who had learned not to expect much from promises. They had been told that Rongerik would be like Bikini. They had been told that the navy would care for them. They had been told that their sacrifice would be remembered and rewarded. Three years had taught them the value of such assurances.

Leonard Mason returned to Rongerik in the fall of 1949 to monitor conditions while the relocation plans were being developed. He found the community in a state of anxious waiting. They knew that another move was coming. They did not know where they would be sent. They did not know whether the new location would be any better than Rongerik. They did not know whether they would ever see Bikini again.

Mason recorded their questions and their fears. They asked about the atomic tests, whether the bombs had destroyed their home. They asked about the ships that had been anchored in their lagoon, whether the navy was finished with them. They asked whether they would be allowed to return, whether the radiation would fade, whether the coconuts would ever be safe to eat again.

Mason had no answers for them. The navy had not provided answers. The Trust Territory had not provided answers. The scientists who had studied the contamination at Bikini had produced reports that remained classified, their conclusions unknown to the people whose home had been contaminated.

What Mason could observe was the resilience of a community that had survived three years of neglect. The Bikinians had continued to fish, even when the fish made them sick. They had continued to gather coconuts, even when the trees produced less each year. They had continued to raise their children, even when those children showed the signs of malnutrition. They had not given up. They had not dispersed. They had remained a community, waiting for the promises to be fulfilled.

The reports from Rongerik accumulated in the administrative record. Each one added to the picture of a disaster that could have been prevented. The atoll’s inadequacy had been predictable. The navy’s own surveys had noted that Rongerik was smaller than Bikini, with fewer resources. The decision to place the Bikinians there had been made for operational convenience, not for their welfare. The atomic tests required Bikini Atoll. The Bikinians were in the way. They had been moved to the nearest available location, regardless of whether that location could support them.

The consequences of that decision were now documented in the files of the Trust Territory. Starvation. Malnutrition. Poisoning from toxic fish. Desperation. A community reduced to waiting for emergency supplies while the administrative machinery slowly, reluctantly, acknowledged that a mistake had been made.

The mistake went beyond the choice of Rongerik. It lay in the assumption that the Bikinians could be moved and then forgotten. The navy had treated the relocation as a logistical problem, a box to be checked before the real work of the atomic tests could begin. Once the Bikinians were off Bikini Atoll, they ceased to be a priority. They became someone else’s problem. The file was closed.

But the file could not stay closed. The Bikinians refused to disappear. They survived, and their survival forced the administration to see them, to document their condition, to acknowledge that the promises had been broken.

In November 1949, the Trust Territory identified a potential site for the second relocation. Kili Island, in the southern Marshalls, was uninhabited and had a reasonable supply of coconuts. It was smaller than Bikini, and it lacked a protected lagoon, but it was better than Rongerik. The survey team reported that Kili could support the community, at least for the immediate future.

The Bikinians were informed that they would be moved again. They asked their questions. Would Kili be like Bikini? Would they be cared for this time? Would they ever be allowed to return home?

The administrators had no answers. They could only promise that the move would be conducted properly, with adequate supplies and ongoing support. They could not promise that Kili would be permanent. They could not promise that Bikini would ever be safe. They could not promise that the atomic tests that had displaced these people would prove to have been worth the cost.

The relocation to Kili was scheduled for late 1949. The navy provided a landing ship, the same type of vessel that had carried the Bikinians from Bikini three and a half years earlier. The sailors loaded the community’s possessions: the cooking pots and fishing gear and woven mats, the few things they had been able to bring from their original home and had preserved through the long hunger of Rongerik.

Juda, the Bikinian leader who had negotiated with Commoditor Wyatt in 1946, watched the preparations. He had been the one who had asked whether the atomic tests would bring peace to the world. He had been the one who had agreed to leave Bikini for the good of all mankind. He had been the one who had led his people to Rongerik and then watched them starve.

Now he would lead them again, to another island that was not their home, to another promise that might or might not be kept.

The landing ship sailed from Rongerik in November 1949. The atoll receded behind the vessel, its sparse coconut groves and shallow lagoon disappearing into the horizon. The Bikinians had spent three years on Rongerik, three years of hunger and waiting and neglect. They had survived, but they had not thrived. They had been preserved, barely, by emergency supplies and their own determination.

The reports from Rongerik had accomplished one thing. They had forced the administration to act. The file was no longer closed. The Bikinians were no longer forgotten. They were a problem that required a solution, and the solution was to move them again and hope that this time the promises would hold.

The navy had learned how to move ships and men and materiel for atomic tests. The navy had learned how to decontaminate vessels, or how to fail at decontamination and dispose of the evidence. The navy had learned about radioactive persistence and the limits of military procedure. But the navy had not learned how to care for the people whose lives had been disrupted by its operations. The logistical success of the initial removal had been mistaken for a permanent solution. Human welfare could not be filed away and forgotten.

The Bikinians on the landing ship carried with them the memory of Bikini and the experience of Rongerik. They carried the knowledge that they had been displaced for atomic tests that had been declared a success while they starved. They carried the understanding that their sacrifice had been recorded in reports that traveled through administrative channels while they waited for food that might or might not arrive.

The ship sailed south. The reports remained in the files. The lesson was there, in the documents and the testimony and the clinical descriptions of malnutrition. Whether anyone would learn from it remained to be seen.

The administrative machinery had finally, reluctantly, been forced to act, setting the stage for a final reckoning with the institutional lessons of the entire operation.
