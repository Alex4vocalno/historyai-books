Chapter 8

The Saratoga and the Sacrifice Fleet

The orange paint still covered the Nevada’s scorched superstructure when the salvage crews began their work. The first atomic test at Bikini Atoll had missed its aim point by nearly half a mile, sinking five ships but leaving the primary target afloat, damaged but defiant. For the Navy, the battleship’s survival was both a reprieve and a reproach. The second test, the underwater shot code-named Baker, would have to deliver what Able had failed to provide: a spectacular, undeniable demonstration of atomic power against the fleet.

On the morning of July second, 1946, salvage teams swarmed over the target ships anchored in Bikini lagoon. Their task was not restorative but preparatory: the ships were not being saved but readied for a second death. The aircraft carrier USS Saratoga, pride of the American flattop fleet, had sustained damage from Able’s blast. The shock wave had buckled her flight deck and ruptured fuel lines; smoke had poured from her hangar bay for hours after the detonation. Now repair crews from the support fleet boarded her, welding torches flaring against the tropical sky. They patched the flight deck, sealed the ruptured tanks, and made her seaworthy enough to be moved.

The Saratoga was not being repaired for service. She was being repaired for sacrifice.

The carrier’s repositioning for Baker was a calculated decision by Joint Task Force One, commanded by Vice Admiral William H.P. Blandy. The Saratoga was to be anchored less than a mile from the underwater detonation point—close enough to be swamped by the blast wave, close enough to guarantee her sinking. The Navy needed a dramatic image for the newspapers and newsreels: a great aircraft carrier broken by the atomic blast, settling into the lagoon. Able had failed to provide that image. Baker would not fail.

The Japanese battleship Nagato rode at anchor a few hundred yards from the Saratoga. She was the only surviving Japanese battleship of the Pacific War, the vessel from which Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto had commanded the attack on Pearl Harbor. Brought to Bikini as a prize of war and positioned near the Able aim point to guarantee her sinking, she had survived with only light damage, her stern-on orientation offering some protection from the blast. After the test, a skeleton crew of American sailors boarded her and found her seaworthy enough for the next trial. For Baker, she was repositioned even closer to the planned detonation point.

The Nagato’s presence at Bikini was a reminder of the conflict these tests were meant to transcend. The war was over; the enemy fleet had been surrendered, scrapped, and now sacrificed to the new weapon that had ended it. But her survival after Able was an inconvenience—she was supposed to have sunk—and a reminder that the atomic bomb was not infallible, that ships were not so easily destroyed. For Baker she would be placed within the kill radius. There would be no ambiguity this time.

Repositioning consumed the first two weeks of July. Tugs nosed damaged ships into their new positions, guided by surveyors who had planted marker buoys across the lagoon; beyond them stood off at safe distance—the support fleet that would carry observers, scientists, and press—waiting while their charges became vessels whose only purpose was to absorb blast and yield data on destruction.

The repositioning of test animals was quieter and grimmer work than any salvage detail could claim credit for being part of: pigs and goats tethered on decks during Able had been subjected to blast; many survived wounded or terrified; now new animals came aboard for Baker because scientists needed data on underwater detonation effects upon living creatures—cages placed on decks with positions marked in plans so they might meet shock wave radiation alike as necessary components of experiment rather than creatures at all.

But beneath the operational rush, a conflict was escalating. The radiological safety officers under Colonel Stafford L. Warren were raising alarms that the operational command was choosing to ignore.

Warren was the chief of the Radiological Safety Section, known as RADSAFE, for Operation Crossroads. A veteran of the Manhattan Project, he had been the medical director at the laboratory where the atomic bomb was developed. He had seen the effects of radiation on the human body. He knew what radiation could do.

In the days after Able, Warren’s teams had monitored the target fleet for residual radioactivity. The airburst had produced minimal fallout. The radioactive cloud had dissipated into the upper atmosphere, carried away by the wind. The ships themselves were largely clean. The radiation readings were low, within safe limits. The crews could board the ships, conduct their salvage operations, and work without protective gear. The radiological safety officers had done their job. The test had been a success from their perspective.

But Warren was not reassured. He knew that Able was an airburst, detonated high above the lagoon. The Baker shot would be different. The bomb would be suspended ninety feet below the LSM-60, inside a watertight steel caisson attached to the ship’s hull. When it detonated, it would vaporize millions of gallons of seawater, blasting them into the air in a towering column. The water would come crashing back down, creating a base surge, a wall of radioactive mist that would roll across the lagoon and coat everything in its path.

The base surge would be the problem. Warren and his officers began to issue urgent warnings about the unprecedented contamination an underwater blast would produce. The Baker shot would not merely sink ships. It would pump fission products into the lagoon water. The radioactive mist would settle on the decks of the target ships, on the instruments, on the animals. It would soak into the paint, the wood, the steel. It would contaminate the entire target fleet in a way that no hose could wash clean.

Warren’s predictions were based on the physics of an underwater detonation. He knew that the water would trap the fission products, holding them in suspension, creating a persistent, pervasive contamination. He warned that the target ships would be so radioactive after Baker that they would be uninhabitable, unapproachable for extended periods. He warned that the support ships, the ones carrying the observers, would have to stay well clear of the lagoon until the radiation levels had fallen.

The warnings constituted a direct challenge to the Navy’s operational timeline. The plan for Baker called for the target ships to be boarded and inspected within hours of the detonation. The damage assessment teams needed to document the effects of the blast. The salvage crews needed to recover instruments and equipment. The press needed to be taken on tours of the fleet. The schedule assumed that the ships would be accessible, that the radiation would be manageable.

Warren was telling them that the schedule was impossible.

The conflict between the radiological safety officers and the operational command did not erupt into open confrontation. There were no shouting matches, no memos stamped with angry red ink. The conflict was quieter, more insidious. It played out in meetings, in briefings, in the margins of the operational plans. Warren’s officers presented their data, their projections, their warnings. The operational commanders listened, nodded, and proceeded with the schedule.

The Navy’s institutional momentum was too strong. The operation had to succeed. The second test had to go forward. The world was watching.

The psychological pressure of Able’s miss was driving the commitment to Baker. The first test had been perceived as a fizzle, a failure to deliver the dramatic ship-sinking imagery that had been promised. The bomb had missed the Nevada. Five ships had sunk, but they were smaller vessels, auxiliaries and transports. The great capital ships, the ones that mattered, had survived. The press had been underwhelmed. The congressmen had been skeptical. The Navy had been relieved, but also embarrassed. The atomic bomb was supposed to be the ultimate weapon. Able had failed to prove it.

Baker had to succeed. The underwater shot would produce the dramatic images that Able had failed to provide. The column of water, the base surge, the ships crushed by the shock wave, the great carriers and battleships settling into the lagoon. The Navy needed those images. The Joint Task Force needed them. The Truman administration needed them. The operation had to proceed.

The three-week interlude was not a period of reconsideration. It was a period of acceleration. The psychological aftermath of Able’s miss did not prompt caution. It exacerbated the commitment to the far more dangerous underwater test. The need for a definitive, visually compelling result overrode the warnings of the radiological safety officers. The fleet was locked into its new positions. The schedule was locked in. The course was irreversible.

The Saratoga, the Nagato, and the other target ships rode at anchor in the lagoon, their decks cleared, their instruments reset, their sacrifice positions marked on the charts. The test animals were in their cages. The cameras were loaded. The observers were in their seats on the support ships. The countdown had begun.

Warren’s officers continued to document their warnings. They wrote memos, they briefed the commanders, they recorded their projections in the official reports. They did what they could. But the operation was too big, too public, too important to stop. The Navy had committed to the test. The President had committed. The world was waiting.

The radiological safety apparatus for Operation Crossroads fell under the Manhattan Project, not the Navy. Warren and his officers were outsiders, scientists in a military operation. They wore uniforms, but they were not Navy men. Their authority was limited. They could advise, they could warn, but they could not command.

The Navy’s operational rush was driven by institutional imperatives. The service had survived the war, but its future was uncertain. The atomic bomb had changed the calculus of warfare. The Army Air Forces were claiming that the Navy was obsolete, that strategic bombing with nuclear weapons would decide future conflicts. The Navy needed to prove that ships could survive an atomic attack, that the fleet still mattered. Able had been inconclusive. Baker had to provide the answer.

The target fleet at Bikini was a cross-section of naval power: American battleships, carriers, cruisers, destroyers, and submarines, alongside surrendered German and Japanese vessels. They were arrayed in the lagoon like pieces on a chessboard, each positioned to test a different aspect of the blast. The Saratoga was the queen, the prize piece, the one whose sinking would make the strongest impression. The Nagato was the captured king, the enemy flagship, the symbol of the war that had ended.

The repositioning of the fleet for Baker was a work of precision. The surveyors had mapped the lagoon, planting buoys at exact distances from the detonation point. The tugs had moved the ships into their designated positions, anchoring them with heavy chains. The ships were now floating targets, stationary and helpless. They would not maneuver. They would not fight back. They would simply wait.

The animals waited in their cages. The pigs and goats stood on the decks, their eyes watching the sky. They did not know what was coming. They could not understand the purpose of their presence. They were subjects in an experiment, data points in a study of the effects of an atomic blast on living tissue.

The support fleet waited at a distance. The observation ships, carrying the press, the congressmen, and the foreign observers, were positioned outside the lagoon, ready to approach after the detonation. The scientists and the damage assessment teams were on standby, ready to board the target ships as soon as the radiation levels permitted.

Warren’s officers had told them that the radiation levels would not permit rapid boarding. The base surge would contaminate the ships. The water in the lagoon would be radioactive. The mist would coat everything. The ships would be hot, too hot to touch, too hot to breathe.

The operational command had heard the warnings. They had incorporated them into their planning, but they had not changed their schedule. The boarding teams would wear protective clothing. They would carry dosimeters to measure their exposure. They would limit their time on the target ships. The schedule would be followed.

The three-week interlude was drawing to a close. The final preparations were being made. The bomb was being lowered into its caisson. The detonation was set for the twenty-fifth of July.

The Saratoga rode at anchor, her flight deck patched, her fuel tanks sealed. She was ready for her final role. The Nagato waited nearby, her hull scarred from Able, her boilers still functional. The fleet was in position. The countdown had begun.

Warren’s memos were filed. His warnings were recorded. The radiological safety officers had done their duty. They had spoken the truth as they understood it. The truth had been heard, and it had been set aside.

The lagoon was calm. The trade winds blew steadily from the east. The sun rose and set over the atoll, casting long shadows across the water. The Bikinians, displaced from their home, waited on Rongerik, their fate in the hands of the Americans. The sailors and the scientists waited on the support ships, their eyes on the target fleet. The world waited for the second atomic test.

The underwater shot would create a new phenomenon, a base surge of radioactive mist that would coat the fleet. The mist would be an unwashable contamination, a poison that defied existing procedures for decontamination. The ships would be infused with fission products, their decks coated with radioactive spray. The hoses would be rigged, the scrubbing parties would be sent down, and the readings would not fall.

But that was still in the future. In the interlude, the work proceeded. The ships were repositioned. The animals were placed. The instruments were reset. The schedule was followed.

The psychological pressure of Able’s miss had accelerated the commitment to Baker. The need for a demonstrable result had overridden the warnings of the radiological safety officers. The fleet was locked into its course. The irreversible escalation was underway.

The Saratoga, the Nagato, and the other target ships waited in the lagoon. They were a fleet of intended ruin, a collection of vessels assembled for destruction. They had been stripped of their crews, their weapons, their dignity. They were floating laboratories, instruments of the atomic age.

The countdown continued. The days passed. The detonation approached.

On July twentieth, the final positioning orders were issued. The target ships were checked and rechecked. The instruments were tested. The cameras were prepared. The observation ships moved into their designated positions.

Warren’s officers conducted their final briefings. They explained the risks, the procedures, the protective measures. They distributed dosimeters and film badges. They issued warnings about the base surge, the radioactive mist, the contaminated water. They did what they could.

The operational commanders issued their final orders. The boarding teams were briefed. The salvage crews were on standby. The press was given their schedules. The congressmen were shown to their observation posts.

The machine was in motion. The test would proceed.

The interlude was ending. The three weeks of frantic preparation, of conflicted activity, of warnings issued and ignored, were drawing to a close. The fleet was in position. The bomb was ready. The world was watching.

The Saratoga and the Nagato rode at anchor, their hulls reflecting the tropical sun. They were the icons of the target fleet, the symbols of the sacrifice to come. The Saratoga, the great American carrier that had fought across the Pacific. The Nagato, the Japanese battleship that had launched the attack on Pearl Harbor. Both were now prisoners of the atomic age, waiting for the blast that would send them to the bottom.

The animals waited in their cages. The instruments waited in their housings. The cameras waited on their mounts. The observers waited on their ships.

The lagoon was still. The atoll was quiet. The countdown continued.

The fleet, now repositioned and locked in place for Baker, represented a concrete consequence of overridden caution, handing off the imminent, physical cataclysm of the underwater detonation.
