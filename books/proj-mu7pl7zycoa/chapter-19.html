Chapter 19

The First Interrogation of George Blake

To understand the true failure of the Berlin Tunnel operation, one must look past its dramatic physical discovery. The most devastating moment for Anglo-American intelligence occurred not when Soviet soldiers staged their breach, but years later in a private London room. That public exposure had been a managed spectacle; the subsequent bureaucratic reckoning proved far more corrosive to the institutions behind the venture. For what was revealed initially was merely a structure, while what began to be revealed later was the timeline of foreknowledge.

The questions that had lingered since the tunnel’s discovery—unanswered and unresolved—had assumed a particular form. Western intelligence officers had wondered whether the Soviets had genuinely stumbled upon the operation or had allowed it to run for reasons of their own. Debate had continued in restricted channels about whether the intelligence yield was genuine or contaminated. A united public front had been maintained while private doubts harbored in silence. Those doubts now found their confirmation.

Blake had been arrested in April 1961, shortly after arriving in London. He had been summoned from Lebanon, where he had been enrolled at the Middle East Center for Arab Studies in Beirut. The summons came through normal channels, giving no indication of the trap that awaited him. The Polish defector Michael Goleniewski had provided information that led to Blake’s identification. The net had been tightening for months. When Blake stepped off the plane, officers were waiting.

The arrest itself was handled quietly. Blake was a serving officer of the Secret Intelligence Service, and his exposure as a Soviet agent represented not merely a security breach but an institutional catastrophe. The service had weathered the Burgess and Maclean defections in 1951. Blake’s case would prove worse in some respects, for he had remained active, embedded, trusted, for a decade after his recruitment. The initial interrogation sought to establish the scope of his betrayal. The interrogators did not yet know how deep they would have to dig.

The location of the interrogation remains a matter of inference. Safe houses in London were standard practice for sensitive debriefings. The service needed controlled environments where officers could question their own without risk of surveillance or leakage. The rooms were anonymous, furnished for function rather than comfort, equipped for recording and observation. In one such space, Blake began to tell his story.

His account of recruitment was straightforward. He had been captured during the Korean War while serving in Seoul. The North Koreans had taken him prisoner in 1950. During his captivity, he made contact with Soviet intelligence officers who recognized his potential value. By the time of his release in 1953, he had committed himself to their cause. He returned to Britain as a hero, his cover intact, his loyalties inverted.

Following his release in 1953, Blake returned to Britain as a hero, landing at RAF Abingdon. In October 1954, he married MI6 secretary Gillian Allan in St Mark’s Church on North Audley Street in London. The following year, he was sent by MI6 to work as a case officer in Berlin. His tasks there included recruiting agents behind the Iron Curtain. The posting placed him at the heart of one of the Cold War’s most active intelligence battlegrounds.

The interrogators pressed for specifics. Names, dates, operations. Blake provided what he could. He had handed over what came his way, which was substantial. His position in the service gave him access to operational files, agent networks, technical programs. Each admission was recorded, catalogued, cross-referenced against known losses and suspected compromises.

Then came the question of the Berlin Tunnel.

The operation had been known by several designations. The Americans called it Operation Gold. The British referred to it as Operation Stopwatch. It had run from 1955 to 1956, a joint CIA-SIS venture to tap Soviet military communications cables running beneath Berlin. The tunnel extended from the American sector at Rudow under the border to Altglienicke, where three trunk cables carried conversations between Soviet headquarters in Karlshorst and their forces in the field. For nearly eleven months, Western intelligence had recorded those conversations—roughly 90, 000 communications in total, according to American estimates.

Blake confirmed what the interrogators had feared. He had betrayed the tunnel before construction began.

The admission was precise and devastating. In 1953, shortly after his return from Korea and his recruitment by Soviet intelligence, Blake had met with his handler to discuss his new posting. He had been assigned to Berlin, a position that would give him access to the station’s operations. The tunnel plan was in its early stages, still being developed by the technical teams. Blake had seen the files. He had passed the information to Sergei Kondrashev, his Soviet handler.

The files Blake had handed over included architectural plans and operational timetables. The Soviets had known the tunnel’s route, its entry point, its target cables, and its construction schedule before a single shovel had broken ground in Rudow. They had monitored the operation from its inception, fully aware of what the Americans and British were attempting.

This admission transformed the interrogators’ understanding of the tunnel’s entire history. The Soviet discovery in April 1956 had not been accidental. A patrol had not stumbled upon the structure during a routine sweep. The elaborate public exposure—the press conference, the propaganda materials, the display of British equipment—had been theater, staged for maximum embarrassment at a time and place of Moscow’s choosing.

Why had the Soviets waited so long? Why had they allowed the tunnel to operate for nearly a year if they had known about it from the start?

Blake’s answers pointed toward a calculation. The tunnel was a valuable source of intelligence about Western methods and priorities. By monitoring what their adversaries sought to learn, Soviet counterintelligence could infer what the West did not know. The operation revealed technical capabilities, analytical assumptions, and intelligence gaps. Moreover, the tunnel’s existence protected Blake himself. Had the Soviets exposed the tunnel immediately, the investigation would have searched for the source of the leak. By waiting, by allowing the operation to run its course, they preserved their agent for future use.

The calculated silence had served its purpose. Blake had continued his work for Soviet intelligence for five years after the tunnel’s exposure, his position secure, his access undiminished. The service had no idea that one of its own had been responsible for the compromise.

The interrogators now faced a painful task. The tunnel had produced roughly 90, 000 recorded conversations. Analysts had spent years processing this material, extracting intelligence assessments, building operational pictures from the intercepts. The product had been considered valuable enough to justify the enormous expense and risk of the operation. Now they had to ask: how much of that product had been poisoned?

The question admitted no easy answer. Blake’s confession established that the Soviets had known about the tunnel from its inception. It did not establish what they had done with that knowledge. Had false information been fed through the tapped lines? Had genuine traffic been allowed to flow while sensitive material was concealed through other channels? Had the opportunity been used to shape Western perceptions of Soviet intentions and capabilities?

The interrogators pressed Blake on these points. He had not been in a position to know what decisions Soviet intelligence had made about the tunnel’s operation. He had provided the plans. What happened afterward was beyond his knowledge. The service would have to conduct its own audit, comparing the tunnel’s intelligence yield against other sources, searching for anomalies, inconsistencies, and possible deceptions.

This was the moment when the true cost of Blake’s betrayal began to emerge. The tunnel had been a technical triumph, an engineering feat that demonstrated Western capabilities in signals intelligence. But its intelligence value now lay under a cloud of suspicion that might never be fully resolved. The Soviets had controlled the timeline. Complete knowledge of the operation’s parameters had been in their possession before construction began. The timing of exposure had been their choice. Their agent had been preserved for five additional years of service. The West had been operating in the dark, believing itself the hunter when it was, in fact, the observed.

The interrogators documented Blake’s admissions in meticulous detail. Each piece of information was recorded, dated, and compared against known events. The timeline they constructed showed a pattern of Soviet patience and strategic calculation that contrasted sharply with Western assumptions about their adversary’s capabilities.

The tunnel’s planning phase had begun in late 1953, after Blake’s recruitment. William King Harvey, the CIA officer who would direct the operation, had been assigned in December of that year. Construction began in September 1954 and was completed in early 1955. The intercept operations ran from May 1955 until April 1956. Throughout this entire period, Soviet intelligence had possessed complete knowledge of the operation’s design and schedule.

Blake had been posted to Berlin in 1955, after the tunnel was already under construction. His role there involved recruiting and running agents behind the Iron Curtain. His access to tunnel operations was limited but not nonexistent. He could observe, report, and confirm what his handlers already knew. More importantly, his presence in Berlin placed him in a position to learn about Western intelligence’s assessment of the tunnel’s product—what they valued, what they dismissed, what conclusions they drew from the intercepts.

The interrogators asked about specific operations Blake had compromised. He provided names, dates, and outcomes. Agents had been arrested, networks rolled up, operations foiled. Each admission was a blow, but these were known losses, already attributed to some source of compromise. The tunnel was different. The tunnel had been considered a success, a rare bright spot in the often troubled record of Western intelligence operations in Berlin. Now that success was revealed as an illusion, or at best, a partial truth whose full dimensions might never be known.

The interrogation continued over several sessions. Blake cooperated to the extent that cooperation served his interests. He had been caught. His career was over. His life as he had known it was finished. What remained was the trial, the sentencing, and whatever years he could salvage in whatever facility would hold him. The interrogators had leverage, and they used it.

They returned repeatedly to the tunnel, seeking more detail, more precision. When exactly had he passed the plans? To whom exactly had he given them? What had his handler said about the operation? What instructions had he received about protecting his own involvement?

Blake’s answers were consistent, though some details remained fuzzy after the passage of years. He had met with Kondrashev in 1953, before his posting to Berlin. The handler had expressed particular interest in any technical operations the service was planning. Blake had reviewed his files and identified the tunnel as the most significant operation in development. He had copied or memorized the relevant details and passed them at his next meeting.

The architectural plans had shown the tunnel’s route, its depth, its entry and exit points. The operational timetable had shown when construction would begin, how long it would take, and when intercept operations would commence. The Soviets had everything they needed to monitor the operation from its earliest stages.

Whether Blake had known what the Soviets intended to do with the information remained unclear. He said he had not asked. His role was to provide intelligence, not to question how it was used. He assumed they would take appropriate countermeasures. The decision to allow the tunnel to operate, to delay its exposure, had not been communicated to him at the time. He had learned of the discovery the same way everyone else had—through the news reports in April 1956.

This claim could not be verified. Blake might have been told more than he admitted, or he might genuinely have been kept ignorant of Soviet operational decisions. The interrogators noted the uncertainty and moved on.

The broader picture that emerged from Blake’s interrogation was one of systematic, long-term compromise. The tunnel was not an isolated case. It was one element in a pattern of betrayal that had spanned a decade and touched dozens of operations. But the tunnel held a special place in that pattern, both for its scale and for what it revealed about Soviet methods.

The operation had been ambitious by any standard. The Americans and British had invested millions of dollars and thousands of man-hours in its planning and execution. The technical challenges had been substantial: digging a tunnel under a heavily guarded border, tapping cables without detection, recording conversations in real time, processing and analyzing the resulting flood of data. The joint nature of the operation had required cooperation between two intelligence services with different cultures, priorities, and methods. It had been a test of what the Western alliance could achieve together.

And the Soviets had known about it from the start.

The interrogators documented this finding with the care that such a revelation demanded. Their report would travel through channels, reaching the highest levels of both services. The implications would ripple outward, affecting assessments, operations, and personnel decisions for years to come.

Blake’s confession provided the first concrete, internal confirmation of what some had suspected and others had refused to believe. The tunnel had been compromised from its inception. The intelligence it produced had been potentially poisoned. The public triumph of 1956 had been a private disaster, masked by Soviet calculation.

Whether the Soviets had fed false information through the tapped lines remained unknown. Blake said he did not know. He had never been told. The Soviets were careful about compartmentalizing information. An agent knew what he needed to know to do his job. Blake’s job had been to provide intelligence, not to receive briefings on how that intelligence was being used. The decision about what to do with the tunnel—whether to shut it down immediately, whether to feed it false information, whether to let it run and then expose it for propaganda value—had been made by people far above his pay grade.

The answer was unsatisfying but plausible. Soviet intelligence operated on strict need-to-know principles. Blake had been a valuable asset, but he was still an asset, not an officer. He provided information. He did not set policy.

The interrogators concluded their sessions on the tunnel. They had extracted what Blake could give. The rest would require analysis, investigation, and difficult judgments about how to proceed.

The service faced a choice about what to do with Blake’s admissions regarding the tunnel. The information was explosive. If made public, it would confirm what Soviet propaganda had claimed all along—that the tunnel had been known and monitored from the start. It would embarrass the service and its American partners, revealing that their celebrated operation had been a failure of security rather than a triumph of technical capability.

But the information could not remain secret within intelligence channels. Too many people had been involved in the tunnel’s planning and operation. Too many questions had lingered since its exposure. Blake’s confession provided answers that could not be suppressed, only managed.

The interrogators prepared their reports. The documents made their way through the bureaucratic hierarchy, each level adding its own assessment and recommendations. By the time the information reached the relevant decision-makers, its significance had been parsed, contextualized, and prepared for action.

The trial that followed would make some of these details public. Blake was charged under the Official Secrets Act, the standard prosecution for espionage cases in Britain. The proceedings were held in camera, closed to the public, to protect sensitive information. But enough emerged to establish the basic facts: Blake had been a Soviet agent, he had compromised numerous operations, and the Berlin Tunnel had been among them.

The press coverage emphasized the scale of Blake’s betrayal. He was sentenced to forty-two years in prison, at the time the longest sentence ever handed down in a British espionage case. The tunnel was mentioned, though its full significance was not immediately grasped by the public. The headlines focused on the agent, the traitor in their midst, the man who had sold out his country for ideology and money.

For the intelligence services, the tunnel’s compromise raised questions that would take years to answer. The audit of the tunnel’s intelligence yield began in earnest after Blake’s confession. Analysts reviewed the 90, 000 recorded conversations, searching for signs of deception, anomalies that might indicate Soviet manipulation. They compared the tunnel’s product against other sources, looking for corroboration or contradiction.

The process was painstaking and inconclusive. Some of the tunnel’s intelligence had been genuine. Soviet military conversations had been recorded, and their content had matched what other sources reported about Soviet force levels and dispositions. But other material was more suspect. Assessments of Soviet intentions, political discussions, references to future plans—these were harder to verify. The possibility that the Soviets had used the tunnel to shape Western perceptions could not be ruled out.

The interrogators had done their job. They had extracted the truth from Blake, or as much of it as he possessed. The harder work came afterward: living with that truth, incorporating it into institutional knowledge, and rebuilding trust in systems that had proven vulnerable to precisely this kind of compromise.

The tunnel’s legacy had been complex from the moment of its discovery. It had been a technical success and an intelligence question mark. Blake’s confession resolved some doubts while creating others. The operation had indeed been compromised from the start, as the skeptics had suspected. But the full extent of that compromise—what the Soviets had done with their knowledge, how they had used the tunnel to their own ends—remained partially obscured, a matter of inference and deduction rather than certain knowledge.

The interrogators filed their final report. The document joined the archive, classified and restricted, available only to those with the clearance and the need to know. The machinery of intelligence assessment ground forward, processing the new information, adjusting judgments, preparing recommendations for those who would have to decide what it all meant.

In the months that followed, the service conducted a damage assessment that touched every operation Blake had known about. The tunnel was one element in a larger picture, but it held a particular significance. It demonstrated what Soviet intelligence could achieve when it positioned an agent inside the right place at the right time. It showed that technical operations, however sophisticated, were only as secure as the human beings who planned and executed them.

The Berlin Tunnel had been an engineering feat, a joint venture, and an intelligence operation. It had also been a lesson in the limits of technical capability when confronted with human betrayal. The cables had been tapped successfully. The conversations had been recorded. The product had been analyzed and disseminated. None of this had mattered, in the end, because the Soviets had known about it from the start.

The interrogators’ report noted this irony. The operation had been a success by every technical measure. Intelligence of apparent value had been produced. Cooperation between allied services had been demonstrated. The boundaries of what signals intelligence could achieve had been pushed forward. And it had been worthless, or worse than worthless, because a single officer had chosen to betray it.

The human factor had trumped the technical achievement. That lesson would shape intelligence operations for decades to come.

Blake’s interrogation marked the beginning of a long process of reckoning. The service had to rebuild its security procedures, its vetting processes, its operational safeguards. The Americans had to reassess what joint operations meant in an environment where the British services might be compromised. Trust, once broken, proved difficult to restore.

The tunnel itself had been filled in by the Soviets after its discovery. The physical structure was gone, its entrance sealed, its cables rerouted. But its intelligence product remained in the archives, thousands of hours of recorded conversations that now had to be viewed through the lens of Blake’s confession. Every assessment that had drawn on tunnel intelligence had to be reviewed. Every conclusion had to be weighed against the possibility that it had been shaped by Soviet design.

The archive grew. The documents accumulated. The tunnel’s history was written and rewritten as new information emerged and old assumptions were challenged. Blake’s confession was one chapter in that history, a turning point that transformed understanding of what had really happened beneath the streets of Berlin.

The questions that had waited five years for resolution had found their answer. The cost of that answer would be counted for years to come. The archive now held the truth, or what could be extracted of it. The harder work lay ahead: determining what the Soviets had allowed the West to hear, and what they had chosen to conceal.
