Chapter 5

The Rudow Blueprint

A memorandum dated January 1954, stamped SECRET in red ink, bore the serial number T-3-54. It presented the southern Berlin area in military detail: contour lines at five-meter intervals, roads by surface type, a dashed sector boundary. Near Altglienicke, three parallel lines in colored pencil, each annotated with a depth measurement, traced trunk cables from Karlshorst to Schönefeld. The corner was initialed WH by the man who had drawn them from reconnaissance.

The document was the product of weeks of planning that had begun in late 1953. In December of that year, the operation was placed under the direction of William King Harvey, a former FBI official who had transferred to the CIA. Captain Williamson of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers took charge of technical design and construction planning. The assignment of these two men marked the transition from speculative proposal to operational blueprint. Harvey brought the intelligence vision; Williamson brought the engineering discipline. The tunnel would require both.

The planning meetings that produced the T-3-54 map and its companion documents were held at No. 2 Carlton Gardens, London, a building that housed the British Secret Intelligence Service headquarters. The West German government was excluded from these discussions because of what the British termed the “highly infiltrated nature” of their service. The Americans and British agreed that the operation would proceed as a joint venture: the United States supplying most of the funding and personnel, the British contributing technical expertise drawn from their earlier tunnel operation in Vienna. That earlier operation, code-named Silver, had run from 1949 to 1955 and had successfully tapped Soviet landline communications in the Austrian capital. Peter Lunn, who had been involved in Silver, now found himself assigned to its Berlin successor.

Lunn’s presence in the planning sessions represented continuity. He had served as station chief in Vienna during Operation Silver and understood both the technical requirements and the operational risks of tunnel-based interception. His staff in Vienna had included officers who now transferred their attention to Berlin: Peter Montagnon, Major John Edward “Jock” McCowan, and others who had learned their trade in Austria. The British brought experience; the Americans brought resources. The resulting agreement, formalized in documents that crossed the Atlantic in diplomatic pouches through late 1953 and early 1954, established that the United States would bear roughly two-thirds of the estimated cost while British engineers advised on construction and British intelligence officers assisted with cable tapping and signal processing.

The target itself emerged from systematic analysis. In early 1951, the CIA began assessing how to replace lost Soviet radio communications intelligence. Wartime and immediate postwar years had given Western services access to Soviet military communications through radio intercepts; by 1950 Moscow had shifted much sensitive traffic to landlines—buried cables beyond conventional interception. The CIA assessment revealed a gap: Western allies were effectively deaf to Soviet military communications in Central Europe. When the Americans raised their concerns with SIS, London proposed tapping Soviet telephone lines through physical access. Vienna had proven the concept; Berlin offered a larger prize.

The three cables traced on Harvey’s map carried some of the most valuable traffic in East Germany: military orders between Karlshorst headquarters and subordinate formations; administrative communications across Altglienicke exchange; possibly high-level policy discussion reaching back to Moscow itself.

Reconnaissance teams began pinpointing where those cables lay and how deep they ran—surveyors whose presence along a border zone could be explained as routine maintenance or mapping work.

The selection of the tunnel’s starting point required different calculations. The entrance shaft and the warehouse that would conceal it had to be located in the American sector, close enough to the border to minimize the tunnel’s length but far enough to avoid detection during construction. The site had to be accessible for the delivery of construction materials and equipment, which would arrive in trucks that could not arouse suspicion. It had to be defensible, if not militarily then at least in terms of cover stories that could explain the presence of American military personnel and vehicles. And it had to be owned or controlled by American authorities who could authorize its use without triggering questions from German officials or other allied services.

The planners settled on a warehouse site in Rudow, in the American sector, close to the boundary line that separated the Western and Soviet zones. The warehouse itself was unremarkable, a structure that could plausibly serve as a storage facility or maintenance depot. The surrounding area was predominantly agricultural, with few residents who might notice unusual activity. The distance from the warehouse to the target cables was approximately 550 yards, a length that pushed the boundaries of tunnel engineering but remained within the realm of feasibility. The site was approved in early 1954, and the warehouse became the cover facility for the entry shaft of the Berlin Tunnel project.

Soil surveys followed the site selection. The ground between Rudow and Altglienicke was composed of sand and gravel deposited by ancient glacial activity, with a high water table that posed significant engineering challenges. The tunnel would have to be dug through saturated soil that would collapse without continuous support, and it would have to be sealed against water infiltration that could flood the entire structure. The engineers consulted geological maps and test borings, calculating the type of tunnel lining required and the pumping capacity needed to keep the working face dry. The technical specifications that emerged from these surveys filled appendices to the main operational plan: soil composition charts, water table projections, structural engineering calculations for the prefabricated tunnel sections that would be manufactured in secret and shipped to Berlin.

The construction schedule that accompanied these technical documents laid out a timeline of eight months from groundbreaking to completion. Work would begin in September 1954, with the excavation of the entry shaft inside the Rudow warehouse. From there, the tunnel would advance at a rate of approximately two yards per day, with work proceeding in shifts around the clock to minimize the total construction time. The target date for reaching the cables was April 1955, with tapping operations to begin immediately thereafter. The schedule was ambitious but realistic, based on the experience of the Vienna tunnel and adjusted for the specific conditions of the Berlin site.

The manpower estimates that accompanied the schedule called for a construction crew of approximately fifty men, drawn from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and operating under military cover. These men would live in Berlin under assumed identities, their true mission known only to a small circle of officers. The warehouse would be staffed by personnel who could maintain the cover story, a logistics depot or supply facility, while construction proceeded underground. Once the tunnel was complete and the taps installed, a separate operations crew would man the listening post, monitoring and recording the conversations that flowed through the Soviet cables.

The planning documents that accumulated through the winter and spring of 1953-54 represented an extraordinary commitment of resources. The estimated cost ran to several million dollars, a substantial sum for an intelligence operation in an era of tight budgets. The personnel requirements drew skilled men from other assignments. The political risk, if the tunnel were discovered, extended to the highest levels of government. Yet the planners proceeded with the confidence that the intelligence payoff would justify the investment. The Berlin Tunnel, they believed, would provide a window into Soviet military thinking that no other source could match.

Harvey’s role in this planning phase was decisive. His colleagues later described him as the operation’s indispensable advocate, the man who pushed the plan forward through bureaucratic obstacles and skeptical superiors. He argued for the tunnel in meetings at CIA headquarters, he negotiated the details of the British partnership, and he maintained pressure on the engineers to deliver a workable design. His signature or initials appeared on key documents throughout the planning period, a traceable record of his involvement. The tunnel was not his idea alone, as the concept had emerged from the 1951 assessment and the British experience in Vienna, but his advocacy gave it momentum.

Peter Lunn’s contribution was quieter but equally essential. As the senior British representative in the planning, he ensured that the lessons of Vienna were applied to Berlin. He coordinated between the American and British teams, smoothing over differences in procedure and terminology. His experience with cable tapping informed the technical specifications for the interception equipment. And his presence signaled British commitment to an operation that would have been difficult for the Americans to undertake alone. The tunnel was a joint venture in name and in practice, and Lunn’s fingerprints were on the planning documents alongside Harvey’s.

The technical advisors who supported Harvey and Lunn remain less visible in the documentary record. Captain Williamson, the Army Corps of Engineers officer assigned to lead construction, appears in the files as the author of engineering reports and the recipient of logistical requests. His team produced the construction drawings, the soil analyzes, and the schedule that transformed Harvey’s intelligence concept into a buildable project. The British technical specialists who had worked on Operation Silver contributed their knowledge of tunnel construction and cable tapping, though their names appear less frequently in the American files. The planning was collaborative, and the documents reflect multiple hands at work.

The reconnaissance missions that gathered information on the target cables proceeded alongside the engineering studies. Teams from the Berlin Operations Base, working under various covers, observed the cable routes and recorded details that could not be obtained from maps or photographs. They noted the timing of maintenance work on the cables, the presence of security patrols in the area, and the patterns of civilian activity that might affect construction or operations. This information fed back into the planning, refining the target location and identifying potential risks. The reconnaissance reports were classified at the highest level and distributed only to those with a direct need to know.

The warehouse at Rudow became the focal point for operational planning as the site selection crystallized. The building itself was acquired or leased through channels that concealed its true purpose, and preparations began for the modifications that would conceal the tunnel entrance. A shaft would have to be excavated through the warehouse floor, and a tunnel portal constructed at the shaft’s base. The warehouse would need ventilation systems, power supplies, and security arrangements that could withstand casual inspection without revealing the operation beneath. The cover story, a supply depot or maintenance facility, would have to be maintained for the duration of construction and operations.

The planning documents that emerged from this process were comprehensive. They included site maps showing the warehouse location and its relationship to the sector boundary, cross-section drawings depicting the tunnel profile and the entry shaft, construction schedules with milestones and deadlines, equipment lists specifying the materials to be procured and shipped, and operational plans describing how the intercepted communications would be processed and disseminated. The files grew thick with paper, each document adding another layer of detail to the operational blueprint.

Throughout this planning phase, George Blake remained at his post in the Berlin station. His name does not appear in the declassified planning documents for the tunnel, but his position gave him access to information about the operation. As a case officer handling Soviet émigrés and maintaining contacts with agents in the Eastern zone, he moved in circles where operational planning was discussed. His fluency in Russian and his experience in covert operations made him a valued member of the station. And his secret allegiance to the Soviet Union made him a conduit for information about Western intelligence activities.

The extent of Blake’s knowledge of the tunnel planning remains contested. His later confession stated that he had reported the operation to his Soviet handlers before construction began, but the details of what he reported and when remain unclear. Some accounts suggest he learned of the tunnel from conversations with colleagues. Others argue he may have seen planning documents or attended briefings where the operation was discussed. What is certain is that the Berlin station, where Blake served, was involved in the reconnaissance and support activities that fed into the planning process. The station provided personnel, local knowledge, and operational cover for activities in and around Berlin.

The paradox of the planning phase lies in this dual reality. While Harvey, Lunn, and their teams worked through the technical and logistical challenges of building a tunnel under the Berlin border, a Soviet agent sat within the Berlin station, potentially aware of their efforts. Every map drawn, every soil sample analyzed, every reconnaissance mission launched added to a body of knowledge that might already have been compromised. The institutional momentum that drove the planning forward was real and substantial. But the foundation on which that momentum rested was, unknown to the planners, cracked.

The British and American planners had considered the possibility of compromise. The operation was classified at the highest level, with access restricted to a small circle of cleared personnel. The planning documents bore code names and cover terms that concealed their true subject. The reconnaissance teams operated under legends designed to deflect suspicion. The warehouse site was acquired through indirect channels. Every precaution that intelligence tradecraft could devise was employed. But tradecraft could not protect against a mole within the circle of trust.

The planning for the Berlin Tunnel drew directly on the experience of Operation Silver in Vienna. That earlier tunnel, which ran from 1949 to 1955, had demonstrated both the feasibility and the intelligence value of cable tapping. The British team that had run Silver applied their lessons to Berlin: the importance of accurate target location, the challenges of tunneling through saturated soil, the need for robust cover arrangements, and the technical requirements for intercepting and recording telephone conversations. The Vienna tunnel had produced valuable intelligence, and the Berlin tunnel was expected to produce even more.

The differences between Vienna and Berlin were significant. Vienna was a four-power city, like Berlin, but the geography and politics of Austria differed from those of Germany. The Soviet headquarters in Karlshorst controlled a far larger force than the Soviet presence in Austria, and the communications passing through the Berlin cables were correspondingly more valuable. The tunnel length required in Berlin, approximately 550 yards, exceeded the Vienna tunnel and posed greater engineering challenges. And the political sensitivity of operations in Germany, at the front line of the Cold War, added a dimension of risk that Vienna had not presented.

The planning documents acknowledged these risks. The tunnel’s discovery would provoke a political crisis, potentially damaging Western credibility and providing Soviet propaganda with a potent symbol of Western espionage. The planners assessed this risk as acceptable, given the intelligence potential, but they took every precaution to minimize it. The construction would be concealed within the warehouse. The tunnel face would be advanced slowly and carefully to avoid surface disturbance. The operations crew would work in silence, monitoring and recording without transmitting signals that might be detected. The cover arrangements would be maintained throughout the operation’s life.

The technical specifications for the tunnel reflected these precautions. The tunnel would be lined with prefabricated steel sections, manufactured in the United States and shipped to Berlin in crates labeled as building materials. The sections would be assembled underground, creating a continuous tube that would support the surrounding soil and exclude groundwater. The internal diameter would be approximately six feet, enough to allow personnel to move through and to house the equipment required for tapping and recording. The tunnel would be ventilated and lit, with a narrow-gauge rail system for moving equipment and removing excavated soil.

The cable tapping itself required specialized equipment and techniques. The target cables were paper-insulated, with multiple pairs of wires carrying separate conversations. Tapping these cables without detection required physical access to the cable sheath, the installation of induction coils or direct connections, and the routing of the intercepted signals back through the tunnel to the listening post. The British had developed techniques for this work during Operation Silver, and similar methods would be applied in Berlin. The tapping equipment would be installed once the tunnel reached the cables, and operations would begin immediately.

The recording and processing of intercepted conversations posed additional challenges. The tunnel would produce a continuous stream of voice communications, in Russian and other languages, that would have to be recorded, transcribed, translated, and analyzed. The listening post would require recording equipment, transcription facilities, and a staff of linguists and analysts. The raw product would be voluminous, with estimates suggesting thousands of conversations per day, and the processing would require significant manpower. The operational plan included provisions for this processing, with recordings to be shipped out of Berlin for detailed analysis at specialized facilities.

The planning documents also addressed the operational lifetime of the tunnel. The assumption was that the tunnel would remain active for an extended period, perhaps years, before discovery or obsolescence ended its usefulness. The equipment would have to be maintained, the personnel rotated, and the cover sustained throughout this period. The logistics of long-term operation required planning: food and water for the listening post, spare parts for equipment, procedures for emergency evacuation. The tunnel was not just a construction project but an ongoing intelligence operation that would require sustained support.

By the spring of 1954, the planning had reached a stage where approval could be sought from the highest levels of government. The operational blueprint, with its maps, cross-sections, schedules, and cost estimates, was packaged for presentation to the Director of Central Intelligence and, through him, to the White House. The British had their own approval process, involving the Foreign Office and the Prime Minister. The planning that had begun in late 1953 was now ready to become operational reality.

The approval process itself generated additional documentation. Memoranda and briefing papers circulated through the intelligence bureaucracies, summarizing the operational plan and its risks. The financial commitments were authorized. The personnel assignments were confirmed. The cover arrangements were reviewed and approved. Each step in the process left its paper trail, a record of decisions that would later be examined by historians and investigators seeking to understand how the tunnel came to be.

The final element of the planning phase was the coordination of the construction timeline with other operational factors. The tunnel could not be built during the winter months, when the ground was frozen and construction activity would be more visible. The work had to be scheduled for the milder seasons, when the soil could be excavated and the tunnel advanced without weather-related delays. The target date for the start of construction, September 1954, reflected these considerations. The tunnel would be built through the fall and winter, with completion targeted for the spring of 1955.

The planning documents that accumulated through late 1953 and early 1954 represented an investment of time, expertise, and institutional commitment that could not easily be reversed. The reconnaissance missions had been flown, the soil surveys completed, the site selected, the cover arrangements put in place. The engineers had drawn their plans, the logisticians had compiled their lists, and the intelligence officers had defined their targets. The Berlin Tunnel was no longer a concept but a project, ready for implementation.

The irony of this achievement lay in what the planners did not know. While they worked through the details of tunnel construction and cable tapping, George Blake remained in place, his secret intact, his access to information unimpeded. The planning documents that crossed desks in Washington and London might also, through channels the planners had not anticipated, have crossed desks in Moscow. The blueprint was complete. The operation was approved. The tunnel would be built. And the Soviets, if Blake had told them, would be waiting.

The approved blueprint, with all its maps and schedules and specifications, was filed in the secure archives of the Berlin Operations Base. The document bore the signatures and initials of the men who had created it: Harvey, Lunn, Williamson, and others whose names remained classified. The paper itself was unremarkable, standard military drafting quality stored in a standard military filing cabinet. But the plan it contained would, within months, become a physical reality: a shaft sunk through a warehouse floor, a tunnel bored under a border, a tap placed on cables that carried the secrets of a superpower.
