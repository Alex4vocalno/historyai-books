Chapter 24

The Engineers’ Verdict

“Deception does not sound like this,” insisted one of Peter Lunn’s senior technicians, reflecting years after the tunnel’s exposure. “Deception sounds rehearsed.” He had spent months monitoring signal quality and replacing worn recording heads, and the traffic crossing his equipment carried the texture of real operational life: hesitations, corrections, bored recitations of supply inventories, sharp exchanges about vehicle maintenance. The intelligence harvested from the cables, he argued, had been genuine; the logistics reports and the mundane procedural chatter of Soviet military communications were not fabricated. What he had heard sounded like work—a judgment that cut against the grain of institutional silence, offered long after the men who had threaded the tape reels were gone.

The technician’s testimony mattered because it came from a man whose hands had been on the machinery. He was not a policymaker weighing strategic gains, nor an intelligence chief calculating the political cost of George Blake’s betrayal. He was an engineer. His verdict rested on what the data stream had shown him: the density, the inconsistency, the human irregularity of thousands of conversations recorded over eleven months. And his judgment was not isolated. Among the technical cadre that had built and operated the Berlin tunnel, a consensus gradually hardened in the years following the operation’s exposure. It formed not in conference rooms but in private correspondence, in conversations between former colleagues, in the quiet retrospective reflections of men who had invested years of their professional lives in a project whose official value remained undetermined.

The institutional silence that followed the tunnel’s discovery in April 1956 had left a vacuum. Neither the CIA nor MI6 issued a formal assessment of the operation’s intelligence worth. The official position, to the extent one existed, was that no definitive judgment could be rendered. Blake’s confession in 1961—that he had informed the KGB of the tunnel’s purpose before construction began—made the situation worse. If the Soviets had known from the outset, then every conversation recorded, every logistics report transcribed, every order-of-battle entry catalogued was potentially contaminated. The question was whether the intelligence had been accurate, and whether the Soviets had permitted the tunnel to operate, feeding selected information through the cables while withholding or altering anything genuinely sensitive. The institutional response to this question was to avoid answering it.

The engineers and analysts refused to accept that avoidance. Their response was technical, not political. They looked at the data itself and asked whether it bore the hallmarks of fabrication. The answer, for most of them, was no.

—-

The tunnel had been built under specific authority. In December 1953, the operation was placed under the direction of William King Harvey, a former FBI official who had transferred to the CIA. Captain Williamson of the United States Army Corps of Engineers supervised the physical work.

The first project was the construction of a warehouse in Rudow, which acted as a disguise and housed an Army radar station above it. The warehouse had an unusually deep basement to serve as the staging area for the tunnel and to house the excavated soil.

The tunnel, roughly a quarter-mile in length, ran from the American sector at Rudow under the border into East Berlin, reaching three Soviet army telephone cables near Altglienicke.

Ground was broken in September 1954, and the excavation took eight months to complete. Over the following eleven months, American and British operators recorded roughly 90, 000 communications from those cables. The Soviet authorities were aware of the tunnel’s existence from the beginning, though they did not publicly stage its discovery until the operation had been running for nearly a year.

The scale of the intercept was, by any technical measure, extraordinary. Ninety thousand communications, recorded across multiple cable lines, translated, transcribed, and distributed to analysts in Washington and London. The volume alone became one of the engineers’ primary arguments. Soviet communications doctrine, even at its most disciplined, could not sustain a fabricated traffic load of that magnitude without producing detectable patterns. The technicians who monitored the recordings noted the irregularities that characterized genuine operational traffic: a supply officer misreading a serial number and correcting himself, a duty officer asking a subordinate to repeat an order, background noise that shifted with weather and time of day. These were the marks of men doing their jobs, not of a scripted feed.

Peter Lunn’s team on the British side had maintained the tap equipment and the recording apparatus. The work was meticulous and unglamorous. Recording heads wore down and required replacement. Tape stock had to be requisitioned, catalogued, and archived. Signal quality varied with the seasons, with the moisture content of the soil above the cables, with the electrical load on the lines themselves. The technicians tracked these variables because the integrity of the recording depended on them. They knew the cables they were tapping carried real Soviet military traffic because the signal characteristics matched known Soviet equipment signatures. The frequency, the modulation, the line noise were all consistent with field telephone wire of the type used by Soviet occupation forces in East Germany.

The engineers’ first line of defense was that the signal was real. The physical properties of the intercepted communications conformed to what they knew about Soviet military communications infrastructure. The cables were where reconnaissance said they would be, carrying the traffic that the cable layout predicted. The taps had been placed correctly, the recording had been clean, and the data stream had been continuous. None of this was in dispute, even after Blake’s confession. The question was whether the content of that data stream had been manipulated.

Here the analysts joined the engineers’ verdict. The translators and intelligence analysts who had processed the intercepted material pointed to the internal consistency of the traffic. Soviet military communications followed established procedures—call signs, routing prefixes, authentication protocols—known to Western signals intelligence from other sources and other theaters. The tunnel traffic adhered to these procedures without deviation. If the Soviets had been feeding fabricated conversations, they would have had to replicate not only the content but the procedural framework in which that content was embedded, across thousands of calls, over eleven months, without a single detectable error.

The probability of that, the analysts argued, was negligible. Not impossible, because the Soviet signals intelligence service was competent, but negligible as a practical matter. The resources required to sustain a deception of that scale—involving dozens of fictitious military units, supply depots, training schedules, and personnel records, all referenced consistently across thousands of intercepted conversations—would have exceeded the value of protecting a single agent, however well placed.

The engineers’ core argument carried a force that the institutional silence could not answer. The cost-benefit calculation did not favor a deception operation. One assessment of the operation held that the Soviets considered Blake so valuable that they feared his exposure more than they feared a breach of their secrets. Over nine years, Blake is said to have betrayed hundreds of Western intelligence operations. If the tunnel was one of them, and the Soviets chose to let it run rather than shut it down and risk exposing their agent, then the intelligence flowing through those cables was the price they paid for his protection. That price was paid in real military communications, not in fabricated ones.

—-

The counter-argument was serious and could not be dismissed. It held that the Soviets, knowing the tunnel was active, would have taken steps to control what it heard. They need not have fabricated all 90, 000 communications. They needed only to ensure that genuinely sensitive material—troop movements, operational plans, strategic intentions—was routed away from the compromised cables, while allowing routine traffic to pass through unaltered. In this reading, the tunnel’s harvest was not fabricated but curated. The conversations were real, but they were the conversations the Soviets wanted the West to hear.

The engineers’ response to this was granular and stubborn. The cable network near Altglienicke carried Soviet military communications for a specific geographic area: the Soviet occupation zone in and around Berlin. The traffic was predominantly administrative and logistical—supply requisitions, maintenance reports, personnel transfers, training schedules, routine command communications. This was the normal traffic of an occupation army. If the Soviets had wanted to feed the West a misleading picture of their military posture, they would have had to alter the routine traffic itself, inserting false unit designations, inflated strength figures, or misleading orders of battle into the daily communications of real units. And they would have had to do this consistently, across multiple cables, for eleven months, while ensuring that none of the altered traffic contradicted the genuine signals intelligence that the West was collecting from other sources.

The engineers considered this scenario unlikely. Not because the Soviets lacked the capability, but because the risk of detection was too high. A single inconsistency—a unit designation that appeared in the tunnel traffic but not in intercepted radio communications, or a supply shipment logged on the cables but not confirmed by other intelligence—would have alerted Western analysts that something was wrong. The Soviets, the engineers argued, were too cautious to take that risk. They had chosen the simpler course: let the tunnel run, let it collect real traffic, and protect Blake by ensuring that nothing of catastrophic value crossed those specific cables.

This judgment carried weight because it came from men who had spent their careers evaluating the reliability of intercepted communications. They were not naive about Soviet deception capabilities. They had seen fabricated traffic in other contexts and knew its characteristics. The tunnel traffic, in their professional assessment, did not exhibit those characteristics. It was too varied, too mundane, too full of the small frictions of daily military life to be a constructed product. The supply officer who misread a serial number, the duty officer who repeated an order, the background noise of a motor pool audible during a phone call—these were the elements of an army at work, not of a deception operation.

The consensus that formed among the technical cadre was never formalized. No committee was convened, no report was commissioned, no institutional finding was issued. It existed in private correspondence, in conversations at retirements and reunions, in the quiet professional judgments of men who had moved on to other assignments but retained their conviction about the work they had done in Berlin. The consensus held that the tunnel’s intelligence harvest had been substantially genuine, that Blake’s betrayal had compromised the operation’s security but not necessarily its product, and that the institutional refusal to assess the intelligence value was a political decision, not a technical one.

The distinction mattered. A political decision to avoid assessing the tunnel’s intelligence value served institutional purposes. It allowed both the CIA and MI6 to avoid the uncomfortable conclusion that they had invested enormous resources in an operation that a Soviet agent had betrayed from the outset. It allowed them to avoid the equally uncomfortable counter-conclusion: that the operation had produced valuable intelligence despite the betrayal, and that the Soviet decision to let it run had cost them real military secrets. Either conclusion carried institutional costs. Silence carried none.

But the engineers’ verdict refused to accommodate that convenience. Their argument was not that the tunnel had been a triumph. It was that the work had been real. The cables had carried genuine traffic. The recordings had captured genuine conversations. The intelligence derived from those conversations had been accurate, corroborated by other sources, and useful in constructing a picture of Soviet military dispositions in East Germany. Blake’s betrayal had not changed the content of the cables. It had changed the confidence with which that content could be trusted. That was a significant loss, but it was not the same as total invalidation.

The engineers drew their line at the data stream itself. They staked their claim not on policy victories or strategic insights but on the technical properties of the intercepted material. The signal characteristics, the procedural consistency, the volume, the mundane detail, the human irregularity—these were the evidence on which they built their case. And they built it not against the Soviet Union, which had its own reasons for letting the tunnel operate, but against their own institutions, which had chosen silence over assessment.

—-

William Harvey’s CIA case officers contributed to this consensus from their own experience. Harvey had driven the tunnel project with a force that left no room for half-measures. The officers who served under him in Berlin had carried out the operation with precision, and their retrospective defense of the intelligence product carried the same quality. They pointed to the order-of-battle data extracted from the tunnel traffic: unit identifications, command structures, communication procedures, logistics patterns. Much of this data had been corroborated by independent intelligence sources, including radio intercepts, defector debriefings, and aerial reconnaissance. The corroboration was not complete—no intelligence source provides complete corroboration—but it was substantial. The tunnel had not been the sole source for any major intelligence finding, but it had contributed to a composite picture that had proven accurate in later years.

This point required careful qualification. The engineers and analysts who defended the tunnel’s intelligence product were not claiming that it had changed Western assessments of Soviet military intentions. They were claiming that it had contributed to the evidentiary base on which those assessments were built, and that the contribution had been genuine. The distinction was important. The tunnel had not prevented a crisis or revealed a secret that altered the strategic balance. It had provided detail, texture, and confirmation. Its value was cumulative, not transformative. But cumulative value was still value, and its genuineness was still a question that could be answered by examining the data.

The engineers answered it by examining the data. And the data, in their professional judgment, was real.

The institutional response to this verdict was characteristic. Neither the CIA nor MI6 contradicted it. Neither agency endorsed it. The technical cadre’s consensus remained private, informal, and unsupported by any official finding. It existed in the space that the institutional silence had created—a space in which the people who had built and operated the tunnel were free to reach their own conclusions because no one in authority would reach one for them.

This was the consequence of the calculated silence that both agencies had maintained since the tunnel’s exposure. By refusing to assess the intelligence value of the operation, they had ceded the ground to the technicians and analysts who had produced it. Those men reached their verdict based on their direct engagement with the material. Their verdict was not infallible. It was, however, informed by a kind of evidence that institutional headquarters could not easily replicate: the daily experience of listening to, recording, transcribing, and analyzing thousands of intercepted conversations over eleven months. The engineers knew what the traffic sounded like. They knew what it contained. They knew whether it bore the marks of fabrication, and they concluded that it did not.

—-

The counter-argument remained. The Soviets had known about the tunnel from the beginning. They had the means and the motive to control its output. The fact that the traffic appeared genuine did not prove that it was genuine, because the most effective deception is the one that cannot be distinguished from reality. The engineers’ verdict rested on a probabilistic judgment—that the consistency, volume, and mundane detail of the traffic made fabrication implausible—but probabilistic judgments are not certainties. The institutional silence that refused to endorse the engineers’ verdict also refused to reject it, and that refusal left the question open.

It remains open. The documents that have surfaced since the tunnel’s exposure—including the declassified CIA history, the Soviet and East German accounts of the staged discovery, and Blake’s own confession and subsequent statements—do not conclusively resolve the debate. Soviet awareness of the tunnel before construction began is confirmed. Blake’s role in providing the information is confirmed. The Soviet decision to let the tunnel operate for nearly a year before publicly discovering it is confirmed. What no document confirms is whether the Soviets altered the traffic flowing through the compromised cables during that period.

The engineers’ verdict addresses this gap with a technical argument that is strong but not conclusive. The traffic was genuine in its physical and procedural characteristics. It was consistent with known Soviet communications practices. It was corroborated by independent intelligence sources. The cost and risk of fabricating or curating 90, 000 communications over eleven months exceeded the likely benefit. These points carry weight. They do not carry finality.

What they carry is the conviction of men who did the work. The technicians who maintained the recording equipment, the analysts who transcribed the conversations, the translators who rendered Russian and German into English—all of them had performed their duties with competence and dedication. They had produced work of measurable quality. They had no way of knowing whether the work was worth the effort, and no one in authority would tell them. The official verdict was that there was no verdict. The engineers’ verdict was that there was.

—-

Rendered not in a formal report but in the accumulated professional judgment of a technical cadre that had spent eleven months inside the largest signals intelligence operation of the early Cold War, the verdict rested on evidence: the data stream itself, its physical properties, its procedural consistency, its corroboration by other sources. A particular force attended it because the people who had been closest to the material delivered it. They had listened to the traffic, transcribed it, analyzed it, lived with it daily, and concluded that it was real.

The consensus that formed among these men in the years after the tunnel’s exposure was not a defense of policy. It was a defense of craft. The tunnel had been built well. The taps had been placed correctly. The recording had been clean. The analysis had been thorough. The intelligence had been genuine. Blake’s betrayal had compromised the operation, but it had not compromised the integrity of the work. That distinction—between the security of the operation and the quality of its product—was the foundation on which the engineers built their case.

They built it well. The technical argument for the genuineness of the tunnel traffic is strong, grounded in physical evidence and professional experience. It does not eliminate the possibility of Soviet manipulation, but it makes that possibility less probable than the alternative: that the Soviets, choosing to protect Blake, accepted the loss of real military communications as the cost of his security. The engineers’ verdict is the last major counter-argument in the enduring debate over the Berlin tunnel. It stands against the institutional silence that refused to assess the operation’s value, and against the suspicion that everything the tunnel produced was tainted by Blake’s betrayal.

The suspicion remains. It cannot be dispelled by technical argument alone, because the question is not purely technical. It is strategic. Did the Soviets let the tunnel run to protect an agent, or did they let it run to feed the West a controlled picture of their military dispositions? Both readings are consistent with the known facts. The engineers’ verdict favors the first reading. The institutional silence accommodates both. And the documents that have surfaced since the tunnel’s exposure do not resolve the question conclusively.

What the documents do show is the scale of Blake’s betrayal and the Soviet calculation that followed from it. Blake had been recruited by the KGB during his captivity in Korea. Following his release in 1953, he returned to Britain as a hero, landing at RAF Abingdon. In October 1954, he married MI6 secretary Gillian Allan in St Mark’s Church on North Audley Street in London. In 1955, MI6 sent him to work as a case officer in Berlin. His assignment included the tunnel operation. He reported its details to his KGB handler before the first shovel broke ground.

The Soviets received the information and made a decision. They would not shut the tunnel down. They would not alter the cables. They would let the Americans and the British dig, tap, record, and analyze, for nearly a year, before staging a public discovery that would expose the operation and embarrass the West. The decision was, by any measure, a strategic one. It weighed the value of Blake against the cost of the intelligence the tunnel would collect, and it concluded that Blake was worth more.

The engineers’ interpretation of this decision is that the Soviets accepted a real intelligence loss. They did not fabricate the traffic. They did not curate it. They let it flow, knowing that the West would hear genuine Soviet military communications, because the alternative—shutting down the tunnel or altering the cables—would have risked exposing Blake. The engineers argue that the Soviets were willing to pay that price because Blake’s continued access to Western intelligence was more valuable than the military communications that the tunnel would intercept.

This interpretation is plausible. It is supported by the technical characteristics of the intercepted traffic and by the known Soviet assessment of Blake’s value. It is not the only plausible interpretation. The Soviets may have calculated that the intelligence loss was acceptable because the cables near Altglienicke did not carry the most sensitive traffic, and that the tunnel would provide the West with a picture of Soviet military dispositions that was accurate but incomplete—accurate enough to be trusted, incomplete enough to be misleading. This interpretation is also consistent with the known facts.

The engineers’ verdict does not exclude this second interpretation. It argues against it, but it does not eliminate it. The distinction between the two readings turns on a question that the available evidence cannot fully answer: whether the Soviets, knowing the tunnel was active, took steps to ensure that sensitive traffic was routed away from the compromised cables. If they did, then the tunnel’s harvest was curated, and the engineers’ argument for its genuineness, while technically sound, misses the point. The traffic would have been real, but it would have been selectively real—a partial picture presented as a complete one.

If they did not, then the tunnel’s harvest was genuine, and the engineers’ verdict stands. The Soviets accepted the loss because Blake was worth more. The traffic was real, the intelligence was accurate, and the operation produced value despite the betrayal.

The question cannot be resolved with the evidence currently available. What can be said is that the engineers’ verdict represents a serious and informed judgment, rendered by the people most directly engaged with the intercepted material, and that it deserves to be weighed alongside the institutional silence that refused to render any judgment at all. The engineers did not have access to Soviet decision-making. They did not know what the KGB had told the Soviet military about the tunnel, or what orders had been issued regarding the cables. They knew what they had heard, and what they had heard sounded genuine.

That judgment—technical, professional, grounded in direct experience—is the engineers’ verdict. It is the last word from the people who built the tunnel and operated its systems. It is not the last word on the tunnel’s intelligence value, because that question is strategic as well as technical, and the strategic question that the engineers did not possess. But it carries the authority of that work, and it stands as the final technical testimony to a project that had been, in its conception and execution, a masterpiece of signals intelligence engineering.

—-

The irony of this consensus is that it affirmed the technical perfection of the system while leaving its strategic implications unexamined. The engineers’ verdict certified the integrity of the acquisition, the reliability of the recording, the accuracy of the analysis. The tunnel had done what it was designed to do: intercept, record, and preserve Soviet military communications with a fidelity that allowed detailed intelligence assessment. What the verdict could not assess was whether the Soviets had allowed that fidelity to exist because it served their purposes. The Engineered Truth—the proposition that technical sophistication in acquisition validates the intelligence product—found its strongest defenders in the men who had built the system. Their confidence in their craft was justified. Their confidence in the product of that craft was the question the verdict could not settle.

The institutional response to the engineers’ verdict was the same silence that had preceded it. No assessment, no endorsement, no rebuttal. The calculated silence that had characterized both agencies’ approach to the tunnel’s intelligence value absorbed the technical consensus without acknowledging it. The engineers had spoken. The institutions declined to answer. And the gap between the technical verdict and the institutional silence defined the space in which the tunnel’s legacy would be contested for decades.

By 1966, the engineers’ consensus had solidified into a durable position. The men who held it had heard the counter-arguments. They knew that Blake’s betrayal cast a shadow over everything the tunnel had produced. They knew that the Soviets had the means and the motive to manipulate the traffic. They knew that their confidence in the genuineness of the intercept was a probabilistic judgment, not a certainty. And they held the judgment anyway, because the evidence supported it.

The evidence was the data stream itself. Ninety thousand communications, with their mundane detail, their procedural consistency, their human irregularity, and their corroboration by independent sources. The engineers had lived with that evidence for eleven months. They had recorded it, transcribed it, analyzed it, and archived it. They knew its characteristics. They knew what fabrication looked like, and they knew that what they had seen did not look like fabrication. This was not a political judgment or an institutional judgment. It was a technical judgment, rendered by technicians, based on technical evidence.

The tapes themselves had been destroyed. The transcripts survived in archives, classified and inaccessible. The machinery had been dismantled. The tunnel had been filled in. But the professional judgment of the men who had operated the system endured, recorded in correspondence and conversation, carried forward by the conviction that the work had been genuine even if the operation had been compromised. Their verdict stood as the final technical testimony to a project that had been, in its conception and execution, a masterpiece of signals intelligence engineering, and that had been, in its strategic outcome, a catastrophe of counterintelligence failure.

The engineers did not reconcile these two truths. They did not need to. Their verdict was about the first. The second was someone else’s responsibility. And the institutional silence that refused to address either one ensured that the two truths would coexist, unresolved, in the space that the tunnel had opened and that Blake’s betrayal had widened. The engineers’ defiant verdict on technical truth set the stage for the book’s final legacy judgments.
