Chapter 7

The Cable in the Clay

A cable identification chart, numbered T-7-54 and dated October 1954, lay spread across a table in the operations room at the Berlin base. Three parallel lines in colored pencil traced the routes of the Soviet Army’s main telephone trunk lines from headquarters in Karlshorst southeast toward the airfield at Schönefeld. Near Altglienicke, the lines converged, passing beneath the sector border into the American zone at Rudow. Depth annotations marked each cable: 47 centimeters, 52 centimeters, 61 centimeters. The clay stratum in which they lay was stable, impermeable, and ideal for what was to come. The chart bore the initials of the engineer who had verified the measurements, and beside them, in different handwriting, a single word: confirmed.

The chart represented the culmination of months of reconnaissance. The three cables it depicted were not speculative. They had been identified through aerial photography, agent reports, and technical analysis of Soviet communication patterns. The lines carried the voice traffic of the Soviet Army headquarters in Karlshorst, the nerve center of military operations in East Germany. They passed through the Altglienicke area in a narrow corridor, converging at a point where the sector boundary between the American and Soviet zones cut across their path. The depth measurements had been confirmed by probes. The clay had been tested. The engineering assessment was complete. The next phase was physical.

The tunnel had been dug. The shaft at Rudow descended through the floor of a warehouse, and from its base a horizontal passage extended 450 meters beneath the border, the sector boundary, and the no-man’s-land of barbed wire and watchtowers. The digging had begun on September 2, 1954, and was completed on February 25, 1955. The covert construction of the tunnel under the world’s most heavily patrolled border to intersect a series of cables less than 47 centimeters below a busy street was an exceptional engineering challenge. The excavation had been conducted in secret, the spoil removed in covered trucks, the entrance concealed within the warehouse structure.

Now the excavation phase was ending, and the intelligence phase was about to begin. The three cables whose positions were marked on the chart had to be located, exposed, and tapped. The work would be done in silence, in a confined space, with the knowledge that any mistake could bring Soviet or East German patrols down upon the site.

The tunnel ended in what the engineers called the tap chamber. It was a widened section of the passage, reinforced with steel plates and equipped with ventilation, lighting, and the mounting points for amplifiers and recording equipment. The chamber had been constructed during the final weeks of excavation, its walls braced against the pressure of the clay and its floor leveled to accommodate the machinery that would process the signals from the cables. The chamber was not large. A man could stand upright, but he could not stretch his arms wide. The air was filtered and circulated by fans that hummed at a frequency designed to mask any sound that might carry through the earth.

The cables themselves were lead-sheathed and armored, standard Soviet military trunk lines carrying voice traffic between the Karlshorst headquarters and the outlying installations. They were not buried deep. The chart showed depths of less than a meter, which meant that the tunnel had to approach them from below, rising slightly in its final section to meet the level at which they lay. The engineers had calculated the angle of ascent with precision. An error of centimeters would mean missing the cables entirely or, worse, breaking through the surface in view of the border guards.

The work of locating the cables fell to a small team of specialists who had trained for this phase of the operation. They used probes, modified listening devices, and the chart that had been compiled from reconnaissance photographs and agent reports. The probes were inserted through the clay ahead of the tunnel face, withdrawn, and examined for traces of the cable sheathing. The listening devices were sensitive enough to detect the electromagnetic field generated by current flowing through the lines. The process was slow. Each advance of a few meters required verification, adjustment, and the approval of the senior engineer on site.

The first cable was located on a Tuesday. The probe came up with a smear of lead on its tip, and the listening device registered a faint but steady signal. The engineers marked the position and began the careful work of excavation. The clay was cut away in small sections, each removed by hand and passed back along the line of workers to be deposited in the tunnel behind them. The cable was exposed gradually, a few centimeters at a time, until a length of the sheathing was visible. It was thicker than expected, the lead casing reinforced with steel wire wound in a spiral. The engineers noted the dimensions and compared them to the specifications on the chart. They matched.

Attaching the tap required specialized techniques that had been developed in laboratory conditions and tested on captured Soviet cable samples. The sheathing had to be opened without damaging the insulation around the internal wires. The connection had to be made without interrupting the signal or creating a detectable change in the line’s electrical characteristics. The tap itself was a small device, no larger than a matchbox, designed to draw off a portion of the signal and route it to an amplifier without reducing the strength of the current flowing through the cable. The installation took hours. The engineers worked in shifts, each pair spending no more than four hours in the chamber before being relieved. The air was thick with the smell of clay and solder.

The technical challenge was significant. Soviet cable systems were designed with security in mind. The lines were monitored for anomalies, and any interruption in service would trigger investigation. The taps had to be invisible to the electrical tests that Soviet technicians might perform. The amplifiers had to draw power without creating detectable interference. The recording equipment had to run continuously without mechanical failure. A mistake carried diplomatic consequences as well as operational ones. Discovery of the tunnel would mean exposure of a covert action, protest from the Soviet government, and embarrassment for the United States and Britain.

When the first tap was in place, the signal was routed through the amplifier to a monitoring station in the tunnel. A technician put on headphones and listened. What he heard was static at first, then a burst of noise, then a voice speaking Russian. The voice was male, the tone conversational, the words indistinct. The technician adjusted the gain and the voice became clearer. It was a routine communication, a logistics officer discussing supply deliveries with a subordinate. The technician recorded the exchange on magnetic tape and marked the reel with the time, date, and cable number. The first intercept from the Berlin Tunnel had been made.

The second cable was located three days later. The process repeated: probing, excavation, exposure, tapping. This cable carried a different type of traffic, the signals stronger and more frequent. The monitoring station recorded continuously now, the tape reels turning in the dim light of the tunnel. The third cable followed, and by the end of the month, all three lines had been tapped. The chamber was fully operational, its amplifiers drawing power from generators in the warehouse above, its recording equipment running twenty-four hours a day.

The Americans wanted to hear any warlike intentions being discussed by the Soviet military. The cables from Karlshorst carried the voice traffic of the army headquarters, the communications of senior officers, the routine and the sensitive alike. The tunnel gave them access to a river of information that had previously been closed. The Soviet Union had moved its most secure communications from radio to landline after the war, depriving Western intelligence of a major source. Operation Gold, known to the British as Operation Stopwatch, was designed to restore that access through physical intrusion.

The scale of the operation was unprecedented. The tunnel itself was a major engineering achievement, dug beneath the most heavily guarded border in the world. But the taps were the crucial element. Without them, the tunnel was merely a passage. With them, it became an intelligence asset of the first order. The recording equipment in the chamber could capture hundreds of hours of conversation. The tapes would be removed daily, transported through the tunnel to the warehouse in Rudow, and from there to the processing center in Berlin. Translators and analysts would review the contents, extracting intelligence and preparing reports for distribution to the American and British governments.

The security arrangements were elaborate. The warehouse in Rudow was guarded by American military police who had been told that it contained sensitive communications equipment. They did not know what lay beneath the floor. The tunnel entrance was concealed behind a false wall, accessible only to cleared personnel. The workers who maintained the tunnel and operated the equipment were vetted and briefed on the need for absolute secrecy. They were forbidden to discuss their work with anyone outside the operation, including family members. The penalty for a breach was severe: dismissal, prosecution, and in the worst case, exposure of the entire project.

The risk of detection was constant. Soviet and East German border patrols passed within meters of the tunnel’s path. The ground above was watched from watchtowers and surveyed by vehicles equipped with listening devices. The engineers had taken precautions to mask the sounds of construction, but there was no way to eliminate them entirely. The ventilation fans in the tunnel were designed to operate at frequencies that would blend with ambient noise, but a sharp listener might notice something unusual. The clay itself was stable but not impermeable. A heavy rain could soften it, a frost could crack it. The tunnel required constant maintenance to prevent collapse.

The tap chamber was the most vulnerable point. The cables passed through it, and the taps were attached to them. If the Soviets discovered the tunnel, the chamber would be the first place they would examine. The equipment inside would reveal what had been done and how long it had been going on. The recording machines bore serial numbers that could be traced. The tapes themselves, if captured, would provide evidence of the scope of the operation. The chamber was a treasure house and a trap.

The work in the chamber continued through the autumn of 1954. The engineers made adjustments to the amplifiers, improving the quality of the signal. The technicians monitored the recordings, noting patterns in the traffic and flagging conversations of interest. The first reports based on the intercepts began to move through the intelligence pipeline. They contained details of Soviet military dispositions, logistics, and training exercises. Some of the information was routine, but some was valuable. The operation was producing.

The British involvement was significant. The Secret Intelligence Service had contributed expertise and personnel, building on experience from a similar operation in Vienna. The British monitoring station there had been disguised as a shop selling tweed clothing, and it had operated successfully until Austria regained full sovereignty earlier in the year. The Vienna operation had provided a model for Berlin, and the British had shared their knowledge of cable tapping techniques with the Americans. The collaboration was close, although there were tensions. The Americans provided most of the funding and the majority of the personnel. The British provided technical advice and a small number of specialists. Both services would receive the intelligence produced.

The operational agreement between the CIA and the SIS had been formalized in early 1951, after the Americans revealed their initial plans and the British responded with the revelation that they had already conducted a similar operation. The joint nature of the project was a reflection of the broader alliance, but it also created complications. Intelligence sharing was never complete. Each service had its own priorities, its own channels, its own secrets. The tunnel was a joint asset, but the control of information flowing from it was a matter of negotiation.

The cables that had been tapped were the Soviet Army’s main trunk lines, but they were not the only communications channels used by the headquarters in Karlshorst. More sensitive traffic moved through encrypted links, through couriers, through means that the tunnel could not intercept. The operation was a partial solution to a larger problem. It provided access to a significant volume of voice traffic, but it did not crack the highest levels of Soviet command. The intelligence it produced was valuable but not decisive.

The engineers who had done the work of tapping the cables were not intelligence officers. They were technicians, specialists in cable systems and electronics. Their role was to make the physical connection and ensure that it functioned. The interpretation of what flowed through the cables was the work of others. The engineers knew that they were tapping Soviet military lines, but they did not know the contents of the conversations they enabled. Their clearance extended to the method, not the message.

The tunnel, now fully operational, required a staff to maintain it and process its output. Personnel were assigned in shifts, each team spending a set number of days in the tunnel before rotating out. The conditions were difficult. The air was stale despite the ventilation. The temperature was constant but cool. The lighting was dim. The work was monotonous. The technicians sat in the monitoring station, headphones on, watching the reels turn, making notes when something of interest passed through. The engineers inspected the taps, the amplifiers, the power systems, looking for signs of wear or failure. The guards above maintained their vigil, watching the perimeter, controlling access, trying to appear as if nothing unusual was happening.

The first month of operation produced a volume of intercepts that exceeded expectations. The Americans were able to listen to telephone conversations for nearly a year, eventually recording roughly 90, 000 communications. The Soviet authorities, at this stage, gave no indication that they knew what was happening beneath their feet. The border patrols continued their routines. The cables carried their traffic. The tunnel ran on.

The success of the tapping phase was a technical triumph. The cables had been located with precision, exposed without detection, and tapped without interruption. The equipment was functioning. The recordings were accumulating. The operation had moved from construction to production. But the technical triumph was also a commitment. The taps were physical objects attached to physical cables. They could not be removed without leaving evidence. The tunnel could not be erased. The operation had created a fact on the ground, and that fact would exist until someone discovered it or the political situation changed.

The security gamble was inherent in the design. The tunnel was dug in secret, but it could not remain secret forever. The cables were tapped in secret, but the taps themselves were detectable if someone looked. The operation depended on the Soviets not looking, or not looking in the right place, or not understanding what they found if they looked. The assumption was that the risk was acceptable, that the intelligence gained would outweigh the consequences of exposure. That assumption had been debated before the first shovel went into the ground. It would be tested in the months to come.

The tap chamber, with its amplifiers and recording equipment, its taps on three cables, its technicians and engineers, was the physical manifestation of that gamble. It was a room carved out of clay beneath a border, filled with machinery designed to steal secrets. It was also a room that could not be easily abandoned. The investment in its construction, the effort of its operation, the intelligence it produced—all of these argued for continuation. The risks argued for caution. The balance between the two was the daily reality of the operation.

The engineers who had completed the tapping returned to their regular duties. The technicians who monitored the recordings settled into their routines. The guards above continued their watch. The tunnel had become a listening post, active and productive. The cables in the clay were live, and the river of voices they carried was flowing west.

A logistics form from the Berlin Operations Base, dated November 1954, requested additional supplies: magnetic tape, replacement vacuum tubes, and spare parts for the recording equipment. The form listed the items as routine office materials. The clerk who processed it did not know their destination. He entered the quantities in a ledger, stamped the form approved, and passed it to the next desk. The bureaucracy of intelligence was as essential as the engineering. Without the forms, the requisitions, the approvals, the tunnel would run out of tape, and the recordings would stop.

The tape arrived from Frankfurt in a diplomatic pouch, listed as office supplies. It was unloaded at Tempelhof Airport and transported by military vehicle to the warehouse in Rudow. The guards checked the driver’s credentials and waved him through. The boxes were carried inside and stored in a locked room. From there, they would be taken down the shaft, through the tunnel, to the tap chamber. The reels would be loaded onto the machines, and the machines would record the voices of Soviet officers discussing the business of an army.

The operation had entered its productive phase. The digging was done. The taps were in place. The recordings had begun. The tunnel was now a living operation, and the voices it captured would fill thousands of reels over the coming months. The gamble had been taken. The commitment had been made. The cable in the clay had become a source.

The first reels of magnetic tape began to turn in Rudow. A river of raw Soviet communications now flowed westward.
