Chapter 1

The City of Wire

The meeting of the Allied Kommandatura on June 16, 1948, produced no communiqué, no joint statement, and no agreement on the matter at hand. The Soviet representative had walked out. The three Western commandants remained at the table, but without their fourth counterpart, the four-power administrative body that had governed Berlin since the war’s end had ceased to function. The minutes recorded the departure without comment. The Soviet delegation offered no explanation, no date for return, no acknowledgment that the walkout marked anything beyond a temporary protest.

For most of Germany, this act merely formalised what had been an obvious fact for some time: the four-power control machinery had become unworkable. For Berlin, however, it signaled a major crisis. On March 25, 1948, the Soviets had issued orders restricting Western military trains passing through the Soviet zone. The currency reform that followed in June brought the confrontation to its peak. When the Western Allies extended the reform to the three western sectors of Berlin, the Soviet Union imposed the Berlin Blockade on the access routes to and from the city. The administrative rupture left the city’s residents, its occupying forces, and its communications infrastructure suspended between two systems that no longer spoke to each other.

The collapse of the Kommandatura was the visible fracture of a division that had been forming since the first days of occupation. From July 17 to August 2, 1945, the victorious Allies had signed the Potsdam Agreement on the fate of postwar Europe, calling for the division of defeated Germany, west of the Oder–Neisse line, into four temporary occupation zones. Berlin, though located deep within the Soviet zone, received the same treatment: four sectors, four garrisons, four flags flying over a ruined capital. The arrangement was meant to be temporary, a holding pattern until a peace treaty could determine Germany’s future.

The temporary arrangement hardened into permanence with remarkable speed. The Soviet Union had accepted the division of Berlin, but it had never accepted the Western presence as permanent. By 1948, the Western powers had approved the extension of the Marshall Plan to Germany, finalised the economic merger of the western occupation zones, and agreed upon the establishment of a federal system of government for them. The Soviets viewed these steps as a violation of Potsdam, a deliberate partition of Germany into two states, and a threat to their security interests in Eastern Europe.

The blockade began on June 24, 1948. Soviet authorities halted all rail traffic between the western zones and Berlin, cut off barge traffic on the canals, and restricted road access to the city. The Western sectors—home to approximately two million people—contained food supplies for thirty-six days and coal for forty-five days. The city produced none of its own fuel and grew little of its own food. The Soviet Military Administration proclaimed that all Berlin came under Soviet authority and predicted the imminent abandonment of the city by the Western powers. The Soviets also harassed members of the democratically elected citywide administration, which had to conduct its business in the city hall located in the Soviet sector. The elected mayor and his staff faced intimidation, arrest, and obstruction. The city government, like the Kommandatura, split along sector lines.

The Western response to the blockade was not withdrawal but supply. The United States Air Force and the Royal Air Force began a sustained operation to fly food, fuel, and medicine into the western sectors. The Berlin Airlift, as it became known, operated around the clock for nearly eleven months. Aircraft landed at Tempelhof Airport in the American sector and Gatow in the British sector every few minutes, their cargo holds filled with flour, coal, powdered milk, and medical supplies. By the time the Soviets lifted the blockade on May 12, 1949, the airlift had delivered more than 2.3 million tons of supplies on more than 278, 000 flights. The operation demonstrated that the Western powers could sustain their presence in Berlin indefinitely, but it also demonstrated that the city had become a fault line in the emerging Cold War.

The physical city that emerged from the blockade was not yet divided by a wall. It was divided by an invisible, yet profoundly tangible, frontier of ideology and wire. The sector boundaries that ran through streets, through buildings, through subway lines, were administrative lines on a map, but they functioned as a de facto border. Crossing from the American sector to the Soviet sector required no passport, no visa, no checkpoint—only the willingness to walk past the soldiers who stood at the crossings and the knowledge that the authorities on the other side operated by different rules. The city remained physically intact, its transportation networks still connected, its telephone lines still crossing the sectors. But the trust that had allowed four powers to govern a single city had evaporated.

The telephone network was both a lifeline and a vulnerability. Berlin’s communications infrastructure had survived the war in damaged but functional condition. The Soviet military administration had taken control of the main exchanges in its sector, while the Western powers operated exchanges in theirs. The lines that connected the sectors carried civilian calls, military traffic, and diplomatic communications. For intelligence services on both sides, the telephone network represented an opportunity. Calls could be monitored, lines could be tapped, and information could be extracted from the flow of daily business. The Red Army had followed the Soviet diplomatic department and transferred its most secure communications from radio to telephone landlines. This shift deprived the Western Allies of a major Cold War source of information. Radio signals could be intercepted at a distance; telephone calls required physical access to the lines.

The Soviet decision to move to landlines was a rational response to the vulnerability of radio transmission. Radio signals traveled through the air, available to anyone with the right equipment to receive them. The Western Allies had developed sophisticated intercept capabilities during the war, capturing German military communications and decrypting them in real time. The Soviet military, aware of this capability, moved its sensitive communications to cables buried underground or strung on poles. These cables carried conversations between Soviet headquarters in East Berlin, military installations in the surrounding countryside, and the higher command in Moscow. The physical wire replaced the broadcast signal. The invisible became tangible, hidden, protected.

This shift created a new problem for Western intelligence. The cables were not impossible to access—they ran through territory that, in some places, lay adjacent to or even within the Western sectors. But accessing them required more than intercept equipment. It required physical penetration, excavation, and installation of tapping equipment. It required an engineering solution to what had once been a listening problem. The genesis of the Berlin Tunnel operation lay not in a single intelligence plan, but in the very nature of the city itself—a place where the superpowers’ spheres of influence met in a chaotic, contested urban space, creating an unprecedented laboratory for espionage.

The laboratory had precedents. In Vienna, British intelligence had already demonstrated that tunneling to Soviet cables was possible. Operation Silver, a British intelligence operation in Allied-occupied Austria which ran from 1949 to 1955, covertly tapped into the landline communications of the Soviet Army headquarters in Vienna. The operation’s staff included Peter Lunn and Peter Montagnon, officers who would later play roles in the Berlin operation. The Vienna tunnel proved the concept: dig from friendly territory under a boundary, locate the target cables, install taps, and record the conversations. It produced intelligence on Soviet military intentions, force dispositions, and internal deliberations. It was, by the standards of the time, a success.

But Vienna was not Berlin. Vienna had been divided into occupation zones, but Austria had signed a state treaty in 1955 that restored its sovereignty and ended the occupation. The Vienna tunnel operated during the occupation, when the four powers shared responsibility for the city and when the boundaries between zones were administratively permeable. Berlin was different. The division of Germany was hardening into two states. Berlin remained under four-power occupation, but the cooperation that had once characterized that occupation had given way to confrontation. The city was not a temporary holding pattern but a permanent frontline.

The frontline status of Berlin had implications for intelligence work. The city contained the headquarters of Soviet forces in Germany, the administrative center of the East German government, and a dense concentration of military communications infrastructure. Soviet telephone cables ran through the eastern sectors and the surrounding countryside, carrying traffic between Moscow and its forward positions. Some of these cables passed near the sector boundaries, close enough that a tunnel dug from the western sectors could reach them. The geography of Berlin—the location of the cables, the distance from the American sector at Rudow to the Soviet cables near Altglienicke—presented both an irresistible target and a unique engineering challenge.

The engineering challenge was significant but not insurmountable. The distance from the American sector to the target cables was approximately 550 yards. The tunnel would need to pass under the sector boundary, under the border fortifications that the Soviets had begun to construct, and under the streets and fields of the eastern sector. It would need to be dug in secret, without alerting the East German civilians who lived above it or the Soviet guards who patrolled the border. It would need to be ventilated, lit, and drained. The tapping equipment would need to be installed without detection, and the recordings would need to be retrieved and transported back to the western sectors.

The technical requirements were formidable. The Americans wanted to hear any warlike intentions being discussed by their military and were able to listen to telephone conversations for nearly a year in Vienna, eventually recording roughly 90, 000 communications. The Berlin tunnel would need to produce even more.

The political context that made the tunnel necessary also made it risky. The Soviet Union had demonstrated its willingness to use force in Berlin. The blockade had been an economic weapon, but there had been armed confrontations as well—harassment of Western convoys, interference with civilian traffic, pressure on the city government. The Western powers had responded with the airlift, a demonstration of logistical capacity and political will. But the underlying tension remained. Any intelligence operation that penetrated Soviet territory risked exposure, and exposure risked escalation. The tunnel would be a violation of the sector boundary, a physical intrusion into territory that the Soviets considered their own. If discovered, it would be a propaganda victory for the Soviet Union and an embarrassment for the Western powers.

The risk was acceptable to the planners because the potential intelligence value was enormous. The Soviet military communications network in Germany carried orders, reports, logistical information, and strategic deliberations. Access to this network would provide insight into Soviet military capabilities, intentions, and concerns. It would reveal how the Soviet high command viewed the situation in Germany, what they feared, what they planned. In a city where the balance of power could shift with a convoy or a communiqué, such information was invaluable. The tunnel was not an isolated gambit but a direct, almost inevitable, product of Berlin’s fractured postwar existence.

Planning for the Berlin tunnel began in earnest in 1953. Operation Gold, as it was designated, was planned jointly by the SIS and the CIA. Initial planning meetings were held at No. 2 Carlton Gardens in London, from which the West German government was excluded due to the ‘highly infiltrated nature’ of their service. The resulting agreement was that the British would provide technical expertise derived from the Vienna operation, while the Americans would supply most of the financing and construct the tunnel (as the closest access point was in their sector). The collaboration reflected the reality of intelligence work in Berlin: the British had experience and local knowledge, the Americans had resources and ambition. Both sides needed the other.

The tunnel itself would be a feat of engineering. Construction began in September 1954 and was completed in eight months. The entrance was located in the American sector at Rudow, disguised as a radar installation. The tunnel extended 1, 476 feet—approximately a quarter-mile—under the border to the Soviet cables near Altglienicke. The excavation required specialized equipment, including a tunnel-boring machine that had to be brought in without attracting attention. The soil conditions were challenging: the water table was high, and the ground was unstable in places. The engineers had to prevent flooding, manage ventilation, and ensure that the tunnel remained hidden from surface observation. The work was done by laborers who were told they were building an underground storage facility.

The taps themselves were installed on three Soviet army telephone cables. The cables carried military communications between Soviet headquarters in East Berlin and military installations throughout East Germany. The tapping equipment recorded conversations on magnetic tape, which was then transported back through the tunnel to the western sector for analysis. The operation produced a vast quantity of intelligence. The recordings captured hundreds of thousands of conversations over nearly a year of operation. The intelligence analysts who processed the material faced a backlog of thousands of hours of tape. The sheer volume of the take was both a measure of success and a practical problem.

The success of the tunnel, however, was shadowed by a fact that the planners did not know. The MI6 officer George Blake had reported the plan to the KGB before the first shovel went in. Blake, a Soviet mole embedded in British intelligence, had been recruited during the Korean War and had been passing information to the Soviets for years. He had been briefed on the tunnel project in its early stages and had immediately informed his handlers.

The KGB decided to let Operation Gold proceed since, in order to attack the tunnel, the Soviets would have to compromise Blake, and they found it preferable to protect their agent. According to a later account, the Soviets ‘valued Blake so much that they feared his exposure more than a breach of their secrets.’ The tunnel would operate, the West would record conversations, but the Soviets would control what was said on the lines.

The decision to let the tunnel proceed was a strategic calculation. The Soviets could have exposed the tunnel at any time, staging a dramatic discovery that would embarrass the Western powers and provide propaganda material. But exposing the tunnel would have revealed that the Soviets had advance knowledge, which would have pointed to a mole in Western intelligence. Protecting Blake was more valuable than preventing the tunnel. Blake was a long-term asset, a penetration of the highest level of British intelligence. His information on other operations, other agents, other secrets was worth more than the embarrassment of a tapped cable. So the Soviets let the tunnel run.

The tunnel operated from May 1955 until April 1956. The Soviets staged its discovery on April 22, 1956, announcing to the world that they had uncovered a Western intelligence operation. The announcement was accompanied by a media event: journalists were invited to view the tunnel, the tapping equipment was displayed, and Soviet officials denounced the operation as a violation of East German sovereignty. The Western powers acknowledged the tunnel but offered no apology. The intelligence take had already been substantial, and the operation was considered a technical success even if its exposure was embarrassing. Only in 1961, when Blake was arrested, tried, and convicted, did Western officials realize that the tunnel had been compromised long before construction began.

The Berlin Tunnel was thus both a triumph and a failure. It was a triumph of engineering, logistics, and technical intelligence collection. It demonstrated that the Western intelligence services could execute a complex operation in hostile territory, maintain secrecy during construction, and produce a significant volume of intelligence. It was a failure of counterintelligence, a demonstration that a mole in the highest levels of Western intelligence could compromise even the most sensitive operations. The Soviets had not prevented the tunnel; they had managed it, using it to protect their asset and potentially to feed disinformation to the West.

The city that produced the tunnel was a city of wire. The physical city—its ruins, its sectors, its checkpoints—was the visible manifestation of the division. But the invisible city—the network of cables that carried military orders, diplomatic communications, and intelligence traffic—was where the real struggle took place. The tunnel was an attempt to penetrate that invisible city, to tap into the nervous system of the Soviet military machine. It succeeded technically, but it failed strategically, because the Soviets knew it was coming and adjusted accordingly.

The Berlin Blockade had established the city as a frontline. The Airlift had established that the Western powers would not retreat. The division of Germany into two states had formalized the split that the blockade had made visible. By 1949, the year the blockade ended, Berlin was no longer a shared administrative zone but a contested space, a western enclave in Soviet territory, a listening post on the edge of the Eastern bloc. The conditions that made the tunnel possible—the concentration of Soviet military communications, the proximity of Western territory, the permeability of the sector boundary—were the conditions that the blockade and its aftermath had created.

The city of wire was thus both the product of political division and the precondition for intelligence operations. The division created the target: the Soviet cables that ran through East Berlin and the surrounding countryside. The division created the opportunity: the Western sectors that provided a base of operations and a route of access. The division created the risk: the possibility of exposure, confrontation, and escalation. The planners of the tunnel understood these conditions and designed an operation that exploited them. They did not know that their operation had been compromised from the start.

The tunnel was not the first intelligence operation to target Soviet communications in Berlin, nor would it be the last. But it was the most ambitious, the most technically sophisticated, and the most thoroughly compromised. It represented a particular approach to intelligence: the belief that technical collection could produce reliable information independent of human sources. The tunnel was a machine for producing truth, a physical apparatus that recorded conversations without the mediation of agents or informants. The recordings were direct evidence, unfiltered by human judgment or deliberate deception. Or so it seemed.

The engineers and intelligence officers who planned the tunnel believed in the value of technical collection. They had seen radio interception produce valuable intelligence during the war. They had seen the Vienna tunnel produce useful information in the early years of the Cold War. They believed that physical access to communications cables would provide a direct line into Soviet military thinking. They were not wrong about the technical capabilities. The tunnel worked as designed. It recorded conversations, it produced intelligence, it functioned without detection for nearly a year. The failure was not in the engineering but in the security. The human element—the mole in the planning process—had undermined the technical achievement.

The discovery of the tunnel in April 1956 ended the operation but did not end the intelligence competition in Berlin. The city remained a frontline, the cables remained a target, and the Western intelligence services continued to seek ways to access Soviet communications. The lesson of the tunnel—that technical collection was vulnerable to human compromise—was not fully absorbed until Blake’s exposure in 1961. By that time, the Berlin Wall had risen, the sector boundary had become a physical barrier, and the conditions that had made the tunnel possible had changed forever.

The Wall, when it came in 1961, changed the nature of the division but not its substance. The invisible frontier of ideology and wire became visible in concrete and barbed wire. The crossings that had once required only a willingness to walk now required documentation, permission, and courage. The telephone lines that had connected the sectors were cut or monitored. The city that had been a laboratory for espionage became a fortress. The tunnel, built in the brief window when the border was administrative rather than physical, could not have been constructed after 1961. It was a product of its moment, a response to the particular conditions of divided Berlin in the decade after the war.

Those conditions had their origin in the administrative collapse of 1948. The walkout from the Kommandatura, the blockade, the airlift—these events established the division that the tunnel would later exploit. The Soviet walkout on June 16, 1948, was recorded in minutes that now sit in archives, a document of failure. The four powers had agreed to govern Berlin together. They had failed. The city they left behind was a city of ruins, a city of sectors, a city of wire.

The Western powers had established their presence in Berlin through the airlift, a demonstration of logistical capacity and political determination. The tunnel was an extension of that presence, a physical intrusion into Soviet territory that sought to establish an intelligence presence as firm as the military presence maintained by the garrisons in the western sectors. The British Garrison Berlin maintained a continuous presence throughout the Cold War, a reminder that the Western commitment to the city was not merely rhetorical. The garrison was a fact on the ground, a military force in a city surrounded by Soviet territory. The tunnel was an extension of that fact, an underground presence that paralleled the above-ground presence of the garrisons.

The Soviet discovery of the tunnel in 1956 was staged for maximum propaganda effect. The timing was deliberate: the Soviets waited until the tunnel had been in operation for nearly a year, until the Western powers had committed resources and personnel, until the intelligence take had demonstrated the operation’s value. Then they exposed it. The decision to wait reflected the calculation that protecting Blake was more important than preventing the tunnel from operating. The tunnel was allowed to run because stopping it would have revealed too much.

The archives do not reveal what the Soviets knew about the intelligence take from the tunnel. They knew the tunnel existed, they knew its location, and they knew its purpose. They could have fed false information through the tapped lines, but doing so would have required coordination across multiple military units and risked inconsistencies that would have revealed Soviet knowledge. Alternatively, they could have simply let the tunnel record genuine conversations, accepting the intelligence loss as the price of protecting Blake. The balance between these options remains unclear. What is clear is that the tunnel operated, the Soviets knew, and Blake remained in place until 1961.

The city that produced the tunnel was a city of ruins, of sectors, of wire. The ruins were the visible legacy of war. The sectors were the administrative legacy of occupation. The wire was the technological legacy of division. The tunnel was a response to all three, an attempt to penetrate the ruins, cross the sectors, and tap the wire. It succeeded in the technical execution and failed in the strategic purpose. The failure was not visible until years later, when Blake’s exposure revealed the compromise. Until then, the tunnel was considered a success, a demonstration of Western intelligence capability in the heart of Soviet-controlled territory.

The success was real, even if compromised. The tunnel produced intelligence on Soviet military operations, force dispositions, and internal deliberations. The recordings captured the voices of Soviet officers discussing matters of genuine interest to Western analysts. The intelligence may have been selectively edited by Soviet counterintelligence, or it may have been largely genuine, accepted as the price of protecting a valuable mole. The question of what the Soviets allowed the West to hear—and what they did not—remains contested. The documents do not provide a definitive answer.

What the documents do provide is a record of the conditions that made the tunnel possible. The Potsdam Agreement established the division. The Soviet walkout from the Kommandatura signaled the collapse of cooperation. The blockade demonstrated the willingness to use pressure. The airlift demonstrated the willingness to resist. The division of Germany into two states formalized the split. The concentration of Soviet military communications in and around Berlin created the target. The proximity of Western territory created the opportunity. The tunnel was the result.

Berlin after the war was a city where truth was sought in wires, not in people. The tunnel was the embodiment of that search, a physical structure dedicated to capturing the conversations that traveled through cables. The search produced intelligence, but it did not produce truth. The truth—that the operation was known to the Soviets from the beginning—was hidden in the human element, in the mole who passed the plans to the KGB. The wires carried conversations. The mole carried secrets. The tunnel captured one and missed the other.

The frontline city that emerged from the blockade presented Western intelligence with both an irresistible target and an engineering challenge. The cables lay just beyond the sector boundary, carrying the Soviet military’s most sensitive communications. The Americans and British had proven in Vienna that such cables could be tapped. The question was whether the operation could be repeated in Berlin, under more dangerous conditions, with greater stakes. The planners believed it could. They would soon find that the greatest risk came not from the border guards above, but from a traitor within their own ranks.
