Chapter 25

The Mole’s Last Transmission

“He showed no visible emotion,” one of the British officers who debriefed George Blake at Wormwood Scrubs later recalled. The remark appears in accounts of the post-trial interrogation program compiled from security service files. The officer described a prisoner who answered questions with what he called a clerk’s precision, narrating his own espionage as though recounting someone else’s career. The date was sometime after May 1961, when Blake had received forty-two years at the Old Bailey, the longest sentence ever imposed on a British spy. The location was the prison where he would remain until his escape. The institutional pressure on the interrogators was specific: they needed a coherent explanation for the central anomaly the Berlin Tunnel had left behind, the eleven months of Soviet silence between the tap’s activation and its staged discovery.

The previous chapter’s engineers had assessed the tunnel as a technical triumph. Their verdict, reached around 1966, refused to address whether the intelligence product itself had been sound. That question had been forced into the open by Blake’s arrest in April 1961 and had remained unresolved through the internal audits that followed. The audits had asked whether the roughly 90, 000 recorded conversations were authentic military communications or whether the KGB had used the known tap to feed manipulated material to the West. The engineers did not answer this question. Blake did.

His account of the KGB’s decision-making, delivered across debriefing sessions during 1961 and 1962, offered a specific and psychologically plausible explanation for the Soviet silence. He told his interrogators that Moscow had allowed the tunnel to operate because shutting it down would have exposed him. His KGB handlers, he said, understood that any premature action against the tap would narrow the circle of suspects to the small group of British officers with access to the joint project’s planning documents. By waiting, by allowing the tunnel to function, by staging its discovery as an accidental find by East German signal troops, the KGB preserved its asset.

The narrative was clean. It resolved the question that had tormented the analysts and engineers who had spent years reviewing the tunnel’s recorded conversations for signs of contamination. And it was delivered by a man whose credibility as a witness was, in the institutional logic of the moment, paradoxically enhanced by his status as a confessed traitor. Blake had nothing left to hide. He was serving forty-two years. He was cooperating. The interrogators accepted his account. Their superiors accepted it. The internal audits that had posed the question could now be closed with an answer that, while damaging, was contained.

The damage was to operational pride. The tunnel had been built, at considerable cost and risk, under the assumption that its existence was secret. It had not been secret. Every conversation recorded during its eleven months of operation had been captured with Soviet knowledge. The intelligence value of those recordings was, at minimum, questionable. But Blake’s testimony offered a specific limit on the damage. If the Soviets had allowed the tunnel to operate solely to protect their agent, then the conversations themselves might still have been genuine military communications, intercepted without the knowledge of the officers speaking. The KGB had not needed to fabricate content. It had simply chosen not to shut the tap.

This distinction was crucial. It separated two scenarios that the internal audits had treated as a single, undifferentiated catastrophe. In the first scenario, the tunnel’s intelligence was poisoned at the source: the KGB had used the known tap to feed fabricated or manipulated conversations to Western analysts. In the second, the tunnel’s intelligence was authentic but compromised: the conversations were real, but the Soviets knew the West was hearing them and could adjust what was said accordingly. Blake’s testimony pointed toward the second scenario and away from the first.

Whether this pointing was conscious cannot be determined from the available documents. What the documents show is that his account, accepted as authoritative, had the effect of channeling the agencies’ reassessment toward a conclusion that served Soviet interests. The conclusion was that the tunnel’s intelligence was authentic but compromised, not fabricated. This conclusion preserved the possibility that the 90, 000 recordings contained real intelligence value. It also preserved the institutional credibility of the analysts who had spent years evaluating those recordings.

The counter-argument is straightforward and must be stated plainly. The tunnel may indeed have been a genuine intelligence achievement whose value was undone only after the fact by Blake’s treachery. Moscow’s primary motive in permitting eleven months of operation may have been, exactly as Blake said, to shield its high-level mole. The recorded conversations may have been authentic military communications, untouched by Soviet manipulation. In this reading, Blake’s testimony was simply true, and its convenience to the institutions that received it was coincidental rather than engineered.

The documents cannot resolve this question. What they show is the sequence: Blake testifies, the testimony resolves the crisis, the resolution serves both Soviet and Western institutional interests, and the alternative reading, that the intelligence was systematically fabricated, drops from active consideration. Whether this sequence represents deliberate KGB design or institutional path dependency cannot be determined from the available record. The effect, however, is traceable.

Parallel to the debriefing sessions in London, William King Harvey was operating in a different theater under pressures that mirrored, in distorted form, the same institutional dynamics. Harvey had moved from the Berlin Tunnel to a new assignment that placed him at the center of American covert operations against Cuba. The CIA’s Cuban campaign was officially authorized on November 30, 1961. Harvey led the agency’s component from Washington. Edward Lansdale, chosen for his counter-insurgency experience in the Philippines during the Hukbalahap Rebellion, led the broader effort from the Defense Department. The operation was suspended on October 30, 1962, but three of ten six-man sabotage teams had already been deployed to Cuba.

Harvey’s trajectory and Blake’s ran in structural parallel. Both men operated within intelligence services that had been shaped by the Berlin Tunnel’s logistics and its aftermath. Both men’s careers had been defined by the tension between technical capability and strategic comprehension. Harvey had built the tunnel’s operational architecture. Blake had betrayed it. Harvey had moved from Berlin to new assignments with the momentum of a man who believed in the engineering of intelligence collection. Blake had moved from Berlin to prison with the compliance of a man who understood that the engineering of intelligence collection was, from the Soviet side, a managed channel.

The connection between these two parallel lines is not causal but structural. Harvey’s work on the Cuban operation represented the same conviction that had driven the tunnel: that technical capability, applied with sufficient ambition and resources, could overcome the problem of denied access. The tunnel had reached under the Berlin border to tap Soviet cables. The Cuban operation reached into Cuba through sabotage teams and covert action. Both operations assumed that the adversary did not know what was being done. Both operations were, in different ways, wrong about this assumption. The Bay of Pigs had demonstrated the limits of covert capability against an adversary who was prepared. The tunnel had demonstrated the limits of technical collection against an adversary who was informed.

Harvey did not participate in Blake’s debriefings. There is no evidence in the available record that he read the transcripts. But the institutional culture that connected his work to the debriefings’ conclusions was the same culture. The CIA’s acceptance of Blake’s narrative about the tunnel, the decision to treat the intelligence as authentic but compromised rather than fabricated, reinforced the agency’s broader confidence in technical collection. If the tunnel’s conversations were real, then the engineering that captured them was sound. The failure was one of source protection, not of collection methodology. The lesson was to guard against moles, not to question the intelligence product.

Harvey could live with that lesson. So could the entire intelligence establishment. And Blake’s testimony delivered it, whether by design or by institutional convenience.

The debriefings continued through 1962. Blake’s accounts of his career, his recruitment in a North Korean prisoner-of-war camp, his handler meetings, and his knowledge of Soviet operations filled files that were circulated between MI6 and the CIA. The process was methodical. The interrogators asked questions. Blake answered. The answers were transcribed, cross-referenced, and filed. The cumulative effect was the construction of a narrative. The narrative had a beginning, in Korea. It had a middle, in Berlin and London. And it had an end, in a cell at Wormwood Scrubs. The end was the point at which Blake’s cooperation closed the book on the tunnel’s central mystery.

The mystery was why the Soviets had waited. Blake’s answer was that they waited to protect him. The answer satisfied because it was specific, plausible, and came from the only source with first-hand knowledge. It also limited the damage. If the Soviets waited to protect a mole, then the intelligence the tunnel collected during those eleven months was not fabricated. It was merely overheard by an adversary who knew it was being overheard. That was a serious compromise, but not the catastrophe that fabricated intelligence would have represented.

The distinction between compromise and fabrication allowed the institutions to function. If the intelligence was fabricated, then every assessment based on the tunnel’s recordings was potentially false. Every strategic judgment that had drawn on those recordings was suspect. The analysts who had evaluated the conversations, the linguists who had translated them, the officers who had written reports based on them, all had been working with poisoned material. That was the epistemological crisis the internal audits had feared. Blake’s testimony, accepted as authoritative, averted it. It replaced fabrication with compromise. It replaced the catastrophic reading with the manageable one.

The mechanism by which this replacement occurred was institutional. The interrogators asked questions shaped by the institutions’ need for a resolution. Blake provided answers shaped by his knowledge of what the institutions needed to hear. Whether this shaping was conscious or unconscious, whether it was directed by the KGB or emerged from the natural convergence of institutional pressure and cooperative testimony, cannot be determined from the available documents. What the documents show is that the resolution Blake provided was the resolution the institutions adopted.

The adoption had consequences. It meant that the tunnel’s intelligence product, rather than being subjected to a systematic audit for signs of fabrication, was treated as authentic but compromised. The audits that had been conducted, which had searched for anomalies in the recorded conversations, could be set aside. Their findings, which had been inconclusive, no longer needed to reach a conclusion. The question of whether the Soviets had used the tunnel to feed specific disinformation to the West could be deferred, then dropped. The institutional memory of the tunnel settled on a narrative that was damaging but survivable.

This narrative held that the tunnel was a technical success whose operational security had been compromised by a mole. The mole had been caught. The tunnel had been discovered. The intelligence it collected was of uncertain value because the Soviets knew about the tap, but it was not fabricated. The recordings were real conversations, even if the Soviets had the opportunity to shape what was said. This narrative allowed the CIA and MI6 to acknowledge the failure without confronting the possibility that the failure was total.

The possibility of total failure, of a tunnel that had been a Soviet instrument from its first planning meeting to its staged discovery, was the possibility that Blake’s testimony foreclosed. It foreclosed it not by disproving it but by providing a more comfortable alternative. The alternative held that the Soviets had been clever but not omniscient. They had protected an asset but had not run a complex deception operation through the tunnel’s tap. They had allowed real conversations to be recorded because shutting the tunnel down would have endangered Blake. That was a story of Soviet pragmatism, not Soviet mastery. The West could live with it.

Blake’s imprisonment and continued communication represented a new phase in the relationship between silence and intelligence. The channel was not the tap under Berlin, which had been dead since April 1956. The channel was Blake himself, whose testimony from his cell was shaping the institutional understanding of what the tap had been. His words were being received, processed, and acted upon by the same intelligence services he had betrayed. The silence was not Blake’s. He was talking. The silence was around the possibility that his talking was itself an operation.

The KGB had run Blake for a decade. They had trained him, directed him, and protected him. They had allowed the tunnel to operate to preserve his cover. When that cover was blown, they had lost their agent. But the agent, even in prison, even in cooperation with his captors, could still serve a purpose. His testimony could shape the way the West understood what had happened. The open questions could be closed with answers that served Soviet interests. The audits could be prevented from reaching the most damaging conclusions. His final transmission from captivity could accomplish what the KGB had accomplished throughout: the management of what the West believed it knew.

The evidence for this reading is circumstantial. No document in the available record states that Blake’s debriefing testimony was prepared by the KGB for this purpose. No memoir by a Soviet officer confirms that the testimony was a deliberate final operation. What the record shows is the convergence: Blake’s testimony resolved the institutional crisis in a way that served Soviet interests, and no alternative explanation for the Soviets’ eleven-month silence was available that served those interests as effectively. The convergence may be coincidental. The convergence may be designed. The documents do not say.

What the documents do show is the sequence of institutional acceptance. Blake testified. The testimony was transcribed. The transcripts were circulated. The narrative was adopted. The audits were closed. The tunnel’s intelligence was reclassified from potentially fabricated to authentic but compromised. The institutions moved on.

By late 1962, the debriefing program had produced what the institutions needed: a coherent account of Blake’s espionage that explained the tunnel’s compromise in terms that did not require a fundamental reassessment of the intelligence product. The account was detailed. Its details were internally consistent. The only available first-hand source had delivered it. And the institutions had accepted it.

The acceptance had a specific institutional consequence. It allowed the CIA and MI6 to frame the tunnel not as a catastrophic intelligence failure but as an unfortunate sacrifice to a higher-priority counter-espionage decision by the KGB. The framing was precise. The tunnel had been compromised because a mole had betrayed it. The mole had been identified, prosecuted, and imprisoned. The intelligence the tunnel collected was authentic but of uncertain value because the Soviets had known about the tap. The uncertainty was manageable. The catastrophe was averted.

This framing depended entirely on Blake’s testimony. Without his account of the KGB’s decision to protect him, the agencies would have been left with the open question that the internal audits had failed to resolve: why had the Soviets waited eleven months? The audits had considered two possibilities, that the Soviets were protecting a source or that they were running a deception operation. Blake’s testimony closed the first possibility and, by closing it, pushed the second into the background. The audits could end. The question could be answered. The institutions could move forward.

The movement was toward closure. The tunnel’s analysis units, which had processed the 90, 000 recorded conversations, were approaching the end of their operational life. The recordings themselves, stored on magnetic tape in American and British facilities, awaited final disposition. The bureaucratic machinery that had sustained the project, the requisitions, the translations, the reports, the assessments, was winding down. What remained was the question of how to close the file. The file could not be closed without a verdict on what the tunnel had been. The verdict that emerged from Blake’s testimony was that the tunnel had been a compromised intelligence operation, not a Soviet deception channel.

The verdict was convenient. The verdict may also have been correct. The two possibilities cannot be distinguished from the available record. What can be said is that the verdict was the one that served both the institutions that issued it and the service that had made the verdict necessary. Blake’s testimony from Wormwood Scrubs provided the foundation. The institutions built on it. The foundation was the word of a man who had lied to his employers for a decade. The institutions accepted his word because they needed it. And because they needed it, they did not ask whether the word itself was the final lie.

The newly institutionalized narrative, that Blake was protected rather than that the intelligence was poisoned, provided the acceptable story. The analysis units could be shuttered. The tapes could be archived. The reports could be filed. The men who had built the tunnel and the men who had run it could move to other assignments. The institution could preserve its confidence in technical collection. The engineered truth, that a quarter-mile tunnel under the Berlin border had captured real Soviet military communications, survived the revelation that the Soviets had known about it from the start. The survival depended on a distinction: between compromise and fabrication, between a mole’s betrayal and a service’s manipulation. Blake’s testimony drew that line. The institutions accepted it. The file could close.

In October 1962, as Harvey’s sabotage teams were operating in Cuba and the world was measuring the hours of the missile crisis, the last of the debriefing transcripts from Wormwood Scrubs was filed and circulated. The transcript contained Blake’s final account of the KGB’s decision-making process regarding the tunnel. The Soviets had known. They had waited. They had protected their agent. The tunnel’s intelligence was authentic but compromised. The file was complete.

A clerk in the CIA’s Berlin Operations Base, the same annex that had once processed the tunnel’s daily take of recorded conversations, received the transcript and placed it in a file marked for closure. The file joined others in a box scheduled for transfer to archival storage. The box was labeled with a project designation that few officers still recognized.

The tapes that had held the tunnel’s recordings sat in an adjacent room, their reels stacked in chronological order, each labeled with a date range in grease pencil. The dates ran from May 1955 to April 1956. Eleven months of conversations, captured at great cost, preserved at institutional expense, and now, by the terms of the narrative that Blake’s testimony had made possible, relegated to the uncertain status of authentic but compromised intelligence.

The box was sealed. The room was locked. The tapes remained in the dark, holding the voices of men who had spoken freely into telephones they believed were secure, recorded by machines they never knew existed, heard by analysts who had believed the voices were unguarded, and now classified by a verdict that said the voices were real but the listening had been known.
