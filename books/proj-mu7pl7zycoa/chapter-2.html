Chapter 2

The Man Who Hated Paper

The memorandum that crossed J. Edgar Hoover’s desk in early 1952 bore the signature of William King Harvey, a former FBI man who had transferred to the Central Intelligence Agency three years earlier. The document itself has not survived in unclassified form, but colleagues who saw it later described its tone as remarkable: a direct challenge to Hoover’s insistence that the FBI retain primacy over all counterintelligence matters within the United States. Harvey argued that the CIA’s foreign intelligence mandate required it to operate independently in certain domestic counterintelligence functions when they touched on overseas operations. Hoover, accustomed to bureaucratic submission, reportedly took the memorandum as a personal affront. The confrontation that followed became legend within the intelligence community. Harvey’s FBI career effectively ended; his CIA career, however, was only beginning to take the shape that would eventually place him in Berlin.

William King Harvey arrived at the CIA in 1947, part of the initial wave of officers who built the agency from the remnants of the wartime Office of Strategic Services. Born in 1915 in Danville, Indiana, he had worked as a lawyer before joining the FBI in 1940. His FBI personnel file, portions of which entered the public record through later congressional inquiries, described him as intelligent, aggressive, and intolerant of what he termed unnecessary procedural obstruction. Colleagues from those years recalled a man who drank hard, worked harder, and harbored a deep skepticism about the value of traditional investigative methods when applied to the new threat of Soviet espionage. The Soviet Union, in Harvey’s view, did not operate like a criminal enterprise; it operated like a state within a state, and catching its agents required methods that fell outside standard police procedure.

The CIA’s early counterintelligence staff gave Harvey room to develop those methods. Assigned to the Office of Special Operations, he worked on cases involving suspected Soviet penetration of American institutions. Declassified agency histories note his involvement in the Venona decrypts, the top-secret program that had begun decoding Soviet cable traffic during the war and continued into the postwar years. The Venona material provided evidence of Soviet spying that could not be used in court without revealing the source, a constraint that frustrated law enforcement professionals but that Harvey saw as an opportunity. If the evidence could not be used to prosecute, it could be used to identify, track, and neutralize. The distinction mattered. Harvey’s approach, as documented in subsequent agency evaluations, favored operational outcomes over legal ones.

This preference placed him on a collision course with Hoover. The FBI director had spent decades building his bureau into the premier domestic intelligence and law enforcement agency in the United States. He viewed the CIA’s creation as an encroachment on territory he considered rightfully his. The National Security Act of 1947 had drawn lines between foreign and domestic intelligence, but those lines blurred in cases involving Soviet espionage, where foreign agents operated on American soil and American citizens sometimes acted as their informants. Harvey’s 1952 memorandum to Hoover asserted that the CIA had not only the right but the obligation to pursue certain counterintelligence leads without FBI involvement, particularly when those leads connected to overseas operations. The specific language of the memorandum remains classified, but the outcome is not: Hoover demanded Harvey’s removal, and he got it. Harvey was transferred out of Washington.

The transfer, however, was not a dismissal. Allen Dulles, who would become CIA director in 1953, had taken note of Harvey’s work. Dulles understood that the emerging Cold War required officers willing to operate in the gray zones between legal and illegal, between diplomacy and sabotage. In December 1953, according to the official CIA history of the Berlin Tunnel operation, Harvey was placed in charge of what would become known as Operation Gold. The operation’s objective was audacious: to dig a tunnel from the American sector of Berlin into the Soviet sector and tap the underground telephone cables that carried Red Army communications. The CIA history notes that the first project was the construction of a warehouse in the Neukölln/Rudow district, with an unconventionally deep basement to serve as the staging area. Harvey had found his calling.

His new posting was Berlin, the divided city that had emerged from the blockade as a frontier of ideology and wire. Harvey arrived in 1952 as the CIA’s base chief, a position that gave him operational control over American intelligence activities in the city. The Berlin Operations Base, known internally as BOB, occupied a compound in the American sector near the headquarters of the U.S. Army’s Berlin Brigade. From this position, Harvey surveyed a landscape unlike any other in the Cold War. The city’s division had created a unique environment: four occupying powers, two German states in formation, and a porous border that allowed intelligence officers from East and West to observe each other at close range. For a man who believed in aggressive technical collection over human intelligence, Berlin was both a challenge and an opportunity.

The challenge was the sheer volume of Soviet activity. The Red Army’s headquarters in Karlshorst, just outside the city center, controlled military operations across East Germany. Its communications passed through underground cables that ran beneath the streets of Berlin, crossing from the Soviet sector into the surrounding countryside. These cables carried orders, reports, personnel matters, and technical specifications, the daily business of an occupying army. Radio intercepts had provided some access to Soviet communications, but after the war the Red Army had shifted much of its sensitive traffic to landlines specifically to avoid eavesdropping. The cables lay buried, protected not by encryption but by physics: you had to physically tap them to hear what they carried.

Harvey’s predecessor in Berlin had explored the possibility of accessing these cables. The British Secret Intelligence Service, which maintained its own Berlin station under Peter Lunn, had experience with cable taps from earlier operations. The idea of digging a tunnel to reach the Soviet cables had been proposed as early as 1951, according to declassified CIA documents, but had stalled due to engineering concerns and interagency wrangling. Harvey’s assignment was to make it happen.

The opportunity was Harvey himself. His personnel file from this period, summarized in the CIA’s in-house history of the operation, described him as forceful and imaginative, willing to take risks. Colleagues used similar language. Richard Helms, who worked with Harvey in Berlin and later became CIA director, recalled him as a man who hated paper, not in the sense that he disliked documentation, but in the sense that he distrusted the bureaucratic habit of substituting memoranda for action. Harvey believed that intelligence work required getting outside the office, outside the law when necessary, and into the physical space where the enemy operated. The tunnel appealed to him precisely because it was a physical solution to a physical problem. The Soviets had buried their secrets underground; Harvey would dig them up.

This operational philosophy had been forged in Washington, in the crucible of the FBI-CIA rivalry and the frustrations of working within a system designed for a different era. The early Cold War presented intelligence officers with a paradox: the Soviet threat was vast, systematic, and patient, while the American response was fragmented, legalistic, and new. The CIA had been created in 1947, the National Security Council in the same year, the FBI had existed since 1908. The institutional cultures clashed. Harvey’s contempt for what he saw as the timid approach of some intelligence work, his own agency’s included, hardened during his years in Washington. By the time he reached Berlin, he had developed a clear preference for what later generations would call black bag operations: burglary, wiretapping, interception, and other methods that fell outside legal boundaries but inside what he considered operational necessity.

The Berlin Tunnel was not, strictly speaking, a black bag operation. It was an engineering project of considerable scale, requiring the excavation of a quarter-mile tunnel from the American sector at Rudow to a point beneath the Soviet sector near Altglienicke, where three cables carrying Soviet military communications ran in a conduit beneath a roadside. The tunnel had to be dug in secret, despite being located in a city swarming with Soviet and East German security forces. The tap had to be installed without detection, and the resulting flood of conversations had to be recorded, transcribed, translated, and analyzed. The logistical challenges were immense. Harvey’s contribution was not engineering expertise, Captain Williamson of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers handled that, but rather the will to push the project forward despite the risks.

The risks were real. If the Soviets discovered the tunnel during construction, the diplomatic consequences would be severe. Berlin’s status as an occupied city, governed by the four wartime allies, meant that any violation of sector boundaries could trigger a crisis. The Soviet Union had already demonstrated its willingness to escalate during the Berlin Blockade of 1948-49. A tunnel under the border might be seen as a provocation, even an act of espionage in peacetime. Harvey’s superiors in Washington were aware of these risks. Some opposed the operation; others supported it cautiously. Harvey pushed.

His pushing took concrete forms. He argued for the operation in meetings with Dulles and other senior officials. He coordinated with the British, whose experience with cable taps in Vienna had demonstrated the technical feasibility of such operations. He secured funding, arranged for equipment, and oversaw the recruitment of personnel. The declassified CIA history of Operation Gold notes that Harvey’s role was central and that his energy and determination kept the project moving through periods of doubt. The same history also notes that Harvey’s relationship with the British station in Berlin, led by Peter Lunn, was cooperative but not without tension. Harvey was an American officer in a joint operation, and his instincts favored American control.

The British, for their part, brought technical expertise that the Americans lacked. The tap equipment used in the tunnel was British-built, a fact acknowledged in most photographs of the operation despite American attempts to claim the project as solely their own. The SIS had developed the technology for accessing cables without cutting them, allowing the tap to listen in without disrupting service. This was crucial: if the Soviets detected a break in their communications, they would investigate. The British equipment allowed the tap to operate undetected, at least in theory. Harvey, who had little patience for technical details he did not understand, deferred to the British on the equipment while insisting on American control of the intelligence product.

This division of labor reflected Harvey’s broader approach to intelligence work. He did not need to know how the tap worked; he needed to know that it would work, and that the resulting intelligence would reach his desk. His distrust of human intelligence, of the traditional spy who recruits sources and runs agents, stemmed from the same impulse. Human sources were unreliable, vulnerable, and often compromised. Technical collection, whether by intercept, photography, or cable tap, produced raw data that could be verified and analyzed. The Berlin Tunnel was, in Harvey’s view, the perfect operation: a technical solution to an intelligence problem, bypassing the messiness of human espionage.

There was, of course, an irony in this view, though Harvey could not have known it at the time.

The tunnel’s planning had been reported to the KGB before the first shovel went in. George Blake, the MI6 officer assigned to the British side of the operation, had been recruited by the Soviets in 1951 and had passed them details of the project during its earliest stages. According to later accounts, including Blake’s own confession at his 1961 trial, he informed his Soviet handlers of the tunnel plan in 1953 or early 1954, while the operation was still being developed.

The KGB, valuing Blake as a high-level source within British intelligence, chose not to expose the tunnel immediately. According to the author of a 2019 book about the operation, the Soviets valued Blake so much that they feared his exposure more than they feared a breach of their secrets. Over nine years of providing information to Moscow, Blake had become too valuable to sacrifice for a single operation.

Harvey knew none of this. What he knew, in late 1953 and early 1954, was that the tunnel project had received approval and that he had been placed in charge. The engineering work would be handled by Williamson and his team; the British would provide the tap equipment; the CIA would handle security on the American side. Harvey’s job was to make it all come together. His method was characteristically direct: he held meetings, issued orders, and demanded results. He drank, argued, and pushed. Colleagues who worked with him in Berlin recalled a man who operated at high intensity, often late into the night, and who had little patience for those who could not keep up.

The Berlin base under Harvey’s command reflected his priorities. He expanded technical collection operations, including photographic reconnaissance and electronic intercept. He pressed for more aggressive surveillance of Soviet and East German facilities. He clashed with officers he considered too cautious, and he championed those who shared his appetite for risk. The base itself, housed in a compound near the American sector’s headquarters, became a center of operational energy. Harvey’s reputation within the CIA grew. Here was an officer who got things done.

But Harvey’s approach had costs. His drinking was legendary, and not always in a positive sense. Some colleagues questioned whether his judgment was impaired at crucial moments; others defended him as a man who could hold his liquor and still function. His confrontational style created enemies, both within the CIA and in other agencies. The FBI never forgot his challenge to Hoover, and Hoover himself reportedly referred to Harvey as that man for the rest of his life. Harvey’s willingness to operate outside legal boundaries made some of his superiors nervous, even as they relied on his results.

The tunnel project crystallized these tensions. On paper, it was a joint operation with the British, requiring careful coordination and shared control. In practice, Harvey pushed for American dominance. The CIA’s official history notes that the question of who would control the product was a subject of ongoing negotiation. Harvey wanted the raw recordings processed through American channels; the British wanted access and input. The compromise, eventually, was a joint processing center where both services would work side by side. But Harvey’s instincts never favored partnership. He trusted his own judgment more than he trusted committees.

This instinct extended to his view of intelligence analysis. Harvey believed that raw intelligence should reach operational officers quickly, before it was filtered through layers of interpretation. The tunnel would produce thousands of hours of recorded conversations, orders, gossip, technical specifications, personal matters. The task of sorting through this material fell to translators and analysts, many of them German-speaking locals hired for the purpose. Harvey’s role was to ensure that the tap worked, that the recordings were made, and that the product reached Washington. What happened after that was, in his view, someone else’s problem.

This division of labor was typical of Harvey’s career. He was an operator, not an analyst. His talent lay in making things happen, in pushing through obstacles, in taking risks that others avoided. The Berlin Tunnel required exactly this kind of talent. The engineering was challenging, the security risks were severe, and the diplomatic consequences of discovery were potentially catastrophic. Harvey’s job was to assess those risks and proceed anyway. He did.

The decision to proceed was not his alone. Dulles and the British SIS leadership approved the operation, and the White House was informed. But Harvey’s advocacy played a role in moving the project from concept to reality. His presence in Berlin gave the operation a champion on the ground, someone who could respond quickly to problems and who had the authority to make decisions. When construction began in 1954, Harvey was there to oversee it.

The construction itself is documented in the CIA’s official history and in subsequent accounts by participants. A warehouse was constructed at the tunnel’s entrance in the American sector, providing cover for the excavation. The tunnel, roughly six feet in diameter, was dug by hand to minimize noise. The soil was removed secretly, a logistical challenge that required careful planning. The British tap equipment was installed on the cables, and the recording began in May 1955. Harvey had achieved his objective.

The recordings, according to later assessments, produced hundreds of thousands of conversations. Translators and analysts worked through the material, extracting intelligence on Soviet military operations, East German politics, and other matters of interest. The tunnel was, by the technical measure of its primary objective, a success. It worked as designed, producing intelligence that would have been otherwise unavailable.

But Harvey’s operational philosophy, his preference for technical collection over human intelligence, contained a blind spot that the tunnel’s success could not address. The KGB knew about the tunnel from the beginning. Blake’s betrayal meant that the Soviets could have exposed the operation at any time, or could have fed false information through the cables, or could have simply monitored what the Americans and British were hearing. The intelligence produced by the tunnel, however voluminous, was compromised from the start.

Harvey did not know this. No one in the American intelligence community knew it, not until Blake’s arrest in 1961. In the meantime, Harvey had moved on. His success with the tunnel burnished his reputation within the CIA, and he was assigned to other high-profile operations. In 1961, according to declassified documents, he was placed in charge of Operation Mongoose, the Kennedy administration’s campaign against Cuba. The operation, co-led by Edward Lansdale of the Defense Department, involved sabotage, propaganda, and other covert actions. Harvey’s role was to oversee the CIA’s contribution, drawing on the same operational philosophy that had driven the Berlin Tunnel: aggressive action, minimal bureaucracy, maximum risk.

The Castro operation was less successful than the tunnel, at least by the measure of its stated objective. Castro remained in power, and the operation’s failures contributed to Harvey’s declining standing within the agency. By the mid-1960s, his career was effectively over. He retired from the CIA in 1966 and died in 1976, at the age of sixty-one. His legacy, such as it is, rests on the Berlin Tunnel, an operation that worked technically but failed strategically, that demonstrated American ingenuity while revealing American vulnerability.

The tunnel itself had a shorter life than Harvey’s career. The Soviets announced their discovery in April 1956, nearly a year after recording began, in a staged revelation that allowed them to protest Western espionage while protecting their source. Blake was still in place, still providing information to Moscow, and the tunnel’s exposure was managed to avoid compromising him. Only when Blake was arrested and convicted did Western officials realize that the operation had been compromised from the start.

Harvey’s role in this story is paradoxical. He was the driving force behind a technically brilliant operation that produced genuine intelligence, and he was the operator who, through no fault of his own, was operating in the dark about the most important fact of all. His distrust of human intelligence, his preference for technical solutions, had led him to a project that was itself betrayed by a human source. The irony is structural, not personal. Harvey did what he was trained to do, what he was good at, and what his superiors asked of him. The result was a monument to American capability and evidence of American blindness.

The documents that record Harvey’s time in Berlin are mostly routine: personnel assignments, budget allocations, operational reports. The memorandum that sparked his confrontation with Hoover has not been released, if it still exists at all. The CIA’s official history of the tunnel, declassified decades after the event, provides a narrative of the operation but little insight into Harvey’s interior life. What remains are the actions: the transfer from Washington, the posting to Berlin, the assumption of control over the tunnel project, the push to make it happen. These actions, documented in files and recalled by colleagues, define Harvey more clearly than any psychological portrait could.

He was, by all accounts, a man of strong opinions and strong drink. He believed in action over analysis, risk over caution, technical collection over human intelligence. He distrusted bureaucracy, resented interference, and operated best when given a clear objective and minimal oversight. The Berlin Tunnel suited him perfectly. It was a problem that could be solved by engineering, a challenge that required determination, and an operation that promised results without the messiness of agent running.

That the results were compromised was not Harvey’s doing. That the operation happened at all was. Without his push, the tunnel might have remained a concept, another good idea that died in committee. Harvey’s contribution was force of will. He made the tunnel real.

The question of whether the Soviets used the tunnel to feed disinformation to the West remains debated. Some scholars argue that the KGB, knowing about the tap, could have planted false information in the cable traffic. Others contend that the volume and routine nature of the communications made such manipulation difficult. The truth may never be fully known. What is known is that the tunnel operated for nearly a year, that it recorded hundreds of thousands of conversations, and that it was blown by a mole within the British service.

Harvey stood at the warehouse in Rudow as 1954 turned into 1955, watching the construction progress. The tunnel would stretch a quarter-mile under the border, reaching toward cables that carried the voices of Soviet officers. Those voices would be captured, recorded, and studied. The operation would run for nearly a year, until the Soviets staged their discovery and the world learned what the Americans and British had done. Harvey, the man who hated paper, had turned a problem into a physical fact. The partnership that made it possible waited just ahead.
