Chapter 17

The Press Conference in Karlshorst

To understand the true purpose of the Cold War’s most famous tunnel, one must look not to its secret life, but to its public death. The most revealing moment occurred after it was dead, when the compound opened its gates to a carefully selected audience. International journalists, summoned on short notice, arrived to find a staged exhibition of Western espionage, with tapping equipment displayed in ordered rows and transcripts laid out for inspection. Soviet officers stood ready to guide the tour. The tunnel, which had spent months extracting secrets from the East, now broadcast Western failure to the world.

The previous chapter traced the technicians’ final walk through the tunnel, their retreat from a space compromised from the start. That walk ended at the western exit in Rudow, in the American sector. This chapter picks up on the eastern side, in the Soviet zone, where the abandoned tap chamber and its contents had been repurposed for exhibition. The engineers walked out on 22 April 1956. By 25 April, the Soviets had transformed the discovery site into a theater. The speed of the conversion suggests preparation. The staging was meticulous.

—-

The invitation list for the press conference reveals its purpose. Correspondents from Western news agencies stood alongside journalists from Soviet-bloc publications. Reuters, Associated Press, and United Press International sent representatives. So did Tass, the Soviet news agency. The mix of Western and Eastern press ensured that the Soviet narrative would reach both domestic and international audiences. Western correspondents, accustomed to covering Cold War events under restriction, found themselves shepherded through a tightly controlled tour. Their movements were guided. Their questions were managed. Their photographs were staged.

The site itself had been prepared for display. The tunnel entrance, excavated from the eastern side, gaped open. Soviet soldiers stood guard at the perimeter. East German border police maintained a cordon around the compound. Journalists were led through the tunnel in groups, their path illuminated by portable lighting. The tapping chamber, where British and American equipment had recorded conversations, now served as the centerpiece of the exhibition. The machines, still in place, bore labels identifying their Western origin. British manufacturers’ names were visible on the recording devices. The equipment had not been removed or concealed. It was displayed as evidence.

The decision to leave the equipment in place deserves attention. In the planning phase, the CIA and MI6 had estimated that the Soviets would cover up any discovery of the Berlin Tunnel, through embarrassment and potential repercussions. This assumption proved wrong. The Soviets did not hide the tunnel. They exhibited it. The choice to display rather than conceal suggests calculation. A cover-up would have protected Western intelligence services from public embarrassment. An exhibition maximized their humiliation. The Soviets chose exposure.

—-

The equipment on display told a story. British-labeled recording machines demonstrated Anglo-American cooperation in the espionage operation. The presence of American personnel during the tunnel’s operation, documented in photographs shown to journalists, implicated the United States directly. The Soviets emphasized the joint nature of Operation Gold. British and American intelligence had worked together to violate East German sovereignty. The equipment labels made this collaboration visible. They also raised a question that would not be answered for five years: why had the British played such a prominent role?

The transcripts displayed alongside the equipment served a specific function. Selected excerpts from intercepted communications were laid out for journalists to examine. The transcripts demonstrated the scope of Western eavesdropping. They also demonstrated Soviet vigilance. The narrative presented to reporters was clear: socialist border security had detected and thwarted Western aggression. The Soviets had discovered the tunnel through diligent surveillance. They had broken in and exposed the operation. The transcripts were evidence of Western intent, not Western success.

The content of the displayed transcripts reinforced this narrative. Conversations between Soviet military headquarters in Zossen and the Soviet embassy in East Berlin had been recorded. Communications between East German and Soviet officials appeared in the intercepts. The West had been listening to military and diplomatic traffic. The transcripts proved aggressive espionage, not defensive intelligence gathering. The Soviets framed the operation as a provocation, a violation of the spirit if not the letter of postwar agreements.

—-

The guided tour followed a prescribed path. Journalists entered through the excavated eastern entrance. They walked through the tunnel, observing its construction. The tunnel’s dimensions, its ventilation system, its rail track for equipment transport—all were pointed out by Soviet officers. The sophistication of the engineering was noted. The Americans had spent significant resources on this operation. The tunnel’s length, approximately 450 meters from the sector boundary, demonstrated Western commitment to penetrating Soviet communications.

The tapping chamber received the most attention. Here, journalists saw the recording equipment in its operational configuration. Cables ran from the tapping points to the recording machines. The setup was explained: Western intelligence had spliced into Soviet military cables and diverted conversations to recording devices. The technical achievement was undeniable. The political implications were emphasized. This was not intelligence gathering in the traditional sense. This was a direct penetration of Soviet military infrastructure, located in territory under Soviet control.

The official statements delivered during the press conference reinforced the visual narrative. Soviet and East German officers addressed the assembled journalists. Their statements followed a consistent line. The tunnel represented Western aggression and technological treachery. Its discovery demonstrated socialist vigilance. The operation violated the principles of peaceful coexistence. The Soviet Union had been the victim of espionage, not the perpetrator.

—-

The Soviet officers who addressed the press presented themselves as defenders of socialist security. Their tone was measured, not triumphant. The discovery of the tunnel was presented as routine border security work. The Soviets had detected suspicious activity, investigated, and uncovered the operation. There was no mention of prior knowledge. There was no hint that the tunnel had been known to Soviet intelligence for nearly two years. The narrative of vigilant discovery was maintained throughout.

The East German officers who spoke emphasized the violation of East German sovereignty. The tunnel had been dug from the American sector, but its destination was East German territory. The cables tapped were on East German soil. The operation represented Western disregard for East German borders and East German authority. This framing served East German domestic purposes. The German Democratic Republic could present itself as the victim of Western espionage, protected by Soviet vigilance. The discovery reinforced the East German government’s legitimacy.

The visual symbolism of the presentation was deliberate. Photographs from the press conference show Soviet officers standing beside Western equipment. The machines are clearly labeled with British manufacturers’ names. The juxtaposition was intentional. Western technology, displayed under Soviet control, demonstrated Western defeat. The equipment that had been designed to extract Soviet secrets now served as evidence of Western failure. The photographs distributed to the press captured this symbolism. They appeared in newspapers across the Soviet bloc and in Western publications as well.

—-

The Western press response to the exhibition was mixed. Some correspondents focused on the engineering achievement. The tunnel was described as a brilliant piece of engineering, a technical feat of significant proportions. The operation demonstrated Western intelligence capabilities, even in its failure. Other correspondents emphasized the diplomatic implications. The Soviet decision to publicize the discovery, rather than handle it quietly through diplomatic channels, represented an escalation. The public exposure of the tunnel would have consequences for East-West relations.

The Soviet decision to stage a press conference rather than issue a diplomatic protest was itself significant. In the planning phase, Western intelligence had assumed that the Soviets would prefer discretion. Public exposure would reveal Soviet vulnerability. The tunnel had operated for nearly a year, undetected—or so the West believed. A public announcement would acknowledge that Soviet communications had been compromised. The CIA and MI6 had calculated that Soviet embarrassment would lead to a cover-up. They had miscalculated.

The Soviets had different calculations. Public exposure served multiple purposes. It humiliated Western intelligence services. It reinforced the narrative of Western aggression. It provided propaganda material for domestic audiences in the Soviet bloc. It also allowed Moscow to control the narrative. By staging the discovery as a triumph of socialist security, the Soviets turned a potential embarrassment into a public relations victory. The tunnel became evidence of Western provocation, not Soviet vulnerability.

—-

The timing of the press conference also served Soviet purposes. The discovery occurred in April 1956, months after Blake had received a transfer out of Berlin. When Blake left, the Soviets were free to discover the tunnel—a fact confirmed by declassified accounts that note his transfer in 1955 allowed them to act without compromising him. The timing was not coincidental. Blake had been the KGB’s source on Operation Gold from its inception in 1953–54, when he reported on it at its very beginning according to source materials. His presence in Berlin had been a constraint. As long as he remained in place, the Soviets could not expose the operation without compromising him. His transfer removed that constraint.

The press conference made no mention of Blake. His role remained concealed. The narrative of vigilant border security, of Soviet detection and exposure, protected the source. The KGB had made a decision early in the tunnel’s planning: they would allow the operation to proceed rather than compromise Blake. The agent was more valuable than the intelligence the tunnel might collect. The press conference was the culmination of that decision. The public exhibition of the tunnel’s discovery concealed the private knowledge that had made it possible.

The staged nature of the discovery was itself staged. The Soviets presented the breakthrough on 21 April as a routine border security operation. East German border guards had detected suspicious activity. Soviet forces had been called in. The tunnel entrance had been excavated and the operation exposed. This narrative, presented to journalists and repeated in official statements, was theater. The Soviets had known about the tunnel since before construction began. The discovery was not detection. It was revelation, timed and managed for maximum effect.

—-

The distinction between detection and revelation matters. Detection implies discovery through vigilance. Revelation implies disclosure by choice. The Soviets chose to reveal the tunnel when it served their purposes. Blake’s transfer removed the constraint on revelation. The press conference provided the mechanism. The narrative of detection protected the source. The reality of revelation served strategic goals. The tunnel’s public exposure was a calculated act, not an accidental discovery.

The equipment displayed at the press conference provides evidence of Soviet intentions. The machines bore British labels. The manufacturers’ names were visible in photographs. The Soviets made no effort to conceal the British origin of the equipment. This visibility served multiple purposes. It implicated the British in the operation, demonstrating Anglo-American cooperation in espionage. It also raised questions that would linger for years. Why had the British played such a prominent role? What did this cooperation reveal about Western intelligence coordination?

The British labels on the equipment would not be fully explained until 1961. Western officials’ understanding that the tunnel had been compromised well before construction began came only after Blake’s arrest, trial, and conviction. The British role in the operation, the prominence of British equipment, the involvement of British personnel—all became legible in retrospect as markers of the operation’s vulnerability. Blake had been posted to the Berlin station in 1953. He had been recruited by the Soviets in 1950. He had reported on the tunnel from its inception. The British equipment on display in Karlshorst was evidence not just of espionage, but of penetration.

—-

The press conference did not reveal this penetration. It concealed it. The narrative of socialist vigilance, of border security triumph, protected the KGB’s most valuable asset. Blake remained in place, transferred but not exposed. His treason would not be discovered for another five years. The press conference in Karlshorst was part of the protection operation. The Soviets sacrificed the intelligence value of the tunnel—the recordings of Soviet military communications—to preserve their agent. The public exhibition was the price of private concealment.

The content of the intercepted communications, recorded over nearly a year of operation, had been substantial. Roughly 90, 000 conversations had been recorded. The Americans had wanted to hear any warlike intentions being discussed by Soviet military leadership. They had listened to telephone conversations between Moscow and the Soviet embassy in East Berlin, between Soviet headquarters at Zossen and field units, between East German and Soviet officials. The West had been unable to break Soviet encryption for the most sensitive communications. Instead, they had exploited a vulnerability: the Soviet military’s reliance on landline cables for routine communications.

The recordings had value. The CIA and SIS processed the intercepts, transcribing and translating the most significant conversations. Intelligence analysts mined the transcripts for insights into Soviet military intentions, organizational structure, and operational security. The tunnel had provided a stream of raw intelligence, unprecedented in volume and quality. The press conference did not mention this intelligence value. The Soviets did not acknowledge what the West had learned. The narrative focused on Western aggression, not Western success.

—-

The absence of any discussion of intelligence value is itself revealing. If the Soviets had been genuinely surprised by the tunnel, they might have speculated about what the West had learned. They might have assessed the damage, evaluated the compromise, calculated the intelligence loss. Instead, they presented the tunnel as an act of aggression, not a source of intelligence. The focus was on the violation, not the information. This framing suggests confidence. The Soviets knew what the tunnel had recorded. They had known all along. The intelligence value was not a surprise. It was a calculated cost.

The calculated cost was acceptable to Moscow. The KGB had decided early on to let Operation Gold proceed. Attacking the tunnel would have required compromising Blake. The Soviets found it preferable to sacrifice some intelligence rather than sacrifice their agent. The press conference was the final stage of this calculation. The tunnel had served its purpose for the West. It had also served its purpose for the Soviets. Now it could be exposed, the West humiliated, the agent protected. The timing was deliberate. The staging was meticulous. The narrative was controlled.

The journalists who attended the press conference filed their reports. The story appeared in newspapers around the world. Western headlines emphasized the tunnel’s engineering and the audacity of the operation. Eastern headlines emphasized Western aggression and socialist vigilance. The narrative split along Cold War lines. But the basic facts were uncontested: a tunnel had been dug from the American sector into East Berlin. British and American intelligence had tapped Soviet military cables. The Soviets had discovered the operation and exposed it publicly. The press conference had done its work.

—-

The diplomatic consequences began immediately. Western governments faced questions about the operation. The United States and Britain had to manage the public embarrassment while assessing the intelligence damage. The Soviet Union and East Germany had scored a propaganda victory. The tunnel, which had been designed to extract secrets, had become a vehicle for broadcasting Western failure. The press conference transformed a clandestine operation into a public relations disaster.

The transformation was deliberate. The Soviets could have handled the discovery quietly. Diplomatic channels existed for such matters. Protests could have been lodged, negotiations conducted, settlements reached. Instead, the Soviets chose publicity. They staged a press conference, invited international journalists, displayed the evidence, and controlled the narrative. The choice reflected calculation, not impulse. The public exposure served Soviet purposes better than private diplomacy would have.

The purposes were multiple. Domestically, the tunnel’s discovery reinforced the narrative of Western threat. Soviet and East German citizens could see evidence of Western espionage. The socialist state had detected and thwarted the operation. The regime’s vigilance protected the people. This narrative served domestic legitimacy. Internationally, the discovery embarrassed Western intelligence services. The CIA and SIS had been caught in a major espionage operation. Their sophisticated technology had been exposed. Their audacity had been punished. This served Soviet prestige in the Cold War competition.

—-

Strategically, the discovery sent a signal. The Soviets had penetrated Western intelligence operations. They had known about the tunnel from the start. They had allowed it to operate, collecting what it collected, until the time was right to expose it. The exposure demonstrated Soviet capabilities. It suggested that Western intelligence operations might be compromised. It planted seeds of doubt and suspicion. The press conference was not just propaganda. It was a strategic communication, designed to achieve multiple effects.

The protection of Blake was the most important effect. The KGB had preserved its agent for five years by the time of the press conference. They would preserve him for another five years after it. The narrative of vigilant border security, of routine detection and exposure, gave no hint of the penetration that had made the tunnel possible. Blake’s name did not appear in any report. His role remained concealed. The press conference was part of a larger operation: not just the exposure of a tunnel, but the protection of a spy.

The exhibition of the tunnel continued after the press conference. The site remained accessible for guided tours. Soviet and East German officials escorted delegations through the tunnel, explaining the operation and its discovery. The tunnel became a propaganda installation, a physical demonstration of Western aggression. The equipment remained in place. The labels remained visible. The narrative remained consistent. The tunnel that had operated in secret now served as a public monument to Western failure.

—-

The transformation of the tunnel from intelligence operation to propaganda installation was complete. The press conference had initiated the transformation. The subsequent tours reinforced it. The tunnel’s physical existence—its location, its construction, its equipment—now served Soviet purposes. The West had built it. The East displayed it. The operation that had been designed to penetrate Soviet secrecy had become a vehicle for Soviet publicity.

The press conference in Karlshorst demonstrated the dual reality of the tunnel. For the West, it had been an intelligence operation, a technical achievement, a source of valuable information. For the East, it had been a known quantity, a calculated risk, a propaganda opportunity. Both realities existed simultaneously. The tunnel was both a genuine intelligence coup and a vehicle for disinformation. The press conference made this duality visible. The equipment on display was real. The transcripts were authentic. The operation had occurred. But the narrative of discovery was false. The Soviets had not detected the tunnel through vigilance. They had revealed it by choice.

The choice to reveal was itself a strategic decision. The Soviets could have continued to let the tunnel operate. They could have fed disinformation through the tapped cables. They could have used the tunnel’s existence as leverage in diplomatic negotiations. Instead, they chose public exposure. The timing—after Blake’s transfer—suggests that the protection of the agent was the primary consideration. The press conference was the mechanism for managing that exposure. The narrative of vigilant discovery concealed the reality of calculated revelation.

—-

The Western intelligence services were left to manage the aftermath. The public exposure created immediate political pressure. Allen Dulles and his British counterparts faced questions about the operation’s planning, its security, and its outcome. The CIA’s internal assessment would have to account for the failure. The operation had been compromised from the start, though the full extent of the compromise remained unknown. The press conference had made the failure public. The intelligence services now had to explain it.

The explanation would take years. Blake’s arrest in 1961 would reveal the full scope of the compromise. The tunnel had been known to the KGB from its inception. The Soviets had allowed it to operate, had controlled what it recorded, had decided when to expose it. The press conference in Karlshorst was the culmination of a long-term KGB operation. The Western intelligence services had been manipulated. The public narrative of Soviet vigilance concealed the private reality of Western penetration.

The headlines appeared the next day. Western newspapers reported the discovery with a mixture of admiration for the engineering and embarrassment for the exposure. The New York Times described the tunnel as an elaborate espionage operation. The Washington Post noted the audacity of the scheme. British papers emphasized the British role, noting the equipment labels and the involvement of SIS. The stories were factual, but the framing varied. Some presented the tunnel as an intelligence failure. Others presented it as a technical success that had been discovered. The split reflected the ambiguity of the operation itself.

—-

The Soviet-bloc press presented a unified narrative. Pravda described the tunnel as evidence of Western provocative activities and espionage aggression. The East German party press emphasized the violation of East German sovereignty and the vigilance of socialist border guards. The narrative was consistent across Soviet-bloc publications: the West had committed an act of espionage, the socialist states had detected and exposed it, the operation demonstrated Western hostility. The press conference had provided the material. The newspapers delivered the message.

The diplomatic fallout began within days. The Soviet Union lodged a formal protest with the United States and Britain. The protest echoed the themes of the press conference: Western aggression, violation of sovereignty, hostile intent. The diplomatic note was delivered through official channels, but its contents were already public. The press conference had preempted the diplomatic process. The Soviets had made their case in the court of public opinion before making it in the corridors of diplomacy.

The Western response was complicated by the public nature of the exposure. Diplomats could not deny what had been publicly displayed. The tunnel existed. The equipment had been photographed. The transcripts had been shown. The facts were established. The Western governments had to acknowledge the operation while managing the embarrassment. Allen Dulles, the director of the CIA, faced questions about the operation’s planning and execution. British intelligence officials faced similar scrutiny. The public exposure had created a political problem as well as an intelligence problem.

—-

The political problem was immediate. The intelligence problem would unfold over time. The Soviets had demonstrated that they could penetrate Western operations. The tunnel had been known to Moscow from the start. What else might be compromised? The question hung over Western intelligence services. The press conference had planted the seed of doubt. The full extent of the compromise—Blake’s role, the duration of the penetration—would not be known for years. But the public exposure of the tunnel suggested vulnerability. The Soviets had turned Western intelligence capabilities against themselves.

The press conference in Karlshorst marked a turning point. The tunnel had been designed to extract Soviet secrets. It ended up broadcasting Western failure. The operation that had produced hundreds of thousands of intercepts concluded with a staged exhibition of Western espionage. The Soviets had controlled the timing, the narrative, and the exposure. They had protected their agent while humiliating their adversaries. The press conference was not merely propaganda. It was a strategic operation, as carefully planned as the tunnel itself.

The strategic operation had multiple audiences. Domestic audiences in the Soviet bloc received a narrative of Western threat and socialist vigilance. International audiences received a demonstration of Western espionage and Soviet capability. Western intelligence services received a message: your operations can be compromised. The KGB received confirmation that their asset remained protected. The multiple messages reinforced each other. The press conference was a single event with multiple effects.

—-

The press conference ended. The journalists departed. The tunnel remained, now a public exhibit. The equipment stood in its ordered rows, British labels visible. The transcripts lay arranged for inspection. The Soviet officers had delivered their statements. The narrative of Western aggression and socialist vigilance had been established. The agent remained protected. The strategic signal had been sent.

The headlines the following morning handed Western intelligence chiefs a problem they had not anticipated. The Soviet protest note arrived at the State Department the same day. Dulles and his counterparts in London would have to explain how an operation of this scale had been exposed so publicly, and why their analysts had believed the Soviets would keep such discoveries quiet.
