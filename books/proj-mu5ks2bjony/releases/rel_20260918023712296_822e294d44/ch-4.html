Chapter 4

The City Above the Narrows

The sugar refinery’s morning whistle cut through the November dark at 6: 15 a. m., and the first shift of the Richmond district began its daily migration toward the water. Men and women emerged from row houses on Roome Street, Creighton Street, and the narrow lanes that ran down to the docks, their breath visible in the cold air, their boots finding the familiar ruts in the unpaved streets. They moved toward the massive stone building that dominated the waterfront, its windows already glowing with gaslight, its smokestacks beginning to feather the dawn with white plumes. The Acadia Sugar Refining Company employed four hundred people in this single plant, and on a typical winter morning in 1917, most of them would pass within two hundred yards of Pier 6 before they reached the time clock.

The refinery stood at the foot of Richmond Street, its back to the slope that rose toward the Halifax peninsula’s interior, its face open to the harbour. This orientation was not accidental. Every aspect of Richmond’s industrial geography had been arranged around the funnel of the port: coal arrived by rail from Cape Breton and Nova Scotia’s inland mines, unloaded at the Intercolonial Railway’s sprawling yards that paralleled the water; raw sugar came in the holds of merchant vessels from the Caribbean and Brazil, hoisted by steam derrick onto the pier; refined product left by the same rail lines, bound for Montreal, Toronto, and the manufacturing cities of New England.

The workforce lived in the spaces that remained—wood-framed houses built in the 1880s and 1890s, two and three stories, pressed close together on small lots, with no zoning ordinance to separate residence from industry, no building code to require fire-resistant construction, no municipal planning department to calculate what might happen if the concentrated energy of the port were ever released in a single moment.

Halifax in the war years was a funnel, and the shape of that funnel determined everything about Richmond’s existence. The British garrison had departed in 1905 and 1906, but the naval presence had returned in a new form: the Royal Canadian Navy, founded in 1910 with its command center at the Halifax Dockyard, and the vastly expanded wartime fleet that by 1917 included patrol ships, tugboats, and minesweepers operating from the same waters where merchant convoys assembled. By 1917, the population of Halifax and Dartmouth had increased to between 60, 000 and 65, 000 people, a figure that included the military personnel transiting through and the workers drawn to the harbour’s expanded operations, but did not fully capture the density of the north-end ward, where multiple families occupied single houses and boarders slept in kitchens and parlours.

The Intercolonial Railway, completed in 1876, had made Halifax a major gateway for Atlantic trade—particularly in winter, when ice rendered the St. Lawrence River ports inaccessible. This geographical advantage, reinforced by the railway’s federal ownership and its deliberate routing through the Maritime provinces, had shaped the city’s industrial development in ways that now concentrated population and vulnerability in the same tight compass. The railway yards at Richmond were not a peripheral facility but a central node: from here, trains departed for the Atlantic ferry at North Sydney, for the industrial centers of central Canada, and for the military staging areas that were multiplying across the country as the war continued.

The morning shift at the refinery included boiler men, who tended the steam engines that powered the crushing and boiling operations; packers, who filled barrels and sacks with the finished granulated product; and the labourers who moved between tasks as directed. The work was heavy and hot, the air in the boiling house thick with sugar dust and steam. Wages ranged from fifteen to thirty dollars per week depending on skill, enough to support a family in modest circumstances but not to accumulate savings or move to a more salubrious neighbourhood. The houses of Richmond were built for rental, owned by small investors and local businessmen who saw no return in improvements. Plumbing was partial, heating dependent on coal stoves, fire protection limited to the municipal hydrants that the Halifax Fire Department maintained with varying reliability.

The fire department itself illustrated the scale of institutional preparation that Richmond could expect. The city maintained several engine companies, with stations distributed across the peninsula, but the equipment was horse-drawn and the water pressure dependent on the municipal reservoir’s elevation. The nearest hospital, the Victoria General, stood more than a mile to the south, across the Citadel hill and down toward the western suburbs. The Marine Hospital, closer to the waterfront, had been converted to military use. For the ordinary injuries of industrial life—burns, crushings, the chronic damage of heavy labor—the workers of Richmond relied on a network of private physicians and, in extremis, the city almshouse. There was no emergency room. There was no ambulance service. There was no municipal department charged with preparing for disasters that had not yet occurred.

The Richmond station of the Intercolonial Railway, where the 45-year-old train dispatcher Vince Coleman and Chief Clerk William Lovett would be working on the morning of 6 December 1917, sat surrounded by these same railway yards, only a few hundred feet from Pier 6. From this small wooden building, Coleman controlled the movement of trains through the congested throat of the harbour district: passenger trains arriving from the west, freight cars being shunted to the piers, coal trains from the eastern mines. The station’s location was determined by the same logic that placed the refinery and the foundry and the cotton mill—proximity to the water, access to the transport network, the compression of functions that wartime demand had accelerated.

Coleman had worked this territory for years. He knew the schedules, the sidings, the dangerous crossings where locomotives nosed across public streets. He knew the ships that tied up at the piers, though his direct concern was the rail traffic they generated. He did not know, on an ordinary morning, what cargo any particular vessel carried, or what hazards might be accumulating in the hold of a ship that passed his window.

The cotton mill, the Nova Scotia Cotton Manufacturing Company, stood a few blocks north of the refinery, its own workforce of three hundred adding to the morning concentration. The Richmond foundry, smaller but equally dependent on the rail and water connections, occupied the ground between. Such concentrations were ordinary for a port city of Halifax’s size; similar districts existed in Saint John, in Portland, in the manufacturing quarters of New England. What distinguished Richmond was not its industry but its adjacency to a harbour that had become, by 1917, the principal North American assembly point for merchant convoys leaving for Britain and France.

The convoy system had transformed the harbour’s rhythm. Where merchant shipping had once arrived and departed on individual schedules, governed by market conditions and seasonal weather, the war had imposed collective movement. Ships gathered in Bedford Basin, the wide inner anchorage that lay beyond the Narrows, waiting for the escort vessels that would shepherd them across the submarine-haunted Atlantic. The basin could hold dozens of vessels simultaneously, and the traffic through the Narrows—the narrow channel that connected this inner pool to the outer harbour and the sea—had increased accordingly. Every convoy meant a pulse of movement: ships entering, ships departing, tugs and pilots and naval craft threading among them.

The harbour master’s office, located in a wooden building at the foot of George Street, recorded these movements in its daily ledger, but the ledger’s neat columns could not capture the density of the traffic, the pressure of wartime schedules, the competing authorities that claimed jurisdiction over the water.

The Royal Canadian Navy controlled the examination service at the harbour mouth, the assignment of anchorages in the basin, the movement of military traffic. The harbour master, a civilian official, maintained the navigation aids, assigned berths in the inner harbour, and enforced the local regulations that governed speed and passing. The pilots, licensed by the Dominion government, boarded incoming vessels and directed their movement through the Narrows, but their authority ended where the master’s began, and the boundary was not always clear. No single authority could see the whole field. No single ledger recorded every decision. The split channel—this condition of overlapping jurisdiction in a narrow waterway—was not a problem to be solved but a fact to be managed, and the management depended on the judgment of individuals working under pressure, with incomplete information, in conditions that the war had made urgent.

Richmond’s population slept above this system, or beside it, or in the houses that climbed the slope from the water. The Acadia Sugar Refinery’s night shift ended at 7: 00 a. m., and the workers who emerged from its doors faced a short walk home or a longer one to the ferry that would carry them to Dartmouth, where housing was cheaper and the land less densely built. The Richmond ferry, operated by the Halifax and Dartmouth Steam Ferry Company, ran every fifteen minutes during peak hours, its wooden vessels—the Halifax, the Dartmouth, the Sir Sandford Fleming—carrying passengers and vehicles across the harbour’s narrowest point. The ferry terminus at the foot of Portland Street placed hundreds of people daily on the Richmond waterfront, waiting, boarding, disembarking, in the routine movements of a working community.

The houses of Richmond were filled with the families of these workers: the sugar refinery employees, the railway men, the dock labourers, the sailors who kept rooms ashore between voyages. Children attended the Richmond School on Roome Street, a two-story wooden building that also served as a polling station and occasional meeting hall. The St. Joseph’s Church on Gottingen Street, at Richmond’s southern edge, served the Catholic population; the Protestant churches were scattered through the neighbourhood, their congregations divided by the fine gradations of denomination and class that structured Halifax society. There were grocery stores, saloons, a few small manufactories of clothing and furniture. The pattern was self-contained, a working-class district with its own institutions and its own boundaries, pressed against the industrial waterfront that gave it employment and that would, on a morning not yet arrived, deliver its destruction.

The city’s prosperity and its vulnerability were the same fact. The docks that made Halifax useful also packed thousands of civilians within a few hundred yards of a harbour in which a fully loaded explosives carrier might at any hour be moored, moving, or holding. Nothing in the city’s ordinary arrangements—its fire department, its hospitals, its charitable societies, its council—was scaled for a mass-casualty event arriving without warning in a single instant.

That absence was not negligence in any simple sense. The city had grown according to the logic of its function, and its function was the rapid movement of goods and people through a narrow channel. Safety, in the modern sense of systematic risk assessment and preventive regulation, had not been part of the design.

The provincial Factory Act governed some industrial hazards; the federal Railway Act others; municipal bylaws addressed fire prevention in a general way. But no authority had calculated what would happen if the concentrated explosives of a munitions ship were released in the heart of the harbour, because such a calculation would have required acknowledging that the harbour’s wartime function made this possibility not remote but structural.

The sugar refinery’s whistle blew again at noon, signalling the lunch break, and the workers who emerged into the November cold could see the harbour’s traffic from the pier. On a typical day, they might observe a tramp steamer being warped into berth, a naval patrol craft returning from the anti-submarine patrol, a cluster of merchant vessels waiting for the tide to enter the basin. The view was ordinary, familiar, unremarkable. The Narrows, that constriction of water between the peninsulas of Halifax and Dartmouth, was visible as a passage between two shores, its limits marked by buoys and range lights, its surface disturbed by the wakes of passing vessels. The workers who watched from the refinery steps did not see a dangerous channel but a working waterway, as familiar as the streets they walked to reach it.

The afternoon shift brought a second pulse of movement through Richmond’s streets, and the evening a third as the refinery’s day workers headed home. The rhythm of industrial time structured the neighbourhood’s existence: the whistles, the time clocks, the ferry schedules, the last departure from the railway station. After dark, the refinery’s windows glowed against the black water, its smokestacks continuing their steady emission, the plant operating through the night on the continuous-process schedule that sugar refining required. The railway yards were never fully still; locomotives shunted cars in the small hours, their headlamps probing the darkness, their bells clanging at crossings. The harbour itself maintained a reduced traffic through the night, vessels entering and departing on the tides, the pilot boats moving to meet them.

The houses of Richmond showed their lights in patterns that corresponded to these schedules: early darkness in the homes of day workers, later hours in those who kept the night shifts. The density of the housing meant that few rooms were fully private; walls were thin, families large, boarders common. The winter of 1917 had been cold, and the coal stoves burned steadily, their chimneys adding to the haze that hung over the district. The municipal services that might have addressed this concentration—sewerage, garbage collection, street lighting—were stretched by the wartime population growth, and the city council’s debates about infrastructure improvement moved slowly through committees and budget constraints.

The council itself met in the City Hall on Grand Parade, a substantial stone building that expressed the municipal ambitions of an earlier generation. The aldermen who gathered there represented wards with sharply different interests: the merchants of the south end, the professional class of the western suburbs, the working-class districts of the north. Richmond’s representative, in the council of 1917, was a hardware merchant who had lived in the district for thirty years and understood its dependence on the harbour’s prosperity. The issues that occupied the council that autumn were typical of wartime municipalities: the price of coal, the adequacy of relief for military families, the regulation of taverns and the suppression of vice. There was no agenda item for harbour safety, no committee on disaster preparedness, no recognition that the concentration of explosives traffic in the Narrows might require special municipal attention.

The provincial government, seated in the same city, was equally absorbed by the war’s demands. The Legislature had adjourned after authorizing additional military expenditure; the Premier, George Henry Murray, had spoken of Nova Scotia’s contribution to the imperial effort and the prosperity that war production had brought to the province’s industries. The Dominion government, in Ottawa, controlled the matters that most directly affected harbour safety: the regulation of shipping, the licensing of pilots, the inspection of vessels. But the Dominion’s attention was fixed on the Atlantic crossing, the submarine threat, the urgent need to maintain the flow of supplies and men to the European theater. The safety of individual ports, in this calculus, was a local matter, to be managed by the harbour authorities and the naval command on the spot.

The split channel was political as well as administrative. No level of government claimed full responsibility for the harbour’s safety, and the wartime emergency had created incentives that pushed against careful regulation. Speed was essential; convoys must sail on schedule; the munitions that filled the Mont-Blanc’s hold were needed in France. The pilots who guided these vessels knew the pressures; the harbour master’s clerks who recorded their movements knew the volume of traffic; the naval officers who assigned the anchorages knew the strategic requirements. What none of them could fully know was how these separate knowledges interacted in the confined space of the Narrows, or what would happen if the system’s tolerance for risk were exceeded.

Richmond’s population lived inside this uncertainty without recognizing it as such. The refinery worker who walked to his morning shift, the railway clerk who checked freight manifests, the mother who sent her children to school with lunch pails—each moved through a landscape shaped by forces they did not control and could not fully perceive. The harbour’s traffic was background noise, the convoy system an abstraction, the specific cargoes of specific ships unknown and unknowable. The split channel was not a concept that would have occurred to them; the risk it embodied was not a subject of daily concern.

Yet the physical facts were there to be seen. The narrowness of the Narrows—less than a quarter of a mile at its tightest point—meant that any large vessel passing through occupied most of the available water. The depth of the channel, scoured by tidal currents, allowed deep-draft ships to pass with margins that were adequate under ordinary conditions but unforgiving of error. The presence of an inbound ship and an outbound ship in the channel simultaneously created a situation that required precise timing, clear communication, and the assumption that both vessels would follow the established rules of the road. The wartime traffic had increased the frequency of such encounters without increasing the space in which they occurred.

The refinery’s evening whistle blew at 6: 00 p. m., and the day shift dispersed into the November dark. The workers who lived in Richmond turned toward their houses; those who commuted from Dartmouth made for the ferry, whose last sailing was timed to accommodate the industrial schedules. The foundry and the cotton mill maintained their own rhythms, their own shifts, their own populations moving through the streets. The railway yards never fully emptied; the locomotives continued their shunting, the signal lamps their communication through the night. The harbour’s traffic, too, persisted: the tide served at midnight, and vessels moved on its schedule, their running lights visible from the shore, their whistles sounding in the channel.

The houses of Richmond settled into the patterns of evening: meals prepared, children put to bed, the small rituals of domestic life in crowded quarters. The coal stoves were banked for the night; the chimneys drew steadily; the temperature dropped toward the freezing point that the forecast predicted. From the upper windows of houses on the slope, the harbour was visible as a darkness interrupted by lights—the anchored ships in the basin, the navigation markers, the occasional moving vessel with its distinctive pattern of illumination. The Narrows themselves were a gap in this darkness, a passage defined by the shore lights of Halifax and Dartmouth converging toward the invisible point of constriction.

The population that slept within reach of this channel numbered in the thousands: the residents of Richmond proper, the workers in the industrial establishments that operated through the night, the sailors in the waterfront boarding houses, the military personnel in the temporary barracks that the war had scattered through the district. They were not prepared for catastrophe because the possibility had not been admitted into the structure of their daily expectations. The fire department maintained its equipment, the hospitals their wards, the council its committees, but all at a scale appropriate to the ordinary accidents of urban life: a building fire, a railway collision, a factory injury. The mass-casualty event, arriving without warning in a single instant, lay outside the horizon of the plausible.

This was not a failure of imagination but a feature of institutional design. The city had grown to serve a function defined by wartime traffic. The funnel of the harbour was an asset to be exploited rather than a problem to be solved. The split channel, overlapping authorities, convoy schedules—these were conditions accepted as part of wartime prosperity. The workers of Richmond lived inside that bargain without knowing it.

The night deepened. The refinery’s windows glowed with the light of the boiling house operations. The railway signal lamps changed from red to green and back, directing movements that continued through the dark hours. The harbour’s traffic, reduced but not stilled, maintained its pulse in the channel that separated the sleeping populations of Halifax and Dartmouth. The Narrows, in this hour, were simply there: a geographical fact, a working waterway, a passage that ships had used for generations and would use again when the tide served and the pilots came aboard.

Richmond lay still within reach of the channel, its population asleep in the houses that crowded toward the water’s edge, its industries maintaining their unceasing operations, its ordinary life proceeding on the assumption that the morning would bring only the familiar pressures of work and weather. The split channel, with all its latent danger, was not visible in the darkness. The funnel of the harbour, with all its concentrated traffic, was not audible in the stillness. The city above the Narrows waited for dawn, unsuspecting, unprotected, and entirely present in the place where the explosion would find it.
