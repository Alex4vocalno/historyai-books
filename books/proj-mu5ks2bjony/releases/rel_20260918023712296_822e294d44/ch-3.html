Chapter 3

The Norwegian Ship Imo and Belgian Relief

She swung her bow into the channel. The Norwegian ship Imo had cleared her pier at 7: 30 a. m., carrying papers that identified her as a vessel of mercy in a war that had made mercy dangerous. Her master, Haakon From, had obtained clearance from the harbour master’s office an hour earlier, an entry in the same ledger that had recorded Mont-Blanc’s expected arrival the day before. Now the Imo made her way against the tide, her engine turning, her pilot translating the rulebook into helm and engine orders, while somewhere behind her the Mont-Blanc crept forward with its own pilot and its own instructions, and the two schedules began their final approach toward one another in the Narrows.

Back in the Netherlands, three days before, the Imo had completed her most recent round of Belgian relief. She had sailed as a charter for the Belgian Relief Commission, her hull empty, her destination New York, where she would load supplies for occupied Belgium. The words “Belgian Relief” painted on her sides constituted both a moral claim and a practical shield. Being neutral, Imo had on her side those words to protect her from German and Austro-Hungarian submarines.

The protection was real but conditional. A U-boat commander who doubted the paint, or who chose to doubt it, could send her to the bottom. The commission’s schedule, meanwhile, admitted no delay. Relief cargoes had to arrive on time or people went without. The occupied population of Belgium numbered in the millions, and the food that kept them alive moved through a narrow pipeline of chartered ships, neutral flags, and negotiated passages through belligerent waters.

The Imo was not a new ship. Built in 1889 as the Runic, she had carried livestock and passengers before conversion to a whaling factory ship in 1912. Renamed Tampican in 1895, Imo in 1912, she would become Guvernøren in 1920. By December 1917 she was a workhorse vessel, 430 feet long, fitted for the particular demands of Arctic whaling—try-pots for rendering blubber, tanks for storing oil, quarters for a crew that processed carcasses at sea.

The Belgian Relief Commission had chartered her not for her design but for her availability. The commission needed hulls, any hulls that could carry grain and dried goods through the submarine zone to Rotterdam. Her previous name, her previous trade, her previous identity as a slaughterer of whales—all of this was irrelevant to the paperwork that now governed her movements. What mattered was her tonnage, her speed, her neutral registry, and the date by which she had to reach her loading port.

She arrived in Halifax on 3 December for neutral inspection, the routine examination that belligerent ports imposed on ships flying the flags of non-combatant nations. The inspection verified what the papers claimed: Norwegian registry, Belgian Relief charter, no contraband cargo, no hidden compartments, no military passengers. The examiners sealed what they had to seal, noted what they had to note, and released her to the inner harbour. She spent two days in Bedford Basin, the protected anchorage where ships waited for convoy assembly or, in her case, for clearance to proceed south. The Basin was full in December 1917—troopships, freighters, colliers, the naval auxiliaries of a port that had become one of the British Royal Navy’s most important bases in North America. The Imo lay among them, a neutral in a belligerent fleet, her crew barred from shore leave by the same neutrality that protected her from torpedoes.

Her business in Halifax was resupply. The Belgian Relief Commission paid for coal, for fresh water, for provisions to carry her crew to New York and back. The commission also paid for haste. Every day in port was a day lost to the schedule, a day when grain rotted in elevators or failed to reach the mouths it was intended for. The commission’s agents in Halifax knew the arithmetic. They negotiated with coal merchants, with chandlers, with the harbour master’s office for the earliest possible departure slot. The Imo’s master, Haakon From, was forty-three years old, a career officer who had worked his way through the Norwegian merchant marine. He understood the pressure. He had accepted the charter knowing its terms. The relief of Belgium was not a speculation; it was a contract with deadlines.

The coal came aboard on 5 December. The loading was slow, manual work in cold weather, buckets swung from the dock to the bunkers by stevedores who worked for piece rates. The Imo needed 800 tons to reach New York with sufficient reserve. The coal merchants delivered it in their own time, their own carts, their own rhythm of labor that answered to no charter and no commission.

From watched the bunkers fill, calculated his departure against the tide tables, and sent his request for clearance to the harbour master’s office. The office received it, entered it in the ledger, assigned a pilot for the morning of the 6th.

The ledger recorded the essentials: ship’s name, master’s name, destination, tonnage, time of requested departure. The clerk who made the entry did not note the charter, the relief mission, the neutral status that made this departure different from the military sailings that filled his pages.

The harbour master’s system treated all vessels as equivalent inputs. A ship was a ship. A channel was a channel. A time was a time.

The pilot assigned to the Imo was William Hayes, a licensed Halifax pilot with seventeen years’ experience in the port. Pilots were the human interface between the rulebook and the water. They boarded incoming vessels at the harbour entrance, took local control from the master, and guided ships through the channel’s shoals and currents. For outbound vessels, they reversed the process, boarding at the anchorage and directing the passage to sea.

The pilot’s authority was practical, not legal. He gave orders to the helmsman and the engine room, but the master retained formal command. The arrangement required cooperation, or at minimum a shared understanding of who would decide what and when.

Hayes had worked with Norwegian masters before. He knew the language barrier, the different signalling conventions, the slight variations in seamanship that distinguished one national school from another. He did not regard these as obstacles. They were conditions of the job, like the tide or the visibility.

Hayes came aboard the Imo at 7: 00 a. m. On 6 December. The ship was ready: bunkers full, hatches secured, crew at stations. From showed him the clearance papers, the destination, the intended route through the Narrows and out past McNabs Island to the open sea. Hayes studied the tide. It was ebbing now, running out toward the Atlantic, which meant that the Imo would be moving against the current as she climbed the channel toward the Basin’s exit. This was the normal condition for outbound departures. Ships timed their movements to enter the Narrows with the flood tide behind them and to leave with the ebb tide opposing them. The opposition slowed the ship but increased the master’s control. A vessel moving with the current in the Narrows could find herself committed to speeds that the channel’s bends and traffic could not accommodate.

Hayes gave his first orders at 7: 20 a. m. The Imo’s engine started. The mooring lines were singled up, then cast off. The ship moved away from the pier, her propeller turning just fast enough to give her steerage way against the ebb. Hayes stood on the bridge with From, watching the channel open before them. The morning was clear, cold, with light wind from the northwest. Visibility extended to the full length of the harbour. The Imo made her initial turn into the stream, her bow coming around to point upstream, toward the Basin, toward the anchored Mont-Blanc, toward the narrowest point where two vessels could not pass.

The Mont-Blanc, meanwhile, had begun her own movement. The French munitions ship had entered the harbour at first light, her pilot guiding her through the anti-submarine net and into the channel. She was bound for Bedford Basin, where she would wait for the convoy assembly that would carry her explosives to Bordeaux. Her cargo was secret, or semi-secret: high explosives, TNT, picric acid, the components of French artillery shells, all of it listed in the manifest that the harbour master’s office had received but not fully understood. The Mont-Blanc’s master knew what he carried. His pilot knew only that he had been assigned to a French freighter with a deep draft and a slow engine, a vessel that would need careful handling in the channel’s tighter turns.

The two ships were now in motion toward one another, separated by approximately four miles of water at 7: 30 a. m. The Imo was climbing against the ebb, making perhaps five knots through the water, less against the ground. The Mont-Blanc was descending with the flood, making perhaps four knots through the water, more against the ground. Their combined closing speed was something less than ten knots, which meant that even at this distance they had less than thirty minutes before they would meet. The meeting point, if both held their courses and speeds, would be somewhere in the Narrows, the kilometre-long stretch between the docks of Halifax and the slopes of Dartmouth where the channel compressed to its minimum width.

The pilots knew the geometry. Hayes on the Imo and the Mont-Blanc’s pilot had both made this passage hundreds of times. They knew the rule that governed two ships meeting in the Narrows: the vessel going against the tide had the right of way, which meant that the Mont-Blanc, descending with the flood, was required to give way to the Imo, climbing against the ebb. They knew also that this rule was frequently modified by practical considerations—by the relative size of the vessels, by their maneuverability, by the presence of anchored ships or cross-channel traffic that constrained their options. The rulebook provided a default. The pilot provided the judgment.

At 8: 00 a. m. The Imo was approaching the middle of the channel, abreast of the naval dockyard on her starboard side. Hayes could see the Mont-Blanc now, a dark shape against the lighter water of the upper harbour, still distant but clearly moving. He assessed her speed, her angle, the available depth on either side of his own ship. The Imo drew twenty-six feet. The channel in the Narrows had a controlling depth of thirty feet at low water, which meant that Hayes had some margin but not unlimited margin. He could not simply sheer off to one side and let the Mont-Blanc pass. The shoals on the Dartmouth shore, the anchored vessels on the Halifax side, the need to maintain steerage way against the ebb—all of these constrained his choices.

The Mont-Blanc was similarly constrained. Her pilot could see the Imo now, a white ship with the black cross of Norway on her stern, moving steadily up-channel. He knew the rule. He knew that his ship was required to give way. But he knew also that the Mont-Blanc was a poor hand at maneuvering. Her engine was slow to answer, her turning circle wide, her draft such that she could not safely enter the shallow water near either shore. The channel in the Narrows was approximately half a kilometre wide at its narrowest point. Two ships of the Mont-Blanc’s and Imo’s size could pass one another, but only with coordination, only with both pilots understanding what the other intended.

The coordination failed. The record preserves what happened next in fragments—in the testimony that Hayes and the Mont-Blanc’s pilot would give to the Wreck Commissioner’s inquiry, in the log entries that survived the explosion, in the accounts of witnesses on the shore who watched the two ships approach one another and fail to pass. The Imo held her course and speed, maintaining her right of way. The Mont-Blanc attempted to give way but misjudged the angle, or the timing, or the Imo’s response. The two ships converged. The pilots exchanged signals—whistle blasts, helm orders, perhaps shouted words across the narrowing gap—that failed to produce a clear understanding of who would do what.

At 8: 30 a. m. The Imo was entering the Narrows proper, the stretch of water between Georges Island and the Dartmouth shore. The Mont-Blanc was descending toward her, still on a course that would bring the two ships into collision if neither altered. Hayes made a decision. He ordered the Imo’s engine stopped, then reversed, the standard maneuver for a vessel that needed to check her way and let another pass ahead. The Imo lost headway against the ebb. She began to drift backward, her stern swinging with the current toward the Halifax shore. The Mont-Blanc, seeing this, attempted to pass ahead of the Imo, to starboard, on the Halifax side of the channel. But the Imo’s swing was wider than expected, or the Mont-Blanc’s turn was sharper than intended. The two ships closed.

The collision occurred at 8: 45 a. m. The Imo’s bow struck the Mont-Blanc’s starboard side, forward of the bridge, at an angle that scraped along the French ship’s hull and opened her forward holds to the air. The impact was not violent by maritime standards. Ships collided in wartime harbours with some regularity, and most collisions produced damage that was repairable, embarrassing, expensive, but not catastrophic. The Imo’s bow was reinforced for ice navigation; it sustained a dent but no breach. The Mont-Blanc’s hull was thinner, her cargo more sensitive. The scrape of metal against metal produced sparks. The sparks fell into the forward hold, where barrels of benzol—high-octane fuel for the French air force—had been loosened by the impact and were leaking their vapours into the confined space.

The Mont-Blanc caught fire. The fire was visible immediately, a pale flame at the forward hatch that darkened and spread as the benzol vapours ignited. The French crew abandoned ship within minutes, lowering boats and rowing for the Dartmouth shore, for the Halifax shore, for anywhere that was not the burning vessel. The pilot went with them, or was taken with them, leaving the Mont-Blanc to drift with the ebb tide toward the Halifax waterfront, a ship on fire in the heart of the harbour, carrying a cargo that none of the abandoning crew had explained to the witnesses who watched from the docks.

The Imo, meanwhile, had lost control. The collision and the immediate reversing of her engine had left her without steerage way. The ebb tide carried her backward, her stern approaching the Halifax shore, her bow swinging across the channel toward the Dartmouth side. Hayes fought to regain control, to get the ship under power and clear of the Narrows, clear of the burning Mont-Blanc, clear of the traffic that was now gathering to watch. The Imo’s propeller bit water, then air, then water again as the ship wallowed in the current. She grounded briefly on the Dartmouth shore, then came free, then grounded again. Her crew worked the engine, the helm, the anchors, anything that might give them purchase against the tide that was carrying them toward disaster.

The Mont-Blanc drifted on. The fire spread aft, toward the holds that contained the TNT and picric acid, toward the deck cargo of benzol barrels that were now themselves burning. The flames were visible from the Richmond railway yards, from the naval dockyard, from the streets of Halifax that climbed the hill above the waterfront. People came to watch. The morning was cold, the fire was warm, the spectacle was unusual. A ship on fire in the harbour was an event, an interruption of the routine, a promise of stories to tell later. The crowd gathered along the waterfront, on the piers, on the slopes of Richmond Street, watching the Mont-Blanc drift toward Pier 6 and the railway yards beyond.

The Imo remained stranded, or nearly stranded, in the Narrows. Hayes had finally managed to anchor her, or to hold her in place with engine and helm, while the Mont-Blanc passed downstream and the fire on the French ship grew from a visible flame to a visible column of smoke to a visible catastrophe in formation. The two ships were now separated by several hundred metres, each damaged, each unable to assist the other, each dependent on the harbour’s resources for rescue or salvage. The harbour master’s office, the naval authorities, the fire departments of Halifax and Dartmouth—all of these would be alerted, would respond, would attempt to contain what the collision had unleashed.

The collision at 8: 45 a. m. Has occurred; two ships are now entangled and one is on fire in the heart of the harbour.
