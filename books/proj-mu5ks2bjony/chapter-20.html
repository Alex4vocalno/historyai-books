Chapter 20

Rebuilding One Street in Richmond

In the winter of 1919, snow still fell on Halifax with a particular weight. Two wooden structures stood on opposite sides of Kaye Street in Richmond, each telling a different story about what reconstruction meant.

On the west side, number 47 Kaye Street rose new from its foundation, a two-storey frame house with a pitched roof and a small front porch. A property owner whose family had held this lot since before the Intercolonial Railway made Halifax a major winter gateway for Atlantic trade supervised the carpenters from the Halifax Relief Commission’s building department. The Commission paid for the materials and the labor. The owner paid nothing.

His old house, a wooden duplex built in the 1890s, had vanished in the fire that followed the explosion at 9:04 on the morning of 6 December 1917. The shock wave had thrown his daughter through a window; she survived with scars. His claim, filed in January 1918, had moved through the Commission’s assessment process with the steady momentum reserved for owners of clear title. Now, in February 1919, he watched the new house take shape and knew that his name would appear in the Commission’s ledgers as a success: property restored, family rehoused, Richmond rebuilt.

Across the street, on the east side of Kaye, a different structure occupied the muddy ground. The relief shack at number 52 measured sixteen by twenty feet, built from rough lumber and tar paper, with a stovepipe punching through the roof. A widow lived there with her four children.

She paid rent—three dollars monthly—to the Commission’s housing department. Her husband, a labourer at the dockyard, had died in the explosion; she had identified his body by the buttons on his vest. The family had rented their previous home on this same block, a flat in a three-storey tenement that no longer existed. The Commission’s survey of damage, completed in early 1918, had recorded the loss of the building but not of her tenancy.

She received a temporary shelter allowance, then this shack, then a series of notices explaining that permanent reconstruction priority went to owners and to tenants who could demonstrate substantial equity in their lost housing. She had no equity. She had four children and a stove that smoked.

The two structures, one rising and one settling into the mud, stood two hundred feet apart. They shared the same street, the same explosion, the same administrative apparatus. They did not share the same future.

—-

The Halifax Relief Commission, established by federal order-in-council on 22 January 1918 and incorporated provincially with broad powers, had inherited a landscape of ruin and a problem of categories. The North End neighbourhood of Richmond, considered working-class and largely unpaved even before the explosion, had borne the brunt of the blast. The Commission’s mandate covered medical relief, pensions, and reconstruction. Its reconstruction programme, administered by a committee under Colonel Robert Low, faced immediate pressure: by late January 1918, around 5, 000 people remained without shelter. The Commission erected temporary housing—relief shacks—while planning permanent reconstruction. The planning took longer than anyone had promised.

The Commission’s records, preserved in the rebuilding papers that tracked each lot through assessment to resolution, reveal a sorting mechanism at work. Property owners received new houses built at Commission expense on their cleared lots. The Commission held title during construction, then transferred the property back to the owner, often with a mortgage arrangement. Tenants received temporary shelter, then displacement. The distinction appeared in the files as a technical matter: owners had lost capital; tenants had lost only housing. That technical distinction produced a streetscape of restored ownership and temporary tenancy, of permanence for some and precarity for others.

Kaye Street, running north-south through the heart of Richmond’s devastation, became a corridor of this sorting. Before the explosion, the street had mixed character: wooden tenements and small houses, some owner-occupied, many rented, a few operated as boarding houses for dockyard workers and railway men. The Richmond Railway Yards and station, destroyed in the blast with the loss of 55 railway workers and over 500 railway cars, had employed many of the street’s residents. The North Street Station, one of Canada’s busiest, stood nearby. The neighbourhood’s economy depended on these institutions; its housing stock reflected their wages.

After the explosion, the Commission’s surveyors mapped each lot. They recorded damage categories: total destruction, major damage, repairable. They noted ownership status from land registry records. They interviewed claimants. The resulting files, thousands of them, became the basis for reconstruction decisions. On Kaye Street, the pattern emerged quickly. Owners with clear title moved toward new construction. Tenants with no property interest moved toward temporary accommodation and, eventually, toward the margins of the rebuilt district.

—-

The file for 47 Kaye Street progressed through the system with the efficiency that came from uncomplicated documentation. The owner held lot 47 outright. His pre-explosion house, assessed at $2, 400, was insured for $1, 800. The Commission’s assessor, visiting the cleared site in March 1918, noted that nothing remained but foundation stones and debris. The owner submitted his claim for full replacement value. The Commission’s policy, emerging from early cases, provided new construction for owners up to assessed value, with insurance proceeds deducted. The house at 47 Kaye, completed in February 1919, cost the Commission $2, 600 to build. The owner received title upon completion, with a mortgage of $800 representing the insurance deduction. His monthly payment to the Commission was $12. His daughter’s scars were not listed in the file.

The construction itself followed standard plans developed by the Commission’s architects. The Type A design, used for this house and hundreds like it, provided four rooms on two floors, a kitchen extension, and a small porch. The materials, local spruce, Nova Scotia pine, and hardware from Saint John suppliers, were purchased through Commission contracts. The labor came from a rotating crew of carpenters employed directly by the Commission’s building department. The house rose in six weeks. The owner, watching from the sidewalk, saw his property restored and his status confirmed.

Across the street, the file for 52 Kaye followed a different path. The widow’s husband’s death entitled her to a pension from the Commission’s separate pension administration: $15 monthly for a widow with children, plus $3 for each child under sixteen. This pension, established by the Commission’s enabling legislation, was among the most generous in Canada for industrial disaster. It did not, however, confer housing priority. Her claim for reconstruction assistance noted her tenancy at 52 Kaye Street, her husband’s employment at the dockyard, her four dependent children. The Commission’s housing officer, reviewing the file in April 1918, classified her as temporary accommodation only, with no equity in destroyed property.

The relief shack erected for this family in May 1918 was one of hundreds built across Richmond that spring. The Commission’s building department constructed them rapidly, using standardized dimensions and minimal materials. They were intended to last two winters. By February 1919, when the new house at 47 Kaye was being painted, the shack at 52 Kaye showed the strain: tar paper peeling at the corners, the plank floor warping from moisture, the stove requiring constant attention to keep the single room habitable.

The widow had received notice that permanent reconstruction would not include rental housing on her lot; the Commission planned to sell the cleared site to an owner-builder. Her options, explained in a letter from the housing department, were to remain in the shack until it was dismantled, to apply for admission to one of the Commission’s workingmen’s homes in a different part of the North End, or to find private rental elsewhere.

—-

The divergence between 47 and 52 Kaye Street represented a larger pattern. The Commission’s reconstruction programme, examined through its ledgers and building records, prioritized property restoration over housing security. This priority reflected both practical constraints and underlying assumptions. Practically, the Commission could leverage its expenditures through mortgage arrangements with owners, recovering costs over time while returning taxable property to the city’s rolls. Assumptions about stability, respectability, and deservedness shaped who qualified for this leverage. Owners were presumed to represent permanence; tenants, transience. The presumption ignored the actual stability of Richmond’s pre-explosion rental market, where families had occupied the same tenements for decades. It also ignored the explosion’s particular destruction of rental housing stock, which had concentrated in the most heavily damaged areas.

The street itself became an argument about these priorities. As construction progressed through 1919 and into 1920, Kaye Street developed a visible topography of recovery. On the west side, where ownership had predominated before the explosion, new houses appeared in steady sequence: 43, 45, 47, 51, each with similar dimensions, similar materials, similar porches facing the street. On the east side, where rental tenements had dominated, the landscape remained irregular: cleared lots awaiting sale, relief shacks in various states of repair, a few private rebuilding efforts by small landlords who had managed to secure Commission loans. The street’s pre-explosion social geography, largely erased by the blast, was being reconstructed with sharper boundaries.

Colonel Robert Low’s reconstruction committee had envisaged a planned neighbourhood, with wider streets, improved sanitation, and better housing standards. Some of this vision materialized: Kaye Street itself was widened by eight feet, the houses on the west side set back from their original line. But planning encountered the existing property grid, the Commission’s financial constraints, and the pressure to rehouse families quickly. The result was compromise: improved infrastructure in some blocks, patchwork recovery in others, and a systematic preference for owner-occupancy that reshaped who could live in Richmond.

—-

The people who moved through this process experienced it as a series of specific encounters with Commission authority. For the owner at 47 Kaye, these encounters confirmed his standing. The assessor who visited his lot treated him as a partner in reconstruction. The building foreman consulted him on minor variations to the standard plan. The housing officer who processed his final papers addressed him formally and shook his hand. His file, preserved in the Commission’s records, contains no notes of dispute or delay. The new house became the physical evidence of his successful navigation.

For the widow at 52 Kaye, the encounters produced different documentation. Her pension file, separate from her housing file, recorded her children’s births, her husband’s dockyard employment, her own character references from a priest and a neighbour. These papers established her deservingness for financial support. Her housing file recorded her lack of property, her temporary status, her eventual notice to vacate. The two files ran parallel without connecting: she could be supported as a widow without being housed as a resident. The relief shack, never intended to last, became the physical form of this administrative distinction.

Her children attended school at the temporary Richmond School, housed in a Commission-built structure on a nearby street. The pension payments, arriving monthly, kept them fed and clothed. The family’s presence on Kaye Street, however, had an expiration date. In March 1920, the Commission sold the lot at 52 Kaye Street to a private buyer, a merchant from the South End who planned to build rental housing for a better class of tenants. The widow received thirty days’ notice. Her file closes with a notation that she removed to private accommodation, address unknown.

—-

The reconstruction of Kaye Street, examined through these parallel experiences, reveals how physical rebuilding carried social judgment. The Commission’s programme was not simply a response to disaster damage; it was an intervention in the neighbourhood’s future shape. The decision to prioritize owner-occupancy, to fund new construction for property holders while providing only temporary shelter for tenants, reflected assumptions about who belonged in Richmond and who did not. These assumptions were not stated as policy. They emerged through thousands of individual file decisions, through the classification of claims, through the allocation of building resources.

The street that emerged from this process was different from the one that had existed before. The new houses on the west side of Kaye, built to Commission standards, were larger and more durable than the tenements they replaced. They housed fewer people per structure. The families who occupied them, some returning Richmond residents and some newcomers attracted by the rebuilt housing stock, had different relationships to the neighbourhood’s institutions. The dockyard and the railway, still major employers, no longer dominated the street’s social composition. The new Richmond, taking shape lot by lot, was becoming a neighbourhood of stable property holders rather than of industrial workers in rental housing.

This transformation was not unique to Kaye Street. Across Richmond, the Commission’s records show similar patterns: priority to owners, displacement of tenants, a rebuilt environment that sorted residents by property status. The process was experienced as individual fortune or misfortune, satisfaction for some and uncertainty for others, but it operated as system. The explosion had destroyed a neighbourhood; reconstruction determined who would live in the new one.

—-

By late 1920, the visible work of rebuilding was largely complete on Kaye Street. The new houses stood painted and occupied. The relief shacks had been dismantled or removed. The widened street, paved for the first time, carried electric streetcars to the reconstructed North Street Station. The Richmond that had existed before December 1917 was gone in fact and was fading in memory. The Richmond that replaced it, planned and built through the Commission’s programme, bore the marks of its origins: better construction, improved services, and a social composition that reflected the priorities embedded in reconstruction decisions.

At 47 Kaye Street, the owner lived among neighbours who had navigated the same system successfully. His daughter, grown now, would marry and move to a house on a nearby street, also Commission-built, also owner-occupied. The family remained in Richmond for two generations, their presence evidence of the stability that property restoration could provide. The Commission’s mortgage was paid off in 1934; the house became fully theirs.

The widow’s trace in the records ends with her removal from 52 Kaye Street. The pension continued until her youngest child reached sixteen, then ceased. The lot where her shack had stood carried a new building by 1921, a three-storey apartment house with six units, larger and more expensive than the tenement that had preceded it. The merchant owner had secured a Commission loan for construction, repaid over fifteen years. The tenants who moved in were not, by the owner’s design, families like the one that had lived in the relief shack.

—-

The reconstruction of Richmond, seen through one street, demonstrates how disaster recovery produces new social arrangements. The Halifax Relief Commission’s programme was not designed to exclude the poor or the propertyless; its pension system was notably generous, its medical care extensive. But its approach to housing reconstruction embedded assumptions about property, stability, and deservedness that shaped who could return to the rebuilt neighbourhood. The physical city that emerged from this process, the houses, the streets, the lots, carried these decisions in its form.

The street has been rebuilt, but according to a plan that sorted its people; this new spatial order now demands a final look back at the harbour to see if its rules were similarly remade.
