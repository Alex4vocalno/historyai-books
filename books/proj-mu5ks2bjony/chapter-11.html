Chapter 11

Soldiers and Sailors Search the Ruins (Afternoon, 6 December 1917)

The launch from HMCS Niobe cut through water littered with burning splinters and bodies. The time was 9:15. The patrol ship had swung at her anchor when the blast wave passed through the harbour, and now her crew pulled toward the Richmond shore where people struggled through rubble that mapped the blast’s exact trajectory. They transported injured toward any available aid in a municipality still incapable of coordinated action.

The sailors found men and women clinging to spars, to floating doors, to the bodies of horses that had been swept into the harbour by the tsunami. They hauled survivors aboard and brought them to the dockyard steps, where a receiving station began to operate without waiting for any health officer’s permission. The template was being drawn in real time: naval personnel would handle transport and logistics, army units would manage the heavy labor of clearing debris, and whatever standing structures remained (churches, schools, surviving private homes) would be requisitioned as wards by whoever held the keys.

The port of Halifax had been a military installation long before it served as a civilian municipality. The British garrison had finally departed in 1906, leaving stone barracks and waterfront fortifications to the Dominion government. In 1910, when the Royal Canadian Navy came into formal existence, the Halifax Dockyard became its command center, a status that concentrated sailors, stores, and signals on the harbour’s western shore. By 1917, the fleet had grown to include patrol ships, tugboats, and minesweepers, their crews living within walking distance of the piers. The harbour itself, one of the world’s largest and deepest ice-free natural harbours, had become a critical wartime assembly point. Merchant ships gathered in Bedford Basin on the northwestern end, protected by anti-submarine nets, to form convoys carrying men and supplies to Europe.

When the Mont-Blanc’s cargo detonated at 9:04 that morning, the explosion had not discriminated between naval and civilian property; it had simply erased the north end’s mixed neighbourhood of dockyards, foundries, and row houses. But the military infrastructure survived with its hierarchy intact, and in the absence of civic government, that hierarchy began issuing instructions.

Among the ruins of the Richmond shore, soldiers from the Halifax garrison had already begun forming a bucket brigade where a fire hose should have been, passing wooden pails hand to hand toward a smoldering timber pile that had been a warehouse. The men wore khaki uniforms caked with grey plaster dust. They worked without orders from the city because the city’s chain of command had snapped at its first link; City Hall stood cracked and empty, its clock stopped at nine minutes past nine, and the police barracks had lost its roof. The soldiers answered instead to the dockyard, and the dockyard answered to the naval command, and this vertical line of authority would shape the afternoon more than any municipal plan.

By ten o’clock, the dockyard workers had joined the soldiers. Civilian employees of the naval establishment, they knew the weight of a crowbar and the leverage required to shift a ship’s timber. They moved into the ruins not as a formal rescue squad but as a labor force accustomed to taking direction from naval officers. They worked in pairs, clearing doorways by hand, lifting beams that had pinned occupants beneath them. The work was silent except for the groan of shifting wreckage and the occasional cry of discovery. When they found a survivor, they carried the body toward the harbour, where the slope of the land made a natural collecting point. When they found only remains, they set them aside in blankets taken from the dockyard stores, establishing the first informal morgue on the grass near the water’s edge.

The American steamship Old Colony had been riding at anchor in the harbour for repairs when the explosion occurred. She suffered little damage from the blast wave; her steel hull and distance from the shore had protected her, and by noon her master had offered the vessel to the naval command. The ship was quickly converted to serve as a floating hospital, her cargo holds emptied of coal and ballast, her decks cleared for stretchers. This conversion required no civilian harbour board meeting; the dockyard superintendent simply sent a launch with an order, and the ship’s crew began rigging awnings over her foredeck.

The Old Colony would remain at anchor for days, receiving the wounded who could not fit into the shattered buildings ashore. Her presence established a precedent: relief would come from military and naval resources first, from chartered vessels second, and from the city’s own disrupted institutions only later, if at all.

At half past two, the USS Von Steuben arrived from the harbour mouth, her grey hull cutting through the smoke that still hung over the Narrows. She had been steaming toward Halifax when the explosion occurred, and her captain had made full speed once the column of smoke became visible on the horizon. The American cruiser carried medical staff, blankets, and stores that the Halifax naval command had not requested because their own signals office had been damaged. The Von Steuben anchored near the Old Colony and began sending boats ashore with supplies, adding a third layer to the improvised relief: Canadian naval organization, Canadian army labor, and now American naval materiel. The lines between national jurisdictions blurred in the afternoon light; what mattered was the capacity to move goods from ship to shore without the delay of customs inspection or diplomatic protocol.

The garrison soldiers worked in sectors that no map had defined. They simply walked until they found an unclaimed pile of rubble, then began to dig. By early afternoon, these informal territories had stabilized: one group worked the area near the railway roundhouse, another concentrated on the devastated streets between Gottingen and Robie, a third moved toward the Richmond printing plant where the Acadian Recorder had published its morning edition. The soldiers marked their progress by the clothing they found—a boot here, a uniform tunic there—and by the bodies they extracted. They had no body bags; they used tarpaulins from the dockyard. They had no tags for identification; they used discarded shipping labels from the nearby piers, writing names in pencil when they knew them, physical descriptions when they did not.

In the absence of the city’s police force, which had been scattered by the collapse of its barracks and the deaths of several constables, the military also assumed control of documentation. Clerks from the dockyard office, men who had spent their mornings recording coal tonnages and ship movements, were now seated at tables in the surviving wings of the Naval Hospital. They began compiling lists. They wrote down names whispered by survivors, descriptions of the missing provided by frantic relatives who walked from the south end to the dockyard gates, and the identifying marks taken from bodies brought in by the bucket brigades. These lists were not official death registers; they were working documents, scraps of paper that would later be consolidated into the formal records of the Halifax Relief Commission. But their origin mattered. The first accounting of the dead was a naval administrative procedure, not a civic one.

The chaplains appeared in the early afternoon, walking among the improvised wards that had sprung up in any building with walls and a roof. The Methodist church on Barrington Street became a surgical station; its pews were pushed aside, and its communion cloths were torn into bandages. The naval chaplains moved between these spaces, assisting the medical officers who had come ashore from the ships. They did not preach; they held cups of water to cracked lips and wrote letters home for men who could not hold pens. Their presence reinforced the military character of the relief; the spiritual care of the dying was being administered by commissioned officers in uniform, not by civilian clergy responding to a call from their denominations.

By three o’clock, the temperature had begun to drop. The sun, visible through the smoke as a pale disc, offered no warmth. The rescuers, sweating from their labor in the ruins, felt the cold settle into their damp uniforms. The bucket brigades slowed as ice formed on the pails. The clerks at the dockyard tables found their fingers stiffening around their pencils. The wounded lying on the decks of the Old Colony began to shiver beneath their blankets. The physical limits of the improvised effort were becoming apparent; muscle and urgency could clear rubble, but they could not generate heat or light once the December afternoon began to fade.

The sectors of search had by now acquired a brutal efficiency. The soldiers and dockyard workers knew which piles had already been checked for survivors and which still held the possibility of a voice beneath the boards. They worked by the sound of tapping—one man would stand still while another struck a pipe or a beam with a stone, listening for a human response. They had no dogs trained for rescue, no acoustic equipment, no lifting machinery except their own backs and the occasional block-and-tackle borrowed from a damaged derrick. What they had was numbers: the garrison could field several hundred men, the dockyard another hundred, and this mass of labor could move debris by hand faster than any machine that could have been brought into the narrow streets.

The lists grew longer. By four o’clock, the clerks had filled pages with names that would later be transcribed into the official Remembrance Book. They recorded a soldier from the 40th Battalion found near the railway tracks, a dockworker pulled from the ruins of the sugar refinery, a child recovered from what had been a bedroom on Duffus Street. The descriptions became more fragmentary as the afternoon wore on: female, brown hair, blue dress; male, approximately thirty years, scar on left hand. The clerks used the same ledgers they had employed for recording naval stores; the format of columns for item, quantity, and condition lent itself grimly to cataloguing human remains. This bureaucratic continuity would persist for decades, as the Halifax Relief Commission later used similar accounting methods to track the pensions paid to survivors.

The medical improvisation followed the same pattern of military priority. The Naval Hospital, though damaged, had retained its surgical theater and its stock of antiseptics. The army medical corps set up triage on the lawn outside, sorting the wounded into categories that determined whether they would be treated immediately, sent to the Old Colony, or moved to the army base at Wellington Barracks in the south end. The decision-making was rapid and unsentimental; it assumed that resources were scarce and that authority flowed from naval command downward. Civilians participated—nurses from the civilian hospitals that still stood, volunteers from the undamaged neighbourhoods—but they worked within a structure that had been established by uniformed officers in the first hour after the blast.

The dockyard workers brought to the ruins a grammar of heavy labor learned from shipbuilding: they understood how weight distributed across three points could be lifted by four men, how a jack improvised from a capstan bar might shift a beam that had crushed a staircase, how to listen for the hollow sound of a void beneath rubble rather than the dense thud of compacted debris. They worked with the tools of their trade—marlinspikes, serving mallets, rigging knives—now repurposed to pry apart splintered frames. Their hands, calloused from hemp and iron, proved better suited to rescue than gloved hands; they gripped splintered two-by-fours without flinching, felt through frozen gloves whether a timber was load-bearing or merely resting.

Moving deeper into the wreckage, they encountered the explosion’s particular geography: walls fallen outward created protective pockets where survivors huddled, while in other places the blast drove structures straight down into cellars, compacting three stories into a mass no taller than a man’s waist. The workers recognized these patterns from harbour salvage—compression, shear, the strange preservation of fragile objects amid destruction—and applied this logic to human recovery, learning to dig toward spaces where air might remain.

The afternoon air carried a metallic taste, a compound of vaporized steel and chemical residue that lingered in coat fibers. Men found their throats burning after an hour; they tore strips from shirts to bind across their noses, creating makeshift respirators that turned grey with dust. The soundscape shifted as the roar of fires gave way to a more terrible quiet, broken by intermittent wall collapses and the rhythmic tapping of search parties striking metal. In this acoustic environment, rescuers began to comprehend the full scope: a soldier digging near Campbell Road might uncover a schoolbook, then a desk, then crushed forms beneath it, realizing that the mound assumed to be a warehouse contained an entire classroom.

The realization spread through the sectors not by word but by posture—men who had worked with frantic energy at noon moved with the care of grave-diggers by two o’clock, understanding they were no longer extracting survivors from an accident but recovering a community from the earth.

Aboard the Old Colony, conversion from collier to hospital required different improvisation than shore parties faced. The holds, designed for coal bunkers, had to be ventilated to clear gas before receiving patients; crewmen rigged canvas chutes to funnel air downward while others hacked away hatchway coamings to allow stretchers to pass. The ship’s master, working under the dockyard superintendent’s verbal orders, requisitioned the vessel’s own stores (blankets meant for North Atlantic crossings, spirits from the medical locker) without consulting the shipping agent. The main deck became a surgical theater: the carpenter tore up planking to create drainage for blood and antiseptic, while engineers diverted steam from boilers to heat water for sterilization. This was not medicine as practiced in civilian hospitals; it was naval surgery stripped to essentials, where rank determined who held the scalpel and the ship’s bell marked triage hours.

The Von Steuben’s arrival introduced American naval stores requisitioned under emergency protocols. Her medical staff brought morphine supplies that dwarfed what remained in Halifax’s shattered pharmacies, and her crew transferred these using a breeches buoy rigged between the cruiser’s deck and the Old Colony’s rail, requiring precise coordination between American seamen and Canadian riggers. The supplies came ashore not through the customs shed—that building lay in ruins—but directly onto the Naval Hospital lawn, where American pharmacists and Canadian medical officers established a joint distribution point without formalizing authority in writing. This operation meant a wounded stevedore might receive American morphine administered by a Canadian surgeon in a church ward stocked with British supplies from the garrison, the paperwork beginning with naval inventory sheets.

By mid-afternoon, informal territories of search hardened into military sectors. A Major from the 63rd Regiment, finding himself senior officer near the railway cuttings, directed fifty men with the assumption of command that came from his rank; when a naval lieutenant arrived with twenty sailors, the two established a boundary along the tracks without discussion, soldiers taking inland ruins while sailors worked the waterfront. This ad hoc territoriality solved the problem of overlapping effort—rescuers no longer wasted hours on piles already cleared—but created a patchwork of jurisdictions that would persist, some streets under army administration, others under naval command. The soldiers marked sectors with accumulated debris: a cleared path indicated army labor, the physical landscape encoding administrative geography.

The clerks working casualty lists faced pressure different from men hauling timber, though no less exhausting. Seated at tables dragged from dockyard offices into weak December sunlight, they wrote with frozen fingers against deadlines imposed by memory itself—every hour meant a survivor’s description grew less reliable. They developed a taxonomy of loss adapted from naval supply accounting: each entry received a hull number corresponding to the street address, a manifest describing injuries or marks, and a port of origin noting where the body had been taken. This bureaucratic process, begun in the first afternoon without authorization from any civilian coroner, established that the disaster’s accounting would be a military administrative function, its records maintained with the precision used for tracking anchors and cordage.

As daylight failed, the exhausted, improvised rescue effort hit its absolute limit. The soldiers and sailors had cleared what they could reach by hand, had established the first casualty stations, and had compiled the initial records of the missing. They had done so without waiting for the city government to reconstitute itself, without requesting permission from civilian authorities, and without the tools that a modern rescue operation would have required. They had set a template: sector-based search, military logistics, naval medical support, and centralized documentation. This template would persist when the formal relief administration arrived from Boston and when the Halifax Relief Commission took up its work in the weeks to come.

But for now, in the failing light of December sixth, the cold was winning. The rescuers could hear the wind shifting, carrying the first flakes of snow that would turn the ruins into a frozen maze. They had saved who they could. The rest would have to wait for morning, if morning brought any warmth at all.

SS Mont-Blanc was a cargo steamship that was built in Middlesbrough, England, in 1899 for a French shipping company. On Thursday morning, December 6, 1917, she entered Halifax Harbour in Nova Scotia, Canada, laden with a full cargo of highly volatile explosives.
