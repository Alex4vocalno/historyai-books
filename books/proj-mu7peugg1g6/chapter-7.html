Chapter 7

The Captain’s First Decision

He recorded the position in his locked notebook and said nothing to the crew. The numbers told him they were drifting westward, away from Herschel Island and any hope of rendezvous with the expedition’s other vessels, the Mary Sachs and the Alaska. Some things a captain carried alone, until the carrying became impossible.

The figure on the ice grew smaller. Vilhjalmur Stefansson walked without turning, his hunting party strung behind him like beads on a line that would soon break against the horizon. Robert Bartlett stood at the Karluk’s rail and watched them go. The ship was locked in the pack off the Alaskan coast, her engine cold, her sails furled, her mission now something no one had planned. Twenty-two people remained aboard. They looked at him.

Bartlett was forty years old, a Newfoundlander who had spent more than half his life in Arctic waters. He had captained the Roosevelt for Peary’s polar attempts, had learned to read ice pressure and wind drift the way other men read harbour charts. Now he turned from the rail and walked aft, his boots finding the familiar rhythm of a deck he had walked for two months. The Karluk was not his ship by purchase. Stefansson had acquired her for ten thousand dollars, a bargain price for a vessel that had chased bowhead whales in the Okhotsk Sea, and the Canadian government had taken her over when it assumed responsibility for the expedition. But she was his by the ancient law of the sea, the law that said when the expedition leader walked away, the captain became the only authority that mattered.

He called his officers together in the cramped chart house. The ship’s position, he told them, was approximately 200 miles from Herschel Island, their intended rendezvous with the expedition’s other vessels, the Mary Sachs and the Alaska. The ice had taken them on 13 August. They had drifted since then, generally westward, while Stefansson had talked of hunting parties and scientific detachments and the impossibility of wasting a winter in idleness. Now the talking was done.

Bartlett’s first order concerned the ship herself. She would be secured for winter, her hull prepared to withstand the pressure that would come when the pack froze solid and began to grind. The crew set to work with the energy of men who needed tasks more than they needed rest. They banked snow against the hull for insulation. They rigged wind sails to ventilate the holds. They checked the caulking, the fastenings, the frames that had softened during twenty years of sealing in the Bering Sea. Bartlett walked among them, noting what they did well and what they missed, saying little, watching everything.

The inventory came next. Bartlett had the stores opened and counted with the methodical precision of a man who understood that numbers would become lives. The Karluk had sailed from Nome on 13 July with provisions for the expedition’s planned operations. Now those provisions would be stretched across an unknown duration, distributed among twenty-two people instead of the larger party that had been intended. The count took two days. When it was done, Bartlett recorded the totals in his locked notebook and made his calculations. They had food for perhaps eight months if they were careful, perhaps less if the winter proved hard or if they were forced to travel.

The possibility of travel hung over these first days like a question no one wanted to ask. Stefansson had spoken of hunting caribou, of establishing a land base, of continuing the expedition’s work while the Karluk drifted. His departure had carried that logic with it: the ship was a prison, the ice was a highway, the men who stayed behind were merely waiting. Bartlett understood this logic and rejected it. He had seen what happened to parties that left their vessels too early, that trusted in their own preparations against an Arctic winter. The ice was not a highway. It was a surface that shifted, that opened into leads of black water, that pressed together with forces measured in thousands of tons. A ship could ride that pressure, distribute it across her hull, survive what would crush a tent or a sledge or a man caught between moving floes.

He announced his decision on the third day after Stefansson’s departure. The Karluk would remain their base. They would improve her, strengthen her, make her as capable of survival as wood and canvas and human effort could achieve. No party would leave for the shore unless the ship herself became untenable. This was not a suggestion. It was an order, delivered in Bartlett’s quiet voice, the voice of a man who had learned that authority did not require volume.

The response was not unanimous. Some of the scientific staff had expected to follow Stefansson, to continue their work, to escape the confinement of a drifting ship. They had not joined the Canadian Arctic Expedition to spend months in idleness, waiting for rescue or sinking or whatever end the ice might bring. Bartlett listened to their objections without interrupting. Then he asked them to consider their equipment.

The expedition had been organized for a different purpose. They had clothing suitable for shipboard work and summer fieldwork, not for winter travel across moving ice. They had sledges, but not enough for twenty-two people and their supplies. They had food, but not the concentrated, lightweight rations that would be needed for a march of hundreds of miles. Most critically, they had no fixed destination. The nearest known land was Herschel Island, 200 miles to the southwest, but the ice was carrying them westward, away from that goal. To reach it would require traveling against the drift, across ice that would be shifting even as they walked.

Bartlett did not need to add what they all knew. The ice that had trapped the Karluk was not stationary. It was the permanent pack of the Beaufort Sea, caught in the gyre that carried it slowly westward toward the Chukchi Sea and the Siberian coast. They were already in waters that no expedition had planned to explore. Every day of drift took them farther from help, farther from any base that might send rescue. To leave the ship now would be to exchange a known shelter for an unknown journey, to trade the protection of the Karluk’s hull for the exposed surface of the ice.

The scientific staff accepted his decision. Some accepted it with relief, glad to have the choice made for them. Others accepted it with resentment, storing their objections for later days when the confinement would grow harder. Bartlett noted both responses and made no comment. A captain, he understood, was not required to be loved. He was required to be right.

The routine he established was designed to do more than maintain the ship. It was designed to maintain the people. Each day had its structure: morning work, afternoon exercise, evening gathering in the main cabin. The crew repaired equipment, improved the ship’s insulation, hunted seals when they appeared at the ice holes. The scientific staff organized their notes, prepared their instruments for the spring that might never come, taught classes in the subjects they knew. Bartlett moved through these activities with the regularity of a clock mechanism, appearing at unexpected moments, asking unexpected questions, letting everyone feel his attention.

This attention was not comfortable. Bartlett had a way of looking at a man that suggested he was weighing something, measuring against standards that were not announced. He had learned this from his years with Peary, from the long Arctic journeys where personality conflicts could kill as surely as cold or hunger. He applied it now with the same precision he applied to his navigation. The crew he understood. They were sailors, accustomed to authority, prepared to follow a captain who knew his business. The scientific staff was different. They had joined Stefansson’s expedition for reasons of their own: discovery, reputation, the romance of the Arctic frontier. Now they found themselves commanded by a man who spoke their language only when necessary, who valued their skills less than their discipline, who treated their theories with the polite indifference of a man who had seen too many theories fail against ice.

William McKinlay, the young Scottish meteorologist, watched Bartlett with particular attention. McKinlay had been hired for his scientific training, his ability to record weather patterns and atmospheric conditions that might illuminate the Arctic climate. Now he found himself recording something else: the emergence of a command structure that had not existed when they sailed from Nome. In his diary, written each evening in the cramped privacy of his berth, McKinlay noted the captain’s orders, the crew’s responses, the gradual transformation of the Karluk from an expedition vessel into a survival unit. He wrote without judgment, describing what he saw, preserving the record that might later be needed.

What McKinlay saw, and what his diary preserved, was the transfer of the “Authority Iceberg”—the visible, formal command structure that had splintered and sunk when Stefansson walked away, leaving submerged, ad-hoc leadership to surface in its place. Bartlett had been the Karluk’s captain in name from the beginning. Now he became her captain in fact, the sole authority over twenty-two lives that the ice had claimed for its own.

The ice itself became a character in these days, as present and demanding as any human. It surrounded the ship on all sides, a plain of white and blue that stretched to every horizon. On clear days, the crew could see pressure ridges rising like frozen waves, evidence of the forces that moved beneath the surface. On stormy days, the ice disappeared into blowing snow, and the Karluk seemed to float in a void, her only connection to reality the solid thump of her hull against the floes that held her. Bartlett studied this ice with the attention he gave to his crew. He noted the direction of the ridges, the pattern of the leads, the quality of the freezing that would determine how the pack behaved when winter came.

His navigation continued throughout these days, though it served purposes that were partly psychological. Each noon, when the sun permitted, he took his sights with the sextant, calculated his position, recorded it in his locked notebook. The positions showed what he already knew: they were drifting westward, carried by the pack’s slow circulation toward waters that no ship of the Canadian Arctic Expedition had been meant to explore. He shared these positions with no one. The crew knew they were moving; they could feel the ship shift against her ice prison, could see the landscape change when visibility allowed. But they did not know how far they had come from any hope of rescue, how completely they were now alone.

This aloneness was the fundamental fact of Bartlett’s command. The Karluk had sailed as part of an expedition with three ships: the Karluk herself, the Mary Sachs, and the Alaska. The plan had called for them to operate together, supporting each other, maintaining contact through the wireless that had proven so unreliable. Now that plan was ash. The Mary Sachs and the Alaska were somewhere to the west, presumably continuing their own operations, presumably unaware that the expedition’s flagship was trapped and drifting. The wireless on the Karluk could transmit, but no one answered. They called at the appointed times, heard nothing but static, and fell silent again.

Bartlett made his preparations without illusion. He had read the accounts of previous Arctic disasters, had studied the fate of ships that had been caught in the ice and crushed. He knew that the Karluk’s survival was not guaranteed, that his decision to keep the crew aboard was a gamble with stakes measured in lives. But he knew also that the alternative was worse. The history of polar exploration was littered with the remains of parties that had left their ships too early, that had trusted in their own strength against an environment that measured strength in months and years. The Franklin expedition, the most famous disaster of all, had begun with a decision to abandon ship and march for safety. The men had died on that march, their bones scattered across the ice for decades afterward.

He would not repeat that error. The Karluk would remain their base until the Karluk herself made it impossible. This was his operational philosophy, stated not in words but in the orders he gave, the preparations he made, the routine he imposed. A mariner’s faith in his vessel, a captain’s responsibility for his crew, a pragmatist’s assessment of their unpreparedness for overland travel: these elements combined in a decision that would define the coming months.

The physical transformation of the ship proceeded through September. The crew extended the deck house, creating additional sheltered space for the winter months. They rigged a system for melting ice, ensuring their water supply when the ship’s tanks froze. They prepared the sledges that would be needed if the worst came, loading them with emergency supplies, checking the harnesses, making ready for a journey that Bartlett hoped to avoid. Everything was done with the methodical care of men who understood that their preparations might be tested at any moment, that the ice could shift without warning and turn their world upside down.

Bartlett inspected each preparation himself. He had learned from Peary that survival in the Arctic depended on details: a loose fastening that would break in cold, a food cache that would be buried by drift, a piece of equipment that would fail when it was most needed. He found errors and had them corrected. He found improvements and had them implemented. He worked alongside his crew when the work required it, showing them that he asked nothing he would not do himself, that his authority was based on competence rather than rank.

The scientific staff participated in this work according to their abilities. Some took to it readily, glad of the physical activity and the sense of purpose it provided. Others struggled, their skills ill-suited to the manual labor of ship maintenance, their temperaments frayed by the confinement and the uncertainty. Bartlett assigned tasks without favor, judging each man by what he could contribute rather than what he had been hired to do. A meteorologist became a caulker’s mate. A zoologist learned to tend the stoves. The categories of the expedition dissolved in the practical necessity of survival.

Through all of this, the drift continued. The Karluk moved westward at the pace of the pack, sometimes a few miles in a day, sometimes stationary for weeks while the ice consolidated around her. Bartlett’s locked notebook filled with positions that traced a curve across the Arctic waters, a curve that bent northward as the gyre carried them toward the center of the Beaufort Sea. He studied these positions in the privacy of his cabin, comparing them with the charts he had, calculating their distance from land, from help, from any possibility of rescue before spring.

The onset of autumn brought changes that everyone could see. The days grew shorter, the sun lower, the twilight longer. The ice thickened, its surface becoming more solid, more capable of bearing weight. The temperature dropped, first below freezing at night, then below freezing during the day, then into the range where exposed flesh would freeze in minutes. The crew responded with the clothing and routines they had prepared, with the stoves that burned constantly now, with the communal life that Bartlett had organized to preserve both body and spirit.

It was in these days that the first signs of strain appeared. The scientific staff, particularly, found the routine grating. They had joined an expedition of discovery, had expected to spend their winters in active research, their summers in exploration. Instead they found themselves in a floating prison, commanded by a man who valued survival over science, who treated their instruments with less respect than his navigation tools, who seemed to regard their presence as a burden rather than a resource. Complaints were made, first in private, then more openly. Bartlett heard them and did not respond. His orders continued unchanged.

The crew, more accustomed to authority, accepted the situation with fewer questions. They had shipped with captains before, had learned that a voyage could take unexpected turns, that the sea—or in this case the ice—was the final arbiter of plans. They trusted Bartlett because he had shown them competence, because he worked as hard as any of them, because he never asked them to do what he would not do himself. This trust was not unlimited. They watched him as he watched them, measuring his decisions against their own experience, ready to question if his orders seemed to endanger them without purpose.

Bartlett understood this watching. He had commanded enough vessels to know that authority was never absolute, that it had to be renewed each day through demonstration and result. He gave the crew what he could: clear orders, fair treatment, the sense that their captain was doing everything possible to bring them through. He could not give them certainty. No one could give them that, not with the ice locked around them and the winter coming on.

The critical decision of these weeks concerned the possibility of establishing a shore base. Some of the scientific staff revived Stefansson’s argument: that they should send a party to the nearest land, that they should prepare for the possibility that the Karluk would not survive, that they should not put all their hopes in a single wooden hull. Bartlett considered this proposal with the same care he gave to everything. He examined the charts, calculated the distance, assessed the condition of the ice between the ship and the coast.

The conclusion he reached was the same he had reached before. The distance was too great, the ice too uncertain, their preparations too inadequate. A party that left now would be traveling into the Arctic winter with equipment meant for summer operations, with no certainty of finding game, with no base to receive them if they failed. They would be gambling their lives on a chance that Bartlett judged too slight to take.

He announced this decision with the finality he had used before. The Karluk would remain their base. No shore party would be sent. Any further discussion of the matter was closed. Some of the scientific staff accepted this with relief, glad to have the responsibility removed from them. Others accepted it with bitterness, convinced that Bartlett was sacrificing their chances from stubbornness or fear. They were wrong, but Bartlett did not trouble to correct them. A captain, he knew, was judged by results, not by explanations.

The last days of September brought the first snow, the first real cold, the first sense that the Arctic winter was upon them. The Karluk was as prepared as wood and canvas and human effort could make her. The routine Bartlett had established functioned with the regularity of a machine. The crew worked, ate, slept, worked again. The scientific staff taught their classes, recorded their observations, waited for spring. The ice held them in its grip and carried them westward, toward waters that no one aboard had planned to see.

Bartlett recorded each day’s position in his locked notebook. The numbers told a story that he kept to himself: they were farther from help than they had been, farther from any base that might send rescue, farther from the world that had sent them here. He did not share this with the crew. Some things a captain carried alone, until the carrying became impossible. What he carried now was the weight of twenty-two lives, the responsibility for decisions that would be judged by their outcome, the knowledge that his gamble—the ship as shelter, the ice as prison, the drift as fate—would be tested in the months to come.

The Karluk, now secured for winter, began her drift westward, locking the community into Bartlett’s calculated gamble.
