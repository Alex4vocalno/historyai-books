Chapter 2

The Leader and the Captain

In June 1913, the whaler Karluk lay in dry dock at Esquimalt, her hull scraped and her seams caulked for a voyage she was never built to make. Captain Robert Bartlett walked her deck with a caulker’s eye, noting where the planks had softened, where the fastenings had worked loose from twenty years of sealing in the Bering Sea. He had spent more than fifty years mapping these waters, had led over forty expeditions to the Arctic, more than anyone before or since.

He knew what a ship could endure and what would kill her. The Karluk, he told the workmen, would need more than paint and pitch to survive the Chukchi pack. For her role in the Canadian Arctic Expedition, she had been acquired by expedition leader Vilhjalmur Stefansson in 1913 for the bargain price of $10, 000, and sold at cost to the Canadian government when it assumed overall responsibility for the expedition.

Vilhjalmur Stefansson saw something else entirely. The anthropologist-turned-explorer had acquired the vessel for ten thousand dollars, a bargain price for a ship that would carry his name into the ice. When the Canadian government assumed responsibility for the Canadian Arctic Expedition, he sold her at cost and kept command. The arrangement suited him. The Karluk was not a ship to be preserved against the elements; she was a mobile base, a platform from which he could extend his reach into the unknown regions west of Parry Archipelago. The ice was a road.

This difference in seeing would become the expedition’s central fracture. Bartlett looked at the Karluk and saw a hull that could be crushed, a crew that could drown, a chain of command that required clarity. Stefansson looked at the same vessel and saw opportunity. The Beaufort Sea remained unmapped. The continental shelf lay waiting. If the ship failed, the ice itself would provide transport, hunting grounds, a stage for human adaptation. He had lived among the Inuit. He had eaten raw meat and worn fur. The Arctic, he believed, was fundamentally friendly to those who understood it.

The two men had met before. Bartlett had captained the Roosevelt for Robert Peary’s attempts on the North Pole, had stood on the ice at 87 degrees north, closer than any human being to that mathematical point. He had learned respect for the pack the hard way, through frozen hands and narwhal hunts and the long darkness of polar winter. Stefansson had made his reputation through the Anglo-American Polar Expedition of 1906–1907 and the Canadian Arctic Expedition of 1908–1912, living with the Inuit of the Mackenzie Delta, demonstrating that Europeans could survive by indigenous methods. Each man had credentials that would have commanded any other expedition. Neither had learned to yield.

Their first open disagreement came over provisions. Bartlett wanted stores calculated for three years, with margins for spoilage, loss, and the unexpected. Stefansson planned for mobility. Heavy supplies meant a sluggish ship, a tethered expedition. He intended to hunt as he traveled, to live off seal and bear and fish, reducing the weight of preserved foods to what could be carried on sledges. He had done it before. But Bartlett had seen hunters lost in fog, seen ice that yielded nothing for weeks, seen men turn to eating their boots while waiting for the weather to break. The Karluk’s hold, he insisted, must carry certainty. Stefansson overruled him. The scientific parties would carry what they needed for their work. The ship’s company would adapt.

William McKinlay, a young Scottish scientist assigned to the magnetic observations, watched these exchanges with growing unease. He had joined for the science, for the chance to measure the earth’s field in regions where no instrument had recorded it. What he found in Victoria and Nome was an organization that seemed to operate on two parallel tracks, neither acknowledging the other. Stefansson issued orders from his headquarters in the Hotel Victoria, dispatching telegrams, interviewing reporters, managing the political dimensions of a government-sponsored venture. Bartlett moved between ship and dockyard, supervising the physical preparation of a vessel he already suspected was inadequate to her task. When their paths crossed, the courtesy was formal, the disagreement immediate and unresolved.

The expedition’s structure compounded the problem. Stefansson held the title of Director. Bartlett held the title of Captain. In nautical tradition, the captain’s word is law aboard his ship. In expedition tradition, the director’s scientific objectives determine the route, the schedule, the allocation of resources. The Canadian government, in its eagerness to sponsor a venture that would assert sovereignty in the western Arctic, had not clarified which authority prevailed when these traditions collided. Stefansson acted as if the answer were obvious: he would determine where the expedition went and what it accomplished; Bartlett would execute the nautical means. Bartlett acted as if the answer were equally obvious: a captain who could not protect his ship against orders he knew to be dangerous was no captain at all.

The Karluk’s condition made every decision consequential. She had been built in 1884 as a sealing vessel, strong in the beam for working through loose pack, shallow in draft for approaching coasts where seals hauled out. She was not reinforced for the pressure ice of the Beaufort Sea, where floes driven by wind and current could mount each other in pressure ridges forty feet high. Her engine was elderly, her boilers patched, her coal capacity limited. For her role in the Canadian Arctic Expedition, she had been acquired cheap and sold to the government at cost, a transaction that saved money but did not transform her nature. Bartlett wanted time for proper modification: steel sheathing, strengthened frames, a reliable auxiliary engine. Stefansson wanted departure. The ice window in the Chukchi Sea was narrow. The scientific objectives were ambitious. Delay meant waiting another year.

They sailed from Nome on July 13, 1913, with these tensions still unresolved. The Karluk carried twenty-five men: scientists, sailors, Inuit hunters, a surgeon, a cook, a cat named Nig. The Mary Sachs and Alaska, smaller vessels with their own parties, would attempt separate routes through the archipelago. Stefansson’s plan was to penetrate the pack east of the northernmost point of Alaska, establish a base on the ice or on one of the unexplored islands, and dispatch sledge parties in multiple directions. The geography was speculative. The Admiralty charts showed blank spaces where the continental shelf might extend far north or drop abruptly into deep water. Stefansson believed he could find land, perhaps even the continent that nineteenth-century theorists had imagined bridging Alaska and Greenland. The scientific program was genuine and substantial: oceanography, meteorology, magnetism, ethnography, zoology. But the geography was personal: his expedition, his discovery.

Bartlett’s responsibility was to keep the ship intact long enough for these ambitions to be attempted.

From the first day in the Chukchi Sea, this required a judgment the expedition’s structure did not support.

The ice was heavy early, a bad sign. The Karluk pushed through loose floes, then found herself against solid pack that stretched to the horizon. Bartlett wanted to work westward along the coast, seeking an opening that might develop later in the season. Stefansson wanted to push north, directly into the barrier, trusting that leads would open or that the ship could find a stable floe for a winter base. They argued on the bridge, within earshot of the helmsman and the watch officer. The captain’s log records the course changes; it does not record the words.

But McKinlay, in his diary, noted that the two men were speaking different languages. One talked of ice pressure and hull strain. The other talked of scientific opportunity. Neither seemed to hear the other.

By August 12, the decision had been made for them. The Karluk was caught in ice twenty miles northeast of Flaxman Island, not by sudden entrapment but by gradual closure of the leads they had been following. For two days she drifted with the pack, her engine useless, her rudder locked in ice. Bartlett worked to protect her: fending off the larger floes with ice poles, pumping the bilges where the pressure opened seams, keeping the boilers warm against the possibility of an opening. Stefansson saw the situation differently. The ship was not imprisoned; she was positioned. From this platform, he could launch the sledge journeys that were the expedition’s true purpose. The ice was doing what he had expected it to do: providing a stable base from which to explore.

On August 20, he announced his intention to establish a hunting camp on the nearest land. Caribou had been sighted on the coastal plain. Fresh meat would supplement the ship’s stores and provide material for the scientific collections. He would take five men, including two Inuit hunters, Kuraluk and Kataktovik, who had joined at Nome. They would be gone days, perhaps weeks, depending on what they found. Bartlett objected. The ice was drifting westward, away from the coast. A shore party might find itself unable to return. Stefansson replied that he knew ice drift, knew how to travel over it, knew the Inuit methods of navigation and shelter. The disagreement went to the expedition’s second-in-command, but there was no second-in-command with authority to resolve it. Stefansson was director. He gave the order.

He left on August 23, 1913, with a small party and light sledges. The Karluk remained in the pack, drifting westward under Bartlett’s command. The ice groaned. The ship shuddered. She carried twenty-five men into the gathering dark of the polar autumn, her captain responsible for a vessel he could not move, her director committed to a geographical program that required her presence but not, apparently, his own.

The weeks that followed exposed every weakness in the expedition’s preparation. The food stores, calculated for mobility and supplemented by hunting, proved inadequate for a stationary winter. The scientific equipment, packed for sledge transport, was inaccessible or damaged by the working of the ship in ice pressure. The men—sailors accustomed to the discipline of the sea, scientists accustomed to the autonomy of research—found themselves in a limbo without clear purpose. Bartlett maintained routine: watches, maintenance, the daily recording of position and weather. But routine could not answer the questions that mattered. Where were they drifting? When would they be free? What was Stefansson doing on the coast, and when would he return?

The director had left instructions that were precise about scientific objectives and vague about command. Bartlett was captain of the Karluk. In Stefansson’s absence, he was responsible for the ship and her company. But he was not director of the expedition. The scientific parties—the geologists, the magneticians, the zoologists—looked to Stefansson for their assignments. With Stefansson ashore, they looked to each other, or to no one. McKinlay recorded the confusion. They were told to continue their observations, but to what end? The ship drifted west. Their base moved with them. They were measuring the field at points that had no fixed relation to anything. The observation of irony was scientific; the experience of it was human.

Bartlett’s practical difficulties multiplied. The Karluk’s coal supply, already marginal for the planned program, was being consumed by the need to keep boilers warm against the possibility of an opening. The fresh water from melting ice required constant labor with picks and stoves. The ship’s position, determined by uncertain observations through overcast skies, suggested a drift that would carry them past the northernmost point of Alaska and into the Chukchi Sea proper, away from any established base or rescue route. He sent messages by carrier pigeon and by Inuit messengers when they encountered hunters on the ice. None brought reply.

Stefansson, meanwhile, had found no caribou. The coastal plain was empty, or the animals had moved, or his information had been wrong. He and his party traveled eastward along the shore, hunting seal and bear, building snow houses, waiting for conditions that would allow return to the ship or continuation of the program by other means. The Inuit hunters were patient; they knew how to read ice, how to wait for opportunity. But Stefansson was not waiting. He was planning. The Karluk, he concluded, would not be released that season. The expedition’s scientific objectives required independent action. He would establish a base on the coast, organize sledge parties from there, continue the work that the Canadian government had funded. The ship was one element of a larger design. If she survived the winter, they would rejoin her in spring. If she did not, the expedition would continue without her.

Stefansson remained director in every legal sense, committed to the program he had designed. But abandonment in practice does not require legal formality; it requires only absence at the moment when presence is owed. The Karluk drifted west through September as ice closed and opened around her hull. On September 20 a heavy gale broke the pack temporarily; she moved several miles before floes closed again. Stefansson would have felt that same wind ashore somewhere to the east and known what it meant for navigation—and still made no attempt to reach her.

Bartlett learned of this only indirectly, through Inuit reports that reached him weeks later. The director had established a camp at Collinson Point. He was organizing shore parties for the winter’s scientific work. The Karluk was not part of these plans. The captain, reading this news in the cramped chart room of a ship that shook with ice pressure, understood what had happened. He had been left with a vessel he could not save, a crew he could not feed adequately, and a chain of command that no longer connected to anything. The expedition’s scientific objectives would be pursued from the coast. The ship’s survival was his problem alone.

The winter set in with darkness and cold that tested every preparation Stefansson had rejected. The Karluk’s hull, never designed for pressure ice, began to suffer. Seams opened under the grinding of floes. Pumps ran continuously. The crew fought ice with ice poles, with saws, with desperate labor that warmed them briefly and exhausted them completely. Bartlett kept position records, maintained the log, preserved the forms of naval command in circumstances that mocked them. But he could not preserve the illusion that this was a normal expedition proceeding according to plan.

McKinlay’s diary from this period shows a scientist trying to maintain professional focus while recognizing that his situation had become something else entirely. The magnetic work continued, he wrote in October, but the instruments were affected by the cold, the ship’s motion, the general uncertainty of everything. By November, the uncertainty had narrowed to specific fears: food shortage, hull failure, the impossibility of rescue before spring. The men talked of Stefansson’s return, of relief from the coast, of some reversal that would restore the expedition to its intended shape. Bartlett did not encourage these hopes. He had seen too much ice, knew too well how it could isolate and destroy.

The relationship between captain and crew became crucial now that everything else had failed them—objectives abandoned by their authorizer; resources fixed by an earlier budget; command structure dissolved into ambiguity about who owed what to whom aboard this drifting hull in pressure ice off an unmapped coast where no rescue route existed yet or would exist until spring opened leads enough for ships to try.

Stefansson’s absence shaped every judgment of him. The men who had admired his energy and confidence in Victoria now measured him against his departure. He had left them. He had chosen his geographical program over their safety. The fairness of this judgment—he would argue later that he had acted to preserve the expedition’s scientific mission, that he had expected the ship to winter in the pack and emerge in spring—did not alter its force. They were on a dying ship. He was on solid ground, organizing other work, sending messages that did not include plans for their relief.

The Karluk’s drift continued through December, carrying her west past the northernmost point of Alaska and into the Chukchi Sea. The ice was heavier here, older, more compressed by the currents that circulated through Bering Strait. Bartlett watched the pressure ridges build around them, forty feet of shattered ice that could mount a ship’s hull and crush it like an eggshell. He had seen the Jeannette’s remains on the coast of Siberia, knew what happened to vessels caught in this particular drift. The Jeannette had been better found than the Karluk, better equipped, better led in some ways. She had still gone down with most of her crew.

On January 10, 1914, the pressure found a weak point. The Karluk’s starboard quarter took the weight of a floe driven by wind and current against her stationary hull. Timbers splintered. Seams opened wide enough to see the black water beneath. The pumps could not keep pace. Bartlett ordered abandon ship preparations: supplies loaded on sledges, camps established on the nearest stable ice, the long labor of survival begun in earnest. The next day she sank, not dramatically but gradually, settling by the stern until her decks were awash and her masts tilted toward the sky that had betrayed them all.

Twenty-five men stood on the ice where their ship had been. They had food for weeks, equipment for travel, a captain who knew where they must go and how little chance they had of getting there. Wrangel Island lay two hundred miles to the west, theoretically reachable before their supplies failed. A smaller island lay closer but offered no shelter, no game, no hope of rescue. Bartlett chose Wrangel because it was land, because it might have bear and fox and the possibility of a passing ship. He organized the march with the same methodical precision he had applied to the Karluk’s doomed defense: sledges loaded with essential weight, parties traveling in sequence so that strength could assist weakness, the daily routine of camp and travel that preserved morale through exhaustion.

They lost two parties on that march. One turned back toward the sinking site, hoping for salvage or rescue by some miracle of navigation. The other pushed too fast, too far, breaking through thin ice or losing direction in the white emptiness. Bartlett learned of their fate only later, through fragments of evidence found by searchers or reported by Inuit hunters. What he knew at the time was that his command was dissolving, that the twenty-five had become fewer, that each decision carried consequences he could not fully foresee.

He reached Wrangel Island with seventeen men, then watched that number diminish through the long polar summer. Scurvy took some; an ill-advised journey to the smaller island took four more who might have survived if they had stayed; quarrels and despair took the will to live from others. By August 1914, eleven remained. Bartlett had walked across the Chukchi ice to Siberia with Kataktovik, the Inuit hunter who had been with Stefansson’s original shore party, to carry news of their situation to any authority who could send rescue. They traveled six hundred miles in six weeks, surviving by methods that Bartlett had learned from decades of Arctic experience and that Kataktovik had known from childhood.

The rescue came on September 7, 1914, when the schooner King and Winge, chartered by Stefansson’s organization with funds raised through desperate cablegrams, reached Wrangel Island’s coast. Eleven men came aboard. They were alive because Bartlett had kept them so, through improvisation and endurance that no expedition plan had anticipated or authorized. The scientific program that justified their original presence had continued elsewhere, directed by Stefansson from his bases on the coast. The Karluk’s voyage, her entrapment, her sinking, the deaths of her missing parties—these were episodes in a larger narrative that would be disputed, investigated, partially explained but never fully reconciled.

What remained was the structural failure that had preceded the ice. An expedition organized around one man’s geographical ambition had encountered conditions that required unified command. The man with theoretical authority had departed; the man with practical responsibility had been left without resources or clarification. The ship had been inadequate to her task; the preparations had been inadequate to her inadequacy; the chain of command had been designed to fail at the first serious test. The ice had done what ice does. The human arrangements had done what humans do when they mistake confidence for preparation.

The survivors carried this knowledge with them, expressed in different ways according to temperament and experience. McKinlay wrote his account with scientific precision and moral indignation equally measured. Bartlett told his story with the reticence of a professional captain who had done what duty required and asked no recognition beyond survival itself. Stefansson defended his decisions with arguments that were not without force: the expedition’s objectives had been achieved, its scientific results were substantial, his departure had been necessary to preserve a larger program from a localized disaster. Each version was true to its teller. None could encompass what had happened to the men left on the ice.

The expedition moved north with this fault line unhealed, this ambiguity unresolved. The Karluk’s company had seen what their leader would do when choice became necessary. They had seen what their captain could do when left alone with responsibility. The ice would test both lessons further, and the testing would not be gentle.
