Chapter 4

The Uncharted Territory

The ice spoke before Bartlett could see it. A low, continuous grinding from somewhere ahead, then the sharper crack of a floe splitting under pressure, then silence. He stood at the Karluk’s rail in the early hours of August 13, 1913, twenty miles northeast of Flaxman Island, and watched the white world rearrange itself in the half-light. The leads that had offered passage the previous evening were narrowing. The open water visible at midnight had become a jagged line of pressure ridges, frozen waves caught at the moment of breaking. By dawn, the line itself had disappeared, replaced by a continuous surface of pack ice that extended to every horizon.

The ship’s hull transmitted each movement of the ice through her timbers. She had been built for chasing bowhead whales in the Okhotsk Sea, for quick turns in open water, for the explosive pursuit of surfacing catch. Her frames were sturdy but shaped for a different violence. Now she lay trapped in a grinding that never stopped, a slow compression that would continue for months. Bartlett had known ice before—in the Newfoundland seal fishery, under Peary in the Greenland Sea, in waters that tested ships and men with predictable rigor. What he had not known, what no navigator of his experience had mapped with precision, was the particular machinery of this basin.

The Beaufort Sea was not simply a place where ice formed. It was a system of ice, driven by winds that circled clockwise across the Arctic Ocean, carrying the pack in a vast rotation that could carry a frozen ship west toward Siberia, north toward the pole, east toward the Canadian archipelago, or hold it for years in the gyre’s center. The theory of this circulation existed in scattered scientific papers, in the drift records of a few whalers, in the speculations of oceanographers who had never seen the ice. It had not reached the practical men who commanded ships. Bartlett understood ice as weather, as immediate hazard, as the surface on which survival depended. He did not yet understand it as geography—a geography whose rules were being written by forces indifferent to human intention.

Stefansson appeared on deck as the sun cleared the horizon. The expedition leader carried his field glasses, sweeping the ice with the methodical patience of a man searching for something he had theorized must exist. He believed in polynyas—persistent areas of open water maintained by wind and current, where marine life gathered and ships might find refuge. The veteran whalers of the Arctic had spoken of such places, had located them by the birds that circled above, the steam that rose from exposed water in cold air. Stefansson had built his expedition plans around this possibility, the chance that even in the grip of the pack, a path might remain open.

He found nothing. The glasses revealed only ice, white on white, the pressure ridges casting faint shadows in the low sun. The Karluk’s position at noon—seventy miles offshore, already west of her intended landfall at Herschel Island—placed her in waters that existed on charts as blank space and conjecture. The Coast and Geodetic Survey had mapped the Alaskan coastline with reasonable precision, but north of the barrier islands, knowledge thinned to scattered soundings, reported ice edges, the occasional position of a whaler that had ventured farther than most. The Beaufort Sea itself, the largest remaining blank on the map of the northern hemisphere, was precisely why Stefansson had chosen this region. It offered the possibility of discovery, of land unseen, of scientific data uncollected, of Canadian presence established before American or Danish interests could consolidate their positions.

The national purpose was explicit. The Canadian government had funded the expedition to claim territory, to extend sovereignty through the continuous exercise of authority in the High Arctic. The Karluk was the flagship of this project, her presence in these waters a statement of intent. But authority required movement, and movement was now impossible. The ice that imprisoned the ship also paralyzed the national project she represented. A frozen vessel could exercise no sovereignty, establish no presence, communicate with no government. She could only drift, carried by forces that recognized no flag.

Bartlett established the first noon position on August 13: 70°21’ N, 146°15’ W. The calculation was routine, automatic, the work of a navigator maintaining discipline in circumstances that offered little else. He recorded it in the ship’s log, plotted it on the chart, drew the connecting line to the last position from open water. The line ran westward. This was the first data point in a pattern that would continue for months, that would trace the ship’s transformation from expedition vessel to drifting cargo, from instrument of national purpose to particle in a system whose scale dwarfed human intention.

The drift began immediately. Each noon sighting showed the Karluk moving, sometimes ten miles in a day, sometimes fifteen, sometimes barely perceptible when the wind held steady. The direction remained constant: west, with a slight northerly component. The ice was carrying her not toward Herschel Island, her destination, but away from it, toward waters where no rescue could reach her, toward the Russian coast where no Canadian expedition had any relationship, any foothold, any expectation of aid.

The scientific staff turned to observation. Their shore-based programs—geological survey, biological collection, ethnographic study of the Inuit—were now impossible. They measured ocean temperatures through holes cut in the ice. They collected ice cores, noting the layers that marked successive seasons of freezing. They recorded the direction and force of the drift, the behavior of leads that opened and closed, the temperature and wind and barometric pressure. The data accumulated in notebooks, precise, patient, disconnected from any immediate purpose. These observations would eventually contribute to the understanding of Arctic oceanography, would help answer questions about convergence and divergence, about the gyre’s boundaries and the fate of ice that entered it. But they could not change the Karluk’s trajectory. The ship was embedded in a system that moved according to its own rules, carrying its human cargo toward a destination unknown.

The ice itself was unlike the polar pack Bartlett had known in the Greenland Sea. The floes were thicker, older, carrying the compressed history of years of circulation. The pressure ridges rose higher, the embedded gravel scored deeper grooves in the hull when the ship ground against them. This was multi-year ice, the permanent pack that survived from summer to summer, that formed the solid surface of the Arctic Ocean. It was not a seasonal prison from which release might come with the autumn winds. It was a long-term sentence, a transformation of the ship’s situation from temporary setback to fundamental redefinition.

Stefansson refused this redefinition. He moved among the scientific staff with the energy of a man who would not accept that his plans had already failed. He continued to scan the horizon for the polynya he believed must exist. He discussed with Bartlett the possibility of establishing an ice camp, of using the ship as a base for sledge journeys that might yet accomplish something of the expedition’s goals. He wrote in his journal, maintained his correspondence, behaved as though the Karluk’s situation were manageable, a deviation from the main program rather than its decisive transformation.

The coordinates told a different story. Each noon, Bartlett reduced his observations to latitude and longitude, plotted the position, drew the connecting line. The pattern was clear. They were not circulating in a gyre that might eventually return them to familiar waters. They were being transported along the northern edge of the gyre, following a trajectory that would carry them past Point Barrow, past the limit of American territorial waters, past the reach of any ship that might be sent from Nome or St. Michael. The speed varied with the wind, but the direction held constant. Westward, always westward, toward a coast where no Canadian ship had any business.

The human geography of the region offered no comfort. North of the Alaskan coast, the Inuit population thinned to scattered hunting parties, families moving with the caribou and the seal. Their knowledge of the ice was profound but local, bounded by the territory they needed to live. They had no reason to venture far into the pack, no resources to mount rescue expeditions, no relationship with the Canadian government that would obligate them to try. The nearest Russian settlement, Uelen on the Chukotka coast, lay hundreds of miles to the west, across ice that no Siberian hunter would cross without compelling reason. The Chukchi and Yupik peoples of that coast had their own lives, their own struggles, their own suspicion of ships that appeared from the east bearing foreign flags.

Bartlett had studied these distances before leaving Nome. He understood that the Karluk was entering a region of institutional absence. The United States Coast Guard, newly established, maintained no presence north of the Bering Strait. The Canadian government, for all its Arctic ambitions, had no infrastructure in the western Arctic capable of reaching a ship in distress. The expedition had been organized as self-sufficient, carrying its own supplies, its own equipment, its own plans for communication and resupply. Those plans assumed mobility. They assumed the ice would be a surface to work from, not a medium of capture.

The wireless telegraph, that miracle of modern communication, was useless in the pack. Its signals could not penetrate the electrical disturbances of the Arctic atmosphere, could not reach the stations at Nome or Point Barrow, could not summon help from a world that was rapidly losing track of the expedition’s existence. The Karluk had reported her position when she entered the ice. After that, silence. The expedition’s agents in Victoria waited for news that did not come. The Canadian government, preoccupied with other matters, had no mechanism for tracking a ship that could not communicate, no protocol for responding to a disaster whose dimensions were unknown.

The first weeks of imprisonment established a rhythm. The ice shifted constantly, opening leads that might offer escape, then closing them before the ship could move. The wind drove the pack against the hull with varying force—sometimes gentle pressure, sometimes grinding that made the timbers scream. Bartlett maintained watches to monitor the ice, to record the ship’s movement, to keep the machinery ready against the day when open water might appear. The men settled into the routines of a ship frozen for the winter, routines that masked the fundamental transformation of their situation.

They were no longer an expedition in control of its movements. They were cargo, carried by forces they could observe but not influence. The scientific observations continued, precise and patient, contributing to knowledge that would outlast the men who collected it. But the immediate reality was the ice, the drift, the gradual disappearance of any connection to the world that had sent them north.

The landscape enforced this transformation. The ice was not merely a surface but a world, with its own topography, its own weather, its own life. Pressure ridges rose twenty feet above the level of the floes, creating a terrain of valleys and hills that blocked vision, channeled wind, determined where a man could walk. The leads that opened between floes were dynamic features, widening and closing with the tide, the wind, the shifting pressure of the pack. A lead that offered passage in the morning might be a pressure ridge by afternoon, or a gap of open water too wide to cross, or a maze of broken ice that would trap and crush any boat that entered it.

Stefansson’s plans for ice camps and sledge journeys represented the only available strategy for salvaging something from the situation. They were not foolish in themselves. But they also represented a refusal to acknowledge that the situation had changed, that the Karluk was no longer a mobile base for exploration but a prisoner of forces beyond any leader’s control. The expedition leader continued to behave as though the main program remained possible, as though the ice were a temporary obstacle rather than a fundamental redefinition of the expedition’s terms.

The coordinates recorded each noon traced this refusal in numbers. August 20: 70°35’ N, 147°40’ W. August 27: 70°48’ N, 149°10’ W. September 3: 71°05’ N, 150°45’ W. The ship moved steadily westward, her trajectory following the arc of the gyre’s outer edge. The distance to Herschel Island increased to three hundred miles, then four hundred. The distance to the Alaskan coast increased. The distance to any possible rescue increased. Bartlett understood this pattern with the clarity of a navigator. He had seen ships frozen in the ice before, had experienced the helplessness of drift. What he had not experienced was this particular combination of geographical ignorance and institutional absence, this particular ocean where no help could reach them because no help existed.

The oceanographers of the period debated whether the Beaufort Sea was a region of convergence, where ice accumulated, or of divergence, where it dispersed. The Karluk’s drift would eventually contribute to this debate, her trajectory providing data points in a scientific argument she had not been sent to join. But the practical consequence was immediate. Every degree of longitude westward increased the distance to any possible rescue. The expedition’s support ships, the Mary Sachs and the Alaska, had been sent to Herschel Island, hundreds of miles to the east. They could not reach the Karluk through the pack, even if they knew her position, which they did not.

The Canadian claim to the Arctic archipelago rested on discovery, on presence, on the continuous exercise of authority. But the Karluk, frozen in the pack and drifting toward Siberia, could exercise no authority. She could only survive, if survival proved possible, and wait for the ice to release her. The men on deck understood themselves to be explorers, pioneers, the advance guard of knowledge. They did not yet fully understand that they were also artifacts of a geopolitical competition whose rules they had not made, caught in a physical system whose rules they had not grasped.

Bartlett continued his noon observations through August and into September. The practice was automatic, professional, a navigator’s discipline that maintained identity and purpose in circumstances that offered little of either. Each sighting, each calculation, each plotted point represented a small assertion of human order against the chaos of the ice. But the pattern they revealed was not subject to human will. The Karluk was moving steadily westward, her position determined by forces that had been set in motion before any man aboard was born.

On September 10, Bartlett recorded a position of 71°15’ N, 152°20’ W. They were now more than five hundred miles from Herschel Island, more than two hundred miles from the nearest point on the Alaskan coast, entering waters that appeared on charts as blank space or speculative contour. The ice remained solid, the leads rare and fleeting, the possibility of escape diminishing with each week of imprisonment. The men had settled into the routines of a ship preparing for winter, routines that masked the fundamental fact of their isolation.

They were alone in a system that moved to its own rhythm, surrounded by ice that answered to no human command, drifting toward a coast where no rescue waited and no welcome was assured. The ship’s log continued, the noon positions accumulated, the line of their passage traced across the chart in a steady arc toward the west. The Karluk was now more than trapped. She was transformed, a particle in a vast machinery of ice and wind and current, carrying her crew toward a destination that none of them had chosen and none of them could prevent.
