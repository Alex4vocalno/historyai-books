Chapter 16

The Long Wait on Wrangel Island

The men Bartlett had led to Wrangel Island now faced a season of stillness. Their captain had departed on foot across the ice on March 18, 1914, a date entered in the expedition record with the same precision that would later measure their decline. By late April, the first sailor could not rise from his sleeping bag. His gums bled at the slightest pressure. His joints had swollen to twice their normal size. The scurvy that Arctic explorers had learned to fear had found them in a place where no fresh vegetables had ever grown and no ship could reach.

—-

First Officer Alexander Anderson understood the mathematics of their situation. He had watched Bartlett calculate rations on the ice, had seen the captain’s jaw tighten when the final figures came up short. Now those calculations belonged to him. Seventeen people remained on the island: eleven naval men and expedition staff, two Inuit women, four children. The family of Kuraluk brought knowledge that the Europeans lacked—how to read the ice for seal holes, how to approach a sleeping walrus, how to keep a small fire burning through a storm with only blubber and bone. But knowledge required strength, and strength required food that the island was not providing.

The camp occupied a gravel spit on the southern coast, positioned to watch for seals from the ice edge yet far enough inland to escape the pressure of shifting floes. Anderson imposed the routines Bartlett had established. Hunting parties rotated through daylight hours. Watches were posted to scan the horizon for ships. Every fragment of salvage from the sunken Karluk—canvas, rope, wood, the remaining rifles and ammunition—was inventoried, guarded, rationed. For ten days, perhaps fourteen, the structure held. Men who had marched across pressure ridges and survived their ship’s destruction found purpose in building shelters, repairing boots with sinew and canvas, tending the small seal-oil fires that provided their only warmth.

Then the seals stopped appearing.

The ice that had ringed the island in March broke into leads of dark water. These openings should have brought ringed seals to the surface, animals breathing at their holes, within range of patient hunters. Instead the leads remained empty. The walrus that Kuraluk expected at the western cape had migrated elsewhere, or sensed the human presence and kept their distance. The polar bear tracks that crossed the snow led nowhere, or to animals that vanished before the hunters could approach. By early April the daily meat ration had shrunk from a pound to half a pound, then to scraps boiled into broth that offered more warmth than nourishment.

Bjarne Mamen kept a diary through these weeks. The young Norwegian ski champion had joined the expedition as athletic instructor, trained for endurance rather than starvation. His entries track the physical decay with the precision he once applied to training schedules. The handwriting steadies, then wavers. On April 7 he notes pain in his knees when walking to the beach. By April 15 he records that two sailors have developed the dark spots on their legs that mark the disease’s advance. The word “scurvy” does not appear. It does not need to.

The illness followed its own calendar. First the gums softened and bled, then teeth loosened in their sockets. Old wounds reopened. Skin bruised at the pressure of a sleeping bag. Men who had hauled sledges across thirty miles of ice in a single march found themselves unable to climb the low ridge behind camp. The mental effects arrived with the physical: irritability, depression, an obsession with food that crowded out other thought. In the diaries that survived, the later weeks of April read as a collective narrowing of consciousness. The world reduced itself to the next meal, the next hour of warmth, the next scan of the empty horizon.

—-

The camp’s social structure began to fail at its center. Anderson held his commission from the Canadian government. Bartlett had recognized him as second-in-command before departing. But Bartlett’s authority had rested on more than rank: his demonstrated competence in ice navigation, his personal history of Arctic survival, his willingness to share the physical labor of his men. Anderson possessed none of these. He was first officer of a ship that no longer existed, serving an expedition whose leader had abandoned it, waiting for rescue that depended on a journey of uncertain length across ice that might not allow passage.

William McKinlay observed this erosion with the particular attention of a man who had never fully accepted naval hierarchy. The young Scottish magnetician shared a cramped shelter with two other scientists. His diary records the small provocations that accumulated into breach. Anderson’s assignments to hunting parties were questioned. His distribution of the remaining tobacco, the last luxury and final currency of morale, was challenged. Men who would have accepted such judgments from Bartlett found them intolerable from his deputy.

The Inuit families maintained their own shelter, following patterns of work and rest that predated the expedition. Kuraluk hunted when his strength allowed, but the scurvy had weakened him too, and his wife Kataktovik could not compensate for his failing efforts. The children, Helen and Mugpi, suffered quietly, their symptoms less advanced but their small reserves of body fat nearly exhausted. Pannigabluk, the apprentice hunter, attempted to maintain the equipment and to teach the naval men what she knew of ice travel, knowledge they might need if rescue failed to arrive.

By late April the camp had divided into factions without formal declaration. Anderson retained nominal command, but his orders were increasingly ignored or modified in execution. Hunting parties went out when individuals chose, not when scheduled. The communal stores, portioned carefully under Bartlett’s system, were raided by men who believed, perhaps correctly, that they would not survive to face punishment. McKinlay recorded one such incident on April 24: a sailor named Breddy discovered with a hoard of biscuit extracted from a cache marked for the Inuit families. The confrontation ended without violence, but the accusation hung in the air, another thread in the fabric of mutual suspicion.

—-

The external world continued its rotation, indifferent to their situation. The diaries note dates that pass without event: May Day, the anniversary of the Karluk’s departure from Victoria, the long Arctic day that brought light without warmth. News of the European war had reached them only through Bartlett’s departure, through his mention of a conflict that had already redrawn the maps they carried. They did not know that on April 24 American and Mexican forces had ceased fighting in Veracruz, or that Swedish elections had returned a conservative government, or that a Scottish-Canadian financier named Lord Strathcona had died in London. These facts would have changed nothing. They mark only the distance between the island and the world that had forgotten it.

What they knew was ice and hunger. The spring thaw that should have brought open water and sealing instead brought fog and new snow, weather that pinned them in their shelters and consumed the last of their fuel. The scurvy advanced in waves: first the sailors who had eaten least fresh meat during the march, then the scientists whose theoretical knowledge of nutrition had not prevented deficiency, finally the Inuit themselves, whose traditional diet of raw meat and fat had been disrupted by ship’s provisions and the constraints of the ice.

Mamen’s diary records his own decline with a detachment that reads, in retrospect, as courage. By mid-May he notes that he cannot walk to the beach without assistance. His gums have receded so far that his teeth appear grotesquely elongated. The joint pain has spread to his hips and shoulders, making sleep impossible without opium from the medical stores. He writes still, in brief entries, tracking the condition of others as his own fails. On May 20 he records that two sailors have begun to cough blood, a symptom of advanced scurvy that he recognizes as approaching death.

—-

The psychology of indefinite waiting has been documented in other contexts: prisoners of war, castaways, besieged populations. The pattern repeats with minor variations. Purposeful activity and belief in imminent rescue give way to routine and diminished expectation. Morale stabilizes at a lower level, sustained by habit and the absence of alternatives. Then, if waiting extends beyond credible hope, comes fragmentation: collapse of social bonds, turn toward private survival, loss of the collective identity that had made endurance possible.

On Wrangel Island, the transition between these phases occurred in late May and early June. The specific trigger is harder to identify than the result. Perhaps it was the death of the first sailor, a man named Morris, who had been among the strongest before the scurvy found him. Perhaps it was a hunting expedition that consumed the last usable ammunition without result. Perhaps it was simply the mathematics of the calendar: three months since Bartlett’s departure, no sign of the rescue he had promised, the Arctic summer advancing toward its brief peak with no vessel on the horizon.

Anderson’s authority, already strained, broke under this pressure. McKinlay’s diary records a confrontation on June 3. Anderson proposed moving the entire party to the island’s eastern shore, where he believed, on evidence no one else found convincing, that sealing might improve. The proposal was rejected by vote of the remaining able-bodied men, a procedural innovation unthinkable under Bartlett’s command. Anderson acquiesced, but the acquiescence marked him as a commander who could be overruled. The camp’s remaining structure dissolved into loose association.

The Inuit families, observing this collapse, made their own calculations. Kuraluk had stopped hunting; his joints had frozen in the positions that scurvy imposed, and his wife could no longer assist him to stand. The children were fed from remaining communal stores, but the stores were nearly exhausted. Pannigabluk attempted to maintain the hunting effort alone, traveling the ice edge on feet that bled with each step, returning empty-handed to a shelter where the others waited with faces she no longer recognized.

—-

The historical record of these weeks is fragmentary, dependent on diaries composed under conditions of physical and mental extremity. Some entries were lost or destroyed. Others were written in shorthand that their authors could no longer decipher. The official expedition records had departed with Bartlett. What remained was personal testimony, shaped by the perspectives and limitations of its creators.

What emerges is not a single narrative but overlapping accounts, each with its own center. McKinlay emphasizes leadership failure, the absence of the captain who might have imposed order. Mamen, when he could still write, focused on physical symptoms, the body as a machine run out of necessary materials. Later sailors’ accounts stress the injustice of their situation: abandonment by Stefansson, the desperate march, final stranding on an island that appeared on no reliable chart.

These perspectives are not mutually exclusive. The disaster was simultaneously a failure of command, a physical ordeal, and an injustice. The argument that it originated in Stefansson’s abandonment of the ship does not require denying other causes. It requires only holding multiple explanations in suspension, recognizing that ice and scurvy and hunger would have killed regardless, but that the manner of dying, the social form of suffering, was shaped by human choices made long before the ship sank.

—-

By mid-June the camp had settled into final equilibrium of exhaustion and waiting. The able-bodied had been reduced to a handful: McKinlay himself, moving with difficulty but still capable of short walks; two sailors whose scurvy had progressed more slowly; Pannigabluk, whose youth and traditional diet had preserved some reserve. The others were confined to their shelters, fed from last stores by caregivers whose own strength was failing. The dead, Morris and two others, lay in graves above the high tide line, marked by stones that the living lacked energy to shape.

The island’s brief summer surrounded them with false abundance. Snow retreated from gravel ridges, revealing tundra of moss and lichen that supported no animal large enough to eat. Sea ice broke into drifting floes, but the seals that should have populated the leads remained absent, or invisible, or beyond range of hunters who could no longer walk steadily. The sun circled the sky without setting, its light harsh and unrelenting, offering no relief from cold that persisted through months other latitudes called summer.

Anderson had withdrawn into the shelter he shared with other officers, emerging only to receive reports he no longer had authority to act upon. His diary from this period, preserved among expedition papers, consists mainly of weather observations and dates of deaths, recorded with a formality that reads as dissociation or as last residue of naval discipline. He did not write of his own symptoms, but McKinlay noted that the first officer’s gums had begun to bleed in early June, and that his walk had developed the characteristic waddle of advanced disease.

The silence of the camp was broken only by necessary communication, by the coughing of the sick, by wind that crossed the island without obstacle or variation. The psychological literature of survival describes this phase as “the long middle”: after initial crisis, before final resolution, when time itself becomes the enemy, when hope must be maintained without evidence, when social bonds are tested by indefinite duration. On Wrangel Island, the long middle extended through June and into July, its end visible to the reader but not to its inhabitants, who faced each morning with the same question and each evening with the same absence of answer.

—-

The concrete details of daily life in this period matter because they constitute the reality that survivors later remembered and that the dead did not survive to forget. The shelters, built from ship’s canvas and driftwood, leaked in the snowmelt and required constant repair. The stoves, improvised from metal sheeting, consumed fuel faster than hunters could replenish it. The clothing, worn through by months of ice travel, was patched with canvas fragments until the patches themselves needed patching. These material conditions shaped survival more directly than any abstract quality of morale or leadership, though the two were connected: social order could not be maintained when the physical basis of existence had eroded so far.

The Inuit families experienced this erosion differently, though no less severely. Their traditional knowledge of Arctic survival, accumulated through generations, had been disrupted by the expedition’s reliance on European technology. The rifles that should have provided food required ammunition that was running out. The clothing that should have protected against cold had been manufactured for a different climate and different patterns of movement. Kuraluk and Kataktovik found themselves dependent on the same failing system as the naval men, their skills devalued by circumstances they had not chosen.

Pannigabluk’s later testimony, recorded by expedition investigators, describes this period with directness that European diaries often lack. She remembered the children’s questions about when they would eat, the silence of adults who had no answers, the slow recognition that the captain who had left might not return. Her own efforts to hunt, to find seal holes in the ice, to wait at breathing cracks for animals that did not come, consumed energy that she might have used for care of the sick. The choice between these imperatives was not a choice at all, but rotation of impossible demands.

—-

This period of suspended animation was a distinct phase of the disaster with its own causal weight, and this claim rests on evidence of transformation. The men who had marched across the ice in January, who had established Shipwreck Camp and organized evacuation to Wrangel Island, were not the same men who waited in the summer shelters of June. Their bodies had been altered by scurvy and hunger. Their social relations had been restructured by collapse of authority and failure of collective purpose. Their expectations had been adjusted, downward and downward again, to a point where survival itself had become the only imaginable goal.

This transformation prepared the ground for events that followed, though their specific form could not have been predicted. The deaths that occurred in July and August were not inevitable consequences of the June condition, but they were made possible by it. A camp with intact leadership, with adequate food, with medical supplies to treat scurvy, might have maintained its population through summer into the rescue that eventually arrived. The camp that actually existed could not.

Historical judgment of this period must therefore be double. The survivors cannot be blamed for physical conditions that constrained them: absence of game, persistence of cold, progress of disease. These were given by Arctic environment, by shipwreck, by accumulated consequences of decisions made before their stranding. But the social failure of the camp, the erosion of authority, the fragmentation into competing groups, the abandonment of collective discipline, was a human product shaped by individual choices and absence of choices that others might have made.

Anderson’s leadership, in particular, must be assessed against the standard that Bartlett had established and that Anderson himself had observed. The first officer was not a cruel or incompetent man; his later career suggests capacities that Wrangel Island circumstances did not allow him to exercise. But he was not Bartlett. He lacked the captain’s experience, his personal authority, his demonstrated willingness to share physical hardship. The comparison is unfair but necessary, because the men themselves made it, and because their survival depended on leadership that Anderson could not provide.

—-

The final weeks of June brought no relief and no visible change. Diaries record dates with mechanical regularity suggesting habit rather than expectation of rescue. Weather remained cold and overcast, sea ice drifted past the island’s coast, hunters returned empty-handed from expeditions that consumed more energy than they replaced. The sick grew sicker; the able-bodied grew weaker; the children grew silent.

McKinlay, in the shelter he shared with other scientists, composed what he believed might be his final entries. He wrote of his family in Scotland, of magnetic observations that would never be completed, of theoretical questions about Arctic climate that the expedition had been designed to answer. These passages read as testament rather than diary, the voice of a man who had accepted the probability of his own death and sought to leave record of its circumstances. He did not blame Anderson, or Stefansson, or the ice itself. He described what he saw and what he felt, with precision that later readers can recognize as resistance: the refusal to surrender to confusion and despair that surrounded him.

In the last days of June, a sailor named Maurer developed symptoms that Mamen recognized as terminal: the bleeding gums, the loosened teeth, the characteristic odor of advanced scurvy that Arctic veterans knew as the smell of approaching death. He was moved to a separate shelter to spare the others the sound of his final hours. The camp’s last reserves of opium were allocated to ease his pain. On June 28, McKinlay recorded that Maurer had stopped speaking, that his breathing had become irregular, that the waiting had entered its final phase for at least one man. The others watched through the endless daylight, measuring their own symptoms against his, calculating their own remaining time against the mathematics of a rescue that had not come.

The silent, depleted camp on Wrangel Island in late June 1914, its occupants physically weakened and socially fractured, waits under a sky that offers no sign of Bartlett or rescue.
