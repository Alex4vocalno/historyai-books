Chapter 5

Stefansson Walks Away

The Karluk was now more than trapped. She had become something else entirely: a wooden hull suspended in a field of forces that answered to no human schedule, her masts tracing slow circles against a sky that offered no bearings. For five weeks she had drifted westward through the Beaufort Sea, her engines dead, her screw locked in ice, her timbers transmitting the continuous low-frequency groan of floes grinding against her sides. On the evening of September 19, 1913, she lay off the Alaska coast near the Colville River, still more than two hundred miles from Herschel Island, still held in a pack that had begun to harden with the approach of winter.

Vilhjalmur Stefansson stood in the chart house with a rifle in his hands, checking the action. The weapon was a. 30-30 Winchester, standard for Arctic hunting, and he worked the lever with the methodical attention of a man who had learned that equipment failures in cold country could be fatal. Around him, the expedition’s photographer and two seamen loaded a small sledge with sleeping bags, a tent, cooking gear, and ten days’ rations. The two Inuit hunters, Keraluk and Kataktovik, watched without comment. They had been engaged at Point Hope for their knowledge of ice conditions and animal movements, their skill with dog teams, their languages that connected the coastal settlements of northern Alaska with the Canadian Arctic. Now they were being asked to follow a qallunaat leader onto shore ice that was already treacherous with new snow and the freeze-thaw cycles of autumn.

Stefansson had announced his decision that afternoon. The ship’s fresh meat was nearly exhausted. The men had subsisted for weeks on preserved provisions—salt pork, canned vegetables, ship’s biscuit—while the tundra visible on clear days promised caribou and muskoxen that no hunting party had yet reached. He would lead a small group ashore to secure game. The stated purpose was logistical: replenish the larder, return within days. The composition of the party suggested something else. A photographer and two Inuit hunters made sense for a serious hunting expedition. The inclusion of two seamen, rather than additional scientists or officers, hinted at mobility and self-sufficiency that a brief foray would not require.

Captain Robert Bartlett received the news on the bridge, where he had been plotting the ship’s drift. He was forty years old, a Newfoundlander who had learned ice navigation from his uncle in the sealing fleets, had commanded Peary’s Roosevelt to within 150 miles of the North Pole, had spent more of his adult life in the Arctic than out of it. He understood immediately what the departure meant, even if he could not yet know how permanent it would prove. The Karluk was drifting west at approximately ten miles per day. In three days, she could be fifty miles from this position. In a week, she could be in the Chukchi Sea, beyond any hope of reunion until spring. A hunting party that failed to return promptly would find itself separated from the ship by an expanse of moving ice that no message could cross.

Stefansson handed him a letter. The language was precise, bureaucratic, the prose of a man who had spent years negotiating with government departments. Bartlett was instructed that, if the ship should move from its present position, he should “send a party ashore, to erect one or more beacons giving information of the ship’s whereabouts.” The instruction said nothing about when the hunting party would return. It said nothing about who commanded in Stefansson’s absence. It treated the Karluk as a fixed point, a base camp, rather than a vessel adrift in a medium that transformed every hour of delay into measurable separation.

Bartlett read it twice. Then he folded the paper and placed it in his jacket pocket. He would comply with the instruction. He would also understand, with the clarity that experience provides, that the expedition’s formal structure had just been severed by a cut that no organizational chart could repair.

—-

The morning of September 20 dawned gray and still. On deck, the crew gathered with the particular attention that men in confined spaces give to any alteration in routine. William McKinlay, the expedition’s twenty-four-year-old magnetician and meteorologist, watched from the rail. He was a Scot, educated in Glasgow, on his first Arctic voyage. In his diary that evening he would record the scene with the particularity of a man who sensed that he was witnessing something whose significance he did not yet understand.

Stefansson appeared on deck at 8:30 a.m., dressed for travel: fur parka, caribou-skin boots, the Winchester slung across his back. He moved among the preparations with the energy of a man embarking on a familiar routine, checking the sledge’s lashings, distributing weight for balance over rough ice. The Inuit hunters stood apart from this activity, their faces offering no interpretation that the watching crew could read. They had their own calculus of Arctic travel, their own experience of qallunaat leaders who made decisions based on maps and schedules that the ice did not recognize.

At 9 a.m., the party departed. They moved across the floe toward a low point on the coast, their figures already diminished by the flat light and the vast geometry of the Arctic plain. McKinlay noted the time, the weather, the composition of the group. He did not speculate about Stefansson’s intentions. The expedition leader had said he would return with meat. The expedition leader had said many things in the months since Nome—about the ice conditions they would encounter, about the ship’s fitness for the voyage, about the scientific program that would proceed regardless of obstacles. McKinlay was young enough to still believe that stated intentions corresponded to future actions.

The men on deck continued to watch after the party had become indistinguishable from the terrain. Their eyes tracked a blankness where something had been. The Karluk sat in her frozen berth, her masts bare, her flag hanging limp, her population reduced by a quarter in a single departure. The sledge’s track, visible for a few minutes as a dark line on the snow, would be erased by wind within hours.

On the bridge, Bartlett took a sighting and marked the position: approximately 70°40’ N, 146°20’ W. The ship was drifting west-northwest, her course determined by wind and current acting on the pack ice that held her. At this rate, she would pass Point Barrow within two weeks, would enter the Chukchi Sea, would continue toward waters that no rescue vessel could reach until the following summer. The geometry was inexorable. The hunting party, if it failed to return within forty-eight hours, would find itself separated from the ship by a gap that widened with every hour of daylight.

Bartlett understood this. Whether Stefansson understood it, or chose to disregard it, or believed that his own mobility and knowledge could overcome any separation that the ice imposed, would become the subject of later dispute. What was visible on September 20 was only action: a man with a rifle walking toward land, a ship continuing her drift, a gap opening between intention and consequence that would widen with every hour.

—-

The afternoon brought weather. A wind rose from the northeast, pushing the pack against the Alaska coast, grinding floe against floe with a sound that carried through the ship’s hull as a continuous bass note. Visibility dropped to a few hundred yards. The temperature fell steadily. By evening, the shore where Stefansson’s party had landed was no longer visible from the deck, obscured by blowing snow and the gathering darkness of an Arctic autumn that would soon extinguish daylight entirely.

Bartlett ordered double watches. The ice was moving more actively than it had in weeks, the pressure building in ways that could crush a wooden hull without warning. Men who had slept through the long drift now found themselves awake, listening, interpreting every shudder of the timbers. The Karluk had survived such pressures before, in her whaling years in the Okhotsk Sea. But she had never been so far from help, so late in the season, so deprived of the leadership that had organized her voyage.

In his cabin, McKinlay wrote his diary entry for September 20 with a pen that required warming in his palm before the ink would flow. He noted the departure, the weather change, the uncertainty that had settled over the ship. He did not speculate about Stefansson’s intentions. The young scientist recorded what he saw and left interpretation for a future that he could not yet imagine.

Other men on the Karluk held different views. The seamen, mostly Newfoundlanders like their captain, had their own experience of Arctic travel. They knew that hunting parties sometimes failed to return, that ice sometimes closed behind a departure, that leaders sometimes made decisions that served their own survival at the expense of those they left behind. They said little, but their silence on deck that evening had a quality of assessment, of waiting for evidence that would confirm or refute what their years on sealing and fishing vessels suggested.

The night of September 20–21 brought a gale. The Karluk shuddered in her ice cradle, her hull timbers working against the pressure points where floes had jammed against her sides. Bartlett spent the hours moving between deck and bridge, the helm, the engine room, the bow where the ice was most active. He could feel the ship’s position changing in the altered rhythm of the grinding, in the new angle of the floes relative to the bow. By morning, they had drifted several miles west of where Stefansson had departed. The shore was no longer visible at all.

On September 21, Bartlett sent a party ashore as the letter instructed. They found the beach empty, the snow undisturbed except by wind. No beacon had been erected. No message waited. The hunting party had vanished into the interior, following caribou trails that led away from the coast, away from the ship, away from any position that the Karluk would occupy in the coming days.

The seamen who made this landing erected beacons as ordered: cairns of stone and ice, messages in bottles placed within. They marked a position that was already becoming obsolete, a coordinate in a system that had moved on. When they returned to the ship, their report confirmed what Bartlett already knew. The separation was no longer potential. It was actual, measurable, irreversible for the duration of the winter that approached.

—-

The gale continued through September 22. The ship’s drift accelerated, carrying her past the last landmarks that connected her to the Alaskan coast. The ice pack was breaking into larger fields, opening leads that froze overnight, rearranging itself according to pressures that no observation from deck could fully interpret. Bartlett worked with the tools available: soundings when leads opened, observations of the stars when clouds permitted, estimates of drift based on the movement of recognizable floes. He was navigating a ship that could not move, locating a vessel that had no destination, responsible for men who had been told to expect leadership that had walked away.

On September 23, the weather cleared. The Karluk lay in a transformed landscape, surrounded by ice that showed no resemblance to the configuration of three days before. The Alaska coast was somewhere to the south and east, no longer visible, no longer relevant to the ship’s immediate situation. They were in the Beaufort Sea’s central pack, drifting west toward the Chukchi Sea, toward Wrangel Island, toward the Siberian coast—toward any of the possible endpoints that Arctic currents might provide, none of them reachable by human effort until spring.

Stefansson had not returned. No signal had been received. The beacons erected on September 21 stood on a shore that the ship had already left behind, marking a position that no longer corresponded to anything in the moving world of the ice.

In the expedition’s later narratives, this moment would be described in various ways. Stefansson, writing from Washington in 1921, would present his departure as a necessary response to circumstances, a decision to continue the expedition’s scientific work by other means while the ship remained immobilized. The Karluk, he would argue, was already lost when he left her; his duty was to salvage what he could of the larger mission. Bartlett, writing in 1916 with the authority of a captain who had brought eleven men through the winter, would describe the departure as an abandonment, a rupture in the chain of command that left him responsible for consequences he had not chosen. McKinlay, whose diary became the most detailed contemporary record, would struggle for years to reconcile what he had seen with what he later learned.

What all accounts share is the image of separation: the six figures diminishing across the ice, the ship continuing her drift, the two movements diverging according to different imperatives.

—-

Stefansson had organized and directed the Canadian Arctic Expedition, 1913–1916, to explore the regions west of Parry Archipelago for the Government of Canada. The Karluk was his flagship, his mobile base, his instrument for reaching the areas that hydrographic knowledge had not yet charted. When he stepped onto the ice on September 20, he was stepping away from this instrument, betting that he could achieve his purposes without it.

This was not a sudden decision. The evidence of the voyage’s first two months shows Stefansson adjusting his plans continuously in response to the ice, the ship’s limitations, the accumulating gap between his ambitions and his resources. He had acquired the Karluk for $10, 000, a bargain price that reflected her age and her specialized design for whaling rather than polar navigation. He had accepted her when the Canadian government, assuming responsibility for the expedition, required a vessel that could be sold back at cost. These constraints had shaped the expedition from its inception, pressing a program of extensive regional exploration into a hull never intended for winter imprisonment.

The ice had forced a choice that the expedition’s structure could not accommodate. Stefansson could remain with the ship, sharing the passive drift and the diminishing rations, waiting for spring release that might never come. Or he could attempt to continue the active program of exploration and scientific collection that justified the expedition’s existence. The hunting party provided formal cover for the second option. Whether Stefansson intended from the first to make his departure permanent, or whether he adapted his intentions to circumstances as they developed, became a question that surviving documents cannot finally answer.

What is visible in the record is the consequence: a ship without its expedition leader, a crew without the authority structure they had been recruited to serve, a captain whose responsibilities had been defined by contract and now extended by default into areas that no contract anticipated. The expedition’s formal chain of command had been designed for a vessel under way, communicating with its base, operating within the networked world of early twentieth-century Arctic exploration. The ice had severed these connections. Stefansson’s departure had severed them further.

—-

On September 23, with the gale past and the ship’s new position fixed by observation, Bartlett called his officers to the chart room. The Karluk was now definitively separated from the hunting party. Whatever Stefansson’s intentions, he could not rejoin the ship without a journey across ice that was already becoming impassable, in weather that was already becoming lethal. The ship’s company must proceed as if they would not see him again until spring—if then.

The meeting established routines that would govern the coming months: watches for ice pressure, conservation of fuel and provisions, maintenance of morale through organized activity. Bartlett assigned responsibilities according to competence rather than the expedition’s original hierarchy. The scientists would assist with navigation and observation; the seamen would learn the instruments of meteorology and oceanography. The Karluk would become a floating station, collecting data that might justify her imprisonment, maintaining the forms of scientific work that gave purpose to her confinement.

This transformation was practical and psychological. A ship in the ice could easily become a prison, her crew sinking into the apathy that polar winters induced. Bartlett had seen it on other vessels, had read the accounts of expeditions that had dissolved into factions, mutiny, madness. His response was to impose structure, to fill the hours with necessary labor, to maintain the distinction between a ship under command and a collection of individuals waiting for rescue.

On deck, the crew watched the empty horizon where Stefansson had disappeared. Some had known him personally, had been recruited by his enthusiasm, had believed in the expedition’s promise of discovery and advancement. Others had signed for wages and experience, indifferent to the larger purposes that their labor served. All of them, now, shared a common situation: dependent on a ship that could not move, commanded by a captain who had inherited rather than chosen his authority, abandoned by the man whose vision had organized their voyage.

The ice continued to move. The Karluk drifted westward through waters that no surface vessel had navigated in winter, carrying her company toward a fate that none of them had imagined when they sailed from Nome in July. The hunting party, somewhere on the Alaska coast or moving inland toward the caribou grounds, pursued its own trajectory according to plans that had not been shared with those left behind.

This division would shape everything that followed. The Karluk’s subsequent drift, her crushing and sinking in January 1914, the desperate march to Wrangel Island, the deaths that reduced her company by nearly half—all would be experienced by men who had already been abandoned once, who had learned that leadership could walk away, that rescue could not be assumed, that survival depended on their own resources rather than the structures that had brought them to the Arctic.

On the evening of September 23, McKinlay completed his diary entry with a sentence that recorded the fact without interpreting it: “No sign of Stefansson.” The words would be repeated, with variations, through the weeks and months that followed. The expedition leader had vanished over the ice, leaving behind a ship and a mission suddenly redefined.
