Chapter 11

Camp on the Ice

The men who remained had no example to follow now, no precedent for what came next. They stood on ice that had been their ship’s deathbed, watching the last of the Karluk settle into the black water of the Beaufort Sea, and they had only their captain and their own senses to trust. What those senses reported was grim: masts tilted at forty-five degrees, deckhouse windows already lapping with dark water, the grinding of pack ice that had sheared the hull open. The temperature dropped through the afternoon of January 11, 1914, as wind swung round and brought snow in place of the mist that had shrouded the sinking. None of this suggested survival was likely.

Captain Robert Bartlett turned from the sinking and began issuing orders before the deck went under. He was fifty years old, a Newfoundlander who had spent more of his life on Arctic ice than on land. He had walked across Smith Sound in 1909 when ice crushed the Roosevelt during Peary’s polar attempt. He knew the distinction between a ship’s death and an expedition’s death, knew it had to matter enough to keep twenty-five people alive through a winter night that had barely begun.

His first orders were concrete and immediate. Direct the erection of a canvas shelter on the highest part of the floe, using the ship’s surviving boats and sleds as windbreaks. Assign watches to monitor the ice for cracks. Guard the salvaged supplies against polar bears already circling the camp. Establish a ration: four ounces of pemmican per man per day, with biscuit and tea measured with the same precision. The scientists and staff who had joined for adventure or advancement now found themselves subject to naval discipline, enforced without explanation or appeal.

The camp that emerged was never meant to be permanent. Bartlett had deposited stores on the ice throughout the autumn, anticipating that the Karluk might not survive the winter. By January 11, two shelters already stood on the floe: one a snow igloo roofed with canvas, the other built from packing cases scavenged from the ship’s hold. To this second structure, the men now dragged what they could salvage before the ice took it—clothing, sleeping bags, cooking gear, chronometers and sextants, radio equipment that would never transmit again through the polar night. They worked in shifts through the darkness, hands numbing in the wind, breath freezing to fur ruffs.

William McKinlay, the expedition’s young magnetician, recorded the labor in his diary without embellishment. He noted hours, temperature, physical facts. The Karluk had drifted westward for four months since August, carried by pack ice that moved according to its own logic of pressure and current. Stefansson, the expedition’s leader, had left in September with a hunting party, claiming he would return with caribou. He had taken the best sleds, the fittest dogs, five of the fittest men. The ice had carried the Karluk away from any land he might reach, and no caribou had appeared. McKinlay wrote the facts without commentary, but the shape of his entries changed after the sinking. Where before he had recorded scientific observations and personal impressions, now he listed stores and noted the behavior of the ice.

The floe that supported Shipwreck Camp was perhaps a mile across, a temporary island in a sea of shifting plates. At night, the men heard it moving: pressure ridges building where floe met floe, leads opening with sounds like gunshots as wind shifted. Bartlett posted sentries to watch for these cracks, knowing their camp could be divided in minutes, that men sleeping in one shelter might wake to find the other shelter drifting away across open water. The watches ran two hours, rotated through the entire company, with no exemptions. Scientists who had expected to conduct independent research found themselves hauling ice for water, standing guard in darkness, mending canvas that wind shredded. Some accepted the necessity. Others resented it.

The resentment stayed unspoken, for now. Bartlett’s authority rested on expertise and demonstrated willingness to share hardship. He stood the same watches, ate the same rations, slept in the same crowded shelter. But the camp’s structure imposed distinctions that could not be ignored. The crew—sailors who had worked the Karluk’s decks and engine room—possessed skills the scientists lacked. They knew how to rig shelters against wind, repair sleds, read ice for safe travel. The scientists, for their part, had joined with contracts and expectations, with understanding that their work would advance knowledge and careers. Now they found themselves dependent on naval routine for survival, their instruments packed in crates they might never open, their specializations irrelevant to the immediate problem of remaining alive.

Bartlett understood this tension without naming it. He had commanded mixed expeditions before, had seen how polar winter isolation could magnify small irritations into dangerous divisions. His response was to crowd the schedule with necessary labor, to leave no hours unassigned, to make exhaustion substitute for contemplation. The camp’s routine began before dawn, when the cook—an Inuk named Kuraluk—lit the stove and began melting ice for water. Breakfast was pemmican and biscuit, eaten in shifts because the shelters could not hold everyone at once. Then came the day’s work: repairing gear, building additional windbreaks, hunting seal or bear when weather permitted, hauling ice, mending clothing, checking stores for cold damage. The afternoon brought more of the same, and evening brought the watches that continued through the night.

This routine was not sustainable. The pemmican would last perhaps three months at current ration. The biscuit already showed spoilage, fat in the pemmican going rancid from temperature fluctuations. The men’s clothing, designed for shipboard use rather than life on the ice, shredded at seams. And the ice itself was unstable, drifting westward at a rate Bartlett could calculate but not control. They were somewhere north of the Siberian coast, somewhere west of Alaska, somewhere in the space between charts. The nearest known land was Wrangel Island, perhaps a hundred miles southwest, perhaps two hundred—the distance changed with every observation, because the ice moved and their instruments could not fix position with certainty.

Bartlett made his calculations in private, using surviving chronometers and occasional glimpses of sun or stars through overcast. He did not share conclusions with the company. What would be the purpose? They could not steer the floe. They could only wait for conditions that would allow them to leave it, and those conditions—stable ice, clear weather, visible landmarks—had not yet arrived. The men knew this without being told. They could see leads opening and closing around their island of ice. They could feel temperature drop when wind came from the north, bringing air that had crossed the polar basin. They could measure their own weakness as rations took effect, as calories consumed failed to match calories expended in the cold.

The Inuit hunters—Kuraluk and his family, and the hunter Kataktovik—adapted to this life with the flexibility of people who had never expected permanence. They built their own shelter apart from the main camp, using snow blocks in the traditional way, and hunted when weather allowed, bringing seal meat that supplemented the pemmican ration. Their presence was essential and awkward. They possessed skills the qallunaat—the white men—lacked, yet they occupied an ambiguous position in the camp’s hierarchy, neither crew nor staff nor passengers. Bartlett consulted them about ice conditions and hunting prospects, and their advice was usually sound. But the social distance between Inuit and expedition members could not be entirely bridged by necessity, and their separate shelter marked a boundary that both sides observed.

The scientists’ response to their situation varied according to temperament and prior experience. Some, like McKinlay, found relief in the reduction of their world to immediate physical problems. The magnetism of polar regions, the atmospheric electricity he had come to study—these abstractions receded before concrete demands of keeping warm and fed. Others struggled against the narrowing of their horizons, continuing to make observations when they could, recording data that might never be used, clinging to forms of scientific work as defense against despair. Still others simply waited, conserving energy, watching Bartlett for signs of what would come next.

What came next, in those first weeks of Shipwreck Camp, was more of the same. The routine ground on, rations diminished, ice drifted westward. Polar bears continued to circle, sometimes approaching close enough to be shot, more often vanishing into whiteout when wind rose. The men learned to sleep through sounds of ice, to wake instantly when a sentry called out, to eat meager portions without complaint. They learned which companions could be relied upon in crisis and which could not, who shared rations and who hoarded, who kept watch faithfully and who fell asleep at post. These discoveries were made in silence, through observation rather than conversation, because cold made extended talk exhausting and because the things learned were not always comfortable.

Bartlett watched this process without intervening. He had selected his officers before the sinking, designating first mate Sandy Anderson as his second in command and assigning specific responsibilities to others. These designations meant less now than they had on the ship, when chain of command was clear and division of labor obvious. On the ice, survival required different qualities: physical endurance, emotional stability, practical ingenuity. Some officers possessed these qualities. Others did not, and Bartlett adjusted his reliance accordingly, without announcement or explanation. The adjustments were noticed, and they contributed to silent currents of resentment and loyalty that ran beneath the camp’s surface.

The physical deterioration of the men was gradual but visible. They lost weight, faces growing gaunt above fur ruffs of their parkas. Hands cracked and bled in the dry cold, healing slowly because rations lacked fat. Eyes suffered from glare of snow in brief hours of twilight, and from strain of watching for bears and cracks in darkness. The youngest and fittest adapted best; older men, and those who had carried extra weight, struggled to maintain strength. Bartlett, lean and hardened by decades of Arctic travel, showed effects least, but he was not immune. He slept less than anyone, rising to check the ice himself when sentries reported movement, working through the day at tasks that younger men found exhausting.

The camp’s location on the floe was chosen for elevation and stability, but elevation was relative and stability temporary. The highest point was perhaps ten feet above sea level, a ridge of pressure ice that offered some protection from flooding if the floe should break. Shelters were clustered on this ridge, with stores arranged in a pattern that would allow quick evacuation if necessary. Bartlett had planned for this contingency, designating which supplies would be saved and which abandoned, which men would go in which direction if the ice split. These plans were not shared with the entire company, but they existed, and the knowledge that they existed provided comfort to those who knew of them.

The question of rescue was never far from anyone’s mind, though rarely discussed. The Karluk had carried a radio, but it had not functioned since before the sinking, its antenna brought down by ice or wind. No message had reached the outside world since August, when Bartlett had wired their position from the ship. The expedition’s organizers in Ottawa and Victoria knew only that the Karluk was trapped in the ice, drifting westward, beyond communication. They would assume the worst, or assume Stefansson had the situation in hand, or assume nothing because they had no information on which to base assumptions. The possibility of a search expedition was remote. No ship could reach them through winter ice. No aircraft existed that could operate in these conditions. They were alone, and would remain alone until they could reach land or until ice carried them within range of human habitation.

This solitude had a quality the men struggled to describe in their diaries. It was not simply absence of other people, though that was part of it. It was absence of any sign that other people existed, that their own existence was noticed or mattered. The ice extended to every horizon, white and empty, moving slowly according to forces the men could not see or influence. The sky stayed gray or black, occasionally clearing to reveal stars whose positions had shifted from familiar patterns as the floe drifted north and west. The sun had not risen for weeks, and would not rise for weeks more. Watches and meals marked time now, the rotation of labor and the slow depletion of stores, not any natural cycle the body could recognize.

Bartlett had experienced this solitude before, had learned to manage its psychological effects through routine and forward planning. He kept his own diary, recording observations and calculations that would be useful if they survived, maintaining habits of documentation that distinguished professional explorers from mere survivors. But he also allowed himself to imagine the future, to plan for contingencies that might never arise, to construct in his mind a chain of decisions that would lead to rescue. This mental work was as necessary as physical labor, and he protected time for it, rising early or staying late in the shelter while others slept.

The camp’s social organization crystallized gradually, through repetition rather than design. Certain men became associated with certain tasks: one reliable for hunting, another for mechanical repair, a third for medical knowledge acquired as ship’s surgeon. These associations were not formalized, but they were recognized, and they created structure that supplemented Bartlett’s authority. Men who possessed essential skills were treated with deference, their opinions sought on matters within their competence. Men who lacked such skills found other ways to contribute, or they withdrew into themselves, becoming peripheral to the camp’s functioning.

The withdrawal was dangerous, Bartlett knew. A man who ceased to participate in collective labor became a burden, and burdens in these conditions could become deadly. He watched for signs: the man who stopped volunteering for tasks, who ate alone, who slept through calls for watch. He intervened when he could, assigning such men to work with others, creating situations that required cooperation. Sometimes this worked. Sometimes it did not, and the withdrawn man continued his retreat, becoming a ghost in the camp, present in body but absent in spirit.

The first three weeks of Shipwreck Camp brought no dramatic crisis, no single event that could be identified as turning point. Ice continued to drift, rations continued to diminish, men continued to adapt to diminished circumstances. Temperature dropped and rose according to wind’s direction, averaging thirty degrees below zero Fahrenheit, occasionally plunging lower. Polar bears came and went, sometimes providing fresh meat, more often vanishing before they could be approached. Leads opened and closed, never quite reaching the camp, always threatening to reach it. And Bartlett continued to calculate, to plan, to wait for conditions that would allow him to act.

Those conditions, when they came, would require a decision he had not yet fully formed. The camp was temporary by necessity. They could not survive on the ice indefinitely; stores would not last, and the ice itself was moving toward conditions that would make it impassable. Land existed to the southwest, Wrangel Island, a place he had never seen but knew from accounts of previous explorers. Barren and uninhabited, it offered no guarantee of rescue. But it was land, solid ground, a destination that could be reached if ice remained stable enough for travel. The alternative was to continue drifting, to hope that pack would carry them within reach of the Siberian coast or that some improbable rescue would arrive.

No option Bartlett weighed carried risks he could calculate but not control. A march to Wrangel Island would consume remaining stores, expose them to full violence of polar winter, require perfect conditions of ice and weather that might never coincide. Remaining on the floe would preserve strength but prolong dependence on an environment that had already destroyed their ship. And underlying both options was the question of leadership: whether men would follow him if he chose to march, whether the discipline he had imposed would hold when tested by extreme hardship.

He did not speak of these uncertainties. To the camp, he presented only certainty: the routine would continue, the watches would be maintained, the rations would suffice. This presentation was itself a kind of leadership, the performance of confidence that allowed others to continue functioning. But the performance required energy, and the energy was drawn from reserves that were not infinite. Bartlett felt the cost in his own body, in exhaustion that sleep could not fully relieve, in constant calculation that occupied waking hours and invaded dreams.

The men saw this without fully understanding it. They knew their captain worked harder than anyone, that he carried responsibilities they could only guess at. Some found this reassuring, proof that leadership was still possible in their extremity. Others found it oppressive, a reminder of their own dependence and helplessness. The division between these responses was not dramatic, not yet, but it existed, and it shaped small interactions of the camp: who sat with whom at meals, who shared the extra blanket, who spoke and who remained silent in crowded shelters.

By late January, the physical conditions of Shipwreck Camp had stabilized into routine that felt almost permanent. Shelters were weatherproof, stores were organized, watches functioned with mechanical regularity. This stability was deceptive, and Bartlett knew it. The ice was still moving, the stores were still diminishing, the polar night was still half over. What had been achieved was not survival but the possibility of survival, a temporary platform from which further action might be taken. Whether that action would succeed depended on factors beyond any human control: the weather, the ice, the physical capacity of men already weakened by hardship.

One evening, as Bartlett made his rounds of the sentries, he found McKinlay standing watch at the camp’s eastern edge. The magnetician had his notebook out, but he was not writing in it. He was staring into the darkness where the ice met the sky, where no line of division was visible. Bartlett stopped beside him, saying nothing. After a moment, McKinlay spoke without turning.

“The compass is useless here. The magnetic variation changes as we drift. I cannot fix our position within fifty miles.”

“I know,” Bartlett said.

“We could be drifting toward land. We could be drifting toward open water. We cannot tell which.”

“I know.”

McKinlay closed his notebook. “Then how do you decide?”

Bartlett did not answer immediately. Somewhere in the darkness, a pressure ridge cracked with a sound like distant thunder. The ice was speaking, as it always spoke, in a language of force and fracture that humans could interpret only after the fact.

“I decide when I must,” he said. “Not before.”

He moved on to the next sentry post, leaving McKinlay with his useless compass and his unanswered question. The watch continued. The ice continued its slow, indifferent drift toward whatever destination the currents and the winds had chosen for it. And in the shelters behind them, twenty-four men slept or pretended to sleep, waiting for their captain to tell them what they would do next, trusting or resenting him according to temperaments they could no longer fully control, their lives suspended between the discipline that preserved them and the uncertainty that would eventually demand a choice.
