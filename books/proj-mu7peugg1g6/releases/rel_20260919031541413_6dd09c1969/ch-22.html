Chapter 22

The Survivors’ Return to Civilization

In 1914, the survivors dispersed into this institutional vacuum, each carrying a version of events that would be tested against the versions of others in inquiries to come. Their clothing still held the grease of seal meat and the salt of Arctic fog; their boots were bound with walrus hide that had softened and tightened across nine months of improvisation.

Yet within hours of boarding the King and Winge, they encountered objects that seemed to belong to another species of existence: a bar of soap that lathered without labor, a sheet that bore no trace of previous sleepers, a cup of coffee poured from a kettle rather than rendered from bones. The smell of diesel oil from the schooner’s engine mixed with the familiar reek of blubber lamps, and in that overlap the men stood suspended between two orders of being, one they had made with their hands, one that had continued without them.

Captain Robert Bartlett was not among them. He had completed his own return three and a half months earlier, having crossed from Siberia to Alaska in May 1914 after his walk across the Chukchi ice with Kataktovik. Ice conditions had prevented his immediate landing at Nome; he had spent additional weeks aboard the whaler Herman under Captain Pedersen, waiting for the pack to release them.

When he finally reached shore on May 30, he carried the first confirmed news that the Karluk had been crushed, that men were stranded, that a rescue was imperative. He was able to send a radio message to Ottawa from St. Michael, Alaska, informing the government of the ship’s fate. Now, in September, he was already in the south, recovering from tonsillitis and preparing the ground for the testimony that would follow. The eleven survivors coming off Wrangel Island would enter a world he had begun to shape.

The King and Winge made for Nome with a speed that disoriented men who had measured distance in the labor of footsteps across shifting ice. The schooner’s motorized propeller covered in hours what had taken Bartlett and Kataktovik weeks of marching. For the survivors, the sensation was not liberation but a kind of sea-sickness of the spirit. Their bodies, trained to the economy of starvation and exertion, could not adjust to stillness and abundance. William McKinlay, the expedition’s magnetician, noted the paradox: they had dreamed of rescue as a restoration, yet found themselves diminished by the very ease they had craved. The ship’s cook offered portions that seemed grotesque after months of rationed ounces. Some men could not finish their plates. Their stomachs had shrunk to the size of their former need.

The physical examination began before they reached port. The ship’s master, Olaf Swenson, had chartered the vessel for the rescue at Bartlett’s urgent request, and he carried enough medical knowledge to see what the men themselves could not yet articulate. Malnutrition had settled into their bones with a permanence that weeks of feeding would not reverse. Frostbite had taken toes, fingers, patches of ear and nose. The skin of their faces had thickened and cracked from windburn and the reflection of ice.

More troubling was the exhaustion that seemed to originate not in muscle but in some deeper reservoir of will. McKinlay, who had kept the meteorological records through the winter and spring, found he could not concentrate on simple calculations that had once been routine. The numbers slid away from him. His mind retained the skills of survival—tracking weather, estimating distance, reading ice—while losing the capacity for abstract thought that civilization required.

They reached Nome on September 14, 1914. The town had prepared for them with the efficiency of a frontier settlement accustomed to sudden arrivals and departures. The telegraph office stood ready. Reporters from the Nome Nugget and the Daily Alaskan had established positions at the dock. The federal marshal maintained a presence to secure any official statements. What the survivors encountered was not a community sharing their relief but a machinery of reception that processed their experience into forms it could use.

The first telegrams went out within hours. McKinlay sent word to his family in Scotland, compressing nine months into the twenty-word limit of the commercial wire. Other men wrote to wives, parents, institutions. The Canadian government received notification through its agent in Victoria, who had already filed preliminary reports based on Bartlett’s May arrival. The Geographical Society in London, which had supported Stefansson’s ambitions, learned that its investment had produced not discovery but disaster. Each message traveled through a network of interpretation that the survivors could not control. The words they chose—“rescued,” “ship lost,” “eleven survivors”—acquired meanings in distant offices that diverged from their intentions.

The medical assessment at Nome’s hospital documented what the men had learned to treat as normal. Dr. L.D. Folsom, who examined them, recorded weights that ranged from sixty to eighty percent of their pre-expedition norms. Blood pressure readings suggested cardiovascular adaptation to prolonged stress that might or might not reverse. The frostbite injuries required immediate surgical attention; several men faced amputations that would be performed in Seattle or the Pacific coast cities, where facilities exceeded anything Alaska could offer. Folsom noted something else in his report, a quality he could not quantify: the men’s responses to questions arrived with a delay, as if each query had to travel through a medium of ice and darkness before reaching the part of them capable of answer.

The reporters pressed for narrative. They wanted the story in sequence: the trapping of the ship, the departure of Stefansson, the sinking, the march to the island, the winter, the rescue. The survivors found they could not supply this order. Their memories operated by association rather than chronology: the smell of a particular day, the sound of pressure in the ice, the face of a man now dead. When they attempted to construct the linear account that journalism required, they produced inconsistencies that the reporters noted with professional satisfaction. Here was material for controversy. Here were gaps that competing versions might fill.

The question of Stefansson’s departure emerged immediately. Had he abandoned his men? Had he left to hunt caribou as he claimed, or had he recognized the Karluk as doomed and saved himself? The survivors themselves held no single position. Some, particularly those who had watched him cross the ice with his chosen party in September 1913, understood the departure as abandonment. Others, mindful of his instructions to Bartlett and his promise to send help, reserved judgment. Their uncertainty did not satisfy the demand for clarity. The newspapers required villains and heroes, and the men who had actually endured the ice found themselves drafted into roles they had not chosen.

The Nome Nugget published its first account on September 15, based on interviews conducted in the hospital ward. The story emphasized Bartlett’s heroism, his walk to Siberia, his persistence in securing rescue, and treated Stefansson’s absence as a mystery demanding solution. The wire services carried the story south, where papers added editorial commentary that transformed the expedition’s scientific purposes into imperial ambition, the Canadian government’s investment into folly. Within a week, the story had reached London, where the Times placed it in the context of other polar disasters, and New York, where the Herald connected it to the growing market for adventure narrative. The survivors’ private experience had become public property.

The machinery of official response began to engage. The Canadian Department of Naval Service, which had commissioned the Karluk and bore responsibility for her crew, requested detailed reports from each survivor. The forms that arrived in Nome asked for dates, positions, decisions, expenditures of equipment and supplies. The men who filled them out discovered that their memories of the ice did not conform to bureaucratic categories. They knew when the ship had been crushed—January 11, 1914—but could not state with confidence the longitude where it occurred. They knew that Stefansson had left in September, but disagreed about whether he had taken five men or six, whether he had carried forty days of provisions or sixty. The forms required certainty; their experience offered only the accumulated doubt of months without external confirmation.

Bartlett, recovering in Seattle, followed these developments through correspondence that reached him in batches. He had already given his own account to the New York Times, published June 1, 1914, which established certain facts in the public record: the Karluk trapped August 1913, Stefansson’s departure September 1913, the drift westward, the crushing in January, his own march to Wrangel Island with those who could travel, the subsequent losses, his decision to walk for help. What he could not control was the interpretation of these facts. The newspaper had headlined his story “Bartlett’s Story of the Karluk,” implying a single perspective among many, and the competing versions that now emerged from Nome complicated the narrative he had tried to establish.

The survivors themselves began to separate according to the accounts they supported. McKinlay, who had maintained meteorological observations throughout the winter and had kept a diary that would become crucial evidence, found himself aligned with Bartlett’s interpretation by the sheer weight of his documentation. Others, who had experienced the same events without recording them, discovered that their memories were less authoritative than they had assumed. The distinction between those who had kept written records and those who had not began to operate as a social division, separating men who could prove their claims from men who could only assert them.

The physical journey south to Seattle, begun September 16 aboard the coastal steamer Victoria, extended this process of differentiation. The survivors traveled as a group but recovered at different rates. Those whose injuries required surgery went directly to hospitals; those who could walk found themselves lodged in hotels where reporters maintained constant surveillance. The ship’s passage through the Inside Passage, with its dense forests and populated settlements, produced in some men a kind of landscape shock: the density of green, the frequency of human habitation, the absence of ice. McKinlay wrote in his diary of feeling “unmoored,” a sensation that applied equally to his physical and social circumstances.

In Seattle, the machinery of reception intensified. The Canadian consul arranged official welcomes that placed the survivors in diplomatic contexts they did not understand. The Geographical Society of the Pacific Coast invited lectures that required them to transform improvised survival into structured narrative. Medical specialists at the University of Washington conducted examinations that treated their bodies as data for Arctic physiology. Each institutional engagement extracted something from their experience and converted it into forms the survivors themselves could not recognize as their own: scientific papers, newspaper features, diplomatic reports.

The competition among accounts sharpened. Stefansson, who had spent the winter and spring of 1914 exploring north of the Karluk’s drift track, arrived in Seattle in October, having learned of the disaster only upon reaching Alaska. His version of events, that he had left to hunt caribou as planned, that the ice had prevented his return, that he had continued scientific work while believing the ship safely wintering, immediately collided with Bartlett’s narrative of abandonment. The survivors found themselves summoned to validate one interpretation or the other. Their own uncertainty, their own conflicting memories, were flattened into testimony for or against.

The legal implications emerged gradually. The families of the dead—Mackay, Murray, Beuchat, Morris, Malloch, and the four who had reached Herald Island—began to inquire about compensation, about negligence, about responsibility. The Canadian government’s investment in the expedition, already substantial, now faced additional costs: survivor benefits, widow pensions, the expense of inquiries that would establish whether public funds had been misapplied. The survivors’ statements, given in hospital wards and hotel rooms and consular offices, became evidence in proceedings they had not anticipated and could not control.

What the men experienced was the translation of one form of struggle into another. On Wrangel Island, survival had been immediate and physical: finding food, maintaining shelter, enduring weather. In Seattle and Victoria and Ottawa, survival became procedural and retrospective: establishing facts, defending memories, negotiating recognition. The skills that had preserved them, improvisation, patience, the capacity to endure uncertainty, proved less applicable to these new demands than they had hoped.

McKinlay’s diary, which he had kept through the winter with the discipline of scientific observation, became a contested document. Stefansson’s supporters requested access to it; Bartlett’s allies cited it as corroboration. The diary itself, composed in conditions of exhaustion and hunger, contained gaps and contradictions that each party interpreted according to its needs. McKinlay discovered that his own record, intended as private testimony, had become public evidence, subject to readings he had not authorized and could not prevent.

The medical assessments continued. The frostbite injuries that had seemed stable in Nome developed complications in the warmer climate of Seattle. Infections set in. Amputations that had been delayed became necessary. The men’s bodies, which had adapted to Arctic conditions with a stubbornness that had kept them alive, now failed to adjust to temperate ease. They lost weight despite abundant food. They slept poorly in soft beds. Their circadian rhythms, attuned to the continuous daylight of Arctic summer and the continuous darkness of winter, could not synchronize with the ordinary alternation of day and night.

The psychological assessments were less formal but equally consequential. The men who had maintained discipline on the island, McKinlay, Mamen, Hadley, found themselves admired for qualities that felt, to them, like mere continuation of necessity. The men who had struggled, Chafe, whose narrative would later diverge most sharply from Bartlett’s, and Breddy, whose conduct on the island had drawn criticism, discovered that their difficulties were now public knowledge, subject to interpretation they could not influence. The solidarity of shared ordeal began to fracture under the pressure of individual reputations.

The official Canadian inquiry, authorized in October 1914, formalized these divisions. The commissioner, appointed by the Naval Service, requested written statements from all survivors and began scheduling hearings that would take testimony under oath. The men who had imagined rescue as an end to their trials found themselves embarking on a new sequence of ordeals, equally prolonged and equally uncertain in outcome. The questions they would be asked—about Stefansson’s departure, about Bartlett’s decisions, about their own conduct—required them to transform memory into evidence, experience into argument.

The first hearings were held in Victoria in November 1914. Bartlett, recovered from his illness, traveled from Newfoundland to attend. Stefansson, already preparing his own defense, engaged legal counsel. The survivors, dispersed to their homes or to hospitals, submitted to depositions that extracted from them the linear narratives they had failed to supply in Nome. The process of official reckoning had begun, and it would continue for years, outlasting the immediate public interest that had greeted their rescue.

What the survivors carried with them, through these proceedings and beyond, was the knowledge that their experience had escaped their control. The ice had taken the ship. The drift had taken their comrades. The rescue had taken their isolation. And now the institutions of civilization, the law, the press, the scientific establishment, were taking their testimony, converting their survival into materials for judgment they had not sought and could not refuse. The transition from Wrangel Island to the hearing room was an entry into a different order of trial, where the stakes were no longer life and death but reputation, compensation, and the assignment of blame.

The last of the survivors to give his initial statement did so in December 1914, by which time the public narrative had hardened into competing orthodoxies. Bartlett’s heroism was established beyond serious dispute; the only question was whether it had been sufficient to overcome the failures of others. Stefansson’s conduct remained controversial, with defenders and accusers occupying positions that would persist for decades. The dead—twenty-two men from a ship’s company of thirty-five—were memorialized in ways that served the living: as martyrs to scientific ambition, or as victims of misguided leadership, or simply as men who had drawn the wrong assignment in the lottery of expedition staffing.

The survivors themselves began to disappear from public attention as their usefulness to the competing narratives diminished. Those who recovered fully returned to ordinary occupations; those who did not became dependent on pensions and charity. McKinlay, whose documentation had made him central to the official proceedings, found himself consulted for years afterward by historians and journalists seeking to fix the final meaning of the disaster. His answers became more guarded with time, as he recognized that each retelling altered the events it claimed to preserve.

The King and Winge, which had carried them from Wrangel Island, returned to its commercial fishing. Olaf Swenson, who had chartered the rescue at his own risk, eventually received partial reimbursement from the Canadian government after prolonged negotiation. The schooner’s role in the disaster narrative was reduced to a single functional note: the vessel that had removed the survivors from the island. The men who had manned her, who had sighted the survivors’ signal and brought them aboard, returned to their own obscurity.

What remained, as 1914 ended and the larger catastrophe of the European war absorbed public attention, was a file of testimony and a set of competing claims that would require official resolution. The survivors’ stories, extracted and transcribed and notarized, accumulated in government offices in Ottawa and London. Their physical presence—their scarred faces, their missing digits, their persistent coughs—gradually withdrew from the sites of official attention. The disaster of the Karluk became, in public memory, a brief sensation of the prewar months, notable mainly for its contrast with the greater slaughter that followed.

Yet for the eleven men who had lived through it, the transition from ice to civilization remained incomplete. They carried in their bodies the permanent marks of their ordeal; in their memories, the permanent uncertainty of events that had never achieved stable meaning; and in their social circumstances, the permanent status of witnesses to something that institutions demanded they explain. The rescue had ended their drift. It had not restored them to the world they had left. That world had moved on, and their accounts of where they had been were now required to justify where they stood.

The final depositions were sealed in January 1915, by which time three of the survivors had died of complications from their injuries. Their testimony, given in hospital wards and hotel rooms, became fixed in the record as their final word on events that had already escaped their control. The living continued to negotiate their positions in a narrative that would outlast them, their individual memories increasingly subordinate to the documentary archive that had begun to accumulate the moment they stepped from Wrangel’s shore onto the deck of a ship that smelled of diesel and fresh bread. The survivors’ stories were now public property, demanding an official reckoning.
