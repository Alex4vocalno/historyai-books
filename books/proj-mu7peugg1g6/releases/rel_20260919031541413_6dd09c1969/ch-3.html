Chapter 3

Into the Gripping Ice

The fault line had not healed. It had been carried north instead, embedded in a voyage already deviating from its promises before the ice made deviation permanent.

On the morning of July 13, 1913, the Karluk cleared Nome with twenty-five men aboard, her holds packed with scientific instruments, her deck crowded with dogs and sledges, her ownership transferred to the Canadian government for the ten thousand dollars Stefansson had negotiated. The whaler turned expedition vessel passed through the Bering Strait and turned east along the Alaskan coast, bound for Herschel Island where the Mary Sachs and Alaska would assemble. For three weeks she held her course, threading through leads of open water, her engines laboring against the current, her crew settling into the patterns of Arctic navigation. The scientists arranged their equipment. The hunters inspected their weapons. The captain studied the ice.

By late July the voyage’s character had begun to shift. On July 31 the Karluk made Point Hope, where two Inuit hunters came aboard—men the crew called Jerry and Jimmy, whose knowledge of ice behavior and game movement would prove more valuable than any Admiralty chart. That same day William McKinlay, the young Scottish magnetician standing his first Arctic watch, entered an observation in his record. The Karluk was handling well. She was not fast, not powerful, not suited to the work ahead of her by any standard measure, but she was handling. McKinlay was learning to read the vessel as he read his instruments, tracking her responses to wind and swell, measuring her against the reputation she had failed to earn during the dry dock inspections of spring.

August 1 brought the first formal threshold. The lookout sighted what the logs recorded simply as “the permanent Arctic ice pack.” The distinction mattered. Where coastal waters had carried broken floes and drifting bergs, the horizon now showed continuous white, the edge of an ocean that had survived the summer thaw and would extend north to the pole. Bartlett altered course to follow the pack edge, seeking the lanes that might carry him east to Herschel Island. The Karluk remained two hundred miles from her rendezvous. The ice was not yet an adversary but a condition to be navigated, a surface requiring interpretation.

For eleven days the ship worked along the ice margin, making easting in the narrow waters between pack and coast. Progress was slow, measured in miles per day, each advance demanding calculation and patience. Bartlett had spent years in these waters aboard vessels more capable than this one. He knew the signs of ice under pressure: the warning sounds of floes grinding, the visual cues that distinguished stable ice from ice in motion. He watched the color of the sky above the pack, the direction of wind, the set of current. The Karluk could not force a passage. Her engine lacked power to drive through thick ice, her hull lacked strength to withstand sustained compression. His strategy depended on finding open water, on reading the ice well enough to avoid the trap that he recognized as a possibility from the first sight of the pack.

The signs accumulated gradually, each one inspectable in isolation, each contributing to a pattern visible only in retrospect. The leads of open water narrowed. The floes pressed closer. The ship’s movement became threading between obstacles rather than holding course. On August 12, with ice closing around him, Bartlett made the first formal acknowledgment of changed conditions. He shut down the engines to save coal. The Karluk was no longer advancing under her own power. She was waiting for the ice to release her.

The pressure did not ease. For two weeks the ship lay frozen while the pack shifted and ground around her. When movement became possible again, it was measured in meters per day, creeping through channels that opened and closed with the ice’s rhythm. The rendezvous at Herschel Island receded from possibility. The expedition’s timetable, strained by spring delays, dissolved into the practical necessities of ice navigation.

Then, on August 13, the gradual became absolute. The Karluk was still more than two hundred miles from her destination when ice closed completely around her hull. Open water vanished. The leads that had permitted progress sealed shut. The ship became fixed in place, her position determined not by her captain’s decisions but by the drift of the pack that held her. She was no longer under way but a prisoner of the ice, committed to a drift that would carry her westward, away from her destination, into waters no one aboard had chosen to enter.

No catastrophe marked the transition: no collision, no sudden crisis, no isolable moment of decision. The ice had completed its embrace, enclosing the ship in a medium responsive to forces beyond human reach. McKinlay had written that she was handling well. What she was handling had changed. She was no longer a ship sailing toward destination but a habitation drifting on frozen sea.

The responses to this transformation divided immediately along lines that would govern the months ahead. Bartlett shifted to survival footing with the instinct of a man who had seen ice take ships before. His concerns were practical and immediate: coal consumption, water supply, hull condition under pressure, morale of men who had signed for voyage and found imprisonment. He established routines for measuring ice movement, monitoring position, maintaining machinery that was now life support rather than means of advance. The Karluk might drift weeks or months. She might be released by wind shift or current change. She might be carried to destruction. In any case, the ship had to be managed as stationary installation, base camp on ice, with all that implied for supplies, discipline, and the psychology of men confined to small space.

The scientists experienced entrapment as a different interruption. They had prepared for shore-based research, specimen collection at fixed stations, systematic coastline exploration. The ice had transformed their ship into something else without transforming their understanding of their purpose. They continued preparing instruments, discussing methodologies, anticipating work that would resume when ship reached open water. The delay was frustrating but not, in their immediate assessment, fundamental threat to expedition purposes. The Canadian Arctic Expedition had been organized and directed by Vilhjalmur Stefansson to explore the regions west of Parry Archipelago for the Government of Canada. Three ships were employed: the Karluk, the Mary Sachs, and the Alaska. The ice had altered route, not mission.

Stefansson’s response occupied its own category. The expedition leader had spent weeks since departure planning for the contingency now confronted, though his planning proceeded along different lines than his captain’s. Where Bartlett managed ship as physical system, Stefansson considered expedition as organizational problem. The Karluk was one of three vessels. The Mary Sachs and Alaska were proceeding to Herschel Island by different routes. Scientific program could be maintained even if flagship failed to reach rendezvous. Hunters could secure meat from ice. Ice itself could become platform for travel and observation. Stefansson had built reputation on proposition that Arctic was not hostile wasteland of popular imagination but habitable environment for those who understood its resources. The Karluk’s entrapment was local difficulty, not expedition-ending disaster.

This interpretation was not irrational—it was consistent with Stefansson’s experience, his theoretical commitments, his plans laid before funding government—but it existed in tension with physical facts Bartlett confronted daily. The ice Stefansson saw as surface for travel was, for the captain, force that could crush hull. The hunting parties Stefansson proposed to dispatch were, for Bartlett, detachments that would reduce capacity for mutual rescue. The confidence Stefansson projected was, for men who slept in vessel whose timbers groaned with ice pressure, either reassuring or obtuse, depending on disposition and trust in leader who had brought them here.

The sensory experience of entrapment accumulated in details that logs recorded without comment and diaries explored with growing intensity. The sound of ice against hull became constant accompaniment to life aboard, grinding percussion varying with temperature and pressure, louder at night when air was still, ominous when new crack opened in floe beside ship. Light changed as Karluk drifted north, days shortening toward autumn equinox, sun describing lower arcs across sky often white with ice haze. Temperature dropped. Deck became treacherous with frost. Dogs, housed in deck kennels, grew restless with confinement, their howling adding to acoustic environment of trapped ship.

The ice itself became object of close attention, its forms and movements catalogued by men who had nothing else to observe. Pressure ridges where floes had collided and buckled, creating walls of broken ice towering above deck. Leads that opened without warning, black water appearing between white surfaces, offering brief hope of release before cold sealed them again. Colors of ice under different conditions: blue of dense glacial ice, white of new formation, dirty gray of ice incorporating sediment and snow. Men learned to read these signs, anticipate ice behavior, recognize difference between stable pack and ice under stress.

Bartlett’s daily routine established rhythm of life aboard. He measured ship’s position, tracked drift, inspected hull for damage from ice pressure. He managed coal supply, calculating how long they could maintain heat and light if entrapment continued through winter. He supervised hunting parties that went onto ice to secure fresh meat, watching progress through field glasses, noting return with anxiety of commander whose resources diminished with each sortie. Jerry and Jimmy proved their value in these expeditions, reading ice surface for safe travel, locating seal holes, bringing back protein that would supplement stored provisions.

The drift carried Karluk westward, away from Alaska, toward empty waters north of Bering Strait. Ship’s position on August 13 had been approximately 70° north, 143° west. By late August she had moved more than hundred miles in wrong direction, her course determined by clockwise rotation of Beaufort Gyre, current system dominating region’s ice movement. Bartlett recorded these positions with precision of navigator who understood that knowledge of location was foundation of any future rescue. The drift was not random. It followed patterns that could be analyzed, predicted, perhaps exploited. But it was not subject to human direction. The Karluk was moving through territory existing on no chart as ship’s route, carried by forces no captain could command.

Stefansson’s activities during this period occupied different register. While Bartlett managed ship, expedition leader pursued program established before entrapment, treating Karluk as mobile base rather than vessel in distress. He planned hunting expeditions to coast, calculating distance to land and feasibility of sledging parties. He discussed scientific objectives with researchers aboard, maintaining structure of expedition still proceeding according to plan. He corresponded with expedition’s agents, composing messages to be carried by any ship passing within signaling distance, reporting progress and requesting supplies at designated rendezvous points.

The disjunction between these two modes—Bartlett’s survival management and Stefansson’s expedition planning—created conditions for crisis that would follow. They were not simply different responses to shared situation. They were incompatible interpretations of what situation was. For Bartlett, Karluk was ship in peril, crew dependent on his seamanship for survival. For Stefansson, she was component of larger system, her difficulties local and manageable within expedition’s overall design. Both men could support interpretation with evidence. Both could point to experience and responsibilities. Tension between them was not matter of personality alone, though personality entered into it. It was structural conflict between two models of authority, two understandings of what Arctic expedition was for.

Physical environment enforced its own priorities regardless of these interpretations. Ice pressure against hull increased as autumn advanced and temperature dropped. Karluk’s timbers, sturdy enough for original purpose as whaler, were not designed for sustained compression from multiple directions. Bartlett heard new sounds accompanying this pressure: sharp cracks of ice breaking, deeper groans of ship’s structure responding to forces never built to withstand. He inspected hull daily, searching signs of strain, calculating how long she could survive intact.

Scientific party adjusted to conditions with varying degrees of success. Some found ways to continue work, measuring ice thickness and temperature, collecting specimens from leads opening near ship, maintaining records that would justify expedition’s existence whatever its difficulties. Others experienced confinement as personal trial, narrow quarters and monotonous routine wearing at temperaments unsuited to imprisonment. Ship’s library saw heavy use. Games and entertainments that Arctic expeditions traditionally employed to maintain morale were organized and participated in with degrees of enthusiasm reflecting individual character. Dogs remained focus of attention, their care and exercise providing necessary activity, their presence connection to world of sledging travel most men had imagined as their Arctic experience.

September brought no release. Ice that had trapped Karluk in mid-August had consolidated around her, summer’s fragmented floes freezing into continuous mass that would not open again until following spring. Ship’s drift continued, carrying her into waters growing more remote from rescue with each passing week. Position reports Bartlett prepared became increasingly theoretical in practical significance—accurate records of location no one on outside was tracking, coordinates describing their isolation with mathematical precision.

It was in this context that Stefansson made decision that would define expedition’s subsequent history. Hunting party he had planned since August could no longer be delayed if it was to reach coast before winter closed ice entirely. Expedition’s scientific program required specimens obtainable only on land. Karluk was stable, adequately supplied, under command of captain whose ice experience was unmatched. Leader’s presence on ship was not essential to her survival. His presence on hunting expedition might secure resources sustaining expedition through winter months ahead.

Reasoning was coherent. It was also, in Bartlett’s assessment, catastrophically mistaken. Captain had spent six weeks managing ship drifting into danger, hull under constant pressure, position becoming more precarious with each westward mile. He had done this while maintaining forms of expedition discipline, reporting to leader whose authority he acknowledged while his own practical knowledge contradicted that leader’s assessments. Proposed departure would leave him in sole command of vessel and crew organized under different principles, responsible for lives Stefansson’s plans had placed in jeopardy.

Confrontation, when it came, was conducted with restraint of men who understood open conflict would damage morale they both claimed to value. Stefansson announced intention to take hunting party to coast. Bartlett recorded objections, his sense that expedition leader was abandoning post at critical moment, his fear that ice would carry Karluk beyond reach before any hunting party could return. Exchange was documented in records each man kept, in testimony they would later give, in interpretations accumulating around this moment as disaster unfolded.

Stefansson departed on September 20, 1913, taking five men, two sledges, and dogs required to pull them. Party left Karluk by route across ice carrying them southeast toward Alaskan coast, journey of uncertain duration through terrain already freezing for winter. Bartlett watched them go from ship’s deck, field glasses tracking progress until they disappeared among pressure ridges. Then he turned to work that would occupy him for months ahead: management of ship that was no longer part of expedition, preservation of lives placed in his hands by leader who had walked away.

Karluk continued her drift. Ice that held her was now her only environment, white horizon her only prospect. Men who remained aboard settled into routines Bartlett established: watchfulness, conservation, mutual dependence that Arctic survival demanded. They were no longer sailing toward destination. They were waiting in closed world where the grinding pressure against the hull measured their remaining time, where each sunset came sooner than the last, where the coordinates Bartlett recorded each noon marked their passage deeper into waters beyond the reach of rescue.
