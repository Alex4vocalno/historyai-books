Chapter 21

The Ships on the Horizon

Seen from above, the three camps had drawn into themselves through August, each preparing for winter with the mechanical discipline of men who no longer believed in deliverance. Near the beach, William McKinlay set down September 3 in his diary: the barometer falling, the wind shifting northeast, snow dusting the gravel slopes where they had buried their dead. He noted the firewood they would need to gather. What he had come to believe, he did not write. Bartlett was dead; the European war they had learned of in July had swallowed whatever resources might have been directed their way; Wrangel Island would become their tomb. Hope, in the quantities they had once consumed it, proved indigestible. Better to attend to the measurable: the cache of walrus meat, the state of the tents, the swelling in Fred Maurer’s legs that had kept him from walking for three weeks.

Four days later, the horizon changed.

September 7, 1914, broke clear and cold. The wind carried the smell of open water from the south. At the northern camp, where five men of the original crew maintained uneasy separation from the scientists, someone (accounts differ between Ernest Chafe and John Williamson) was first to see the smudge on the southern horizon. It appeared and disappeared, a dark brushstroke against pale sky, resolving finally into two distinct columns. Smoke. The product of coal fires, of engines, of vessels under power.

The signaling began immediately. They had prepared for this possibility through the long months, had discussed semaphore flags, bonfires, the arrangement of dark stones on light beaches. Now preparation scattered into improvised action. Men who had not run for weeks hobbled across the shingle, shouting between camps that had not spoken in days. Someone dragged canvas to the beach. Someone else struck a match to grease-soaked rags they had kept ready.

The ships (two of them, as the smoke confirmed) were making for the island from the southeast, running along the coast the Karluk had never successfully navigated. They moved slowly, fighting the same ice that had imprisoned the expedition, the same variable winds. The survivors watched through field glasses borrowed from salvaged stores; watched them stop and start; watched the smoke thin and thicken as engines labored. The interval between sighting and arrival stretched across hours. The sun moved overhead. The wind rose again, threatening to blow signal fires into uselessness.

What passed through those eleven minds during those hours cannot be fully recovered. McKinlay’s later account speaks of “a curious numbness,” a failure of anticipated emotion. Chafe recorded relief mixed with something like resentment, as if the intrusion broke a terrible equilibrium they had learned to inhabit. The rescue they had prayed for, cursed, and finally abandoned hope of receiving arrived as an administrative fact, a transaction between vessels, stripped of the narrative climax their suffering seemed to demand.

The ships were the King and Winge, American schooners chartered by expedition agents after Bartlett’s arrival in Alaska and his frantic telegraphing eastward. They had sailed from Nome in August, carrying supplies assembled through the same network of commercial and official connections that had launched the Karluk the previous year. Their masters knew the Arctic coast from decades of whaling and trading. They had not expected to find survivors. The King & Winge was an American-registered walrus hunter, chartered for the season by Olaf Swenson for a voyage to Siberia when Bartlett, in Nome organizing rescue, requested it attempt Wrangel Island.

The prevailing assumption in Nome, in Victoria, in the Ottawa offices that had authorized the expedition, was that the Karluk’s company had perished with their ship or in the attempt to reach land. Bartlett’s reports, arriving through Russian telegraph stations and American cable relays, had been fragmentary, delayed, sometimes contradictory. The rescue mission proceeded from obligation rather than expectation.

This dissonance between rescuers’ brisk efficiency and survivors’ frozen trauma shaped every interaction of the days that followed. The King and Winge anchored offshore in late afternoon, unable to approach closer through ice that still choked the beaches. Small boats came across gray water carrying men with news of a wider world: medical supplies; bread and fresh meat that survivors’ digestive systems could no longer properly process. The rescuers had prepared for castaways in a conventional sense: hungry men; grateful men; men ready for evacuation according to established procedures. What they found was something else.

The eleven survivors presented a medical and administrative challenge that rescue vessels were equipped to address only partially. Scurvy had advanced in most of them to open sores; loose teeth; joint swelling that made movement agonizing. Maurer could not walk unassisted. Several showed neurological symptoms of prolonged vitamin deficiency: irritability verging on paranoia; depression; cognitive slowing that made coherent narrative difficult. Psychological damage was less visible but more disruptive to evacuation. The three camps had functioned as separate societies through summer; bound by grievance and competing interpretations of their ordeal. Now they were expected to compose themselves into a single body of rescued persons; submit to common authority; abandon structures of suspicion that had preserved individual identities against group collapse.

The rescuers’ perspective, recorded in the master’s log and in accounts later provided to expedition agents, reveals the operational difficulties of this collision between systems. The King and Winge carried coal for limited steaming, provisions for finite weeks, crews with their own contracts and destinations. Every day at Wrangel Island represented deviation from planned routes, consumption of resources, risk of becoming trapped themselves in advancing ice. The masters had expected to confirm absence of survivors, to search briefly, to return to Nome with certainty rather than passengers. Discovery of eleven living men imposed obligations their vessels were not ideally configured to meet.

Evacuation therefore proceeded through negotiation and improvisation rather than predetermined protocol. Survivors were brought aboard in groups across September 8 and 9; a process complicated by physical condition and resistance to common direction. Some insisted on bringing personal possessions (diaries; scientific instruments; records of observations) that rescuers considered excess weight. Others demanded specific arrangements for burial sites of their dead; conditions for documentation of their experience; guarantees about future employment that masters were not authorized to provide. The King and Winge had come to save lives; not adjudicate expedition’s failures or guarantee its survivors against consequences of its collapse.

Between these demands and operational constraints provisional order emerged. The sickest (Maurer; Templeman; Morris) were transferred first: carried across ice in improvised litters and hoisted aboard with block and tackle. Departure from Wrangel Island occurred September 9, 1914, forty days short of a year since the Karluk had first become imprisoned in ice. The survivors left behind graves of eleven men—some marked with crosses fashioned from ship’s lumber, others identified only by positions recorded in McKinlay’s surveys. They left as well physical traces of their survival: stone cairns, caches of frozen meat, collapsed tents that would gradually surrender to Arctic wind. The island returned to its uninhabited state, its only permanent inhabitants the lemmings and arctic foxes and occasional polar bears that had watched their struggle without comprehension.

The voyage to Nome took twelve days, a passage that exposed gaps between rescue and recovery. The King and Winge were working vessels, not hospital ships. Their medical supplies—adequate for injuries and illnesses of whaling crews—proved insufficient for systematic treatment of advanced scurvy. Survivors required fresh vegetables, citrus, specific antiscorbutics the vessels did not carry. What they received was warmth, rest, psychological relief of movement toward known destinations. Some improved visibly; others continued to decline, bodies too depleted to respond quickly to changed conditions.

Social dynamics of the rescue vessels replicated in miniature the conflicts of the island. The three camps had been distributed between two ships according to logistical necessity, not social reconciliation. Men who had not spoken in weeks found themselves sharing cramped quarters, dependent on common rations, subject to authority of masters who knew nothing of their history. Accusations surfaced—of theft, of favoritism, of responsibility for specific deaths—that rescuers could neither adjudicate nor ignore. The master of the King recorded a confrontation between Williamson and McKinlay that required physical separation of the parties. The master of the Winge noted “difficulties of discipline” among his four passengers that interfered with shipboard routine. The expedition’s internal fractures, preserved through months of isolation, did not heal simply because salvation had arrived.

The wider world into which they sailed had itself been transformed during their imprisonment. The European war that McKinlay had noted in July dispatches had expanded into continental catastrophe. The cables that Bartlett’s reports had traveled were now congested with military traffic. The governmental and commercial networks that had supported the Canadian Arctic Expedition were redirecting themselves toward mobilization, attention shifting from polar exploration to survival of a different order. The Karluk disaster, which had occupied front pages in September 1913, had become a minor thread in larger fabric of loss.

This transformation affected reception of the survivors in ways they would spend years comprehending. They arrived at Nome on September 21, 1914, to a harbor busy with preparations for winter, with vessels being laid up or converted, with ordinary commerce of a frontier port adjusting to wartime disruption. News of their rescue had preceded them through radio and cable, but resources for their reception had not been mobilized accordingly. There were no government representatives waiting with instructions, no medical facilities prepared for their specific conditions, no systematic procedure for debriefing or documentation. They were processed through same mechanisms that handled shipwrecked sailors and destitute miners: customs inspection, medical examination of perfunctory thoroughness, dispersal into town’s boarding houses and hospitals according to individual capacity to pay or to claim official support.

Bartlett was not there. He had reached Alaska in April, had spent summer attempting to organize relief expeditions, had finally secured charter of the King and Winge through combination of official pressure and personal guarantee. Then tonsillitis had felled him, a prosaic illness that his Arctic travels had not prepared him to resist. He was recovering in a San Francisco hospital when rescue vessels sailed, dependent on telegrams for news of their progress. The man who had walked across ice to save them, who had navigated bureaucratic labyrinth of three nations to mount rescue, was absent from its conclusion—another of the dissonances that would shape survivors’ understanding of their experience.

What happened in Nome during weeks following their arrival established patterns that would persist through years of inquiry and litigation. Survivors were separated by their physical conditions and immediate prospects. Those with family connections or financial resources—McKinlay, with his Scottish academic sponsors; Mamen, with his Norwegian network—arranged their own passages south. Others remained dependent on expedition funds that were themselves uncertain, caught between Stefansson’s dispersed authority and Canadian government’s reluctance to assume obligations it had not clearly authorized. The King and Winge returned to their commercial routes, their masters filing reports that were absorbed into expedition’s growing archive of documentation.

The medical consequences of rescue proved slower to manifest than immediate relief of evacuation. Scurvy, once established, does not reverse simply with provision of antiscorbutics. Survivors carried its effects in their joints, their gums, their cardiac function, for months and years afterward. More persistently, they carried psychological adaptations that had preserved them on the island: suspicion of authority, habit of individual calculation, difficulty of trust. These were not conditions rescue vessels could address, not symptoms that medical examinations in Nome would record.

Documentation of their experience began immediately, driven by competing imperatives. Stefansson, informed of rescue through cables that reached him in his own Arctic travels, initiated process of narrative control that would characterize his subsequent management of the disaster. His agents collected diaries, secured statements, established interpretive frameworks before alternative accounts could solidify. The survivors themselves, emerging from physical crisis into unfamiliar demands of public explanation, found their memories shaped by questions they were asked, forms they were required to fill, emerging consensus about what had happened and who was responsible.

This process—rescue as beginning rather than end of their ordeal—challenges conventional arc of disaster narrative. The King and Winge had performed mechanical function of evacuation: they had removed eleven men from uninhabited island and delivered them to port where further transport was possible. The administrative systems that received them, however, proved no more adequate to their condition than ice had been. The Canadian government, which had authorized expedition, had not established procedures for its failure. Scientific institutions that had supplied its personnel had no protocols for preservation of data from collapsed enterprises. Legal frameworks that might have adjudicated responsibility for deaths were themselves uncertain, stretched between national jurisdictions and novel circumstances of polar exploration.

The survivors dispersed into this institutional vacuum, each carrying a version of events that would be tested against versions of others in inquiries to come. McKinlay would write his account in immediate aftermath, preserving details that memory would later blur. Chafe would compile his narrative from ship’s records and personal recollection, attentive to technical failures that had preceded human ones. Bartlett, recovering in California, would begin constructing his own authoritative version, drawing on navigational logs he had preserved through ice journey and Siberian trek.

What united these emerging accounts, beneath their competing emphases, was common recognition that rescue had not restored world that existed before Karluk sailed. The eleven survivors had been returned to civilization, but civilization had not been prepared for their return. Systems of support—medical, financial, narrative—that they had assumed would activate automatically proved to require individual negotiation, persistent advocacy, expenditure of energy that their depleted conditions could not easily supply. The King and Winge had completed their mission and departed. Consequences of that mission’s success would unfold across years of administrative process, public controversy, private struggle.

The arithmetic of the expedition remained stark, unaltered by rescue’s intervention. Twenty-two men had left Karluk for ice in January 1914. Eleven had survived to reach Nome. Fourteen dead could not be restored by any subsequent process. Rescue had preserved possibility for living without redeeming losses of dead. This balance sheet, which would occupy inquiries and lawsuits of coming years, was already visible in dispersal from Nome: eleven men proceeding southward by various routes, carrying various injuries, variously equipped to make their experience comprehensible to world they reentered. The vessels that had carried them from Wrangel Island would continue in Arctic service, their part in Karluk rescue becoming one entry in long logs of voyages to remote coasts. The expedition they had interrupted would dissolve into its institutional aftermath, its scientific purposes unfulfilled, its human costs still incompletely tallied.
