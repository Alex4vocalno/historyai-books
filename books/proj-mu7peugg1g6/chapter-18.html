Chapter 18

Captain Bartlett’s Frantic and Bureaucratic

By 1914, the machinery he had set in motion was already too slow.

Captain Robert Bartlett stood in the mud of Emma Harbor on the Chukchi coast, seventy-six days removed from the ice where he had left his shipmates, and found himself facing an enemy he could neither navigate nor command. The village sprawled in its usual disorder—skin tents, rough plank houses, dogs chained to stakes, the perpetual reek of walrus oil and decay. He had walked out of nothing into this. Kataktovik stood beside him, equally filthy, equally alive. They had crossed four hundred miles of moving ice to reach this point. Now Bartlett needed a telegraph, a ship, and a government that would believe him.

The nearest Russian telegraph station lay at Cape Dezhnev, three hundred miles to the south along a coast without roads, without regular transport, without any certainty that the line still functioned. The coastal mail runner, when it ran, moved by dog sledge at the pace of weather and ice. Bartlett had no money, no papers, no language. What he possessed was a story that sounded like delirium: a Canadian ship crushed in the ice, twenty-five persons marooned on an island that appeared on no chart the Russians acknowledged, a rescue that had to happen before the summer breakup turned the Chukchi Sea into an impassable slurry of floes and open water.

He began with what he knew. The local Chukchi had traded with American whalers; some spoke fragments of English learned in the Bering Strait. Through gestures, through Kataktovik’s translations into his own Inuktitut-tinged Chukchi, Bartlett established the basic facts. Yes, there was a telegraph. No, it did not reach to Emma Harbor. Yes, a ship sometimes came. No, not yet this season. The ice still held.

Within hours of his arrival, Bartlett had drafted his first telegram. He wrote it in the blank pages of the notebook he had carried across the ice, the same book where he had logged their daily positions, their dwindling food, the deaths that had marked their passage west. The message went to Ottawa, to the Department of Naval Service, to anyone who might still remember that the Canadian Arctic Expedition had existed. Karluk crushed January ice, he wrote. Survivors Wrangel Island. Need immediate relief ship. He did not know if the words would arrive. He did not know if anyone would act. He knew only that the writing of them was the first necessary thing.

The distance between Emma Harbor and Cape Dezhnev measured more than miles. It measured the gap between a man who had kept human beings alive through an Arctic winter and the administrative machinery that might, eventually, authorize their rescue. Bartlett understood ships. He understood ice. He did not understand the Russian telegraph system, the protocols of the Imperial Ministry of Communications, or the willingness of Ottawa bureaucrats to spend money on men they had already written off.

He started south. Kataktovik came with him, though the walk had earned him the right to rest. They traveled by dog sledge when they could borrow or barter dogs, by foot when they could not. The coast revealed itself in gray repetition: headlands of frozen shale, beaches where the tide had left ice sculptures of its own withdrawal, the occasional settlement where smoke rose from holes in the snow. Bartlett kept no journal of this journey. The habit of documentation had belonged to his life as captain, to a ship with a logbook and a routine. Now he moved through a landscape that offered no purchase for routine, only the relentless arithmetic of days passing and the island’s food supplies diminishing.

At Cape Dezhnev, the telegraph station occupied a wooden building indistinguishable from the storehouses around it. The operator, a Russian with a beard that had not seen scissors in months, received Bartlett’s draft with the indifference of a man who transmitted messages he would never understand. The captain watched him encode the words, watched the key begin its chatter. Then he waited. The operator offered tea. Bartlett accepted. They sat in silence while the message traveled along wires that crossed tundra and taiga, that reached eventually to Vladivostok, to Tokyo, to the cable ships that connected the Pacific rim. From there, presumably, it would find its way to Ottawa, though no one in the room could confirm this.

Bartlett asked for confirmation of receipt. The operator shrugged. Confirmation came from the receiving station, when it came. The line to Vladivostok was often interrupted. The line to Petropavlovsk was worse. The captain understood, finally, that he had crossed from a world where his decisions meant immediate consequences to a world where decisions disappeared into systems, where cause and effect were separated by thousands of miles of wire and the variable competence of strangers.

He sent a second message, and a third. He wrote to the American consul in Vladivostok, to the governor of Alaska, to anyone who might have influence or interest. The telegraph fees consumed the small fund he had raised by selling his watch and Kataktovik’s rifle to a Chukchi trader.

When the money ran out, he appealed to the station master’s discretion, to the vague authority of a shipwrecked captain representing a nation that had no formal presence on this coast. The responses, when they arrived, traveled the same uncertain path.

A telegram from Ottawa acknowledged receipt of his message. A telegram from Victoria, British Columbia, asked for clarification: Please confirm number of survivors and exact position. Bartlett confirmed what he knew. He could not confirm what he did not.

The exact position of Wrangel Island remained approximate, a latitude and longitude fixed by dead reckoning during the Karluk’s drift, corrected by observations he had made during the march. The number of survivors had been twenty-five when he left. By now it could be anything.

The revenue cutter Bear arrived in St Michael, Alaska, midway through June. Her master, Captain Cochran, agreed to go to Wrangel Island as soon as he got permission from the United States government. It would be impossible, in any event, to attempt the rescue before mid-July; ice conditions in the Arctic that year were reported as severe.

The search for a ship introduced him to another dimension of frustration. The Chukchi Sea in April remained solid to the horizon, but solid ice meant nothing to the masters of the few vessels that worked these waters. They had seen ice crush ships with more strength than the Karluk had possessed. They had seen men walk onto the pack never to return. The American whalers who had once filled these waters had withdrawn north, following the bowhead migration toward the Beaufort Sea. The Russian traders who replaced them operated on margins too thin to risk for castaways.

Bartlett found his first possibility in a coastal schooner owned by a Siberian merchant who had made money in the fur trade and lost it in vodka. The captain, a Norwegian who had jumped ship in Nome and never found his way back, listened to Bartlett’s proposal with the weary patience of a man who had heard many proposals and accepted few. He could sail in June, he said, when the ice began to move. He would need provisions for his own crew, payment in advance, and a guarantee from the Canadian government that the debt would be honored. Bartlett had no authority to give guarantees. He had only his uniform, his reputation, and the desperation that had become visible in his hands, which shook when he unrolled his chart to show the island’s position.

The Norwegian declined. He had a cargo to fetch, furs to collect, a schedule that did not include a detour to an island where men were probably already dead.

This became the pattern. Bartlett would locate a vessel, usually through the network of traders and missionaries who maintained the coast’s thin web of connection. He would present his case, unfolding his evidence with the methodical care of a man who had learned that preparation often survived where inspiration failed. He would negotiate terms, always terms, always the translation of human urgency into the language of money and risk. And he would fail, because the terms exceeded what he could promise and the risk exceeded what any reasonable captain would accept.

Through April, he moved between Cape Dezhnev and Emma Harbor, between the telegraph station and the beach where ships might appear. Kataktovik stayed with him, though the Inuk had fulfilled his obligation and more. They had developed a partnership that required little language: the shared competence of men who had crossed ice together, who had watched each other fail and continue. When Bartlett despaired, Kataktovik maintained the practical rhythm of survival—finding food, securing shelter, managing the dogs. When Kataktovik withdrew into the silences that seemed to belong to his people, Bartlett accepted the withdrawal without demanding explanation. They had never discussed what they had left behind on Wrangel Island. They did not discuss what they might find when they returned.

The telegrams continued. Bartlett learned to compress his messages into the fewest possible words, to eliminate any information that might be questioned or delayed. Survivors Wrangel Island, he wrote. Urgent need relief vessel. The responses grew more specific, more bureaucratic. The Canadian government had authorized an investigation. The investigation would determine whether a rescue was warranted. The cost of a rescue would require parliamentary approval. The parliamentary session had ended. The matter would be considered in the fall.

He received this news in a hut at Emma Harbor, sitting on a crate of dried fish while a snowstorm erased the world beyond the doorway. For a moment, the captain who had crossed the Chukchi Sea on foot, who had kept his crew alive through a winter that killed better men, felt the particular despair of a man who has outlived his own competence. He could not sail a ship through parliamentary procedure. He could not navigate the ice of administrative delay.

Then he moved. The response was physical, automatic, the same impulse that had sent him walking across the ice when waiting meant death. He would go to Alaska. He would find a ship himself, or he would find Americans who would listen, or he would find some other path that the logic of Ottawa had not foreclosed. The decision carried its own cost. Leaving the coast meant leaving the telegraph, meant severing his only connection to the survivors he could not stop imagining. But staying meant accepting a pace that would kill them.

Five days later, the whaler Herman appeared off Emma Harbor.

Her master, Pedersen, was Danish, a veteran of the Arctic trade who had heard rumors of a shipwrecked captain in the Chukchi villages and had come to investigate. Bartlett met him on the beach with the directness that desperation permits. He stated his need without preamble: passage to Alaska, access to American shipping, any connection that might reach Wrangel Island before the ice broke. Pedersen listened. He had space, he said. He was bound for Nome. He could take passengers.

They sailed without delay. The Herman was a working whaler, not a passenger vessel, and Bartlett spent the voyage in the fo’c’sle with the crew, learning what he could of the season’s ice conditions, the location of the American fleet, the practical possibilities of a rescue attempt. Kataktovik remained below, seasick in a way that suggested he had never traveled by water before. The irony was not lost on Bartlett: a man who had crossed hundreds of miles of moving ice, who had survived storms that broke ships, incapacitated by the gentle roll of a coastal steamer.

They arrived off Nome on May 24, 1914. The ice prevented them from entering the harbor.

This was the reality that Bartlett had fled and could not escape. The Bering Strait remained locked, the same ice that had trapped the Karluk now blocking his return to civilization. The Herman stood off the coast for days while Pedersen waited for an opening, for a shift in wind or current that might create a lead through the pack. Bartlett watched the ice with the professional attention of a man who had spent his life reading its signals. He saw pressure ridges where plates had collided, open water where currents had maintained their force, the general drift that carried everything westward toward the Siberian coast. The ice would break. It always broke. The question was whether it would break in time, and in a configuration that allowed passage.

When the Herman finally entered Nome harbor, Bartlett found a town that had grown fat on gold and was losing weight fast. The rush of 1900 had left a litter of false-front buildings, saloons with imported mirrors, a federal courthouse that adjudicated claims on land the government did not control. The Arctic fleet that had once supported the whaling industry had shrunk to a handful of vessels, most of them laid up for lack of profit. The men who remained were the ones who could not afford to leave, or who had nowhere else to go.

Bartlett began again. His uniform, battered but recognizable, earned him audiences that his words alone might not have secured. He presented himself to the collector of customs, to the commander of the revenue cutter Bear, to the editors of the two newspapers that served the district. Each presentation required the same narrative: the Karluk, the drift, the abandonment, the march, the survivors on Wrangel Island. Each listener received the story through their own filter of interest and skepticism. The customs official wondered about duties on any recovered property. The newspaper editors calculated the circulation value of a rescue. The revenue cutter commander, a veteran of Arctic service himself, understood what Bartlett was asking and what it would cost.

The Bear could not sail until July. Her mission was law enforcement, not rescue, and her schedule was bound to the fishing season and the annual relief of the whaling fleet. The commander was sympathetic but constrained. He would request authorization from Washington. He would prepare his ship for the possibility. He could promise nothing.

Bartlett had heard this before. He had walked out of the Chukchi Sea to escape exactly this rhythm of request and deferral, of sympathetic faces and immovable systems. Now he found the same rhythm in American guise, the same translation of urgency into administrative procedure. The difference was scale. In Nome, he could reach Washington by cable in hours rather than weeks. He could invoke the name of Peary, with whom he had sailed, to establish credentials that opened doors in Ottawa. He could, if necessary, charter a vessel with private funds and the guarantee of future government reimbursement.

This last possibility became his focus. Through the network of merchants and shipowners who controlled Nome’s commercial life, he identified three vessels that might be capable of reaching Wrangel Island. The King and Winge was a new fishing schooner, built for the Arctic trade with a reinforced hull and auxiliary engine. The Silver Wave was older, slower, but available immediately at a price that Bartlett could not meet without outside support. The Herman, which had brought him from Siberia, was already preparing to return north for the whaling season; Pedersen would consider a detour for compensation that reflected the risk.

Bartlett negotiated with all three, playing each against the others in a market where he had no leverage except his story and his certainty. The King and Winge offered the best combination of speed and strength, but her owners demanded cash in advance, proof of Canadian government backing, and a release from liability that no lawyer in Nome would draft without payment. The negotiations consumed days while the ice in the harbor thinned and the fishing fleet prepared to sail. Every captain knew that the window for reaching Wrangel Island was narrow. Once the main pack broke up, the Chukchi Sea would become a maze of drifting floes and sudden gales that no prudent mariner would enter.

Bartlett sent more telegrams. To Ottawa, demanding authorization for expenditure. To Victoria, requesting that the expedition’s agent advance funds against future claims. To Stefansson, though he did not know where Stefansson was or whether he would respond. The replies, when they came, offered variations on the theme of delay. The Canadian government would consider the request. The expedition’s funds were encumbered by prior obligations. Stefansson was unreachable, presumed to be continuing his scientific work in the western Arctic.

The captain who had crossed the ice to save his crew now faced a barrier he could not walk across. Money. Authority. The permission of distant men who had never seen Wrangel Island and could not imagine what waited there. He sat in the lobby of the Golden Gate Hotel, a building whose name advertised aspirations rather than geography, and composed a final telegram to the Minister of Naval Service. He stated the facts without emotion: twenty-five persons on an island without food, the ice breaking, the available vessels preparing to sail without them. He requested immediate authorization to charter the King and Winge at a stated cost. He noted, as a postscript that violated every protocol of official correspondence, that he had crossed four hundred miles of Arctic ice to deliver this message, and that he would not have done so if the need were less than absolute.

The response arrived the following morning. Authorization granted. Funds would be wired to Nome. The King and Winge was to proceed with all possible speed to Wrangel Island, locate survivors, and return to Nome or the nearest Alaskan port.

Bartlett held the telegram for a long moment, reading it twice to confirm that the words meant what they appeared to mean. Then he moved. The King and Winge lay at anchor in the outer harbor. Her master, a Norwegian named Jochumsen, had already provisioned for the fishing season and was waiting only for the ice to clear. Bartlett presented his authorization, watched Jochumsen verify the bank draft that accompanied it, and secured the agreement that had cost him six weeks of walking, waiting, and pleading.

They sailed on June 5, 1914.

The asymmetry of knowledge haunted him. He knew that rescue was coming. They did not. They would have watched his departure with Kataktovik in March, would have calculated the impossibility of his journey, would have adjusted their expectations to a world that no longer included him. Some would have believed him dead. Others would have believed he had abandoned them. The truth—that he had walked across a sea to find them help—would arrive with the same suddenness as any other change in their fortunes, unheralded, unexplained, carrying its own burden of adjustment.

The King and Winge made slow progress. The ice in the Bering Strait had begun to move, but movement meant danger as much as opportunity. Jochumsen threaded his vessel through leads that opened and closed with the tide, anchoring to floes when the pack closed in, waiting for wind or current to create passage. Bartlett recognized the pattern from his own Arctic experience, the patience that masked itself as caution, the constant calculation of risk against time. He had no control here. He was a passenger on a vessel he had chartered but could not command, dependent on a master whose priorities were not entirely his own. The fishing season waited. Every day spent searching for castaways was a day not spent catching herring.

They reached Wrangel Island on September 7, 1914.

The landing was anticlimactic. The eleven survivors—eleven, where there had been twenty-five—were gathered on the beach, gaunt, hollow-eyed, barely able to comprehend that the ship on their horizon was real. Bartlett went ashore in the small boat, recognizing faces that had changed beyond easy recognition, missing faces that he did not need to ask about. The questions that followed—who had died, how, when—would be asked later, in the formal setting of official inquiry. What mattered in the moment was the physical fact of rescue, the translation of bureaucratic authorization into human presence, the end of a waiting that had lasted longer than any of them had expected to survive.

But this chapter is not about that arrival. It is about what preceded it: the walk across the ice that brought Bartlett to Emma Harbor, the telegrams that disappeared into Russian wires, the negotiations in Nome that turned authorization into action. The rescue of the Karluk survivors was not a single event but a chain of conversions—physical endurance into information, information into permission, permission into money, money into a ship, a ship into presence. Each conversion introduced delay, distortion, and the possibility of failure. That they succeeded, that eleven people survived where twenty-five had been left, is a measure of Bartlett’s persistence and the luck that sometimes rewards it. That they succeeded so slowly, through mechanisms so poorly adapted to the urgency they faced, is a measure of something else: the cost of distance in a world that had not yet learned to compress it.

Bartlett, trapped in a web of telegrams, negotiations, and reluctant shipmasters, had set a rescue in motion. Its arrival was now hostage to distances, diplomacy, and the melting ice of the Chukchi Sea.
