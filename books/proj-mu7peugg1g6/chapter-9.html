Chapter 9

The Ice Closes Its Jaws

The Karluk had become, in the most literal sense, a vessel for twenty-two lives, carrying them toward a future that the ice would determine. She had never been built for this. A whaler constructed to chase bowheads through open leads, she now drifted westward through the Beaufort Sea in the grip of pack ice that had already held her for four months, her hull groaning with each shift of pressure, her crew counting the days by the darkening of the sky and the thickening of the frost on her inner walls.

At 6: 30 in the morning of December 2, 1913, the ice struck. Not the slow, grinding pressure they had learned to sleep through, but a sudden, directed assault. A pressure ridge, ice forced upward by the collision of floes, sometimes forming walls twenty feet high, drove against the Karluk’s starboard bow with a violence that shook men from their bunks. The sound reached them first: a thunder-crack of splitting timber, then the shudder that travelled the length of the ship, vibrating through the deck planks into their boots. Captain Robert Bartlett was on deck before the echo faded, his Newfoundlander’s frame bent against the wind that swept across the frozen pack, his eyes finding the fresh wound in the ship’s side where the oak ribs had splintered against the pressure.

He made his entry in the log with the precision that would characterize every record of the coming weeks. Time, position, wind, temperature, damage, action taken. The language of a man who understood that survival in the Arctic depended on the discipline of documentation, on the refusal to let fear or hope distort what the instruments and his own eyes reported. The Karluk had taken water. The pumps were engaged. The carpenter and his mates were shoring the damaged timbers with whatever wood could be spared from the already-dwindling stores.

This was the first unmistakable signal that the ice’s grip was turning violent. The assault would not stop.

—-

The weeks that followed established a rhythm of deterioration that Bartlett documented with mounting exactitude. Each pressure ridge that slammed into the hull left its signature: a new leak, a fresh buckle in the planking, a section of deck that no longer sat true. The engineers’ reports, compiled from daily inspections below the waterline, traced a ship growing porous. The pumps ran almost continuously now, their rhythmic thump becoming the background music of life aboard, broken only when the ice struck hard enough to throw the men from their feet and send them scrambling to check the sounding rods, the bilge levels, the integrity of the repairs they had made the day before.

The Karluk drifted westward through waters no survey vessel had charted, no sledge had crossed, no rescue could reach. The Canadian Arctic Expedition, conceived as a sweeping scientific enterprise with multiple parties and coordinated objectives, had reduced itself to this single point of struggling existence: twenty-two men, one dying ship, and the ice that surrounded them on every horizon. On the maps of the living world, they had already ceased to appear.

Bartlett’s response to the December 2 damage revealed the command style that would define the final weeks. He imposed what he called “winter routine” with renewed strictness. Every man had assigned duties. Every hour had its purpose. The scientific instruments that had justified the expedition’s existence were packed, inventoried, prepared for transfer to ice or land whenever the ship finally became untenable. The stores were rechecked, recounted, redistributed. The dogs, already suffering from the cramped conditions on deck, were exercised in rotation across the floes, their handlers watching for the cracks and leads that could swallow a man or an animal without warning.

Bartlett had seen ships die before. He understood that the margin between survival and catastrophe was measured in preparation, in the refusal to let morale dissolve into the despair that came from inaction. But the preparation itself told its own story. Each crate secured for evacuation, each cache of supplies marked for retrieval, each contingency plan rehearsed with the crew, these were admissions that the Karluk’s life was now measured in days or weeks, not months.

—-

Inside the ship, the deterioration was as much atmospheric as structural. The Karluk had never been spacious. A 129-foot whaler converted to expedition flagship, she had been crowded with twenty-five men even before Stefansson’s departure reduced the number by three. Now, with hatches battened against the deepening cold and the stove in the forecastle burning almost continuously, the air grew thick with the condensation that froze in beards and hair, with the smell of damp wool and unwashed bodies, with the particular odor of a wooden vessel taking water into her timbers and beginning, slowly, to rot from within. The ship had been built in 1884 as a tender for the Alaska salmon fishery, not for this.

The scientists who had signed on for geographic discovery and anthropological collection found their purposes narrowing to the immediate physics of endurance. William McKinlay, the young magnetician, maintained his instruments as long as conditions permitted, recording the drift’s movement through variations in the magnetic field. But his diary entries, like those of his colleagues, increasingly focused on the sensory experience of the siege: the sound of the ice, the quality of the light, the texture of fear.

They learned to distinguish the ice’s moods. The grinding of floe against floe, a constant low rumble like distant artillery, meant the pack was shifting, rearranging itself under the influence of wind and current. That was normal, survivable. The sharp reports of splitting ice, like rifle shots, signaled pressure building somewhere in the matrix, forces seeking release. These demanded attention, a readiness to move if the crack opened near the ship. But the deep, resonant boom that came when a pressure ridge formed, that was the sound of assault, of ice finding its maximum expression, and it brought every man to deck or to the pumps with the knowledge that the Karluk had been struck again.

By mid-December, the temperature had dropped so far that exposed flesh froze in minutes. The deck became a treacherous surface of ice and snow that had to be cleared continuously, for safety and for a second reason Bartlett had calculated: every pound of accumulated weight pressed the hull deeper into the water, made it more vulnerable to the upward thrust of pressure from below. The men worked in shifts, chipping and shoveling, their breath forming instant frost on their scarves and eyelashes. The work was mechanical, necessary, and it kept them from the contemplation of their position that came with stillness.

—-

The institutional roots of their predicament became visible in these weeks, not as abstract analysis but as concrete constraints that shaped every decision. The Karluk had been acquired by Vilhjalmur Stefansson in 1913 for a sum that reflected her age and her unsuitability for the purpose to which she was now being put. She had been built for the Okhotsk Sea whaling grounds, for pursuits that required speed in open water and capacity for processing blubber, not for surviving the crushing pressure of polar ice. The Canadian government, assuming responsibility for the expedition, had accepted this vessel as adequate for its flagship. The inspection at Esquimalt had noted the thickness of her timbers, the soundness of her frames, but had not, and perhaps could not, addressed the fundamental mismatch between her design and her mission.

The failure ran deeper than engineering. Arctic exploration in the early twentieth century required vessels built to new specifications, and the traditions of whaling and sealing that had produced men like Bartlett were not automatically transferable to the scientific and geographic ambitions that Stefansson represented. The expedition had been an idea that transcended its vessel. The vessel was now demonstrating the cost of that transcendence.

Stefansson’s departure in September, presented at the time as a hunting expedition to secure caribou for the winter, had removed from the ship the one man whose authority derived from the expedition’s founding conception. Bartlett commanded by default, by the maritime tradition that made the captain sovereign aboard his vessel, but his authority was undermined by the ambiguity of his position. He had not been chosen to lead the expedition. He had been hired to navigate and to manage the ship. Now he found himself responsible for lives that had been committed to purposes he had not devised, in conditions that the expedition’s planning had failed to anticipate.

The men knew this. The scientists, in particular, had signed on for Stefansson’s vision, not for Bartlett’s competence. The division between the ship’s crew and the scientific staff, always present, hardened under pressure. The crew respected Bartlett absolutely; they had seen him navigate, had watched him work alongside them in the worst conditions, knew that his knowledge of ice and ship and survival was genuine and hard-won. The scientists, many of them younger, less experienced in Arctic conditions, oscillated between gratitude for Bartlett’s competence and resentment at the narrowing of their ambitions. They had come to discover unknown lands, to record unknown peoples, to advance the boundaries of knowledge. They were now engaged in a struggle to keep a leaking ship afloat, and the disproportion between their training and their circumstances generated a particular kind of despair.

—-

Christmas came and went with the minimal observance that conditions permitted. The galley produced what hot food the stove could manage. The men exchanged such small gifts as they had prepared during the autumn, before the full severity of their situation had become apparent. There was no tree, no carols, no recreation beyond the cards and conversation that passed the hours of darkness. The sun had disappeared below the horizon in November; they would not see it again until late January. The perpetual twilight of midday, the absolute darkness of the long nights, compressed their world to the dimensions of the ship, to the circle of lamplight in which they ate and talked and tried to sleep through the ice’s continuous grinding.

The pressure increased through the holiday. On December 29, a ridge formed so close to the port side that the ship heeled visibly, her masts describing arcs against the faint luminescence of the sky. The men braced themselves against the tilt, waiting for the release or the final crack of surrender. It did not come. The ice held her at that angle for hours before shifting again, leaving new stresses in her frame, new leaks for the pumps to manage.

Bartlett’s log recorded each incident with the same format: time, circumstances, damage, response. But the accumulation of entries told a story that the individual lines withheld. The ship was dying by increments, and the increments were growing shorter. What had been weekly damage became daily, then hourly. The carpenter’s store of timber for repairs dwindled. The pumps, worked beyond their design, began to fail and required constant attention. The crew, sleeping in shifts, never fully rested, approached the exhaustion that made accidents inevitable.

—-

The new year brought no relief. January 1914 opened with temperatures that froze the condensation in the rigging into formations of ice that had to be beaten off to prevent their weight from adding to the ship’s burden. The Karluk continued her westward drift, now deep into waters that Bartlett could only approximate on his charts, extrapolating from current and wind and the magnetic variations that McKinlay recorded. They were beyond help. The expedition’s other vessels, the Bear and the Alaska, had been released from their supporting roles and had returned to Nome. The nearest inhabited settlement was hundreds of miles away, across ice that was increasingly broken and dangerous. No wireless signal could penetrate the atmospheric conditions of the Arctic winter. They were alone with the ice, and the ice was winning.

The assault of January 10 marked a qualitative change. Early that morning, McKinlay recorded in his diary that a severe shudder had shaken the whole ship, not the localized impact of a pressure ridge against the bow or beam, but a convulsion that seemed to come from everywhere at once, as if the ice had finally found the configuration to grip the Karluk from all sides and begin the compression that would destroy her. Bartlett emerged from his cabin to find the deck tilted at an angle that made movement hazardous, the pumps choked with ice that had formed in the bilges, and new leaks opening faster than they could be located.

He gave the orders that acknowledged what the morning had made clear. All hands to lighten ship. The accumulated snow on the decks, which they had fought to keep cleared, was now joined by every movable object that could be thrown overboard or secured on the ice: spare lumber, excess stores, the scientific equipment that could not be carried in evacuation. The dogs, sensing the crisis, howled and strained at their chains. The men worked with the desperate energy that comes when physical labor seems the only alternative to panic.

Bartlett also ordered every man to prepare his personal gear for immediate departure. This was the formal acknowledgment that the Karluk’s survival was no longer the primary objective. The ship had become a platform for evacuation, a temporary refuge that could not be expected to last. Each man was to assemble the clothing, the sleeping bag, the rations that he would carry when the order came to abandon ship. The preparation itself was a kind of therapy, a focus for minds that might otherwise have fixed on the sounds of the hull’s ongoing destruction.

—-

The final days of the Karluk’s life were marked by a terrible clarity. The ice’s assault was no longer intermittent but continuous, a sustained pressure that found every weakness in her construction and exploited it. The men could hear the structure failing, could trace the progress of damage by the new notes in the symphony of groaning timber, the changed pitch of water entering the hull, the altered rhythm of the pumps as they struggled with volumes beyond their capacity.

Inside the ship, routine collapsed into emergency. The watch system, maintained for months, gave way to continuous all-hands duty. Men snatched sleep when they could, fully clothed, ready to rise at the next impact. The galley fires were kept burning only with extreme difficulty, and hot food became a memory. They ate cold rations, standing, listening, waiting.

The psychological strain revealed itself in small ways. Quarrels broke out over trivial matters: the allocation of a warm corner, the priority for pump access, the distribution of tasks that no one wanted. Bartlett suppressed these with the authority he had earned, but he could not suppress the fear that underlay them. Each man faced the recognition that his survival depended on factors beyond his control, on the random distribution of pressure in the ice, on the captain’s decisions, on the physical capacity to endure conditions that were approaching the limits of human tolerance.

The scientists, in particular, faced a double loss. Their expedition, conceived on a scale that would have placed their names in the record of Arctic discovery, had reduced itself to this single desperate situation. Their instruments, their notes, the materials of their specialties, would have to be abandoned or destroyed. They were becoming refugees from their own ambitions, stripped of the purposes that had justified the risks they had accepted.

—-

Through this deterioration, Bartlett maintained the documentation that would later allow the reconstruction of these days. His log entries, made sometimes at intervals of hours rather than days, recorded the progression with the same format he had used since August: position, weather, ice conditions, ship’s status, crew condition. The formality was a discipline against despair, a way of maintaining the professional identity that was his only resource against the chaos of their circumstances.

But the log also recorded decisions that revealed his strategic thinking. The location of supply caches on the ice, marked with flags that might survive the ship’s loss. The preparation of the small boats for transfer to stable floes. The selection of equipment that would be essential for a march to land, however distant. The assignment of responsibilities for the evacuation, ensuring that each man knew his role when the order came.

These preparations acknowledged a reality that the men had been slow to accept. The Karluk was not going to survive. The ice that had held her for four months, that had carried them westward beyond any hope of rescue, was now completing the destruction that had been implicit in their situation from the first. The question was no longer whether they would abandon ship, but when, and under what conditions, and with what prospects for the survival that would follow.

The physical symptoms of the ship’s death were unmistakable. The deck tilted at angles that made normal movement impossible. The pumps, even when they functioned, could not keep pace with the inflow. The timbers that had been shored and reshotred were failing faster than they could be repaired. The Karluk was becoming a wreck, her structure compromised beyond the possibility of restoration, her only remaining function to provide shelter until the shelter itself became untenable.

—-

The human cost of this progression was recorded in the diaries that several of the men maintained. McKinlay’s entries, later published as The Voyage of the Karluk, and its Tragic Ending, traced his own movement from scientific purpose to raw survival. The magnetician who had joined the expedition to advance knowledge of the Earth’s field found himself recording instead the temperature, the wind, the quality of the ice, the physical and mental state of his companions. The displacement was complete. The expedition had become an ordeal, and the ordeal was measured in the body’s capacity to endure cold, hunger, exhaustion, fear.

Other diaries, some lost with their authors, some recovered later, told similar stories. The anthropologist who had expected to study living cultures found himself reduced to the most primitive conditions of human existence. The photographer whose equipment had been intended to document unknown landscapes struggled to keep his fingers functional in temperatures that destroyed manual dexterity. The young seamen, less educated, perhaps less imaginative, found their advantage in the simplicity of their duties, in the capacity to follow orders without the distraction of disappointed ambition.

The ice continued its assault without reference to these human variations. Pressure ridges formed and reformed, driven by currents and winds that the men could measure but not predict. The Karluk drifted westward at a rate that Bartlett calculated from his observations, a rate that carried them further from any known land, any possibility of rescue, any connection to the world they had left. They were becoming, in the most literal sense, a floating island, a self-contained world drifting toward a destination that the ice would determine.

—-

The final days of January brought the culmination that the preceding weeks had made inevitable. The ice’s pressure, distributed across the Karluk’s hull in patterns that the men could not fully perceive, found the weaknesses that months of strain had prepared. The leaks became floods. The pumps failed. The structure that had been their home, their fortress, their only protection against the Arctic environment, began to collapse around them.

Bartlett’s orders in these hours were precise and limited. Save what could be saved. Prepare for evacuation. Maintain discipline and hope. The men responded with the mixture of efficiency and desperation that characterizes such moments, carrying supplies across the tilting deck, lowering boats to the ice, securing the dogs that would be essential for any travel that followed. The Karluk was dying, but the community she had sustained would survive if the evacuation could be completed before the final collapse.

The last entries in the ship’s log, made by Bartlett as water reached the cabin where he had worked and slept for four months, recorded the position where the ice finally completed its destruction. The details would be disputed later, in the inquiries and the memoirs, but the essential fact was clear. The Karluk, flagship of the Canadian Arctic Expedition, acquired to serve the scientific ambitions of Vilhjalmur Stefansson, had been destroyed by the ice that she was never built to survive.

The men who abandoned her, twenty-two in number, reduced by death and division from the twenty-five who had sailed from Nome, carried with them the equipment and supplies that Bartlett’s preparation had made possible. They established themselves on the ice, in camps that would be their home through the remaining weeks of winter, and began the wait for conditions that would allow them to travel. An advance party of four would later lose their way on the march and be found dead on Herald Island years later. Another party of four would detach themselves and never be seen again. Of the seventeen who would eventually reach Wrangel Island, three would die before rescue arrived in September 1914.

But that outcome lay in the future, unknown to the men who watched the Karluk settle into the ice that had destroyed her. In the immediate aftermath of abandonment, they faced the simpler and more urgent problem of survival: the construction of shelter, the distribution of rations, the maintenance of morale in conditions that tested the limits of human endurance. The ice had closed its jaws on their ship. Whether it would close on them as well remained to be determined.

In early January 1914, with the ship’s condition beyond salvage, Bartlett must begin planning evacuation onto the ice.
