Chapter 23

The Gathering of Testimony

In October 1914, the telegraph lines of North America carried the urgent traffic of a world at war. One cable reached Victoria, British Columbia, on the morning of October 14, having traveled from Ottawa. It carried no news of battle or diplomacy, which the conflict in Europe had already swallowed whole, but concerned only a ship that no longer existed and a voyage already finished. Yet its language bore the unmistakable weight of a state in motion, ordering an inquiry and demanding testimony from survivors delivered to safety weeks before.

The clerk who received it in the Victoria telegraph office had handled stranger traffic that autumn—coded military dispatches, fishing fleet positions, a frantic series of wires about a sealing disaster in the Gulf of St. Lawrence.

Hundreds of anxious spectators had gathered in St. John’s harbor to meet the SS Bellaventure as it arrived with the frozen bodies of sixty-nine sealers caught in a sudden gale. The clerk remembered reading that account in the newspaper. The description of corpses stacked on deck like cordwood stayed with him; the captain’s face was visible through the wheelhouse glass, gray with exhaustion.

The Karluk business seemed distant by comparison, a matter of administrative tidying. He made his copy and sent the boy with the original to the address listed for the expedition’s local agent, a shipping merchant who had handled supplies and now found himself responsible for locating men scattered between hospitals, boarding houses, and the first steamers south.

The commission’s machinery did not move quickly. Ottawa required three weeks to appoint its members, to secure funds from a parliament distracted by war appropriations, to locate a hearing room in a capital filling with military offices. By early November, formal notices had gone out: Robert Abram Bartlett, master of the Karluk, to appear November 16; William Laird McKinlay, magnetician, to follow; other survivors as their conditions permitted. The summons reached Stefansson’s southern party through more complicated channels. Rudolph Anderson, leading the biological division on the mainland coast, received his wire at a temporary station on the Coronation Gulf. He had not seen Stefansson since the previous autumn and had conducted his own operations independently through the winter and spring.

The hearing room occupied the second floor of a government building on Wellington Street, borrowed from the Department of Marine and Fisheries whose normal business—lighthouse inspections, harbor dredging, fishing regulations—had been pushed to one side. The commissioners were three: a retired naval captain, a lawyer from the Department of Justice, and a scientist from the Geological Survey who had himself wintered in the Arctic and understood something of ice conditions. They sat at a long table covered with green baize, facing the witness chair. Behind them, windows looked onto a courtyard where clerks came and went with files.

Bartlett arrived first, as his rank demanded. He had come down from Alaska on the mail steamer and stopped in Victoria long enough for doctors to examine him; they found his heart sound but his nerves considerably shaken. He continued east by rail. The journey gave him time to compose his account and decide what needed saying and what could be left between the lines. He wore his captain’s uniform; though he had polished its brass buttons for this room rather than for any sea duty ahead.

The commissioners began with preliminary matters: name, rank, relationship to the expedition. Bartlett supplied these in flat tones—a man who understood that an official inquiry has its own architecture and that he would be asked to inhabit it.

His answer took forty minutes. Bartlett moved through events with precision: departure from Nome on July 13; progress north through open water; first encounter with pack ice on August 2; gradual thickening; decision to follow the ice edge westward toward Herschel Island; final imprisonment on August 13. He named positions and dates without consulting notes until he corrected himself once—the lead closed on August 11th rather than August 12th—and then resumed.

The difficult matter came with Stefansson’s departure. Here Bartlett’s language grew more careful, more laden with the weight of things unsaid. He described the hunting party that left on September 20: Stefansson, two Inuit hunters, one white assistant, sledges, dogs, supplies for several weeks. He described his own understanding at the time, that the expedition leader intended to hunt caribou on the mainland, that he expected to return, that the ice conditions would permit rejoining the ship. He described his own growing certainty, as days passed and Stefansson did not return, that this understanding had been mistaken.

The chairman asked whether he had received any communication from Stefansson after his departure. Bartlett replied that he had received none, not until he reached Siberia. The chairman pressed further: had he considered that Stefansson’s departure left him in command of the expedition?

Bartlett hesitated. The question touched the wound that had not healed, the fracture in authority that had defined everything that followed. He was master of the Karluk, he said finally. He remained master of the Karluk. The expedition was another matter. Stefansson had his own instructions from the government. He had his own. The ship was his responsibility.

The commissioners understood what he was not saying. They had read the expedition’s founding documents, the ambiguous division of authority between the scientific leader and the ship’s commander, the assumption—never tested until now—that the two would operate in concert. They pressed him gently on the point: Had he considered taking the ship out of the ice, forcing a passage, abandoning the position? Bartlett explained the physics of their situation, the pressure of the pack, the Karluk’s inadequate engine power, the certainty that any attempt to break free would have crushed the hull weeks earlier than it was crushed. He spoke without self-defense, simply, as a man explaining weather to landsmen.

The second day brought McKinlay, whose testimony complemented and complicated Bartlett’s. The young Scotsman had kept the expedition’s magnetic records, had maintained a diary through the drift and the march and the long winter on Wrangel Island, had watched his companions die with the particular helplessness of a scientist trained to measure rather than intervene. He spoke more freely than Bartlett, with less instinct for institutional self-protection. He described the morale of the crew after Stefansson’s departure, the rumors that circulated, the gradual realization that they had been abandoned. He described Bartlett’s conduct during the crisis: the captain’s daily inspections of the ice, his preparations for evacuation, his refusal to surrender to the despair that infected others.

The chairman asked whether he considered Captain Bartlett’s decisions to be correct. McKinlay looked at Bartlett, who sat in the room’s corner, a witness to his own witness. He considered that Captain Bartlett had saved the lives of those who survived, he said. He considered that any other course would have killed them all.

The commissioners noted this. They noted, too, McKinlay’s account of the pemmican, the stores that had spoiled, the illness that had killed Mamen and Malloch—nephritis, the doctors later called it, brought on by faulty rations. They noted his description of the final weeks on Wrangel Island, the scavenging for bird eggs, the deaths of the last two men, the arrival of the King and Winge on September 7, 1914, the American schooners that had carried them to safety.

The testimony of the other survivors followed in descending order of their ability to travel. Some could not come at all: Bjarne Mamen was dead of the illness, John Munro had died in the attempt to reach Herald Island, six others had perished in the ice or on the island or in the small boat that had tried for the Siberian coast. Those who appeared—Hadley, the seaman who had become a hunter; Chafe, the fireman who had kept the last fires burning; the Inuit families who had endured the winter and would not speak in this room but would provide statements through interpreters—added layers of detail, of contradiction, of human texture that the commissioners struggled to organize into a single narrative.

The southern party’s testimony arrived by mail, then in person when Anderson and his colleagues returned to civilization in the spring of 1915. Their account was different in kind, concerned not with survival but with scientific work, not with a ship lost but with a program continued. Anderson described his own operations on the mainland coast, the collections completed, the geographical discoveries made, the three new islands that Stefansson would later claim. He described his relationship with the expedition leader: distant, professional, marked by disagreements over methods and priorities. He described his own petition to the government, filed before the Karluk survivors had even been rescued, complaining of Stefansson’s management and requesting independent status for the southern division.

The commissioners read this petition with attention. They read, too, the accounts that Stefansson himself had begun to publish, the articles in the New York Times and the popular magazines, the interviews that presented the Karluk disaster as an unfortunate episode in an otherwise successful enterprise. They noted the dates: Stefansson had reached Alaska in June 1914, had learned of the ship’s fate by wireless, had continued his own journey to the east, had not returned to Canada until 1918. They noted his explanation for the pemmican failure: their pemmican makers had failed them through carelessness. They noted that this explanation shifted responsibility from the expedition’s organization to an unspecified manufacturing error.

The documentary record accumulated on the commissioners’ table: the ship’s log, recovered from the ice and water-damaged but legible; McKinlay’s diary, with its daily observations of position and weather and morale; the meteorological records; the medical reports from Nome; the correspondence between Stefansson and the Department of Naval Service during the months when the Karluk was drifting and he was traveling. The commissioners worked to align these documents with the testimony, to find the points where memory and record converged or diverged, to construct a narrative that would satisfy the government’s need for explanation and the public’s need for accountability.

The process revealed the limits of official inquiry. The commissioners could establish what had happened: the dates, the positions, the sequence of deaths. They could not fully establish why. Stefansson’s decision to leave the ship remained opaque, defended by him as necessary for the expedition’s scientific mission, condemned by others as abandonment of his responsibility. The division of authority between scientific leader and ship’s commander, never clarified in the expedition’s planning, could not be clarified in retrospect. The commissioners could note that the Karluk had been acquired cheaply, that her condition had concerned experienced seamen, that her equipment for polar work had been inadequate; they could not say whether better preparation would have altered the outcome, whether the ice that took her would have taken any ship.

They worked through the winter of 1914-1915, their hearings continuing as the war in Europe settled into trench stalemate and the Arctic faded from newspaper headlines. They heard from the suppliers in Victoria who had provisioned the ship, from the naval inspector who had examined her hull, from the wireless operators who had handled the fragmentary communications. They compiled a file that grew to hundreds of pages, a paper reconstruction of a voyage that had ended in ice and silence.

The witnesses understood what was at stake. Bartlett, returning for a second round of questioning in January 1915, found himself defending not only his decisions but his reputation, his standing in the maritime community that had shaped his life. He spoke of the walk across the ice to Siberia, the 700 miles with Kataktovik, the arrival at Emma Town, the desperate negotiations for rescue. He spoke of his return to Wrangel Island, too late for those who had died in the final weeks, in time for those who still lived. The commissioners asked about his relationship with Stefansson, about whether he had considered himself abandoned, about whether he had received adequate support from the expedition’s organization. His answers grew shorter, more guarded, as the questioning touched the wound that would not close.

McKinlay, recalled in February, brought his diary to read from, the entries that recorded the daily struggle, the deaths, the slow erosion of hope. For March 1914, he read the passage about the advance parties setting out across the ice, about watching them disappear. For June, he read of the first bodies found. For August, he read of the last survivors waiting for rescue that did not come, not yet, not for weeks more. The commissioners listened to this voice from the ice, this precise Scottish accent describing hunger and cold and the particular despair of not knowing whether anyone in the world remained aware of their existence.

The southern party’s testimony, when it finally arrived in person, complicated the narrative that had been forming. Anderson and his colleagues had their own grievances, their own account of expedition management, their own evidence of Stefansson’s failures. They had completed their scientific work, had returned with collections and data that justified the expedition’s existence, but they had done so despite rather than because of the leadership they had received. Their petition, filed before the Karluk survivors were rescued, took on new weight in light of the disaster: it suggested that the problems of the expedition were systemic, traceable to the top, not merely the result of ice and bad luck.

Stefansson himself did not appear. By the time the commission was prepared to hear him, he had already departed for his second ice journey, the four-year absence that would take him to the edge of the Canadian archipelago and back. He submitted written answers to questions, prepared with legal assistance, that acknowledged certain errors of organization while defending his fundamental decisions. He explained his departure from the Karluk as necessary to secure game for the ship’s party, to maintain the expedition’s scientific program, to prevent the entire enterprise from being immobilized by a single ship’s misfortune. He noted that the southern party had continued its work, that geographical discoveries had been made, that the expedition’s overall objectives had been advanced despite the Karluk’s loss.

The commissioners studied these answers against the testimony they had heard. They noted the dates: Stefansson had left on September 20, had reached the mainland, had continued eastward, had not attempted to return to the ship or to communicate with it after the ice conditions changed. They noted his explanation for this: that he had understood the Karluk to be in no immediate danger, that he had believed Bartlett capable of managing the situation, that his own presence would have added nothing and would have sacrificed the expedition’s broader purposes. They noted, too, his silence during the months when the ship was drifting, when the survivors were struggling, when rescue might have been organized earlier if information had flowed more freely.

The documentary record provided its own commentary. The ship’s log, kept by Bartlett with the regularity of a man who understood that paper might outlast ice, showed the gradual deterioration of position and hope. McKinlay’s diary showed the human cost, the daily struggle to maintain routine and morale, the specific deaths and their circumstances. The meteorological records showed the weather that had shaped every decision, the winds that drove the ice, the temperatures that killed. The medical records from Nome showed the condition of the survivors: the frostbite, the scurvy, the nephritis that had killed Mamen and Malloch and weakened others.

The commissioners struggled to weave these strands into a coherent judgment. They were not a court; they could not assign criminal responsibility or civil liability. They were an administrative body, charged with advising the government on what had happened and what should be learned. Yet they understood that their report would shape reputation, would influence future employment, would enter the historical record as the official account of the Karluk disaster. They worked carefully, revising their findings, consulting experts on Arctic navigation and expedition organization, delaying their conclusions as the war demanded more and more of the government’s attention.

By the spring of 1915, the hearings had concluded. The witnesses had returned to their lives: Bartlett to a new ship, new responsibilities, the continuing effort to convert his Arctic experience into sustainable employment; McKinlay to teaching, to the slow process of physical recovery, to the memoir he would eventually publish; the others to scattered paths, some remaining in maritime work, others leaving the sea entirely. The commissioners remained with their files, with the mass of testimony and documentation that they had accumulated, with the responsibility of rendering judgment on events that had already passed into history.

They understood what they had not been able to resolve. The fracture in authority that had defined the disaster, the visible command structure that had splintered and sunk, leaving submerged loyalties and improvised leadership to surface, could not be mended by administrative finding. Stefansson’s departure, Bartlett’s assumption of responsibility, the survival of some and the death of others: these events remained open to interpretation, would remain so as long as the records were read. The commissioners could establish the facts of drift and position, of dates and distances, of who had died and who had survived. They could not finally establish the truth of command, the meaning of duty, the proper balance between scientific ambition and human care.

A mountain of conflicting evidence now exists, awaiting a verdict.
