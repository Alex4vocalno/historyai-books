Chapter 25

The Captain’s Private Reckoning

Seen from above, the transcontinental railway traced a dark line across the frozen Quebec countryside, its windows filmed with ice that fractured the winter light into pale prisms. On a morning in late February 1915, among the travelers bound for Ottawa, was a man recently returned from the Arctic, wearing a heavy wool coat that now smelled of coal smoke and railway dust. He was summoned to appear before a government commission, a journey that followed a procession of handshakes and banquets, leaving behind a ship with survivors and frozen bodies in its hold.

The Canadian Arctic Expedition Commission had begun its work in November 1914, while Bartlett was still making his way across Siberia. Its mandate was narrow: to inquire into the organization, equipment, and conduct of the expedition, and to determine whether any public money had been misapplied. The commissioners were respectable men—senators, civil servants, a judge—who understood that their findings would shape not only reputations but the future of northern exploration itself. They had already heard from Stefansson’s staff in Victoria, from the naval architects who had examined the Karluk before her departure, from the families of the dead. Bartlett was the last witness of consequence, and his testimony would carry the weight of having been there, of having led the expedition members after Stefansson left the ship with a small party, claiming he intended to hunt for caribou.

He took a room at the Russell House, the hotel where he had stayed a decade earlier after returning from Peary’s polar attempt. The city had grown in the interval, new government buildings crowding the skyline, but his room was unchanged: the same heavy furniture, the same gas fixture converted to electricity, the same view of Parliament Hill rising through the bare trees. He unpacked his papers slowly. There were the expedition records he had preserved through the drift and the wreck, wrapped now in oilcloth against the damp. There was the diary he had kept during the march to Wrangel Island, its pages warped by salt water. There were letters from family, from survivors, from strangers who had read of his trek across the ice and wished to congratulate him on his heroism. He read none of them.

The commission met in a committee room of the Langevin Block, a temporary chamber arranged while the Parliament buildings were being rebuilt after the fire. The room was overheated, the radiators clanking against the February cold. Bartlett sat in the witness chair on the morning of February 23, facing the three commissioners across a table covered in green baize. The chairman, a senator from Ontario, began with the formalities: name, rank, position with the expedition. Then he came to the substance.

Bartlett was asked whether he had been left in command of the Karluk when Stefansson departed. He confirmed that he had. He was asked whether this command extended to the entire expedition. He answered carefully, stating that he had understood himself responsible for the ship and her company, while Stefansson had taken his own secretary and several scientific staff with him. The commissioners pressed him on whether he had protested the leader’s departure. He had not. He had considered Stefansson the leader of the expedition, and the ice was closing. The leader believed he could reach the coast and organize relief. Bartlett’s duty, as he saw it, was to remain with the ship.

The questioning continued through the morning and into the afternoon. The commissioners wanted to know about the Karluk’s condition, her stores, her fitness for the voyage she had been assigned. They wanted to know about the drift, the wreck, the decision to abandon ship. They wanted to know about the march to Wrangel Island, about the parties that had been sent to Herald Island and had never returned, about the deaths on the island during the months of Bartlett’s absence. He answered each question with the same careful precision, the same refusal to speculate beyond what he had seen.

When the chairman turned to the trek to Siberia, the room grew quieter. Bartlett confirmed that he had set out from Wrangel Island on March 18, 1914, with one companion, an Inuk hunter named Kataktovik. He had left twenty-two persons on the island, with provisions for several weeks and the expectation that he would return with a rescue vessel before the ice broke up. He reached the Siberian coast on the morning of April 7, after traveling approximately 700 miles across the ice.

The chairman consulted his notes. Four men had died on Wrangel Island during Bartlett’s absence. Three others had died on Herald Island, where they had gone to hunt. Bartlett was not present at any of these deaths. Had he formed any opinion as to their causes?

Bartlett’s hands rested on the arms of the witness chair. He had held this information for months, carrying it with him across the ice, through the Russian settlements, down the coast to Nome. The men on Herald Island, he said, had been found in their tent, apparently overcome by some noxious fumes. The island was volcanic. There may have been gas. The men on Wrangel Island had died of various causes—scurvy, exposure, the effects of starvation. One man had been shot. Bartlett learned of these matters when he returned with the rescue vessels in September.

He had left them alive.

The commission adjourned for the day. Bartlett walked back to his hotel through streets that were freezing hard after a late thaw, the pavement treacherous underfoot. He had said nothing that was not true. Nothing, either, about the dreams that came to him now, the images of ice and darkness that interrupted his sleep in warm rooms. He had not mentioned the letter he had received from a widow, whose husband had been among the Herald Island party, asking whether Albert had suffered. He had not mentioned his own calculations, repeated endlessly, of whether a different decision on some particular morning might have altered the arithmetic of survival.

The hearings continued for three more days. The commissioners pressed him on the adequacy of the Karluk’s provisioning, on Stefansson’s recruitment practices, on the division of authority that had left a ship’s captain responsible for a scientific expedition he had not designed. Bartlett answered what he could and declined to answer what he could not verify. When the chairman asked finally whether he had any recommendations for the conduct of future Arctic expeditions, he was silent for a moment.

Ships should not be sent where ships cannot go. Men should not be divided against themselves. And no leader should abandon his people to the ice.

The commission’s report would not be issued for another eleven months. Bartlett left Ottawa without waiting for its conclusion, taking the train west to Vancouver, where a second inquiry awaited him.

—-

The Vancouver hearing was smaller, less formal, concerned primarily with the settlement of claims against the expedition’s estate. Bartlett appeared as both witness and creditor, having advanced his own funds for supplies in Alaska and not been reimbursed. The room was a maritime court near the harbor, smelling of tar and salt water, and the questions were practical: dates, amounts, the disposition of equipment left behind in Nome. He answered them efficiently, anxious to be finished.

But the Vancouver sitting brought one encounter that Ottawa had not. On the morning of his second day in the city, a man approached him in the corridor outside the courtroom—thin, nervous, with the hollow eyes of someone who had not slept properly in months. William McKinlay, the young magnetician, had survived Wrangel Island and returned to civilization on the same vessel as Bartlett, though they had scarcely spoken during the voyage. McKinlay had been invalided to Scotland after the rescue, had spent months in hospital, and had come back to Canada only recently to settle his own affairs with the expedition.

They stood awkwardly in the corridor, two men who had shared an extremity that neither could yet describe. McKinlay spoke first. He had told the commissioners in Ottawa what he could. About the island. About the waiting. He had told them Bartlett did everything that could be done.

Bartlett thanked him.

The commissioners had wanted to know about Stefansson. Whether his leaving was justified. McKinlay’s voice had taken on an edge. He had told them it was not. He had told them what it meant to be left there, not knowing if anyone would come.

Bartlett looked at the younger man, seeing in his face the same strain he recognized in his own mirror. The commission would make its own determination. His business was with the ice, not with the judging of men.

But McKinlay pressed him. Bartlett had been there. He knew what it cost.

Bartlett knew what it cost.

They parted without further words. Bartlett watched him go down the corridor, this young man who had kept scientific instruments running while men died around him, who had measured magnetic variation while the food ran out. He understood McKinlay’s anger. He shared it, in ways he could not yet articulate. But he had learned something in fifty years of Arctic travel that McKinlay had not yet absorbed: that survival was its own verdict, that the living owed something to the dead that could not be paid in testimony.

He finished his business in Vancouver and took passage east, not to Ottawa or to the expedition’s offices in Victoria, but home.

—-

Brigus, Newfoundland, sat on the western shore of Conception Bay, a fishing village that had sent its sons to the ice for generations. Bartlett had been born there in 1875, had left at seventeen to sail with his uncle on Arctic sealing voyages, had returned between expeditions to a house that his mother kept ready for him. The house was smaller than he remembered, the rooms darker, his mother older. She asked few questions about the Karluk. She cooked the meals he had eaten as a child—salt fish, potatoes, bread baked in the iron stove—and let him sit by the window looking out at the harbor where the sealing fleet would soon be fitting out for the spring hunt.

He had arrived in March 1915, while the commission was still deliberating in Ottawa. The village knew who he was. The St. John’s newspapers had carried his photograph, the account of his trek, the list of the saved and the lost. Neighbors came to the door with congratulations, with questions he could not answer, with the comfortable assumption that heroism was a condition one returned from unchanged. He received them politely and retreated to the room upstairs where he kept his charts and his journals from earlier expeditions.

The work of memory began there, in the evenings after his mother had gone to bed. He spread his papers across the small desk and began to set down the story of the Karluk—not for the commission, not for the newspapers, but for himself. The narrative moved slowly, each paragraph requiring him to revisit decisions that had seemed clear at the time and now appeared contingent, reversible, weighted with consequence he had not measured. He wrote of the morning in September 1913 when Stefansson had walked away across the ice, of the long drift westward through waters no ship had charted, of the crushing and sinking in January, of the march to Wrangel Island with the temperature at forty below.

He wrote, too, of the men who had died. The writing came harder. He had not seen most of the deaths; he had been on the ice, walking toward Siberia, while they occurred. But he was the captain. The phrase carried a legal meaning in maritime law, a practical meaning in the hierarchy of ships, and now, in the quiet room in Brigus, a moral meaning that attached to his name with the persistence of frost to metal. He wrote the names: Malloch, Beuchat, Morris, Kukoff, Mamen, Williamson, Hadley. He wrote the circumstances as he had learned them—secondhand, from survivors’ accounts—and his own judgments of where responsibility lay.

The spring came late to Newfoundland that year. Ice clogged the harbor into May, and when it cleared, the sealing fleet put out with a sense of foreboding that the older men recognized. On April 2, 1915, while Bartlett was still writing in his upstairs room, the SS Bellaventure returned to St. John’s with seventy-eight sealers frozen to death on her deck. They had been caught in a storm on the ice fields, separated from their ship, surrendered to the same cold that had taken the Karluk’s men. Hundreds of anxious spectators gathered in the harbor to meet the vessel, to search the bodies for sons and brothers, to witness what the ice could do even to those who knew it best.

Bartlett read the accounts in the St. John’s Evening Telegram. He knew the Bellaventure’s master, had sailed with some of the dead. The parallel was inexact—the sealers had been working their trade, not exploring unknown waters—but it struck him with the force of recognition. The Arctic took its toll regardless of purpose. The difference, if there was one, lay in the preparation, the leadership, the willingness to acknowledge that the ice made its own rules.

He put down his pen and walked to the harbor, watching the fishing boats prepare for the cod season. The work of his own narrative seemed suddenly insufficient. He had been telling the story of what happened, the sequence of events that had carried the Karluk from optimism to disaster. But the story he needed to understand was anterior to that: the story of how twenty-five men had come to be aboard a vessel unfit for her assignment, under a leader who would abandon them, in pursuit of objectives that dissolved even as they were articulated.

He returned to his desk and began again, this time from the beginning. The Karluk had been purchased for $10, 000, a price that reflected her age and her history as a whaler in the Okhotsk Sea. Stefansson had acquired her because she was available, because the government would fund an expedition but not the time to prepare it properly, because the Arctic demanded haste if one wished to claim its territories before competitors arrived. Bartlett had examined her in the Esquimalt dry dock and known her limitations. He had said so, he believed, to whoever would listen. But he had taken command nonetheless, had steered her north into the ice, had accepted the structure of authority that made him responsible for a vessel he had not chosen.

The commission could not require this reckoning and no public commendation could absolve it. He had survived. He had brought eleven others to safety. He had walked 700 miles across the ice and raised the alarm that made rescue possible. These were facts that would appear in newspaper accounts, in the commission’s report, in the expedition’s official history. But they were not the whole story, and they were not what kept him awake in the small hours, listening to wind off Conception Bay.

The whole story involved a question he could not yet answer: whether his own competence, his own endurance, his own success in reaching Siberia, had masked a failure of a different order. He had kept the Karluk afloat for four months in the ice, had organized the evacuation when she finally sank, had established a camp on Wrangel Island with the discipline that kept most of his party alive. But he had also accepted Stefansson’s departure without formal protest, had divided his forces by sending parties to Herald Island, had left twenty-two men on Wrangel Island with no certainty of return. Each decision had seemed necessary at the time. Each had been made under conditions of imperfect information and mounting desperation. But the cumulative result had been eleven deaths, and the captain who returned to civilization carried those deaths in a way that medals could not lighten.

He wrote through the summer of 1915, filling notebooks with the narrative that would eventually become his published account. The commission’s report arrived in January 1916, exonerating him of misconduct while distributing blame for the expedition’s failures across its organization, its leadership, and the inadequacy of its ship. He read it without satisfaction. The document was careful, judicious, designed to close an episode rather than to understand it. It noted that Stefansson had acted within his rights as expedition leader in leaving the Karluk, though it questioned the wisdom of his subsequent failure to organize immediate relief. It praised Bartlett’s conduct without examining the structural constraints that had shaped his choices. It recommended better preparation for future expeditions without acknowledging that the Karluk disaster had occurred precisely because preparation had been sacrificed to speed.

Bartlett put the report aside and continued his own work. By autumn 1915 he had moved to New York, where lecture agents were eager to book him on the circuit that served the nation’s hunger for Arctic adventure. He spoke in assembly halls and opera houses, projecting lantern slides of ice and Eskimo villages, describing his trek across the Chukchi Sea in terms that emphasized the heroic without approaching the haunted. After the lectures, in hotel rooms similar to the one in Ottawa where he had prepared his testimony, he returned to the manuscript.

The writing was changing him. He had begun with the intention of setting down the facts, of creating a record that would survive the distortions of newspaper accounts and official reports. But the facts resisted arrangement. Each time he described a decision he had made, he found himself describing the alternatives he had rejected, the information he had lacked, the pressures that had compressed his deliberations into hours or minutes. The narrative became an argument with himself, a prolonged examination of whether he could have done otherwise.

He corresponded with other survivors, with scientists who had been on the expedition’s southern party and had learned of the disaster only upon their own return. He wrote to McKinlay in Scotland, receiving in reply detailed accounts of the Wrangel Island winter that filled gaps in his own knowledge. He wrote to Stefansson, once, asking for clarification of certain dates and decisions. The reply was courteous, administrative, offering no acknowledgment of the fracture that had divided the expedition into those who had left and those who had remained.

By late 1915, the manuscript had grown to several hundred pages. He had described the Karluk’s acquisition and fitting-out, her voyage north, the entrapment in the ice. He had reached the point of Stefansson’s departure, that September morning when the hunting party had walked away across the floes, and here his pen moved more slowly. He wrote the scene as he remembered it: the leader’s assurance that he would return with caribou, the small group of men with their rifles and sledges, the diminishing figures against the white horizon. He wrote, too, of his own silence, his failure to demand written orders or to protest the division of his command.

The center of his narrative was coming clear: not the wreck itself—inevitable once the ice closed—nor even his trek to Siberia—which succeeded—but rather that September morning when Stefansson walked away across the floes. That moment contained every structural failure that followed: a ship’s captain responsible for a scientific expedition he had not designed; a leader responsible for men he had not chosen; a survivor responsible for deaths he had not witnessed. The commission examined this moment and found no violation of regulations. Bartlett examined it and found there was no absolution.

He wrote through the winter, in New York and in Brigus, accumulating pages that he would revise and expand over the following years. The book that emerged—The Last Voyage of the Karluk, published in 1916—was restrained, factual, almost dry in its recounting of extreme events. Readers found in it the confirmation of Bartlett’s heroism, the documentation of his endurance. They did not find, because he had not included it, the private reckoning that had produced it: the nights of doubt, the correspondence with survivors’ families, the slow recognition that survival was not absolution.

In the spring of 1916, as his manuscript went to press, Bartlett received a letter from the widow of Bjarne Mamen, the young Norwegian ski champion who had died on Wrangel Island of scurvy and exhaustion. She wrote to thank him for bringing home her husband’s diaries, for his kindness in describing Bjarne’s final days as peaceful. Bartlett read the letter several times. He had not described those days at all, having been absent when they occurred. The kindness was hers, in imagining a death she could bear.

He sat at his desk with the letter in his hand, the afternoon light failing over the harbor. The book would be published, the lectures would continue, the commission’s verdict would fade into the archives. But the reckoning would not conclude. It would extend through the remaining decades of his life, through forty more expeditions to the Arctic, through the gradual transformation of the man who had walked across the ice into the legend who had survived. The cost of that transformation was measured in the silence between his sentences, in the careful neutrality with which he described events that had scarred him, in the persistence of names that appeared in his private papers long after they had disappeared from public memory.

He took up his pen and wrote a final note in the margin of his manuscript, a sentence that would not appear in the published version. The captain is responsible for all that occurs aboard his ship, and for much that occurs after he has left her. Then he closed the book and went down to meet the evening, carrying with him the weight that no commission could measure and no narrative could finally discharge.
