Chapter 12

The Decision to March

The watch continued. The ice drifted toward whatever destination currents and winds had chosen.

At Shipwreck Camp, twenty-five survivors had settled into a rhythm suspended between living and waiting. They rose from snow houses, checked meat caches against bears, repaired sledges that might never carry them anywhere, and returned to sleep with the sun circling low on the horizon. The Karluk had been gone three weeks. The ice had become their country.

Captain Robert Bartlett measured this country in shrinking numbers. On January 29, 1914, he recorded the tally: pemmican enough for sixty days at half rations, flour for perhaps forty, fuel for the stoves already down to what two sledges could carry. The meat from the ship’s stores was nearly exhausted. Seals killed at the leads had grown scarce, and bears had learned to avoid the camp. Bartlett calculated the distance to Wrangel Island at something over one hundred miles southwest—a figure derived from dead reckoning, from the pack’s drift since the sinking, from star positions when clouds permitted. He wrote these numbers without comment, but their arithmetic was relentless. Each day of inaction consumed provisions that could not be replaced. Each week of waiting reduced the margin between survival and starvation.

William McKinlay, the expedition’s magnetician, kept a different record. His diary entry for January 29 noted the temperature at thirty below, the wind building from the northeast, and his own state of mind with a candor Bartlett’s log never permitted. The prospect of marching over this ice, he wrote, was terrible. They could not see the island. They did not know if the ice between here and there was passable. To leave this camp, where they had shelter and some security, for an unknown journey across moving pack— The sentence broke off. McKinlay was thirty-one, trained in scientific observation, accustomed to weighing evidence. The evidence of his senses told him that Shipwreck Camp, for all its privations, was a known quantity. The ice beyond its perimeter was not.

These two documents, the captain’s log and the magnetician’s diary, sat in the same frozen present. They did not contradict each other. They occupied different positions within the same narrowing space: Bartlett measuring what remained, McKinlay measuring what might be lost. The tension between them was not yet open conflict. It was the tension of a group that had not yet been asked to choose.

Bartlett had not asked. He had spent the weeks since the sinking establishing routines, distributing labor, maintaining the appearance of command as continuous activity rather than a series of decisions. The Inuit hunters, Kuraluk and Kataktovik among them, had built the snow houses and taught the qalunaat how to sleep in them. The sailors kept the sledges in repair. The scientists continued observations when instruments and weather permitted. This was the authority Bartlett understood: not the giving of orders but the projection of confidence that tomorrow would resemble today in its basic possibilities.

But the numbers in his log were eroding that projection. On January 31, he called a council of officers and senior men. The meeting took place in Bartlett’s snow house, the largest in camp, with the stove burning low to conserve fuel. Those present included Alexander Anderson, the first officer who had commanded the Karluk during the early drift; Bjarne Mamen, the Norwegian ski champion who served as athletic instructor; and several scientists whose expertise Bartlett needed to assess ice conditions. McKinlay was there, and so was John Munro, the ship’s carpenter who had become indispensable in the camp’s construction.

Bartlett laid out his calculations without rhetoric. The food would not last until summer. The ice was drifting west and north, away from the Alaskan coast where any rescue would originate. Their position was uncertain, their last reliable fix having been before the sinking, but the pack’s general movement was known from whaling records and exploratory voyages. They were being carried into the Chukchi Sea, toward waters that even the whalers avoided. To stay was to gamble on a rescue that had no reason to find them, in a region where no ship would venture until the ice broke in late summer. By then, the pemmican would be exhausted, the dogs dead or eaten, the men’s strength depleted beyond recovery.

The alternative was to march. Wrangel Island lay somewhere to the southwest, a landmass large enough to offer game, to permit extended survival, perhaps to attract passing vessels. The distance was uncertain. The ice condition was unknown. But the island was land, with the possibilities land contained: seals on its shores, birds on its cliffs, material for shelter more durable than snow.

Anderson spoke first. As first officer, he had kept the Karluk afloat through four months of drift, had supervised salvage during the final hours, had watched the ship go down with the methodical attention of a man recording damage for an inquiry that might never convene. He was not inclined to dramatic gestures. He asked Bartlett for his estimate of ice conditions between camp and island.

Bartlett had none. The pack was in constant motion. Pressure ridges formed and shifted overnight. Leads opened and froze with temperature fluctuation. What sentries could see from camp was surface of sufficient solidity to bear sledges in the immediate vicinity, but that visibility extended only to the horizon, and the horizon moved with the observer.

Mamen offered what he could. He had scouted northward in the days after the sinking, traveling with Anderson on a reconnaissance that found only more ice, more pressure, more featureless obstruction. The skiing was possible but exhausting: thin snow cover over rough ice, temperature making waxing a constant problem. He did not say whether a march of one hundred miles was feasible. He described what he had seen, and let the description stand as evidence.

The scientists spoke of what they knew. The geologist measured ice thickness under camp: enough to be safe, not enough to be reassuring. The meteorologist summarized weather patterns: the stability of high-pressure systems that could strand a party in open ice, the sudden storms that could make navigation impossible. None had experience with over-ice travel of this distance. Their expertise was in observation, not in translation of observation into tactical decision.

McKinlay said little. His diary that evening recorded the meeting’s atmosphere more than its content. The Captain, he observed, was determined to go. He listened, but he had already decided. This was perception, not accusation. McKinlay understood that command in these circumstances could not be collective. The council was consultative; the decision would be singular. What he feared was not Bartlett’s decisiveness but its object: the march itself, the leaving of relative security for a journey whose end was invisible and whose path was unstable.

The meeting ended without resolution. Bartlett had not asked for a vote. He had presented his assessment and received theirs, and the gap between them was not matter of fact but of valuation. To Bartlett, the certainty of eventual starvation outweighed the uncertainty of the march. To others, the uncertainties multiplied: the island’s exact position, the ice’s load-bearing capacity, the physical condition of men already weakened by shipboard routine and sudden trauma, the dogs’ ability to work in temperatures that killed motivation along with tissue.

In the days that followed, this gap became visible in the camp’s daily life. Bartlett began preparations with the methodical energy that characterized his command. Sledges were loaded and reloaded, weight distributed according to calculations of necessity and of men’s capacity to pull. The strongest dogs were selected for the trail; the weakest were designated for food, a decision the Inuit accepted and the qalunaat found harder to acknowledge. Snow houses were stripped of interior fittings, the stove disassembled for transport, medical supplies packed with attention to accessibility that anticipated injury.

Others moved more slowly. McKinlay continued his magnetic observations, recording needle variation with a precision that seemed increasingly abstract. His diary entries grew longer, more reflective, as if writing could delay departure. We are to leave this place, he wrote on February 2, but he could not feel it as real. The camp had become a world. The ice beyond was— The sentence broke off.

The breaking point came from outside the camp’s deliberations. On February 4, 1914, Bjarne Mamen returned from a scouting mission with news that forced the abstract into concrete form. He had traveled with a four-man party led by Alexander Anderson, a reconnaissance toward the north shore of Wrangel Island that Bartlett had authorized to verify the island’s position and ice conditions. Mamen had turned back; Anderson’s party had continued, following leads when they could, hauling the sledge across pressure ridges when they must. They had not returned. Mamen reported to Bartlett that he had left the group a few miles short of land that was evidently not Wrangel Island, and was probably Herald Island, 38 miles from their intended destination.

Mamen’s report was factual, exhausted, precise. He had left Anderson’s party on February 1, with instructions to return to camp if ice became impassable or if the island did not appear within reasonable distance. The ice had been passable. The island had not appeared. Anderson had chosen to continue, and Mamen had chosen to return. The distance between these choices was now measured in miles of ice and days of silence.

Bartlett received this news in his snow house, with Mamen standing in the entrance where cold seeped through and light was failing. The implications were immediate. Anderson’s party, four men, one sledge, supplies for perhaps a week, were now either on Wrangel Island, still traveling, or lost in the pack. If they had reached the island, they had no means to communicate their arrival. If they were still traveling, their situation grew more desperate with each hour. If they were lost, the camp had lost its first officer and a quarter of its able-bodied men in a single decision whose wisdom could not now be assessed.

Bartlett’s response was characteristic. He recorded Mamen’s report in his log with the same precision he applied to fuel consumption and position fixes. Then he completed preparations for the main body’s departure. The loss of Anderson’s party, if it was loss, did not alter the arithmetic that had prompted the march. It simply removed one of the voices that had counseled caution, and demonstrated with brutal clarity what caution could cost.

McKinlay recorded a different response. Mamen came in at dusk, he wrote that evening. He had left them. He did not know if they were alive. And they were to follow the same path. The accusation was implicit, perhaps unintended: Mamen had not abandoned Anderson, he had followed instructions, had turned back when the party divided. But the structure of the sentence revealed McKinlay’s state of mind. The connection between Anderson’s fate and their own was not causal but parallel, not “therefore we must be careful” but “we will share their end.”

The council that convened on February 5 was different from the first. Anderson was absent. Munro, the carpenter, had assumed some of his responsibilities, but Munro was not an officer and did not pretend to strategic judgment. The remaining scientists, McKinlay among them, were more explicit in their doubts. They asked Bartlett to reconsider. They pointed to the loss of Anderson’s party as evidence of the ice’s unpredictability. They suggested that camp could be sustained longer than Bartlett’s calculations allowed, that seals might return to the leads, that drift might carry them closer to inhabited coasts than Wrangel Island offered.

Bartlett listened. His own diary, if he kept one separate from the official log, has not survived; we have only his actions and the accounts of those who watched him act. What is clear is that he did not modify his plan. He addressed specific objections with specific answers: the seals were not returning, the drift was carrying them northwest into emptiness, the physical condition of the men would deteriorate faster than their hopes could sustain. He did not dismiss the fear that McKinlay and others expressed. He weighted it differently, placed it in a balance where the certainty of eventual death outweighed the probability of immediate disaster.

The decision was announced on February 6. The main body of survivors would march for Wrangel Island on February 8, following the route that Mamen had traveled and Anderson’s party had continued. Remaining supplies would be cached at Shipwreck Camp for any who might follow or return. The weakest men, and the one woman, Ada Blackjack, the Inuit seamstress who had joined the expedition with her young son, would travel with the first sledges, protected from the heaviest labor. The Inuit hunters would serve as scouts and pathfinders, their knowledge of ice travel essential to the qalunaat’s survival.

This was the structure of Bartlett’s authority: not imposed by rank alone, but exercised through management of detail, projection of confidence in particulars when confidence in the whole was impossible to demonstrate. He could not promise that the march would succeed. He could promise that sledges were loaded correctly, that rations were measured, that daily stages were planned with reference to men’s demonstrated capacity rather than their hoped-for endurance.

Some accepted this structure. Munro, whose carpentry had built the camp, applied the same practicality to its dismantling. The sailors, accustomed to command hierarchies, prepared their gear without visible complaint. The Inuit, whose survival skills were now the expedition’s most valuable resource, followed Bartlett’s instructions while maintaining their own counsel, their own assessment of ice and weather that they did not always share with the qalunaat leaders.

Others resisted in ways that did not challenge Bartlett directly. McKinlay continued his magnetic observations through February 7, the last full day at Shipwreck Camp. His diary entry for that day recorded the readings with his usual precision, then added a sentence that broke from scientific objectivity. He did not believe they would reach the island. He did not believe they were meant to reach it. This was not addressed to Bartlett. It was not addressed to anyone. It sat in the private record, a judgment that the march was wrong without an alternative that could be declared right.

The night of February 7 was spent in the snow houses for the last time. Stoves were allowed to burn down, sleeping bags laid out on platforms that would not exist tomorrow. Bartlett made his final check of sledge loads by lantern light, verifying what he had verified before, the repetition serving perhaps as occupation for hands that had no other task in the waiting hours.

In Memphis, Tennessee, on this same day, Donald Smith died. He was ninety-three years old, a Scottish-Canadian fur trader who had become one of the wealthiest men in the British Empire, a philanthropist whose name was attached to railways and hospitals and the territorial expansion that made expeditions like the Canadian Arctic Expedition possible. As Lord Strathcona, he had served as High Commissioner to the United Kingdom from 1896 to 1914, a living connection between the imperial center and the colonial periphery.

His death was reported in newspapers that would not report the Karluk’s sinking for months, that would not report the march from Shipwreck Camp at all. The two events shared only a date, a coincidence of mortality that no narrative can make meaningful.

But they shared something else: the assumption that human will, properly organized and sufficiently funded, could overcome the resistance of distance and climate. Strathcona had built railways across the Canadian Shield. Bartlett was about to test whether the same will could build a path across the Arctic pack.

The morning of February 8 broke clear and cold, the temperature near thirty below, the wind light from the northeast. The camp rose in darkness; at this season, the sun remained below the horizon for all but a few hours of twilight. They assembled for departure with the mechanical efficiency that Bartlett’s preparations had made possible. Dogs were harnessed, sledges aligned in the order of march, the weakest travelers assigned to positions where trail-breaking would be easiest.

Mamen led the first party. His scouting experience made him the natural choice for advance guard, though that experience included his return from Anderson’s still-missing party. He carried instructions to mark the route, to send back word if ice conditions changed, to make contact with Anderson’s group if they could be found. These instructions assumed a coherence in the ice surface that Mamen’s own report had not confirmed, but they provided a structure for action that the alternative, marching blind into the pack, did not offer.

Bartlett traveled with the main body, positioned to observe and intervene as the day’s march developed. His sledge carried the expedition’s records, the logs and scientific observations that represented the official purpose of the enterprise, along with medical supplies and emergency rations that were his responsibility as commander. He had not explained to the men, and perhaps could not have explained, why the preservation of these documents mattered in comparison to the preservation of their lives. The explanation was embedded in the structure of authority he represented: the expedition was not merely survival, it was the record of survival, the account that would be rendered to the institutions that had authorized the voyage.

McKinlay traveled with the scientists, his magnetometer disassembled and packed, his diary accessible for the entries he continued to make. He recorded the departure without the dread of his earlier entries, as if the decision once made had released him from the burden of opposition. They left Shipwreck Camp at 9 a. m., he wrote. The ice was rough but passable. The dogs pulled well in the cold. They made perhaps four miles before halting for the night. The precision was restored, the observation of fact replacing the expression of fear. Whether this represented genuine acceptance or the discipline of a man who had learned to record what he could verify and withhold judgment on what he could not, the diary does not reveal.

The first day’s march established patterns that would be repeated or broken in the days to follow. The ice was not uniform. Pressure ridges rose like frozen waves, requiring sledges to be unloaded and carried across while dogs found their own paths. Leads of open water appeared without warning, forcing detours that added miles to the distance. Temperature fluctuated with the wind’s shifts, and the wind itself carried snow that reduced visibility to the length of a sledge team.

Bartlett managed these conditions with the attention to detail that was his method. He set the pace, slower than the strongest men preferred, faster than the weakest could manage comfortably. He adjusted sledge loads when a dog collapsed or a harness broke. He consulted with Kataktovik and the other Inuit hunters about the ice’s stability, the likelihood of leads ahead, the signs of changing weather that their experience could read and his could not. This was the authority that functioned: not the solitary decision-maker of the council, but the coordinator of diverse competencies, the translator of individual knowledge into collective action.

But the coordination had limits. The march separated the survivors into groups that could not always maintain contact: Mamen’s advance party, the main body, the stragglers who fell behind despite Bartlett’s pacing. The ice that had seemed a single surface from Shipwreck Camp revealed itself as a fragmented field, its movements independent, its capacity to bear weight variable across distances that could not be surveyed. The island remained invisible, its position a matter of calculation rather than confirmation.

On the second day, February 9, the first signs of serious difficulty appeared. A lead opened across the intended route, wide enough to require a detour of several miles to find a crossing. The temperature rose unexpectedly, making the snow surface sticky and exhausting for the dogs. One of the weaker travelers, a sailor whose name appears in McKinlay’s diary only as Morris, collapsed and had to be carried on a sledge, reducing that team’s capacity for cargo. Bartlett redistributed the load, but the redistribution took time and energy that the march could not spare.

That evening, in the snow shelter improvised for the night, Bartlett revised his calculations. The distance covered was less than half what he had projected. The consumption of food and fuel was greater, the men’s fatigue more profound. The island that had been a destination was now a hypothesis, its existence assumed but its accessibility increasingly doubtful.

He did not record these doubts in terms that others could read. His orders for February 10 were to continue, to follow Mamen’s trail markers, to maintain the discipline of march and rest that was the only protection against the ice’s chaos. The authority he projected required this continuity, this refusal to acknowledge that the decision to march might have been mistaken, that the camp they had abandoned might have offered better odds than the journey they had undertaken.

McKinlay recorded what Bartlett did not. The Captain was anxious, he wrote on February 9. He did not show it, but McKinlay had watched him calculate and recalculate, and he knew the look of a man whose numbers did not come out right. This was observation without accusation, the recognition of shared predicament rather than the assignment of blame. McKinlay had opposed the march. He was now committed to it. The opposition and the commitment coexisted in his record, unresolved, as they coexisted in the experience of the march itself.

The third day brought worse conditions. A storm from the northeast reduced visibility to the length of a man’s arm, made navigation by sun or stars impossible, turned the ice surface into a featureless white where pressure ridges and level pack were equally dangerous to traverse. Bartlett called a halt at midday, assembling the parties that had become separated in the morning’s march and establishing a temporary camp where sledges could be drawn into a protective circle and men could wait for the weather to clear.

This halt was not retreat. It was the recognition that forward motion in these conditions was not progress but suicide, that the ice they could not see could kill them more efficiently than any decision they could make. Bartlett used the enforced stop to redistribute supplies again, to examine the feet of men whose boots were failing, to consult with the Inuit about the storm’s likely duration. These were the activities of command in circumstances where command could not determine outcomes.

The storm lasted through February 11 and into the morning of February 12. When it cleared, the ice had changed. Leads that had been frozen were now open water. Pressure ridges that had been passable were now fractured, unstable, dangerous to cross. Mamen’s trail markers were invisible, buried or drifted over or simply absent in a landscape that had rearranged itself. The advance party that had been ahead of the main body was now out of contact, their position unknown, their fate as uncertain as Anderson’s party before them.

Bartlett made the decision that his authority required. They would continue, not toward the original objective but toward the last known position of Mamen’s party, establishing contact before proceeding further. This was a modification of plan rather than its abandonment, a tactical adjustment that preserved the strategic commitment to movement over stasis. But the modification acknowledged what the original plan had not: that the ice was not a surface to be crossed but a medium to be negotiated, that the island was not a destination but a possibility, that the march was not an escape from Shipwreck Camp’s dangers but an exchange of known for unknown risks.

The survivors resumed their travel on February 12, following a route that Bartlett determined from his last fix of position and his estimate of the drift during the storm. The ice remained treacherous, the progress slow, the contact with Mamen’s party not achieved by nightfall. They camped on a floe that creaked and shifted beneath them, the sound of moving pack a continuous reminder that their shelter was temporary in the most literal sense.

McKinlay’s entry for that night was brief. They were lost, he wrote. Not lost in position; the Captain knew where they were, or believed he did. Lost in purpose. They moved to find Mamen, who moved to find Anderson, who moved to find the island. Each step followed the last with logical necessity, and each step took them deeper into— The sentence broke off. The diary does not resume for three days.

Bartlett’s log continued without interruption. February 13: marched six miles, ice conditions poor, no contact with advance party. February 14: halted at pressure ridge, crossed with difficulty, one sledge damaged. February 15: encountered open lead, too wide to cross, forced to detour northward. The entries recorded what happened without interpretation, the voice of a man who maintained his function when the purpose of the function had become doubtful.

On February 16, contact was reestablished with Mamen’s party. They had found Anderson’s trail, followed it to a lead they could not cross, and been forced to return. Anderson’s party remained missing. The island remained unseen. The two groups now combined, their numbers reduced by separation and exhaustion, their supplies depleted by days of travel that had covered less distance than Bartlett’s original estimates had allowed for a single stage.

The council that convened that evening was different from the councils at Shipwreck Camp. Bartlett was no longer presenting a plan for approval or dissent. He was managing a situation that his plan had created, adapting to circumstances that his calculations had not anticipated. The decision he announced was to continue the march, not toward Wrangel Island directly but toward a point on the ice where the Inuit hunters believed, based on signs that the qalunaat could not verify, that the pack was more stable and the island more accessible.

This was leadership without certainty, command without confidence in its object. The men who followed Bartlett’s orders followed them not because they believed in the march’s success but because the alternative, fragmentation, individual survival in an environment that killed individuals, was worse. They had left Shipwreck Camp because staying meant certain death. They continued marching because stopping meant admitting that the leaving had been mistaken, and that admission was more than their present circumstances could bear.

Bartlett’s order given, the camp struck, and the first sledges moved out onto the vast, fractured ice.
