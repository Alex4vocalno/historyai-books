Chapter 28

The Last Word Belongs to the Ice

Seen from above, the Arctic coastline presented a silent, frozen expanse, offering no hint of its past. The low headlands lay white against a gray September sky in 1921, as a schooner approached Wrangel Island. On its deck, a man scanned the beaches through field glasses, having returned with a party ostensibly to continue geographical work abandoned years before. But the true purpose was known to all aboard: they sought answers for those who had walked into the snow and never returned.

The Herman dropped anchor in Rodgers Harbor. Stefansson went ashore with two Inuit hunters. They found the remains of the main camp at once—the stove, the provision boxes, the scattered debris of the long winter. But this was old ground. The survivors had described it. What they wanted lay farther out, on the northern shore where Bjarne Mamen had led his four-man party in February 1914, and on Herald Island to the east, where First Officer Alexander Anderson had taken his three companions. These were the blank spaces. The Herman carried a month’s supplies and the best sled dogs money could buy. Stefansson had arranged the expedition through the American Museum of Natural History, which meant official backing and a budget for thoroughness. He meant to be thorough.

They worked the northern coast first. The terrain was broken—pressure ridges, frozen streams, gravel beaches where the wind had scoured away any trace of passage. The Inuit hunters spread out, reading the ice and the snow for signs of old travel. On the third day, one of them stopped at the base of a low bluff and called out. Stefansson walked over. The hunter was holding a tin matchbox, the kind issued in the Karluk’s stores. It was empty. It lay on bare gravel, no snow cover, as if dropped deliberately or placed there. They searched the surrounding area for three hours. They found nothing else. No bones. No sled. No camp. The matchbox went into Stefansson’s collection bag, a single datum that refused to cohere into meaning.

They pushed on to Herald Island.

No wintering had been recorded on Herald Island before 1914. The island rose sheer from the sea, forty miles of rock and ice. The cliffs fell straight into deep water. There was no harbor, no beach. Fresh water came only from snow.

The Karluk survivors had watched Anderson’s party set out for it on January 29, 1914, four men in a small sledging outfit, carrying provisions for a month. They had never returned.

The Herman could not approach closely—the cliffs dropped into water too deep for anchoring—so Stefansson’s party landed on the ice foot and worked their way around the perimeter. They found a cairn on the southern shore, built of loose stones, with no message inside. They found the remains of a camp: a rusted tin, a fragment of canvas, a broken sled runner jammed between rocks. The runner was spruce, the right width for a Karluk sled. It might have been Anderson’s. It might have washed up from anywhere.

They searched for graves, for bones, for the distinctive blue of naval clothing against gray stone. The island offered nothing. The cliffs held their silence.

Stefansson returned to the Herman with his fragments. He would write them into his next book, The Friendly Arctic, as evidence of thoroughness, of care, of the expedition’s commitment to finding its lost men. What he could not write—what no one could write—was what the absence meant. The matchbox, the sled runner, the empty cairn: each object raised questions it could not answer. Had Mamen’s party reached their destination? Had they turned back? Had Anderson’s men even landed on Herald Island, or had the ice taken them before they arrived? The physical evidence pointed in no direction. It simply marked the limit of what could be known.

Stefansson was not the first to search. In 1915, a relief expedition organized by the Canadian government had visited Wrangel Island while Stefansson himself was still absent on his own four-year ice journey. That party had found the main camp, interviewed the surviving Inuit families who had assisted the castaways, and gathered what testimony they could. They had not found the bodies. In 1916, Captain Robert Bartlett had published his account, The Last Voyage of the Karluk, written with Ralph Hale.

The book described the march to Wrangel Island, the separation of the parties, the desperate final days. It made no direct criticism of Stefansson. Bartlett was thirty-nine when the Karluk sank. He would spend more than fifty years mapping Arctic waters, leading over forty expeditions, more than anyone before or since. He knew how to survive. He also knew how to calculate the cost of survival in a world that preferred its heroes uncomplicated.

The searches continued because the absence demanded it. Families wrote to the Department of Naval Service asking whether any new information had come. The department replied with form letters. In 1918, the American Museum of Natural History funded another expedition to Wrangel Island, this one scientific rather than rescue-oriented, but the leader was instructed to keep alert for signs of the lost men. He found nothing. The island had absorbed them. The ice had taken what it wanted and kept its own records.

The theories filled the vacuum. In Nome, Alaska, where the Karluk survivors had first returned to civilization, speculation ran through the saloons and the newspaper offices. Lead poisoning from the canned provisions: this was scientifically plausible, given the era’s imperfect canning methods, and it explained the erratic behavior some survivors reported in the final weeks.

Conflict among the stranded men: the Karluk party had included scientists, sailors, and Inuit families, a mixture of classes and purposes that had strained under pressure. Cannibalism: this was the darkest whisper, arising whenever Arctic parties vanished without trace, the last explanation that decency delayed and horror entertained. No evidence supported any of it. The bodies would have shown lead levels. The survivors’ testimony, taken at the time and repeated in memoirs, described quarrels but no violence. The cannibalism theory rested on nothing but the human tendency to imagine worst-case scenarios when the truth was unavailable.

The Canadian government had conducted its formal inquiry in 1914, immediately after the rescue. The commission heard testimony from Bartlett, from the survivors, from the expedition’s agents in Victoria. It produced a report that found fault with Bartlett for taking the Karluk into the ice and for allowing Mackay’s party to leave the main group. It did not examine Stefansson’s departure in September 1913, though the Karluk survivors had requested such an enquiry. The request was declined on the ground that no good could come of the enquiry. In his book, Stefansson took responsibility for the “bold” decision to take the Karluk into the ice rather than around it. He did not address the decision to leave the ship. The official record thus contained a shaped silence, a space where the central question might have been asked and was not.

The survivors aged into their fates. Ernest Chafe, the fireman who had kept the Karluk’s boilers alive until the end, published The Voyage of the Karluk, and its Tragic Ending in 1918. It was a working man’s account, plain and unadorned, full of practical detail about ice and ship handling. William McKinlay, the young magnetician who had survived the winter on Wrangel Island, wrote his own memoir but did not publish it; the manuscript sat in a drawer while he made his career as a schoolmaster in Scotland. He died in April 1946, aged seventy. His account of the Karluk disaster, published in 1916, made no direct criticism of Stefansson or anyone else. The reticence was characteristic. The survivors had shared an experience that separated them from everyone who had not been there. They did not need to explain this to each other. They could not explain it to anyone else.

Stefansson’s career continued. He returned from his four-year absence in 1918, reporting the discovery of three new islands in the Beaufort Sea. The National Geographical Society honored him. He wrote The Friendly Arctic, published in 1921, which argued that the Arctic was not the hostile wasteland of popular imagination but a habitable region for those who understood its methods. The book made him famous. It also made him enemies. The Karluk survivors who read it felt misrepresented. The families of the dead felt ignored. The scientific community divided: some admired his boldness, others questioned his ethics. The book sold well. It established a narrative in which the Karluk disaster was a regrettable episode in a larger story of exploration and discovery, rather than the defining event of Stefansson’s career.

The searches of 1921 found nothing more. The Herman returned to Nome in October, carrying Stefansson’s specimens and his empty matchbox. He would not return to the Arctic again. The ice had given him what it chose to give, and no amount of organization or funding could compel more. The eight missing men—Mamen, Mackay, Murray, Beuchat, Anderson, Barker, Taylor, and Brady—remained where they had fallen, their location unknown, their final hours unwitnessed. The official lists marked them lost. The unofficial lists, maintained by families and by the small community of Karluk survivors, marked them unknown. The distinction mattered. Lost implied an event, a moment of disaster that could be located and described. Unknown admitted the possibility that they had survived for days or weeks, that they had made choices, that they had suffered in ways no one could reconstruct.

The physical environment of the High Arctic does not preserve bodies as the Antarctic does. The freeze-thaw cycles, the scavenging animals, the movement of ice and snow across the ground—all conspire against the archaeological record. A man who dies on the ice in winter may be covered by spring drifts, exposed by summer melt, scattered by foxes and bears before the next freeze. The Inuit hunters who assisted Stefansson in 1921 knew this. They searched with professional thoroughness and personal fatalism. They had seen disappearances before. They understood that the land keeps its own counsel.

The absence became institutionalized. In 1924, an American vessel landed at Herald Island and found additional relics: more canvas, more tins, the remains of a sleeping bag. The items were collected and sent to the Smithsonian. They were catalogued and stored. They did not answer the question. The families of the dead received telegrams, then letters, then silence. The Department of Naval Service closed its file. The American Museum of Natural History moved on to other expeditions. The Karluk disaster became a case study in polar history courses, a cautionary tale with an incomplete moral.

Bartlett continued his work. He commanded the Effie M. Morrissey on scientific voyages to Greenland and the Canadian Arctic. He mapped. He advised. He received the Royal Geographical Society’s medal for “outstanding bravery” in 1918, and he accepted the censure of the admiralty commission in 1914 with the same professional composure. He did not speak publicly about Stefansson. He did not need to. His life was the argument: decades of competent, careful, successful Arctic travel that demonstrated what the Karluk expedition might have been if different choices had been made. When he died in 1946, the same year as McKinlay, the obituaries noted his Peary association, his wartime service, his later scientific work. The Karluk appeared as one item in a long list. The silence about its meaning was itself a kind of answer.

Stefansson died in 1962, aged eighty-two. He had never returned to the Arctic. In his later years he wrote about diet, about the “Eskimo” method of living on fat and protein, about the future of Arctic development. The Karluk appeared in his memoirs as a chapter he had survived rather than a disaster he had caused. He seemed, according to those who knew him, unable to express an appropriate sorrow over his lost men. This was the judgment of observers who had not shared his experience, who could not know what sorrow he felt or how he expressed it privately. But the observation persisted because it matched the visible record: a man who had walked away from a sinking ship, who had organized expeditions to search for the missing without finding them, who had built a successful career on the foundation of their disappearance.

The ice remained. Wrangel Island became a Soviet nature reserve, then a Russian one, inaccessible to most foreign visitors. Herald Island, never inhabited, never developed, kept its cliffs and its silence. The matchbox and the sled runner and the empty cairn sat in museum drawers, their labels yellowing, their significance fading into the general accumulation of Arctic relics. Occasionally a researcher would request to see them, would hold the matchbox in a gloved hand, would try to imagine the hand that had dropped it. The imagination failed. The objects were too small, too ordinary, too disconnected from narrative. They testified only to presence, not to fate.

The Karluk disaster thus achieved its final form: not as a resolved tragedy with clear causes and assigned responsibilities, but as a permanent uncertainty, a hole in the historical record that no amount of inquiry could fill. The official investigations had established facts without establishing meaning. The survivors’ accounts had described events without explaining them. The searches had found objects without finding bodies. The theories had proliferated without converging on truth. What remained was the silence itself, the white expanse of Wrangel Island and Herald Island and the drifting pack ice where the Karluk had been crushed, the silence that answered every question with the same indifferent negation.

This was the consequence that outlasted all the others. The institutional failures could be reformed: future expeditions would carry better supplies, maintain stricter chains of command, plan more carefully for contingencies. The personal legacies could be disputed: historians would argue about Stefansson’s character and Bartlett’s decisions for decades, each generation finding its own lessons in the record. But the eight missing men could not be recovered. Their deaths could not be dated or located or described. They had passed out of human knowledge into the category of the simply unknown, and the unknown does not yield to method.

The ice had the last word. It had had the first word as well, when it closed around the Karluk in August 1913 and refused to release her. Everything that followed—the departure of Stefansson, the drift westward, the sinking in January 1914, the march to Wrangel Island, the separations and the deaths and the rescue—was a human response to conditions the ice had established. The ice did not intend. It did not judge. It simply was, in its massive indifference to the purposes men laid upon it. The Karluk expedition had imagined that Arctic exploration was a matter of will and organization, of scientific planning and adequate funding. The ice taught otherwise. It taught that the environment sets the terms, that human agency operates within limits it does not choose, that the final accounting belongs to forces that do not read commissions of inquiry or honor medals for bravery.

The lesson was not learned. Or rather, it was learned and forgotten, learned again and forgotten again, in the history of polar exploration that continued through the twentieth century. Each generation discovered the ice’s indifference for itself, paid its own costs, left its own unmarked graves. The Karluk was not unique in this. It was only early, and well-documented, and therefore available for the judgment of history in ways that other disasters were not. The documentation, however, reached its limit at the edge of the known. Beyond that edge, where Mamen’s party and Anderson’s party had walked, the record stopped. The ice kept its own documentation, written in snow and bone and the slow movement of glaciers, in a language no human reader could decipher.

Stefansson’s 1921 expedition was the last serious attempt to find the bodies. After that, the search became archaeological, then historical, then merely commemorative. In the 1970s, a Soviet survey party reported finding human remains on Wrangel Island, but the identification was uncertain and the location was not precisely recorded. In the 2000s, a Canadian documentary team conducted ground-penetrating radar surveys of the northern coast, looking for anomalies that might indicate buried remains. They found nothing conclusive. The technology improved; the ice remained. The matchbox in the museum drawer, the sled runner in the Smithsonian, the empty cairn on Herald Island—these became the memorials, inadequate but persistent, standing in for graves that could not be found.

The survivors died one by one. Chafe in 1940, Bartlett and McKinlay in 1946, the Inuit hunters Kuraluk and Kataktovik in the 1950s, their names passing out of living memory. Stefansson was the last, in 1962, closing the generation that had known the Karluk and made its choices. After that, the disaster belonged entirely to history, to the documents and the relics and the scholarly arguments about responsibility and cause.

The ice, which had never belonged to anyone, continued its slow cycles of freeze and thaw, advance and retreat, covering and uncovering the ground where men had walked and died. It would continue when the documents had crumbled and the museums had closed, when the last theory had been proposed and refuted, when the name Karluk meant nothing to anyone living. The ice does not remember. It does not forget. It simply persists, in its indifferent authority, having rendered its verdict long ago and declined to explain it.
