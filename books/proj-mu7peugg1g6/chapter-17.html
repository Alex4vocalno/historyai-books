Chapter 17

The Walk Across the Chukchi Sea

Seen from above, the Arctic spread as a vast, broken expanse of ice and endless daylight, with Wrangel Island a desolate shore on which twenty-one men remained alive—though “alive” had become a provisional term. There the others watched, measuring their own symptoms against his and calculating their own remaining time against the mathematics of a rescue that had not come. On Wrangel Island, William McKinlay turned from the dying man to his diary. It seemed impossible, he wrote, that Captain Bartlett could reach Siberia. The date was March 20, 1914. Their captain had departed eleven days before with one companion, one sled, and the conviction that open water lay somewhere to the west. The island party had no way to know he had already covered twenty miles of broken ice. They had no way to know he had already nearly drowned.

The contrast between these two positions—stationary despair and desperate motion—would define the next forty days. For while the stranded men of the Karluk expedition entered their third month of waiting, consuming the last stores of pemmican and nursing the fiction that rescue might arrive from the east, Captain Robert Bartlett and the Inuit hunter Kataktovik were engaged in a journey that would test the absolute limits of human endurance and Arctic travel. The island was a trap. The ice was a highway. Both propositions were true, and both were lethal.

Bartlett had prepared for departure with the methodical care that characterized his leadership under the Karluk’s drift and after her sinking. He selected Kataktovik not merely for his hunting skill but for his complete mastery of ice travel—how to read pressure ridges for stability, how to time the crossing of leads before wind and current transformed them into impassable barriers, how to extract drinking water from old ice when fuel failed. The captain brought his sextant, his chronometer, and his logbooks. He brought a rifle and ammunition for sealing. He brought the certain knowledge that seventeen men on Wrangel Island would die unless he reached inhabited Siberia, found a vessel, and returned before the summer ice broke.

They left on March 9, 1914, from the camp on the island’s southwest shore. The ice stretched westward in a chaos of pressure ridges and frozen leads, the legacy of months of wind and current. Bartlett’s first day’s entry recorded what the island party could not imagine: progress measured not in hope but in miles, in the brutal arithmetic of sled-hauling across terrain that fought every inch. The temperature stood at thirty below. The sledge, loaded with sleeping bags, a small tent, cooking gear, and provisions for three weeks, weighed heavily on the harness. They made camp that night on a floe that groaned with tidal movement. Bartlett noted the position. He did not note what he felt.

The second day brought the first open lead. These fractures in the ice pack, created by wind stress, current shear, or the simple thermal expansion of a million tons of frozen seawater, were the governing terror of ice travel. A narrow lead might be crossed by jumping, by bridging with ice blocks, by waiting for the cold to knit the edges together. A wide lead meant detour, delay, or death. Bartlett and Kataktovik reached their first significant lead in the afternoon of March 10. The water was black and moving. The far side lay perhaps fifty yards distant, though distance on ice was deceptive, the flat light and absence of scale playing tricks on judgment. Kataktovik studied the edges, the current, the distribution of new ice. He made his decision. They would wait.

This was the pattern that would repeat across the Chukchi Sea. The two men were not masters of their route. They were petitioners before a landscape that granted passage according to rules they could observe but not command. Bartlett’s log entries from these days, preserved in his 1916 account and reported to The New York Times upon his eventual arrival in Alaska, record a journey of agonizing slowness punctuated by moments of genuine peril.

On March 12, they crossed a lead by jumping from floe to floe, the sledge passed between them like a stretcher bearing a wounded man. On March 14, they encountered a pressure ridge thirty feet high, the product of colliding ice masses, and spent four hours hauling the sledge up one side and controlling its descent down the other. Bartlett’s hands froze to the traces. Kataktovik’s boots filled with snow that melted and refroze against his skin. They did not stop.

The hunting began in earnest on March 15. Seal meat was their principal sustenance, their only source of fresh food and essential fat. Kataktovik’s method combined patience with an almost supernatural stillness. He would locate a breathing hole in the ice, identified by a slight doming of the surface, a faint discoloration, sometimes by sound alone, and wait. The seal surfaced to breathe every few minutes, every few hours, according to patterns the hunter learned to anticipate.

The rifle shot had to come at the precise moment of emergence, before the animal’s sensitive whiskers detected vibration. Bartlett, by his own account, was a novice at this work. He learned by watching, by the accumulation of failures that taught what success required. They killed their first seal on the third day of hunting. The meat was frozen before they could eat it, and they chewed it raw, their body heat softening each mouthful.

The polar bears came later. These animals, the apex predators of the Arctic pack, were drawn to the smell of blood, the presence of human waste, the simple fact of movement across their territory. Bartlett recorded encounters with bears on at least four occasions during the crossing. The confrontations were not romantic tests of courage. They were calculations of risk: whether the bear could be driven off by rifle fire, whether the ammunition expenditure was justified, whether the animal’s meat and hide outweighed the danger of approach.

Kataktovik’s knowledge proved decisive. He could read bear behavior, the postures of curiosity, of threat, of retreat, where Bartlett saw only a white mass against white ice. On March 18, they killed a bear that had followed them for six hours. The meat sustained them for a week. The hide became part of their bedding, an insulation layer against cold that their government-issue equipment could not match.

The navigational problem consumed Bartlett’s evenings. Each night in the tent, with Kataktovik sleeping the sleep of exhaustion, the captain worked his sextant observations, calculating latitude from the altitude of stars, estimating longitude from dead reckoning checked against magnetic variation. His chronometer had been set to Wrangel Island time before departure.

He needed to know where he was, not merely for the record but for decision: whether to continue west toward the Siberian coast, whether to angle north toward the probability of thicker ice, whether the drift of the pack was carrying him faster than his own effort could overcome. The sextant was a delicate instrument, its mirrors vulnerable to condensation and frost. The observations required removing mittens, exposing fingers that numbed within minutes.

Bartlett’s log shows positions marked with increasing uncertainty, the calculated latitude crossed out and recalculated, the longitude bracketed between estimates. He was navigating a moving surface toward a coast that might be ice-locked, that might be open water, that might not exist in the form his charts suggested.

By late March, they had covered approximately two hundred miles. The ice conditions had worsened. Spring was coming to the Arctic, and spring meant instability: the weakening of ice under solar radiation, the multiplication of leads as the pack lost its winter rigidity, the increased frequency of fog that blinded the traveler and wet the ice surface to a treacherous slick. Bartlett’s entries from this period show a man confronting the possibility that his gamble had failed. They were moving west, yes, but were they moving fast enough? The provisions carried from Wrangel Island would not last beyond mid-April. If the Siberian coast did not appear by then, they would face the choice of turning back to starvation or pressing forward to the same.

The lead that nearly killed them came on March 28. Bartlett’s account, published in The New York Times on June 1, 1914, describes the crossing of many leads, some of them dangerous. The private record was more specific.

They had been hauling across a flat pan when the ice beneath them began to move. Not the slow tidal shift they had learned to expect, but a sudden opening, a crack propagating faster than they could run. The sledge was on the far side before they recognized the danger. Bartlett jumped. The ice edge crumbled under his weight. He was in the water to his chest, his arms flung across the solid floe beyond, his legs kicking against the void. Kataktovik reached him in seconds, not minutes, lay flat on the ice, distributed his weight, seized Bartlett’s collar and hauled. The captain came out streaming, his clothing already freezing, his core temperature dropping toward the lethal threshold.

They made camp within the hour, not because they had found good ice but because Bartlett could not continue. Kataktovik erected the tent, started the stove, stripped the captain of his wet clothes and dressed him in the bear hide and sleeping bags.

They did not travel the next day. Bartlett’s log for March 29 records only: “Camp. Recovered from immersion.”

This was the partnership that the journey revealed. Bartlett was the navigator, the decision-maker, the bearer of responsibility for seventeen lives on Wrangel Island and for the success of this desperate mission. Kataktovik was the practitioner of survival, the reader of ice and animal, the man whose skills had been developed not through expedition training but through the accumulated generations of Inuit adaptation to this environment. They communicated in a mixture of English, Inuktitut, and gesture. They developed routines that required no speech: the preparation of camp, the division of watch, the approach to hunting. Bartlett’s later accounts emphasize this equality without sentimentality. He recognized what he owed, and he recorded it.

April brought the final phase. The ice was breaking up around them, the leads widening from obstacles to barriers, the pressure ridges softening from solid masses to unstable heaps. They were traveling now in twelve-hour marches, resting only when exhaustion made further progress dangerous. The seal hunting had become more difficult as the animals dispersed into open water, but Kataktovik’s success rate remained sufficient to sustain them. They were burning their own body fat, the metabolic reserve that Arctic travel consumed relentlessly. Bartlett’s face had thinned to bone and beard. His hands, repeatedly frozen and thawed, had lost their dexterity for fine work. He recorded none of this in the log. He recorded positions, distances, weather, ice conditions, the data that might assist a future traveler, that might justify the decisions he had made.

The land appeared on April 15, 1914. Bartlett’s sextant observation that morning had placed them at 69° 40’ N, 178° E, close enough to the Siberian coast that sighting was possible in clear conditions. The haze lifted in the afternoon. They saw mountains, the first fixed geographical feature in thirty-seven days of ice travel. The emotional content of this moment is absent from Bartlett’s record. He notes only: “Sighted land.” But the subsequent entries show an acceleration of pace, a willingness to risk travel in conditions that would have halted them earlier. They were no longer crossing ice toward an abstraction. They were approaching a coast with inhabitants, with settlements, with the possibility of communication to the world beyond.

The final miles were the worst. The ice pack was breaking against the shore, the seasonal disintegration that transformed the Chukchi Sea from highway to obstacle course. They traveled by night when the cold hardened the surface, by day when fog obscured the sun’s weakening of the floes. On April 18, they reached solid ground at Cape Yakan, on the eastern extremity of the Siberian coast. Bartlett’s log records the position with precision. It does not record what they felt, what they said, how they looked back at the ice that had nearly killed them and had finally released them. The entry for April 19 reads: “Marched to Emma Harbor.” They had completed the crossing. They had walked approximately seven hundred miles from Wrangel Island to the Siberian coast in forty days of continuous travel.

Emma Harbor was not salvation. It was a fishing settlement of a few dwellings, inhabited by Chukchi people whose language Bartlett did not speak, whose connection to the Russian administrative system was tenuous at best. The captain’s task now shifted from physical endurance to bureaucratic navigation. He needed to reach a settlement with telegraph facilities, to communicate with the American authorities in Alaska, to arrange a rescue voyage before the summer ice made Wrangel Island inaccessible until autumn. Kataktovik’s role diminished in this new phase. His skills had brought them across the ice. They could not penetrate the Russian colonial administration.

Bartlett’s subsequent movements, documented in his contemporary accounts and in the later records of the American and Canadian governments, trace a frantic path through the limited infrastructure of northeastern Siberia. From Emma Harbor, he traveled south to St. Lawrence Bay, then by native boat to East Cape, the easternmost point of the Asian continent. There he found a Russian trading post with seasonal contact with Alaska. The date was early May. The ice was breaking. The window for a rescue voyage was narrowing toward closure.

The telegram, when it finally went, was addressed to the United States Revenue Cutter Service in Nome, Alaska. Bartlett reported the position of the Wrangel Island party, their condition as of his departure, his estimate of their survival prospects. He requested immediate dispatch of a vessel capable of penetrating the ice. The response, when it came, embodied the friction between individual heroism and institutional capacity. No cutter was available. The ice conditions were prohibitive. A rescue attempt would be organized when possible.

This was the machinery Bartlett had gambled everything to reach. It moved at the speed of bureaucracy, constrained by budgets, seasons, competing priorities, the simple fact that seventeen men on an unknown island were not the only emergency requiring attention. In the same weeks that Bartlett was struggling across the Chukchi Sea, the SS Bellaventure steamed into St. John’s, Newfoundland, carrying the frozen bodies of sixty-nine sealers who had died in a March ice storm. The world contained many disasters. The Karluk survivors were one among them.

Bartlett did not wait for official response. From East Cape, he traveled by whatever means presented themselves: native boat, dog sled, Russian coastal trader, toward the Bering Strait and Alaska. He arrived in Nome on May 24, 1914, seventy-six days after leaving Wrangel Island. His appearance, emaciated, bearded, dressed in clothing that showed the marks of ice travel, created immediate sensation. The New York Times correspondent who interviewed him recorded the captain’s first demand: not medical attention, not rest, but the organization of immediate rescue. The story that appeared on June 1, 1914, under the headline “Bartlett’s Story of the Karluk,” was the first definitive word to reach the outside world about the expedition’s fate since the ship’s crushing in January.

The narrative Bartlett constructed in those interviews, and elaborated in his 1916 book The Last Voyage of the Karluk, established the terms in which the disaster would be understood. It was a story of leadership abandoned and leadership assumed: Stefansson’s departure, the ship’s drift, the sinking, the march to Wrangel Island, his own decision to attempt the ice crossing. The seventeen men remaining on the island were described in terms that emphasized their survival prospects without minimizing their danger. The two parties that had separated from the main group on the march, one to Herald Island, one attempting an early crossing of the ice, were noted as missing, their fates unknown. The tone was factual, restrained, appropriate to a captain reporting the loss of his ship and the peril of his men.

What Bartlett could not know, as he stood in Nome demanding action, was that the machinery he had set in motion was already too slow. The summer of 1914 would bring no rescue vessel to Wrangel Island. The ice that had released him from Siberia would close around Wrangel Island, imprisoning the survivors through months of hunger, disease, and psychological collapse. Three of them would die before the American fishing schooners King and Wing finally reached the island in September 1914, carrying the news that members of the Canadian Arctic Expedition, organized by Vilhjalmur Stefansson, had been marooned on Wrangel Island for nine months after their ship, Karluk, was crushed in the ice pack.

The eleven who survived would be the subjects of medical study, journalistic sensation, and eventually official inquiry. The eight who did not survive, including the four on Herald Island whose bodies would never be recovered, would become evidence in the argument about Arctic exploration, about leadership, about the cost of scientific ambition.

Bartlett and Kataktovik stand on the Siberian coast at Emma Harbor, their heroic journey complete, but the fate of the seventeen on Wrangel Island now hinges on the sluggish machinery of the world beyond the ice.
