Chapter 6

A Ship Without Its Expedition

The ice had taken the Karluk on August 13, 1913, and now the man who had sent her into it was gone. Vilhjalmur Stefansson departed the trapped vessel on September —with five men, two sledges, and the explanation that he would hunt for caribou. He left behind twenty-five souls, a ship whose hull groaned against the Beaufort Sea pack, and a silence where leadership had been. The Karluk drifted westward at the pace of a walking man, carried by currents that moved her away from Herschel Island and toward no charted harbor. Her captain, Robert Bartlett of Newfoundland, faced a transformation he had not sought and could not refuse: a vessel without her expedition, a crew without their mission, a community sealed in ice that would not release them before spring at the earliest.

Bartlett had spent more than fifty years in Arctic waters. He had accompanied Peary toward the Pole, had commanded the Roosevelt through ice that crushed lesser ships, had learned the particular grammar of survival that polar navigation required. At forty-two, he carried the compact build of a man who had hauled lines in freezing sleet, the gray-flecked beard of someone who had watched younger men die of scurvy, exposure, and the particular madness that Arctic darkness induced. He understood ships. He understood ice. What he faced now was neither precisely: it was the management of human beings who had watched their leader walk toward the horizon and disappear, who had been shown that authority could be provisional, that responsibility could be delegated by absence.

The morning after Stefansson’s departure, Bartlett called all hands on deck. Twenty-five men assembled in the thin Arctic light of late September, their breath forming clouds that vanished into air barely above freezing. Among them stood scientists who had signed on for discovery and publication, seamen who had expected a season’s work and a return to Nome, and two Inuit hunters whose skills had been engaged for a journey that no longer had any destination. Bartlett stood before them without notes, without the documents of authority Stefansson had carried from Ottawa and Washington, without any commission that made him more than the master of a single vessel.

He announced a new watch schedule. Three hours on, six hours off, rotating through daylight that dwindled by minutes each day. He appointed William McKinlay, a young Scottish scientist who had joined the expedition for its meteorological program, to maintain the observation log—work that would now serve navigation rather than research. He instructed the cook to reduce the daily meat ration by one-third, and to burn coal only during the hours when men were awake and working. The orders were practical, specific, and delivered without the explanatory courtesies that might have softened their impact. Bartlett had learned from Peary that authority in the Arctic was exercised or it dissolved, and he had until darkness to establish that this ship had a commander.

The crew received these instructions in silence. McKinlay noted in his diary that evening that the captain had spoken as though addressing a naval crew under wartime discipline, a comparison that revealed both the strangeness of the moment and the precedent Bartlett was trying to invoke. The Karluk was a converted whaler, purchased for ten thousand dollars, carrying a scientific staff that had never sworn oaths of obedience. Yet the ice had made her something else: a closed system where every calorie, every hour of fuel, every decision about movement or stillness would determine who survived to see open water.

The first days after Stefansson’s departure maintained a surface normalcy that fooled no one. Men went about their assigned tasks—sounding the ice, recording temperatures, repairing the damage that pressure did to the hull. The Karluk drifted westward, carried by a current that moved her away from Alaska and toward no land that offered harbor. Her position on September 23 placed her roughly 140 miles from Herschel Island, where the expedition’s other vessels waited without knowledge of her situation. By October 1, that distance had grown to nearly 200 miles. The ice that imprisoned her was not stationary; it was a river, and she was a leaf.

Bartlett spent hours each day on the ice, testing its thickness, tracing its leads, searching for the dark water that would allow escape. He found none. The pack had closed around the Karluk as a fist closes, and the brief openings that appeared were narrow, temporary, always in the wrong direction. On October 5, he ordered the engines fired for the first time since Stefansson left, hoping to force a passage through a lead that ran northeast. The ship moved fifty yards before the ice closed behind her, grinding against the rudder, forcing her to stop. The attempt cost half a day’s coal. Bartlett did not try again.

The psychological transformation of the crew proceeded in stages that McKinlay’s diary captured with growing precision. In the first week, there was anger—at Stefansson for leaving, at Bartlett for taking charge without consultation, at themselves for having signed on to an expedition that now seemed to have no expeditionary purpose. They were not explorers now, one of the scientists told McKinlay. They were prisoners, and their jailer was the ice. By the second week, the anger had curdled into something more dangerous: a suspended disbelief, a waiting for rescue that no one had promised and no one could deliver.

Men spoke of Stefansson’s return as though it were a scheduled event, like the arrival of a supply ship. They calculated how long a caribou hunt might take, how far a sledge party could travel, when the ice might break up enough for a rescue attempt. The calculations were exercises in hope, not reason.

Bartlett understood this waiting for what it was. He had seen it before, in the winter quarters of the Roosevelt, in the stranded camps of less fortunate voyages. Hope of rescue was a resource to be managed like food or fuel: necessary in measured doses, fatal in excess. He began to conduct his own calculations, private ones, written in the navigational notebook he kept locked in his cabin. The Karluk carried provisions for eighteen months at full ration.

At the reduced allowance he had imposed, they might stretch to two years. The coal would last through one winter if they were careful, perhaps into a second if they stripped the ship’s interior and burned the furniture, the paneling, the scientific equipment. The ice would not release them before spring 1914 at the earliest. More likely 1915. The possibility that they might drift two years without reaching open water was one he did not share with the crew.

The Inuit hunters, Kataktovik and another man named Kuraluk, observed this management with the patience of people who had never assumed that any season would end in survival. They had been engaged for their knowledge of ice and animal movement, skills that Stefansson had valued for the original expedition’s scientific goals. Now those skills became something else: the practical intelligence of men who knew how to find food where no food seemed to exist.

Kataktovik began to spend hours watching the pressure ridges, reading the ice as other men read weather. He reported to Bartlett that the seals were moving, that their breathing holes followed patterns, that a hunter who understood these patterns might supplement the ship’s stores indefinitely. Bartlett authorized the attempt. The first seal was taken on October 12, its meat distributed to the crew with a ceremony that acknowledged both the nutritional value and the symbolic weight of fresh food taken from the ice itself.

McKinlay recorded the event in his diary with a precision that marked his scientific training: weight of the seal, distribution of the meat, caloric value compared to canned rations. But he also recorded something else, a shift in the ship’s atmosphere that he struggled to name. The captain had made them hunters now, he wrote. They were no longer waiting for the expedition to resume. They were making their own. The observation was half-right. Bartlett had indeed transformed the Karluk’s purpose, but the transformation was not complete, and could not be complete, because the crew could not fully abandon the structure that had brought them here.

They were still employees of the Canadian Arctic Expedition, still bound by contracts signed in Victoria and Ottawa, still expecting that at some point the normal world would reassert its claims. The hunting was adaptation, not acceptance. The ship remained a ship, flying her flag, maintaining her routines, preserving the forms of naval discipline even as her situation grew more desperate.

October brought darkness and cold that tested these forms. The sun, which had circled the sky without setting in August, now appeared for shrinking intervals above the southern horizon, then disappeared entirely after October 24. The temperature dropped to twenty below, then thirty, then forty degrees Fahrenheit below zero. Ice formed on the interior walls of the ship, a quarter-inch skin that the men scraped away each morning and that returned each night. The coal ration, already reduced, became the focus of intense attention. Bartlett posted guards over the bunkers, not because he distrusted his crew but because he understood that in the mathematics of survival, temptation and necessity drew closer together with each degree the mercury fell.

The captain’s authority, established in that first morning assembly, faced its first serious test in the last week of October. A party of scientists, led by a man named Hadley, requested permission to establish a research station on the ice, arguing that the expedition’s scientific purposes could still be served even if the ship could not move. They proposed to drill ice cores, to measure temperature gradients, to continue the work that had justified the expedition’s government funding. Bartlett refused. The ice was moving, he pointed out. A station established now would be miles away within days, cut off from the ship’s shelter and supplies. The scientists pressed their case, suggesting that Bartlett was exceeding his authority, that Stefansson had intended scientific work to continue regardless of circumstances, that the captain’s proper role was to facilitate research rather than prevent it.

The confrontation ended without resolution, but with a clear demonstration of where power now lay. Bartlett did not invoke his formal title—he remained, technically, only the master of the Karluk, subordinate to the expedition leader who had left him. He invoked instead the practical reality of their situation: the ice, the cold, the distance from any possible assistance. He told Hadley that he was responsible for the men’s lives, not for their publications. The statement was recorded in McKinlay’s diary, and in the accounts of others who witnessed it, as a defining moment in the ship’s transformation. The Karluk had become a lifeboat, and her captain would make the decisions that lifeboats required.

This redefinition carried costs that would not be fully visible until later, but that began to accumulate immediately. The scientists, deprived of their purpose, fell into the lethargy that Arctic winters inflicted on idle minds. Some continued their observations—McKinlay maintained his meteorological log with a dedication that approached religious practice—but the work no longer connected to any larger project. It became routine and discipline: a way of passing hours that would otherwise expand into dread. The seamen adapted more easily to waiting; even they felt the strangeness of a voyage with no destination and no promise of discharge in a familiar port. The Inuit hunters alone seemed unchanged—their skills required now more than ever—their relationship to the ice too fundamental to be altered by administrative redefinition.

By late October, the Karluk had drifted so far west that Herschel Island was no longer the nearest land. Wrangel Island, 300 miles to the northwest, had become the theoretical refuge—too distant to reach, too small to sustain a party of twenty-five—but present in navigational calculations as one possibility among others worse. Bartlett studied charts each evening and traced how wind and current carried them toward this unknown shore. Whalers’ reports described Wrangel as barren and uninhabited; it lacked game enough to support castaways through winter. The drift might carry them past it entirely into the Chukchi Sea and toward Siberia beyond.

The crew’s awareness of this uncertainty grew through October—fed by Bartlett’s investigations and by calculations men made in idle hours against remembered landmarks or stars appearing in lengthening darkness or an intuitive sense sailors develop when enclosed waters offer no fixed point except drift itself.

On October 30, Bartlett conducted the first full inventory of supplies since Stefansson’s departure. The results confirmed what his daily management had suggested: they were consuming resources faster than the reduced rations should allow, losing coal to inefficient burning, watching the margin of safety narrow with each week. He instituted new conservation measures—shorter watches, colder quarters, the elimination of all non-essential activity—and announced them without explanation, as he had announced the first changes six weeks before. The crew accepted these measures with the resignation of men who had no alternative, but McKinlay noted a new quality in their response. They obeyed, he wrote, but they did not believe. They had seen too many calculations fail, too many promises dissolve in the ice.

This disbelief was the true legacy of Stefansson’s departure, more lasting than any specific hardship the ice imposed. The expedition leader had demonstrated that authority could be provisional, that responsibility could be delegated by absence, that the structures of command were themselves negotiable. Bartlett’s assertion of control, however necessary, however skillful, could not restore what had been broken. He commanded a ship whose crew had learned that leadership could walk away, that the institutions which had sent them here were too distant to enforce their claims, that survival depended finally on individual adaptation rather than collective purpose.

The last days of October brought the first storm of the Arctic winter, a three-day blow that piled snow against the Karluk’s hull and reduced visibility to the length of the ship. Men moved through the darkness by touch, by memory, by the sound of ice grinding against ice. Bartlett ordered all hatches secured, all fires damped to embers, all non-essential personnel below. The ship became a sealed container, her interior lit by oil lamps that consumed the fuel he was trying to conserve, her atmosphere thick with the breath of twenty-five men who could no longer see the ice that held them.

When the storm broke on November 2, the Karluk had moved forty miles west, carried by wind and current toward a position that placed her closer to Siberia than to Alaska. Bartlett took his sights through clearing air and confirmed what his dead reckoning suggested: they were now in waters that no expedition had planned to enter, drifting toward a coast that offered no harbor, no settlement, no possibility of rescue until the following summer at the earliest. He recorded the position in his locked notebook and said nothing to the crew. Some things a captain carried alone, until the carrying became impossible.

The ship was now a sealed world under Bartlett’s command, drifting into an unknown winter with survival as its only goal.
