Chapter 15

Bartlett and Kataktovik Strike Out Alone

The survivors at Shipwreck Camp faced only the knowledge that two attempts had failed, that more men had walked into the ice and not returned, and that the remaining options narrowed to a single, desperate calculation. They understood that the Arctic did not always return those it took. The ice had already claimed four men on Herald Island and four others, including Alistair Mackay who had been part of Shackleton’s Nimrod Expedition, who had tried reaching Wrangel Island on their own and perished.

Captain Robert Bartlett studied the faces around him. Sandy Anderson, the first officer now charged with whatever came next. The scientists and sailors who had already buried their dead. The Inuit hunters who understood better than any of them what the ice permitted and what it refused. Bartlett had spent seven weeks since the Karluk’s sinking trying to move these men to safety through collective effort. The ice had answered each attempt with separation, loss, and death. Now he would try something else.

Bartlett’s instructions to Anderson ran to the practical and the absolute. The party should remain at Shipwreck Camp if rescue seemed possible within a reasonable frame. If the ice conditions improved, they should attempt the march to Wrangel Island, where at least there was land beneath their feet and the chance of game. Anderson would command. The stores would be rationed. No one was to attempt another crossing of the broken pack without certainty of a route. Bartlett spoke these words on March 18, 1914, standing on the frozen camp that had become his headquarters, his courtroom, and his burden. He was fifty-one years old. He had led more expeditions into the Arctic than any living man. He knew the mathematics of survival as well as he knew the tides of the Labrador coast.

Then he turned to his own preparations. Where the instructions to Anderson filled pages and contingency, what Bartlett selected for himself and his companion reduced to radical simplicity: a sledge, sleeping bags, a tent, ammunition for hunting, a small store of pemmican and biscuits, his navigation instruments, and the clothing on his back. The contrast was deliberate and severe. To leave the others with authority was to weigh them down with possibility; to leave himself with so little was to make his own body the instrument of their deliverance. He would travel with one man only: Kataktovik, an Inuk hunter from the Mackenzie Delta who had joined the expedition as a hunter and dog driver, who had survived the Karluk’s sinking, who had watched the failed marches with the patience of someone who understood that the ice answered to different masters than those Bartlett had been addressing.

The choice of Kataktovik was itself a calculation. Bartlett needed a navigator of the ice, someone who could read the pressure ridges and the leads, who could find game where a white man would see only desolation, who could judge when to travel and when to wait. The Inuk hunter had demonstrated these capacities through the long winter. Now they would be tested across two hundred miles of the Chukchi Sea, from the drifting pack where the Karluk had died to the Siberian coast, where Bartlett might find the native settlements that lined the shore and, through them, reach the world that had forgotten them.

They departed on March 19, 1914, moving west-northwest across the pack. Bartlett kept a log in the terse style he had learned from Peary, from his own years of command: distances, bearings, ice conditions, the state of their provisions. The entries reveal a journey stripped to its essentials. On the first day they made five miles, the sledge heavy on soft snow, the temperature at twenty below. On the second day they covered eight miles, the ice firmer, the sky clearing enough for Bartlett to take a sight. The third day brought open water, a lead they could not cross, and they camped early to wait for the pack to shift.

The rhythm established itself quickly. Travel when the ice permitted. Stop when it refused. Hunt when game appeared, though game was scarce. Ration the food against the certainty of delay. Bartlett’s entries record Kataktovik’s contributions without commentary, as facts among facts: Kataktovik shot a seal. Kataktovik indicated that ice would open to the north. Kataktovik said they must wait. The captain who had commanded ships and men was now the student of his companion’s judgment, and the log shows him accepting this reversal without resentment. The ice did not care about rank.

Meanwhile, at Shipwreck Camp, the men Bartlett had left behind entered a different phase of their ordeal. Sandy Anderson, the first officer, found himself commanding a party whose morale had been eroded by successive failures, whose physical condition deteriorated with each week of short rations, whose faith in rescue depended now on a single man’s ability to cross an ocean of ice. The camp routines continued: snow walls maintained, snow holes dug for storage, the daily lottery of hunting that seldom produced more than a few birds. But the dynamic had shifted. Where Bartlett’s presence had imposed structure, his absence left a vacuum that authority alone could not fill.

William McKinlay, the young magnetician who had survived the sinking and the failed marches, recorded the atmosphere in his diary with the precision of a man trained to measure invisible forces. The waiting, he wrote, was its own form of labor. Each day without news from Bartlett confirmed both the desperate nature of the captain’s gamble and the slender thread by which their lives now hung. The ice around Shipwreck Camp continued its slow drift westward, carrying them toward Wrangel Island whether they marched or not, but the movement was too slow for rescue and too fast for comfort. They were passengers on a raft they could not steer, awaiting a verdict from a court they could not see.

The parallel tracks of the chapter (Bartlett and Kataktovik moving with purpose across the ice, the Shipwreck Camp party stagnating in place) mirror each other with cruel exactness. Where the two men on the march suffered the immediate hardships of cold, wind, and uncertain ice, they also possessed the relief of action, the sense that each mile covered brought them closer to a resolution. The larger party, safer in numbers and sheltered by the accumulated stores of their dead ship, suffered the more subtle erosion of hope deferred. Both conditions were unbearable. Only one offered the possibility of change.

Bartlett’s log entries for late March record the grinding difficulty of the crossing. The Chukchi Sea ice in that season was not the continuous plain that mapmakers imagined but a chaos of pressure ridges, open leads, and drifting floes that shifted with every wind. On March 22, they made only three miles, forced to detour around a ridge system that rose twenty feet above the surrounding pack. On March 24, they encountered open water too wide to cross, camped for two days on a floe that drifted while they slept, and found themselves on March 26 farther from their goal than they had been when they stopped. Kataktovik studied the ice with the patience of someone reading a language in which complete sentences were rare. When he judged that a lead had frozen sufficiently to bear their weight, they crossed. When he shook his head, they waited.

The food situation pressed upon them. Bartlett had calculated for twenty days of travel; by March 28, they had consumed half their pemmican and seen no seals for a week. Kataktovik set a seal hook at a breathing hole, checked it through the long Arctic night, and on March 29 pulled out a young bearded seal that provided meat for three days and blubber for their lamp. This was the knowledge Bartlett had purchased with his radical simplification: the skills of an individual hunter, yes, but more than that, a whole way of reading the ice, accumulated across generations and now pressed into service for men who had come from a world of charts and engines.

The same days brought no relief to Shipwreck Camp. Anderson maintained discipline as he could, organizing hunting parties, distributing rations, keeping the snow walls repaired against the wind that came steadily from the northeast. But the psychological cost of waiting without knowledge proved heavier than the physical labor of survival. Men who had borne the cold and the hunger while Bartlett commanded now found themselves arguing over trivialities, withdrawing into silence, staring at the ice horizon as if willing it to produce their deliverance. McKinlay noted that the Inuit hunters in the camp (Kuraluk and his family, who had also survived the sinking) seemed to accept the waiting with a different quality of patience, neither hopeful nor despairing but simply present to each day as it came. This was the same quality Kataktovik brought to the march, and it was not available for transfer.

By early April, Bartlett and Kataktovik had reached the region of the Chukchi coast where the ice pressed against the land. The final miles proved the most dangerous. The pack ice, compressed against the shore by wind and current, formed a jumble of broken floes and grinding bergs that made sledge travel nearly impossible. On April 3, they abandoned the sledge and carried what they could on their backs, picking a route through the chaos by instinct and Kataktovik’s reading of the ice scars. The temperature had risen to ten below, warm enough to make the snow sticky and the traveling worse. Bartlett’s log records no complaint, only the facts: they made two miles, camped on a small floe, found the ice very bad.

They came ashore on April 4, 1914, at a point on the Siberian coast north of Cape Dezhnev. Bartlett’s navigation had brought them within fifteen miles of his intended landfall, a precision that testified to his instruments and his skill but also to the luck that any Arctic traveler requires. The land they found was empty: no settlement visible, no smoke on the horizon, only the rolling tundra and the distant mountains of the Chukotka Peninsula. They had crossed the ice. They had reached Asia. They were still alone.

The next phase of their journey required a different set of skills. Bartlett needed to find the native villages that dotted this coast, to communicate his need for help, to reach the Russian administrative posts that might have telegraph connections to the outside world. Kataktovik’s value shifted accordingly. Where he had been navigator and hunter on the ice, he became translator and cultural mediator on land. The Inupiaq dialect he spoke was related to the Chukchi languages of the region; his presence signaled to the coastal people that these strange arrivals were not merely enemies or spirits but human beings with recognizable needs.

They found their first contact on April 6, a small encampment of Chukchi reindeer herders who provided food and shelter and, more valuably, information about the Russian settlements to the south. The herders had heard nothing of white men lost in the ice, knew nothing of the Karluk or the Canadian Arctic Expedition. The world had not been searching. Bartlett learned from them that the nearest telegraph station was at Uelen, a coastal village some eighty miles south, and that the journey could be made by following the shoreline if the sea ice remained stable.

The days that followed brought a rhythm of travel and encounter that contrasted sharply with the isolation of the ice crossing. Bartlett and Kataktovik moved from camp to camp, receiving hospitality according to the customs of the region, offering in return ammunition and the news of their passage. Bartlett’s log records these transactions with the same factual brevity as his ice travel, but the entries reveal a man adapting to circumstances he had not anticipated. He had prepared for the ice. He had not prepared for the political and cultural terrain of revolutionary Russia, where the collapse of imperial authority had disrupted the coastal trade routes and left the native communities uncertain of their relationship to any central power.

At Uelen, they found the telegraph station unmanned, the wire down, the Russian official who should have been there fled or dead in the chaos of the previous winter. The Bolshevik revolution had reached even this remote coast, though its manifestations were less dramatic than in Petrograd: a silent office, a broken connection, the absence of the state that had claimed sovereignty over these waters. Bartlett, who had crossed the ice to find civilization, discovered instead a civilization in fragments.

From Uelen, they pressed south along the coast, seeking the American trading stations that had operated in the region before the war. On April 15, they reached East Cape, the easternmost point of Asia, and turned west into the Bering Strait. The ice here was less stable than in the Chukchi Sea, broken by the currents that flowed between the continents, and their progress slowed to a crawl. Kataktovik’s skills remained essential; without his judgment of ice conditions, they would have been stranded repeatedly on floes that drifted backward as fast as they could walk forward.

Meanwhile, at Shipwreck Camp, the waiting had entered its terminal phase. By mid-April, Anderson recognized that the ice drift would carry them past Wrangel Island if they did not attempt the march soon. The party had been reduced by death and separation; those who remained were weaker than they had been in March, less capable of the exertion that any crossing would require. Anderson made the decision that Bartlett had authorized: they would attempt the march to Wrangel Island, using what remained of their strength before the ice carried them beyond hope.

McKinlay’s diary records the preparations with the clarity of a man who knew he might not survive to revise his account. The sledge was loaded with the essential stores; the weakest members were given the lightest burdens; the Inuit hunters were asked to scout ahead for routes through the pressure ridges. They departed Shipwreck Camp on April 21, 1914, exactly one month after Bartlett and Kataktovik had left them, carrying with them the knowledge that their captain had not returned and might never return.

The parallel lines of the narrative now diverged with finality. Bartlett and Kataktovik, moving south through the Bering Strait, were approaching the possibility of rescue; the main party, abandoning the relative security of their camp, were entering the same ice maze that had swallowed the earlier expeditions. The captain who had delegated his authority had also, necessarily, delegated the consequences of his absence. What he could control (his own body, his own route, his own determination) he had committed to the westward crossing. What he could not control (the condition of the ice, the decisions of those he left behind, the simple luck of survival) he had left to play out without him.

Bartlett found his connection to the outside world on April 23, at the American trading station at Emma Harbor, on the Siberian coast opposite St. Lawrence Island. The trader, a man named Lomen who had operated in the region for years, possessed a radio capable of reaching Nome, Alaska. Bartlett composed his message with the care of a man who knew that every word would be weighed and that the truth, in this case, was complicated enough to resist summary. The Karluk had sunk. Eleven survivors remained on Wrangel Island, or possibly on the ice approaching it. He was proceeding to Alaska to organize rescue. He requested that the message be relayed to the Canadian government and to any vessels that might be in Bering Sea waters.

The message reached Nome on April 24. From there, it traveled by telegraph to Ottawa, to Washington, to the newspapers that had already begun to wonder what had become of Stefansson’s grand expedition. The world, which had not been searching, now knew that there were men to search for. The machinery of rescue, slow and inadequate as it would prove, began to turn.

But machinery takes time, and time was what the men on Wrangel Island did not have. Anderson’s party reached the island on April 28, after a march that cost them what remained of their strength. They found no game, no shelter beyond what they could construct from driftwood and snow, no sign that Bartlett had succeeded in his crossing. The eleven survivors (reduced by one more death during the march) settled into a camp on the island’s north shore, rationing their food against a rescue that might come in weeks or might never come at all.

Bartlett and Kataktovik continued their journey, crossing from Siberia to Alaska by the route that native traders had used for centuries, reaching Nome on May 2, 1914. The town, swollen with gold rush survivors and the transient population of the Arctic coast, received them as curiosities and then, as the story spread, as heroes. Bartlett found himself suddenly visible, interviewed by reporters, asked to explain decisions that had been made in darkness and silence, expected to account for losses that had occurred beyond any witness he could summon.

He did what he could. He contacted the Coast Guard, the naval authorities, the trading companies that maintained vessels in Bering Sea waters. He learned that no ship could reach Wrangel Island before July, that the ice conditions would prevent any earlier approach, that the men he had left behind must survive through another two months of hunger and uncertainty. He arranged for the schooner King and Wing, a small vessel that had operated in the region, to stand ready when the ice permitted. He wrote to the families of the dead, to the expedition’s sponsors, to the government officials who would soon enough ask why so many had died.

Kataktovik, his part in the rescue accomplished, prepared to return to his own world. Bartlett arranged payment for his services, offered references that would be meaningless in the Inuk hunter’s future life, tried to express a gratitude that transcended the transactional. The log that had recorded their daily progress across the ice, that had noted seal sightings and ice conditions and miles covered, offered no vocabulary for this separation. They had been, for forty-seven days, the only two human beings in a landscape that refused human presence. Now they would return to societies that measured survival differently.

The survivors of the Canadian Arctic Expedition, organized by Vilhjalmur Stefansson, were marooned on Wrangel Island for nine months after their ship, Karluk, was crushed in the ice pack. They were rescued by the American motorized fishing schooner King & Winge in September 1914, after Bartlett’s crossing and the long wait that followed. This was the outcome that Bartlett had purchased with his gamble, though when he stood on the Nome waterfront on May 2, arranging ships that could not sail and writing letters that would arrive too late, he knew only that he had reached the world and that the world would move slowly.

The men he had left behind on the ice would spend the summer in a camp they had not chosen, on an island that offered nothing, waiting for a rescue that Bartlett had set in motion but could not accelerate. The captain who had become a messenger now became a petitioner, moving between offices and telegraph stations, repeating his story to officials who had other concerns, watching the calendar advance toward July when the ice might release its hold on the Bering Sea. He had crossed the Chukchi ice in forty-seven days. The men on Wrangel Island would wait one hundred and forty-seven.
