Chapter 10

The Ship Gives Up the Ghost

The ice spoke again at first light on 10 January 1914, and this time its voice carried the timbre of finality. William McKinlay felt it through his bunk boards, a vibration that began as a low harmonic in the ship’s iron and rose to a shudder that rattled his instruments on their shelves. The Karluk had been groaning for months, protesting her imprisonment in the pack that carried her westward through the Beaufort Sea. But the sound that woke him was different, the noise of something giving way.

Captain Robert Bartlett reached the deck before the tremor subsided. He had spent fifty years in Arctic waters, had watched ships die in ice before, had learned to read the pack’s moods the way other men read weather vanes. The pressure that morning was active, purposeful, the ice searching for weakness. It was not the slow, grinding squeeze that had held them for four months. Bartlett gave orders to lighten the ship. All hands cleared snow from the decks, tons of accumulated weight that might make the difference between a hull that held and one that split. He ordered the men to have their gear ready. The words hung in the frozen air without explanation. They all understood.

The Karluk had never been built for this. Acquired by expedition leader Vilhjalmur Stefansson for ten thousand dollars, sold at cost to the Canadian government, she was a whaler constructed for chasing bowheads in open water, not for wintering in polar pack. Her frames were sturdy but shaped for a different war. Now she drifted in ice beyond any chart, 235 miles east of Point Barrow when last calculated, carried westward by currents no hydrographer had measured. Stefansson had left the ship with a small party on 19 September, claiming he intended to hunt for caribou. The ice, carrying Karluk with it, began to drift more rapidly westward, so that Stefansson and his party were unable to return. The drift had continued without him.

By mid-morning the timbers were speaking continuously. The ship’s carpenter, John Brady, moved through the hold with a lantern, searching for the wound. He found it in the engine room: a seam opened, water rising around the bilge pumps. The crew set to work with the desperate efficiency of men who had rehearsed this emergency in their minds for weeks. They rigged the hand pumps. They hauled coal from the bunkers to the deck—fuel that might keep them warm when the ship could not. They stacked provisions where they could be grabbed quickly: pemmican, condensed milk, the medical supplies that Dr. Alistair Mackay had inventoried so carefully.

Bartlett worked the bridge and the engine room by turns, a compact man in his early forties with the shoulders of a seaman and the eyes of someone who had calculated too many risks. His log entries that morning were spare, almost clinical, but the mathematics told their own story. The pumps were gaining on the leak, then holding even, then losing. The ice pressure came in pulses, each one finding a new weakness. By afternoon the water in the hold had risen to the firemen’s floor plates. The coal bunkers were flooding. The ship’s life—the heat, the cooked food, the illusion of shelter—was draining away with each bucket the men threw overboard.

McKinlay recorded the sounds in his diary: not the crash of catastrophe but the steady, intimate noises of disintegration. The grinding of ice against the starboard bow. The sharp crack of a timber parting. The rush of water into spaces that had been dry for decades. The Karluk was dying by inches, and the men were trapped inside her body, listening to her organs fail.

Bartlett made his first preparation for abandonment that afternoon. He ordered the small boats readied—the whaleboat and the two cutters that would carry them to whatever land the ice might offer. The men worked with frozen fingers, stripping covers, checking oars, loading emergency rations. Each bundle represented a calculation: weight versus survival, the known need against the unknown journey. They had drifted past Point Barrow’s longitude. Herald Island lay somewhere to the west, Wrangel Island farther still. No rescue could reach them. The ice was their only road, and it was closing behind them.

The night of 10 January brought no relief. The temperature dropped to thirty below. The pressure ridges built around the ship, frozen ramparts that would become their prison walls. Bartlett kept watches in the engine room, rotating men through twenty-minute shifts—the limit of human endurance in the flooded, freezing space. The pumps clanked on, powered by the last of the ship’s functioning boiler. The water rose.

By morning the situation had clarified. The Karluk was holed in multiple places. The ice had found her vulnerabilities—the old seams, the repaired frames, the places where twenty years of Bering Sea sealing had stressed her beyond design. Bartlett called his officers together: Mackay, the surgeon; Munro, the chief engineer; Hadley, the first officer; McKinlay, who had kept the most careful records. The decision was not whether to abandon ship but when, and what they could salvage before the end.

The second day brought a strange rhythm. The ice would press, the ship would shudder, the men would rush to a new leak. Then the pressure would ease, and they would catch their breath, and the pumps would gain a few inches. This was postponement, not salvation, and they knew it. Bartlett used the intervals to organize the evacuation. Tents, stoves, sleeping bags, rifles, ammunition, the chronometers and sextants that might let them navigate across the ice. The scientific instruments that McKinlay had tended so carefully, his magnetometer, his meteorological equipment, were judged too heavy, too fragile, too useless for the journey ahead. He watched them being left in the ship, his year of measurements abandoned to the flood.

The crew worked in three parallel streams, each with its own urgency. On deck, sailors under Hadley’s direction loaded the boats and sledges, weighting each bundle, lashing each tarpaulin against the wind that came off the ice. Below, firemen and engineers under Munro fought the water, their efforts growing more desperate as the leaks multiplied. And in the cabins and laboratories, the scientists packed what they could carry, discarding the accumulations of four months’ drift—the specimens, the notes, the instruments that had justified the expedition’s existence.

Bartlett moved among them, his decisions shaping what would survive. The coal went first, piled on deck in black pyramids. Then the pemmican, the expedition’s engineered food, calculated to sustain life at the cost of pleasure. Then the personal kits, limited to what a man could carry on his back across uncertain ice. The captain’s own logbooks, his charts, his navigational calculations—these he entrusted to no one, carrying them himself between ship and ice as the hours passed.

By the afternoon of 11 January, the Karluk had settled perceptibly. The water in the hold had reached the lower deck, and the ship’s list to starboard had steepened. The ice continued its work, pressing and releasing, pressing and releasing, each cycle finding new purchase on the weakening hull. Bartlett ordered the final preparations. The men would sleep on the ice that night, in tents pitched on the floe that held them. The ship was no longer shelter. She was liability, a mass of wood and iron that would drag them down when she went.

The abandonment was orderly, which was Bartlett’s doing. He had seen panic kill men in the Arctic, had watched discipline dissolve when the ice took a ship. The Karluk’s crew moved to their stations, loaded their assigned burdens, stepped onto the ice with the mechanical precision of men who had rehearsed this in nightmare. McKinlay carried his diary, his navigation tables, the small bag of personal letters that he had never expected to mail. Around him, others carried similar weights—the totems of identity that would become more precious as their world contracted.

They made camp fifty yards from the ship, close enough to hear her death, far enough to be safe when she foundered. The tents went up in the fading light, canvas walls against a wind that carried the temperature to forty below. The stoves were lit, small victories of flame in an ocean of cold. And they waited.

The night of 11 January was the longest McKinlay had known. The sounds carried across the ice with terrible clarity. The deep groan of a frame surrendering. The sharp report of a deck plate buckling. The rush of water into spaces that had held air for hours, then minutes, then not at all. The men in the tents did not sleep. They lay in their sleeping bags, listening to the ship die, measuring their own survival against each new failure of wood and iron.

Bartlett maintained his log through the night, writing by the light of a candle that guttered in the cold. The entries tracked the disintegration: 10 PM, ship settling fast; midnight, ice pressure increasing; 2 AM, hull believed broken amidships. He wrote without emotion, the discipline of a lifetime, but the facts accumulated their own weight. The Karluk, which had carried them from Nome in July, which had been their world for four months of drift, was becoming debris.

Dawn on 12 January revealed the extent of the destruction. The ship had settled until her rails were level with the ice, her masts canted at angles that spoke of broken keel and twisted frames. The pressure ridges had climbed her sides, frozen berms that would hold her when she sank. Bartlett made a final circuit, entering compartments that were knee-deep in water, retrieving the last of the chronometers, the ship’s papers, the small store of spirits that would serve as medicine and, perhaps, as comfort in the days ahead.

The crew watched from the ice. They were twenty-five men now, reduced from the thirty-one who had sailed from Nome. Stefansson’s departure had taken five; the drift had claimed one to illness. They stood in their heavy clothing, breath frosting in the still air, witnesses to the end of their last fixed point. The Karluk had been inadequate, ill-chosen, poorly prepared for the mission assigned her. She had also been warmth, structure, the pretense of connection to the world they had left. Now that pretense was drowning.

The final collapse came in the afternoon. The ice, as if sensing weakness, pressed with renewed force. The men heard the keel break—a sound like a great tree falling, transmitted through the floe to their feet. The ship settled rapidly then, her stern submerging first, her bow rising slightly as the weight redistributed. Water poured through the hatches, the companionways, the gaps where deckhouses had torn free. The masts swayed, held by rigging that was now anchor rather than support.

Bartlett gave no order to save her. There was nothing to save. He stood on the ice with his logbook, recording the time, the position by dead reckoning, the final depth to which she sank. The Karluk’s deck disappeared at 4:15 PM, 12 January 1914. Her masts remained for hours, markers in the ice that would guide no one, signposts to a depth of eighty fathoms where the hull finally rested.

The men watched until dark. There was nothing else to do. The ship had been their employment, their shelter, their definition. Now they were twenty-five men on an ice floe in the Chukchi Sea, equipped with what they had carried, dependent on what they could improvise. The scientific expedition that Stefansson had envisioned—geographic discovery, anthropological study, the mapping of unknown waters—had reduced to this: bodies in canvas tents, waiting for the ice to release them or to kill them.

Bartlett called his officers together in the largest tent, the command space that had become their wardroom. The stove burned low, conserving fuel. The maps showed their position by inference: somewhere west of Point Barrow, somewhere north of the Siberian coast, somewhere in the drift current that might carry them to Wrangel Island or might not. The chronometers gave them time. The sextants would give them latitude, if they could find a horizon in the perpetual twilight. The rest was calculation, guesswork, and will.

He laid out the options without rhetoric. They could wait on the floe, hoping for a shift in the ice that would bring them within reach of land. They could march westward, hauling their supplies across the pressure ridges toward Wrangel Island, whose position was uncertain and whose conditions were unknown. Or they could divide, some waiting, some marching, betting survival against the odds in both directions. The decisions would be his, but the knowledge must be shared. These men were not crew now. They were partners in survival, bound by necessity rather than wage.

Mackay, the surgeon, spoke of medical realities. The cold injuries already present—frostbitten fingers, faces touched by the beginnings of gangrene—would worsen with travel. The food supply, calculated for shipboard consumption, would not stretch to a march of unknown duration. The pemmican, designed for exactly this contingency, would sustain life but not health. Scurvy was inevitable without fresh meat. The hunting that Stefansson had promised, that had justified his departure, had not materialized for those left behind.

Munro, the engineer, spoke of equipment. The sledges were adequate for ice travel, but the dogs were few and weakening on frozen fish. The boats could be hauled across level floes but would need to be launched at open leads, a maneuver that required luck and timing. The stoves were efficient but heavy, the tents warm but vulnerable to wind. Every choice involved trade-offs between mobility and shelter, speed and safety, the present hour and the uncertain future.

McKinlay said little, recording the discussion in his diary with the same precision he had brought to his magnetic observations. He noted the positions taken, the questions raised, the decisions deferred. The log he would keep in the days ahead would become, he understood, the only record of what happened to them. If they survived, it would be evidence. If they did not, it would be their testament.

The night of 12 January passed in the tents, the men crowded together for warmth, the stoves banked against the morning’s need. The Karluk’s masts were still visible when they woke, black lines against the gray sky, but the wind was rising and the snow beginning to drift. By noon the masts had disappeared, swallowed by the ice that had built around them, the pack closing over the wound as if the ship had never been.

Bartlett spent the day organizing the march. The supplies were redistributed, each man’s load calculated to thirty-five pounds—enough to slow him on rough ice, not enough to guarantee survival. The sledges were loaded with the communal equipment: tents, stoves, spare clothing, the medical kit, the ammunition for the rifles that might feed them if they found game. The boats were dragged to the edge of the floe, ready for the leads that might open or might not.

The crew worked with the strange energy of men who had passed through crisis into a new phase. The ship’s loss, which had loomed as catastrophe, had become fact. The facts required response, and the response required labor. They had not chosen this situation, but they could choose their conduct within it. Bartlett’s presence enforced that choice. He had not saved the Karluk; no captain could have saved her from the ice that found her weakness. But he had preserved order, and order preserved possibility.

On 13 January, the third day of their new existence, they began to move. The ice was rough, pressure ridges rising ten and fifteen feet where floes had collided and frozen together. The sledges caught and had to be lifted, the boats portaged around obstacles that could not be crossed. The temperature held at thirty below, cold enough to freeze exposed flesh in minutes, warm enough—by Arctic standards—to permit travel. They made three miles by Bartlett’s calculation, a distance that would have been contemptible on open water, significant across the pack.

They camped on a floe that seemed stable, the tents pitched in the lee of a ridge that broke the wind. The stoves burned the coal they had salvaged, precious fuel that would not be replaced. The pemmican was issued, a measured ration that left hunger unsatisfied but life sustained. The men slept in their clothes, ready for the emergency that might come with any shift in the ice.

Bartlett lay awake, as he had lain awake since the shudder of 10 January, calculating positions and possibilities. The drift continued westward; he could feel it in the movement of the floe beneath him, a slow rotation that matched the current’s flow. Wrangel Island lay somewhere ahead, a hundred miles or two hundred, its exact location uncertain in the charts. Herald Island was closer but smaller, a rock that might offer no shelter. The Siberian coast was south, reachable perhaps if the ice opened, deadly certainly if they missed their landfall and drifted into the open sea.

The choices multiplied without clarifying. To wait on a floe was to trust the drift, to hope that the current would carry them to safety before their supplies failed. To march was to consume those supplies faster, to risk the ice breaking under their feet, to gamble mobility against stability. The men who had left with Stefansson had chosen mobility, had walked into the white unknown with the confidence of hunters who knew the ice. They had not returned. The men who remained must make their choice without that example, trusting only in their captain and in the evidence of their senses.

The huddled survivors on the ice floe, surrounded by their salvaged gear, with their ship gone and no shelter but the canvas they had saved.
