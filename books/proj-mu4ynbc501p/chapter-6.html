Chapter 6

Whispers in the Tea Room

────────────────────────────────────────

Alec Dunbar’s defensive hunch over the fault log book still burned in Ruth’s memory as she walked away from the technical room, her heels striking the linoleum with measured precision. Guilt or pride, she had asked herself. The question had no answer yet, and she carried it with her.

The corridor stretched before her, fluorescent panels casting their familiar pallid glow over walls that had witnessed thirty years of telephone exchange history. Ruth knew every crack in the plaster, every water stain that maintenance had promised to repair and never had. She had walked this path thousands of times, but today the familiar felt foreign, as though the building itself had become complicit in something she could not name.

She was heading toward the break room when she heard the voices.

A ribbon of steam and a low, urgent murmur escaped from the tea-room door left slightly ajar. Ruth slowed her pace. The exchange hummed with its usual mechanical symphony, relays clicking and connections forming in the vast chamber beyond, but here in the corridor, the human element had gathered in a configuration she had not anticipated.

Marjorie Finch’s voice drifted through the gap, low and crisp, carrying that particular inflection she reserved for information that granted her social power.

“Poor dear,” Marjorie was saying, and Ruth could picture the concerned tilt of her head, the careful positioning of sympathy over gossip. “The electricity board sent a final demand. They’ll cut her off if she doesn’t pay by Friday.”

Ruth pressed herself against the wall, just beyond the door’s sightline. Her pulse quickened, though she kept her breathing even. Eavesdropping was beneath her position as supervisor, but the ledger page had changed everything. The rules that once governed her conduct had become elastic, stretched by the weight of a forgery she could not ignore.

“Her husband,” another voice continued, one of the newer operators, Ruth thought. Brenda, perhaps, or Carol. “He left her with nothing?”

“Crippling debts,” Marjorie replied, and Ruth heard the rustle of clothing, the settling into chairs that suggested this conversation had found its natural habitat. “The house is mortgaged to the hilt. She’s been juggling for months, borrowing from Peter to pay Paul, and now Peter wants his due.”

Ruth’s hand found the doorframe. She leaned closer, her ear toward the gap.

“How much?” someone asked.

“Fourteen pounds, give or take. Might as well be fourteen hundred, the way things stand.” Marjorie’s voice dropped further. “She cried in the lavatory yesterday. I heard her through the door. Proper sobbing, not just a sniffle. Something’s got to give.”

The words settled into Ruth’s chest. Violet Sykes. The name rose unbidden, connected to the gossip by the invisible threads of office knowledge. Violet, who had been late twice last week. Violet, whose smiles had grown thinner over the past month, whose laugh had lost its music.

Ruth stepped back from the door. Her reflection caught in a glass-covered notice board across the corridor, and she saw her own face, tight with something she refused to name. She turned and walked toward the locker room, her footsteps quicker now, purpose driving her forward.

The locker room sat at the end of a short hallway, tucked away from the main traffic of the exchange. Ruth pushed open the door and found the space empty, a row of metal lockers lining the walls, a wooden bench running down the center. The smell of lavender soap and stale perfume hung in the air, the residue of dozens of women’s daily rituals.

She knew which locker was Violet’s. Twelfth from the left, bottom row, a small photograph of a cat taped to the inside of the door. Ruth had seen her open it a hundred times, had never given the action a second thought.

Now she stood before it, her hand hovering over the metal handle.

The ledger doesn’t lie, she told herself. Only people do.

She pulled the locker open.

Violet’s handbag sat on the top shelf, brown leather worn at the corners. A cardigan hung from a hook, and below it, a pair of galoshes stood neatly aligned. Ruth’s gaze swept the space, cataloguing the ordinary details of a colleague’s private life.

Her eyes caught on the edge of paper, half-tucked beneath the handbag.

Ruth reached in and slid the document free. The electricity board’s letterhead stared back at her, bold and official. FINAL DEMAND, the words announced in red ink. PAYMENT REQUIRED IMMEDIATELY. The amount made her breath catch: twelve pounds, eight shillings, and sixpence. Not fourteen, as Marjorie had claimed, but close enough to confirm the gossip’s substance.

The date on the letter was three days ago.

Ruth turned the paper over. A handwritten note on the back in Violet’s cramped, careful script: “Sister says she can help with half. Ask Mrs. W. about advance.”

Ruth’s fingers tightened on the paper. The forgery in the ledger had been precise, deliberate, the work of someone who knew the exchange’s systems. Violet had been an operator for four years. She knew the logging procedures, understood the rhythm of the records.

But knowing the system and corrupting it were different things. Ruth forced herself to hold the thought at arm’s length. Suspicion was not proof. The ledger page was forged, yes. The handwriting was her own, though she had not written it. But that meant someone had studied her hand, practiced her loops and crosses until they could reproduce them.

Violet’s handwriting was nothing like hers.

The door swung open behind her.

Ruth spun around, the final demand still in her hand. Violet Sykes stood in the doorway, her face cycling through recognition, confusion, and finally landing on a naked terror that confirmed everything the gossip had implied.

“Mrs. Calloway.” Violet’s voice came out thin. Her eyes dropped to the paper in Ruth’s hand, and her face crumpled.

“Violet.” Ruth kept her voice level, though her heart had begun to pound. “I was looking for you.”

The younger woman stepped into the locker room, her movements jerky and uncoordinated. She looked as though she might bolt, but the door swung shut behind her, and there was nowhere to go. Her eyes were red-rimmed, the skin beneath them puffy and dark.

“I can explain.” Violet’s hands twisted together. “That’s not what it looks like.”

Ruth took a step forward. The fluorescent light hummed overhead, casting its unforgiving glare across Violet’s stricken features. Ruth had supervised this woman for years, had watched her grow from a nervous trainee into a reliable operator. She had praised her work, corrected her mistakes, treated her with the firm fairness that defined her approach to leadership.

Now she held evidence of desperation in her hand.

“What is it, then?” Ruth asked. She kept her voice gentle, though the question carried an edge. “I’m asking you to think, Violet. Not to remember. To think.”

Violet’s lower lip trembled. A tear spilled over, tracing a silver line down her cheek. “It’s my sister’s,” she said. “She’s the one in trouble. I was keeping it safe for her. Her husband, he… he gambles. I didn’t want her husband to find it.”

The lie fell between them like a stone dropping into still water. Ruth saw it for what it was, the fabrication of a panicked mind reaching for any explanation that might deflect judgment. Violet’s sister had been mentioned in the handwritten note on the back of the letter as someone who might help, not as the letter’s owner.

Ruth did not call her out on it. Instead, she studied the younger woman’s face, the way her eyes skittered away from direct contact, the way her fingers kept moving, twisting and untwisting against each other. These were the gestures of someone caught in a trap of her own making, someone whose desperation had pushed her into a corner.

“Your sister,” Ruth repeated, watching Violet’s reaction.

“Yes.” Violet nodded too quickly, too eagerly. “She asked me to hold onto it. I keep telling her she needs to sort things out, but she won’t listen. I’ve offered to help, but money is… well, you know how it is.”

Ruth did know. She knew exactly how it was. She also knew that Violet had no sister living in Manchester, had mentioned only a brother in Sheffield during the brief personal conversations they had shared over the years.

The lie was plausible enough to fool someone who did not pay attention. But Ruth paid attention. The ledger had taught her that much.

“Violet.” Ruth took another step forward. The paper crinkled in her grip. “If you’re in trouble, I need to know. Not as your supervisor. As someone who might be able to help.”

Violet’s face collapsed. Another tear fell, and then another, her composure dissolving like sugar in hot water. She pressed her palms against her eyes, her shoulders shaking with silent sobs.

“I can’t,” she whispered. “I can’t lose this job. Please, Mrs. Calloway. Whatever you think, please don’t…”

“Don’t what?”

Violet’s hands dropped from her face. Her eyes met Ruth’s, and in them, Ruth saw something that made her stomach turn. It was the look of someone who had already calculated the cost of truth and found it too high.

“Don’t tell anyone,” Violet said. “I’ll pay it back. I’m getting an advance on my wages. Mrs. Whitmore said she’d approve it. I just need a few more days.”

Ruth’s mind raced. An advance on wages. A final demand. A woman crying in the lavatory, juggling debts she could not meet. None of it proved forgery. None of it connected Violet to the tampered ledger page.

But the lie did. The lie sat between them, obvious and ugly, and Ruth could not unsee it.

“I haven’t told anyone about anything,” Ruth said carefully. “I found a letter in your locker. I asked you whose it was. You told me.”

Violet’s relief was visible, her shoulders dropping, her breath escaping in a rush. “Thank you,” she breathed. “Thank you, Mrs. Calloway.”

Ruth placed the letter back on the shelf, exactly where she had found it. She closed the locker door with a soft metallic click.

“Get yourself sorted,” she said. “Whatever’s going on, whatever you need to fix, fix it. This exchange runs on precision. One wrong connection can throw the whole system off.”

Violet nodded, her eyes still wet. “I will. I promise.”

Ruth walked out of the locker room, her back straight, her face composed. The corridor stretched before her, familiar and foreign at once. She passed the tea room door, now closed, the voices within reduced to a muffled murmur. She passed the entrance to the exchange floor, where operators sat at their positions, connecting calls with mechanical precision.

She did not stop until she reached her own small office. The door closed behind her, and she sat at her desk, her hands flat against the wood.

The ledger page was forged in her handwriting. Someone had studied her penmanship, learned its rhythms and quirks, reproduced it well enough to fool a casual glance. Violet Sykes was in financial trouble. Violet Sykes had lied to her face.

But Violet’s handwriting was nothing like hers.

Ruth opened her desk drawer and withdrew the ledger. She turned to the page in question, studying the entries again. The forgery was good, but she could see the differences now, the places where the pressure of the pen had been too heavy, where the loops of her own script had been approximated rather than replicated.

Someone had forged her handwriting. Someone with enough access to study her entries, enough knowledge to understand the logging system, enough desperation or motive to risk everything on a deception.

Violet had motive. She also had an alibi for at least part of the night in question. She had been on shift until ten, had signed out in the presence of three other operators.

But Alec Dunbar had been present late. Alec Dunbar, who guarded his fault log book as though it contained state secrets. Alec Dunbar, whose name she had seen in the access log, logged in after midnight on the date in question.

Ruth closed the ledger. Her mind refused to settle, the pieces refusing to form a coherent picture.

A missing trunk call to Terence Bolt had existed. Someone had erased it, had covered the erasure with a forged page in her handwriting. The question burned in her thoughts: who had the skill to forge her hand, and who had the desperation to try?

Violet Sykes had the desperation. Alec Dunbar had the skill.

But Ruth could not shake the feeling that she was missing something, some connection that would pull the threads together into a pattern she could recognize.

She looked at the closed ledger, its leather binding worn smooth by years of use. The exchange hummed around her, relays clicking, connections forming, the vast machinery of communication operating with its usual mechanical indifference.

Somewhere in that machinery, a gear had slipped. A call had been erased. A page had been forged.

And Ruth Calloway, who had spent her career ensuring the accuracy of every entry, found herself on the other side of the record, hunting for a truth that someone did not want found.

The question was no longer whether she would find the forger. The question was whether she could afford to.

She thought of Violet’s tear-stained face, the tremble in her voice, the lie that had fallen so easily from her lips. She thought of Alec’s defensive hunch, the way he had closed his fault book when she approached.

The ledger didn’t lie. But people did. And now Ruth had to decide which lies she was willing to expose, and which truths she was prepared to bury.

She opened the ledger again, and began to read.
