Chapter 5

The Fault Log

────────────────────────────────────────

The door to the engineers’ closet stood ajar, but today it felt like a threshold Ruth Calloway was not certain she could cross back over. The fluorescent tube above the frame flickered twice, casting the narrow corridor in a stuttering pallor that made the walls seem closer than they were. She adjusted the leather portfolio under her arm—a prop, borrowed from the supervisor’s office when no one was watching, its contents blank paper and purpose.

The ledger’s forgery still burned behind her eyes. Her own handwriting, or close enough to fool the inattentive. Someone had sat at her station, dipped a pen in her inkwell, and committed a lie to the permanent record. The thought sat in her chest, cold and unmoving. Now she needed to know if that lie had left footprints elsewhere.

She thought again of that single entry she had isolated—where an ink blotch obscured one digit, suggesting a correction made after the fact. The timing discrepancy still gnawed at her. Her personal log showed the call lasting two minutes, but the trunk record confirmed three minutes. A one-minute window unaccounted for in the councillor’s timeline. A small gap, but enough to hide something. Now she needed to know if that lie had left footprints elsewhere.

Alec Dunbar’s domain smelled of solder and machine oil, sharp enough to taste on the back of the tongue. The engineers’ closet was barely larger than a telephone box, cramped between the main switchboard hall and the stairwell that led down to the cable vaults. Racks of testing equipment lined the walls—oscillators, voltmeters, spools of insulated wire in various stages of unraveling. A workbench dominated the far end, its surface scarred by years of soldering iron slips and utility knife cuts. Above it, pinned to a corkboard that had seen better decades, a calendar from 1947 displayed a pin-up girl holding a telephone receiver to her ear, her red lipstick matching the corporate logo in the corner.

Alec sat on a wooden stool beside the workbench, his back to the door, hunched over an open log book with the concentration of a jeweler examining a flawed diamond. His brown engineer’s coat stretched tight across his shoulders. The left cuff was frayed, she noticed—the same frayed cuff she had seen a hundred times, catching on racks and doorframes as he passed. His hands, never quite clean, moved with practiced precision as he made an entry in the log.

“Mr. Dunbar.”

He did not turn. “Door’s open.”

“Routine audit.” The words came out steadier than she felt. She stepped inside, pulling the door halfway closed behind her. The fluorescent tube buzzed overhead, casting everything in that flat, institutional light that made colors seem tired. “I need to review the fault log for the past fortnight.”

Now Alec turned. His moustache, that perpetual yellow-brown stain from years of cheap tobacco, twitched once as he regarded her. His eyes were small and dark, set deep in a face that had weathered forty-five years of Manchester mornings and shift work that never quite let go. He did not smile.

“Audit.” He repeated the word as if testing it for impurities. “Since when do supervisors conduct field audits personally?”

“Since the chief engineer requested a thorough review of all maintenance documentation following the councillor’s death.” The lie slid out smoothly enough. She had learned, over fifteen years at the exchange, that authority rarely questioned itself. If you acted as though you belonged somewhere, most people assumed you did.

Alec’s stool scraped against the floor as he swiveled to face her fully. He did not offer her a seat. There was no other seat to offer.

“Chief engineer, is it?” His voice carried the flat vowels of Ancoats, stripped of any pretense. “Funny. I saw Mr. Whitmore in the canteen not twenty minutes ago, and he didn’t mention any audit.”

Ruth kept her expression neutral. “The directive came through this morning. You’d know that if you checked your in-tray.”

Alec’s gaze flicked to a small metal tray mounted on the wall beside the door, overflowing with memoranda and repair tickets. He grunted, a sound that might have been acknowledgment or dismissal. Then he turned back to the workbench and flipped the log book open to an earlier page.

“Fault log. Past fortnight.” His finger traced a column of entries. “You want the abridged version or the full catalogue of Manchester’s telecommunications woes?”

“The full catalogue, please.”

He pulled a second stool from beneath the workbench—an awkward motion, the stool’s legs catching on a cable spool—and set it beside him without ceremony. “Sit, then. This will take a moment.”

Ruth settled onto the stool, feeling its uneven legs wobble beneath her weight. The space between her and Alec was barely an arm’s length. She could smell the tobacco on his coat, see the grease ground into the creases of his knuckles. The log book lay open between them, its pages dense with his careful, blocky script.

“Let’s see.” Alec ran his finger down the entries, muttering dates and fault codes under his breath. “Third of October—junction box 47, moisture ingress. Fourth—line fault on the Didsbury circuit, traced to a rodent bite through the insulation. You’d be amazed what rats can do to copper wire. Fifth—“

“Stop.”

Alec’s finger halted. He looked at her.

“The seventh,” Ruth said. “What entries do we have for the seventh?”

Something shifted in Alec’s expression. It was subtle—a tightening around the eyes, a stillness in his shoulders that had not been there before. He turned the page with deliberate care.

“Seventh of October.” His voice was flat. “What’s your interest in that particular date, Supervisor?”

“The councillor’s death was reported on the eighth. I want to see what maintenance activity occurred the night before.”

“The police already reviewed our records.”

“I’m aware. Humor me.”

Alec was silent for a moment. Then he turned the log book toward her, spinning it one hundred eighty degrees so she could read the entries right-side up. His finger pointed to a block of text near the bottom of the page.

Ruth leaned forward. The fluorescent light buzzed overhead, casting its flat glow across the paper. Alec’s handwriting was precise, almost mechanical—each letter formed with the same deliberate pressure, each number sitting squarely in its allotted space. She scanned the entries for the seventh of October, her eyes catching on the familiar codes: line faults, equipment failures, routine maintenance calls logged by the night shift engineers.

And there it was.

Her finger stopped on the fault log entry. The ink was the same dark blue as the ledger’s forgery—not the standard black that most of the exchange used, but that particular deep blue that came from the inkwell at station three. Her station.

22: 47 — Line outage reported, extension 214. Traced to loose connection at junction box 12. Rectified 23: 15. — A. Dunbar

She read the entry twice. The time stamps were precise: twenty-two minutes between the reported outage and its rectification. Twenty-two minutes when extension 214—the councillor’s private line—would have been dead.

But the timing gnawed at her. The discrepancy she had found in the ledger—a call with a digit obscured by an ink blotch, the timing mismatch between her personal log and the trunk record—fell within this very window. Her log showed two minutes. The trunk record showed three. Somewhere in that one-minute gap was an explanation she hadn’t yet found.

And now here was Alec Dunbar’s fault log, providing a convenient explanation for why the line might have appeared dead, why any calls during that window might not have been recorded. A loose connection at junction box 12. Rectified in under half an hour.

Except she knew the timing discrepancy pointed to something else. The call had gone through. She had seen the trunk record.

“The ink,” she said, before she could stop herself.

Alec’s shadow fell across the page as he leaned closer. “What about it?”

“Dark blue.” She looked up at him. “We use black at the exchange. Standard issue.”

“My own ink.” Alec’s voice was clipped. “I prefer it. Better flow, less smudging. You’ll find that entry’s not the only one in blue.”

Ruth scanned the page. He was right—scattered throughout the log were other entries in that same dark blue, interspersed with the standard black. Alec Dunbar’s personal preference, marking his own work with a signature more distinctive than any name.

“The seventh was your shift,” she said. “You were here late that night.”

“I’m here late most nights.” His tone carried an edge now. “Someone has to keep this equipment running while the rest of you are home with your feet up.”

“I didn’t mean—“

“What exactly are you looking for, Supervisor?” Alec cut her off. He pulled the log book back toward himself, his hand covering the entry she had been studying. “Because this ‘routine audit’ of yours seems remarkably focused on one particular date. One particular night.”

Ruth felt the weight of his gaze. The closet suddenly seemed smaller, the walls pressing in. The fluorescent light buzzed overhead, casting shadows that flickered with each tiny fluctuation in the current.

“I’m following procedure, Mr. Dunbar. Nothing more.”

“You want a story, read a paperback.” Alec’s voice dropped, the words coming slow and deliberate. “I give you facts. That’s all that’s in this book. Facts. Technical truth. Not whatever administrative truth you people are looking for.”

The distinction hung in the air between them. Technical truth. Administrative truth. She had heard engineers use those terms before, drawing a line between the physical reality of their equipment and the recorded reality of the paperwork. The machines did not lie. Only the people who wrote about them did.

“I appreciate that distinction, Mr. Dunbar. I really do.” Ruth held his gaze. “But sometimes facts need context. And sometimes the context is missing.”

“Meaning?”

“Meaning I’m asking you to think.” She gestured toward the log book. “That entry—the line outage on extension 214. Can you tell me more about it? How was the fault reported? Who reported it?”

Alec’s jaw tightened. “Standard procedure. The fault board lit up, I traced it to junction box 12, found a loose connection, rectified it. Took twenty-two minutes.”

“Who reported it?”

“The board doesn’t record names. It records fault codes.”

“And the loose connection—was that a common fault? Had it happened before?”

Alec was silent. His hand remained on the log book, covering the entry as though he could make it disappear by sheer force of will.

“Mr. Dunbar.”

“I don’t recall.” The words came out flat. “It was a busy night. I had six other faults to attend to. You want me to remember every loose connection from a fortnight ago?”

“I’m not asking you to remember. I’m asking you to think.”

Something flickered in Alec’s expression. For a moment, she thought she saw fear there—or was it anger? The two emotions wore similar masks.

“You know,” he said slowly, “Marjorie came by asking questions too. Last week. Wanted to know who was on shift the night of the councillor’s death. Who had access to the switchboard. Who might have seen something.” He paused. “Now here you are, with your ‘routine audit,’ asking about the same night. Makes a man wonder.”

Ruth kept her face still. “Marjorie Finch asks a lot of questions about a lot of things. That’s hardly news.”

“True enough.” Alec’s moustache twitched. “But she’s not the one sitting in my closet, inspecting my log book with the look of someone who’s found what she was looking for.”

The words landed like a slap. Ruth felt her pulse quicken, though she gave no outward sign.

“I don’t know what you mean.”

“Don’t you?” Alec leaned forward, his voice dropping to a murmur that barely rose above the hum of the fluorescent light. “Because from where I’m sitting, Supervisor Calloway, it looks like you came in here with a theory. And it looks like you think that entry proves something.”

“I told you. Routine audit—“

“Routine audit, my eye.” Alec stood abruptly, his stool scraping against the floor. He was not a tall man, but in the cramped space of the closet, he seemed to fill it entirely. “You’re not here for the chief engineer. You’re not here for procedure. You’re here because you think I had something to do with whatever happened to that councillor.”

Ruth rose to face him. The closet left barely a foot of space between them. She could see the yellowed tobacco stain on his moustache, the thin sheen of sweat on his forehead.

“I think,” she said carefully, “that a man was found dead in suspicious circumstances. And I think someone at this exchange knows more than they’ve said.”

“And you think that someone is me.”

“I think you were here that night. I think your fault log shows an outage during the very window where I’ve found a timing discrepancy. And I think you’re defensive in a way that suggests you have something to hide.”

For a long moment, neither of them moved. The air in the closet seemed to thicken, heavy with the smell of solder and machine oil and the unspoken accusations that hung between them.

Then Alec laughed—a short, bitter sound that held no humor.

“You think I forged my own log?” He shook his head. “You think I created a fake outage to cover up what, exactly? I’m an engineer, Supervisor. I maintain the lines. I don’t listen to the calls. I don’t know who called whom or what was said. That’s your domain, not mine.”

“Then why are you so defensive?”

“Because you’re standing in my workspace, accusing me of—“ He stopped, his jaw working. “Because I’ve given twenty years to this exchange. Because I’ve built my reputation on the truth of my work, on the reliability of my equipment. And now you come in here with your questions and your theories, treating me like—“

“Like what?”

“Like a suspect.” The words came out hard. “Like someone who can’t be trusted. Like my word means nothing.”

Ruth watched him. His indignation seemed genuine, but then, so had Marjorie’s. Everyone at this exchange had something to protect. Their jobs, their reputations, their secrets. The question was which of those secrets mattered.

“I’m not accusing you of anything, Mr. Dunbar. I’m asking questions. That’s all.”

“And if I don’t have answers that satisfy you? What then?”

The question hung in the air. Ruth thought of the ledger page, forged in her handwriting. She thought of the timing discrepancy she had uncovered—that one-minute window between her personal log and the trunk record. She thought of the councillor’s driver, Terence Bolt, and the questions that still surrounded his movements that night.

“Then I keep asking,” she said. “Until I find the truth.”

Alec stared at her for a long moment. Then he sat back down on his stool, pulling the log book toward him with a gesture that was clearly a dismissal.

“The fault log is accurate. I stand by my work. If you want to verify the outage, check the fault board records. They’re kept in the main office.” His voice was cold, stripped of any pretense of professional courtesy. “Now if you don’t mind, Supervisor, I have work to do.”

Ruth stood frozen for a moment. She had what she came for—the knowledge that Alec’s fault log showed an outage during the precise window where she had found the timing discrepancy, recorded in the same dark blue ink as the ledger forgery. But she had also made an enemy. Whatever rapport she might have built with Alec Dunbar, whatever trust might have existed between colleagues, was gone.

She turned toward the door, her portfolio clutched tight under her arm.

“Thank you for your time, Mr. Dunbar.”

He did not respond. He had already turned back to his workbench, his back to her, his shoulders rigid with the tension of a man who knew he was being watched.

The corridor outside the engineers’ closet was cold, a draft coming from somewhere in the building’s old ventilation system. Ruth walked slowly, her heels clicking against the linoleum, her mind turning over what she had learned.

Alec Dunbar had been in the exchange late on the night of the councillor’s death. His fault log showed a line outage on the councillor’s extension during the very window where she had uncovered the timing discrepancy. The ink matched the forgery. And he was defensive, territorial, clearly hiding something.

But was it guilt? Or simply the wounded pride of a man whose competence had been questioned?

She thought of his words: Technical truth. Administrative truth. The machines did not lie. Only the people who wrote about them did.

The fault board records. He had mentioned them almost as an afterthought, a way to verify his story. If the fault board showed no outage that night, then his log entry was a lie. But if the board confirmed the outage, then he was telling the truth—or someone had manipulated both records.

She needed to check. But that meant going to the main office, where she had no legitimate reason to be. It meant risking further exposure, further questions.

And it meant confronting the fact that she was now truly alone in this investigation. Marjorie had deflected, Alec had turned hostile, and every question she asked narrowed the circle of people she could trust.

The ledger doesn’t lie. Only people do.

Her own words echoed in her mind, bitter now with the knowledge that someone had used her handwriting, her identity, to commit a forgery. Someone who knew her well enough to mimic her pen strokes. Someone who had access to her station, her inkwell, her ledgers.

Someone at this exchange.

She paused at the end of the corridor, looking back toward the engineers’ closet. The door was still ajar, that flickering fluorescent light casting its stuttering glow across the threshold. She could see Alec’s silhouette through the gap, hunched over his workbench, his hands moving with mechanical precision.

Alec defensively guards his fault log book, leaving Ruth wondering if his defensiveness is guilt or just pride.

The question turned in her mind like a key in a lock, refusing to catch. She had a clue now—a thread to pull. But she had also made her suspicions known, and whoever had forged that ledger page would know she was getting closer.

She touched her forearm, feeling the old scar beneath her sleeve—the one from the childhood accident that had taught her, at eight years old, that the world was full of hidden dangers. That scar had made her cautious. It had made her watchful. It had made her the kind of person who noticed when a ledger page was written in the wrong ink, who caught the timing discrepancy between logs, who could not let go of a question once it had taken root.

The truth was in these records somewhere. Hidden in fault logs and ledgers and the gaps between entries. And she would find it, whatever it cost.

But as she walked away from the engineers’ closet, the weight of Alec’s hostility heavy on her shoulders, she could not shake the feeling that she had just made a dangerous mistake.

Because if Alec Dunbar was the forger, she had just shown him her hand.

And if he wasn’t, she had just alienated the one person who might have helped her find the truth.
