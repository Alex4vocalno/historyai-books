Chapter 8

Dunbars Fabricated Record

────────────────────────────────────────

The technical office door was ajar, a wedge of pale light cutting across the corridor’s linoleum. Ruth paused at the threshold, her hand still wrapped around the reference sheet she had folded into her palm. The paper had grown damp in her grip. She could feel her pulse in her fingertips, a rapid, shallow beat that had nothing to do with the February chill seeping through the exchange’s walls.

Alec Dunbar sat with his back to the door. His shoulders formed a solid barrier between her and the desk, and in the taut architecture of his spine, she read the posture of a man defending territory. The metal-bound fault log lay open before him, its pages glowing amber under the lamp. He was writing something. His pen moved with slow deliberation across the page, each stroke precise.

Ruth knocked twice on the doorframe.

Alec’s hand paused. He did not turn.

“Come.”

His voice carried the flat, resigned tone of a man who had expected interruption and resented it in advance. Ruth stepped inside. The technical office smelled of hot metal and ozone, the distinctive scent of the relay racks that lined the far wall. A soldering iron cooled on a ceramic tray, trailing a thin wisp of acrid smoke. The single window looked out onto the fire escape, its glass fogged with condensation.

“I need to verify a time in your fault log.” She kept her voice level, professional. “The line outage from the fourteenth. The trunk connection that dropped at thirteen-forty-seven.”

Alec’s pen resumed its motion. He did not look up.

“Administrative concern, is it?” The words came clipped, each syllable bitten off. “I don’t recall the supervisor’s schedule including technical audits.”

“Routine verification.” Ruth moved closer. Her heels clicked against the concrete floor. “The police have requested our records. I’m ensuring the ledgers align before I submit them.”

That made him stop. His pen lifted from the page, hovering an inch above the paper. The lamp threw shadows across his face as he finally turned to look at her. His expression was unreadable, a mask of professional neutrality that she had seen him wear during budget meetings and disciplinary hearings alike.

“The police.”

“City councillor’s death.” She stood at the edge of his desk now, close enough to see the page he had been writing on. He had covered it with his forearm, a casual gesture that seemed practiced. “They want the call ledger and the fault logs. I’m cross-referencing the entries.”

Alec set down his pen. The metal barrel clicked against the desk’s surface. He straightened in his chair, and the motion made him seem larger, his presence filling the cramped office. His hand remained where it was, obscuring whatever he had been writing.

“Thirteenth of February,” he said. “Yes, I remember. Line fault on the trunk exchange. Cleared within the hour.”

“I have the time as thirteen-forty-seven. I need to confirm that against your log.”

“Why?”

The question hung between them. It was a simple word, three letters, but it carried weight. Ruth felt her jaw tighten. She forced herself to maintain eye contact.

“Because the ledger shows a call routed through that line at thirteen-fifty-two. Five minutes after you marked it cleared.” She let the implication settle. “If the times don’t align, I need to note the discrepancy.”

Alec’s gaze shifted to the fault log. His fingers drummed once against the metal cover. The lamp hummed overhead, a low, constant drone.

“My records are accurate.” His voice had dropped half an octave. “I don’t make mistakes in my log, Ruth.”

“I’m not suggesting you have.” She kept her tone even. “I’m asking to see the entry. Routine verification. Nothing more.”

“Routine.” He repeated the word as though testing its weight on his tongue. “You’ve never asked to verify my log before. Not once since you took over supervision of my shift.”

“There’s a first time for everything.”

Alec stood. The motion was sudden, fluid, and it brought him to his full height, blocking her view of the desk entirely. He was not a large man, but in the confined space, his presence felt substantial. His hands came to rest on his hips, and she could see the grease stains on his cuffs, the permanent shadow of his work etched into his skin.

“Let me ask you something, Ruth.” His head tilted slightly. “Why now? Why this entry? What’s really going on?”

The question pressed against her. She thought of Marjorie’s face in the break room, the way her eyes had slid away when Alec’s name came up. She thought of the ledger page upstairs, the loop of her own signature forged by someone else’s hand. She thought of the police sergeant’s flat stare, the way he had looked at her as though she were already guilty of something.

“An irregularity was noted.” She chose each word with care. “I’m following up. That’s all you need to know.”

“That’s not all I need to know.” Alec stepped closer. The distance between them narrowed to two feet, then one. She could smell the ozone on his clothes, the metallic tang that clung to everyone who worked the relay racks. “You come into my office, you ask about a specific time on a specific date, you mention the police. And you expect me to believe this is routine?”

“I expect you to comply with a supervisory request.”

“Supervisory.” He laughed, a short, sharp sound. “You supervise the operators, Ruth. You don’t supervise me. You don’t supervise my work, and you don’t supervise my log.”

“The fault log is exchange property. It falls under my purview.”

“Does it?” His hand moved to the logbook, pressing flat against its metal cover. “This log is technical record. It’s engineers’ business. Not administrators’.”

“I’m asking to see it.”

“And I’m telling you no.”

The word dropped between them like a stone into still water. Ruth felt her breath catch. She had expected resistance, but she had not expected a direct refusal. Not from Alec. Not from someone who had worked beside her for six years, who had shared tea in the break room and complained about the heating and traded stories about the day shift’s incompetence.

“Alec.” She heard her own voice change, the professional veneer cracking. “Show me the log.”

“No.”

“This is a direct order.”

“You don’t have the authority.”

“I’m the supervisor on duty.”

“Of the operators.” His hand pressed harder against the logbook. “Not of me. Not of my records. Not of this.”

Ruth looked at his hand. The knuckles had gone white with pressure. The logbook’s metal cover gleamed under the lamp, its surface unmarked, pristine. Whatever lay beneath that cover, whatever entry he had been writing or checking when she walked in, he was determined she would not see it.

“Step aside.”

“Ruth—“

“Step aside from the desk.”

He didn’t move. His eyes held hers, and in them she saw something she had never seen before. Not anger. Not resentment. Something colder. Something calculated.

“What are you looking for?” His voice was quiet now, almost gentle. “Tell me what you’re really looking for, and maybe we can talk about it.”

Ruth’s hand moved before she had made the conscious decision. She stepped forward, closing the remaining distance between them, and reached for the logbook. Alec’s arm came up to block her, but she was faster. Her fingers closed around the metal edge and pulled.

The book slid across the desk.

Alec’s hand slammed down on the pages, pinning them before she could turn them. His other hand grabbed her wrist. His grip was hard, professional, the grip of someone who knew how to hold things that struggled.

“Let go.”

“Let me see the entry.”

“It’s my log.”

“It’s exchange property, and I’m taking it.”

She pulled. The book came free, and she stumbled backward, the heavy metal binding clutched against her chest. Alec’s chair scraped against the floor as he moved to follow, but she was already at the door, already through it, already walking fast down the corridor with her heels striking the linoleum in sharp, deliberate beats.

She did not run. Running would admit something she was not ready to admit. But she walked with purpose, her grip tight on the logbook, her reference sheet still crushed in her other hand.

The vacant supervisor’s desk sat at the end of the corridor, outside the operators’ bay. The night shift had gone quiet, the usual hum of calls and connections reduced to a skeleton rhythm. No one sat at the desk. Its lamp was off, its surface bare except for a blotter and a pencil tray.

Ruth sat down. Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the desk’s surface, felt the cool linoleum beneath her palms, and forced herself to breathe.

The logbook lay before her. Its metal cover was cold against her fingers. She opened it.

The pages were dense with entries, each line recording a fault, a clearance, a time. She traced her finger down the columns, scanning dates and line numbers until she found what she was looking for.

Thirteenth of February. Trunk exchange fault. Line 47.

The entry read: 13:47.

Ruth stared at the numbers. She unfolded the reference sheet in her other hand, smoothing its creases against the desk. Her own notation stared back at her: the call ledger showed the trunk active at 13:52. Five minutes after the fault had been marked cleared.

She looked back at the logbook.

The ‘3’ in ‘13:47’ was a shade darker than the surrounding ink. Its loops were slightly uneven, compressed where the original digit would have been open. She leaned closer, tilting the page toward the lamp she had switched on. The light fell across the paper, and she saw it clearly: the ‘3’ had been written over something else. The original digit had been a ‘2’. Someone had transformed a ‘2’ into a ‘3’ with careful, patient strokes.

The time had been altered from 23:47 to 13:47.

A twelve-hour shift.

Ruth sat back in the chair. Her breath came shallow, and her lungs felt tight. The reference sheet trembled in her hand. She looked at the entry again, at the careful forgery that had changed night to day, that had moved the fault from the dark hours to the light ones.

This was not a mistake. This was not negligence, not a hurried clerk’s error, not a tired engineer’s slip. This was deliberate. Someone had taken a pen to this page and changed the record. Someone had known exactly what they were doing.

Alec’s face swam in her memory. The way his hand had covered the page. The way he had stood between her and the desk. The cold calculation in his eyes when he asked what she was really looking for.

She knew the answer now.

The technical office door stood open at the end of the corridor. Alec had not followed her. The office sat empty, its lamp still glowing, the chair pushed back from the desk where he had risen. The silence pressed against her ears.

Ruth gathered the logbook and the reference sheet. She stood on legs that felt uncertain and walked back toward the technical office. Each step echoed in the empty corridor. The exchange hummed around her, the relay racks clicking and whirring in their endless rhythm, indifferent to the small human drama unfolding in their shadow.

Alec stood in the doorway. He had not moved. His arms hung at his sides, and his face was a mask of waiting.

Ruth stopped three feet from him. She held up the logbook, open to the entry.

“The time was altered.”

Alec’s eyes moved to the page. He did not flinch. He did not look away.

“The ‘1’ was written over a ‘2.’ The original time was 23:47. Someone changed it to 13:47.” Her voice was steady. She was proud of that. “That’s a twelve-hour difference. That’s not a slip of the pen.”

Alec said nothing.

“You were here that night. Marjorie said you stayed late. Your name is in the access log.” Ruth felt the words building, each one falling into place like the components of a machine. “You had opportunity. You have the technical skill. And now I find your log has been altered.”

Still, Alec said nothing. His face remained still, his eyes fixed on hers. The silence stretched between them, filled only by the distant hum of the exchange.

“Why?” Ruth heard her own voice crack. “Why would you change it? What call were you trying to hide?”

Alec’s lips parted. For a moment, she thought he would speak, would explain, would offer some reason that would make sense of this. But then his mouth closed again, and his expression hardened into something she recognized: the face of a man who had decided what he would say and what he would not.

“You have the log.” His voice was flat. “That’s all you’re getting from me.”

“Tell me why.”

“Read it however you want.”

“Someone died, Alec. A city councillor is dead, and the police are asking questions, and I found a forged page in my ledger. Now I find a forged entry in yours. Do you understand what this looks like?”

“I understand what it looks like.”

“Then explain it.”

“No.”

The word was final. Ruth stared at him, searching for some crack in his composure, some sign of the man she had worked beside for six years. But his face was closed, his posture rigid, his eyes flat and unreadable.

She thought of the ledger upstairs, the page in her own handwriting that she had not written. She thought of Marjorie’s deflection, the way she had pointed toward Alec with such careful casualness. She thought of the missing trunk call, the gap in the records where a connection should have been.

Alec’s name in the access log. Alec’s hand on the fault log. Alec’s silence now.

“You forged it.” The words fell out before she could stop them. “You altered the record. You—“

“I said read it however you want.” His voice dropped low. “You have the book. You have your theory. Do what you need to do.”

Ruth’s hand tightened on the logbook until its metal edge bit into her palm. She wanted to demand more, to push past his silence, to shake him until the answers fell out. But she recognized the set of his shoulders, the locked jaw, the flat refusal in his eyes. She would get nothing more from him tonight.

She turned. The corridor stretched before her, empty and humming. The stairs at the far end led back to the operators’ floor, to the supervisor’s desk where the forged ledger waited, to the records that would have to be submitted to the police.

Ruth walked. Each step felt heavy, weighted with what she had found and what she had not. The logbook pressed against her chest. Alec’s silence pressed against her thoughts.

The exchange hummed on, its machinery clicking and connecting in the dark, routing calls through lines that had been cleared and cleared through faults that had been recorded. The system worked. The system was reliable. The system told the truth.

Except when someone decided to change it.

Ruth reached the stairs. She paused at the bottom step, looking back down the corridor toward the technical office. Alec had retreated inside. The door was closing, its wedge of light narrowing to a sliver, then disappearing entirely.

In her hand, the logbook felt heavy. Its altered entry burned in her memory. The ‘1’ that had been a ‘2.’ The night that had been changed to day. The fault that had been moved, twelve hours forward, to cover something that had happened in the dark.

Someone had made that change. Alec had been in the exchange. Alec had guarded the log. Alec had refused to explain.

But someone had forged her handwriting too. Someone had rewritten the ledger page. Someone had erased a trunk call from the record.

Ruth thought of the ‘1’ in ‘13:47,’ its rounded base, its darker ink. She thought of her own signature, the loop that had been almost perfect but not quite. Two forgeries. Two records altered. Two pieces of the same puzzle.

She climbed the stairs. The night shift stretched ahead of her, hours of darkness still to endure. The police would return. Questions would be asked. The ledger and the fault log would be examined.

And Ruth would have to decide what to say about what she had found.

The supervisor’s desk waited at the top of the stairs. Its lamp glowed under her hand as she sat down. The forged ledger lay where she had left it, its pages open to the crucial hour.

She placed Alec’s fault log beside it. Two books. Two lies. Two threads of the same deception.

Somewhere in the exchange, a trunk line clicked open. A connection formed. A voice began to speak into the night.

Ruth did not answer it. She sat in the silence, her hands flat on the desk, her eyes fixed on the records before her, and she began to think.

The voice on the trunk line was asking for connection charges. Ruth ignored it, her mind turning over the implication that settled over her like cold ash.

The fault log hadn’t been sloppy. It had been surgical.

She turned Alec’s pages back to earlier entries—routine failures, standard faults, the ordinary wear of a system under strain. Then she compared them to the ledger records from those same hours. The numbers matched. The timestamps aligned. Every previous entry was correct, verified, and honest.

Only the crucial hour had been altered. Only those seven minutes.

Someone had gone through the fault log with precision, changing exactly what needed changing and leaving everything else untouched. This wasn’t a tired man making mistakes at the end of a long shift. This was a man who knew exactly what he was doing.

Alec’s face rose in her memory—the way he had moved to block the desk, his body positioned between her and the logbook, his hand resting on the corner with casual ownership. Not the defensive posture of a careless worker facing a supervisor’s inspection. The territorial stance of a man protecting something hidden.

He hadn’t tried to explain. He hadn’t offered excuses or blustered through technical jargon. He had simply placed himself in the way, certain that his physical control of the space would be enough.

And when she had pushed past his arm, when her fingers had closed around the logbook’s spine and pulled it free from his desk, he had not come after her. He had stood frozen, watching her retreat up the stairs, his silence heavier than any denial.

Ruth looked down at the two books again. The system she had trusted—the records she had been taught to rely upon—had been actively, deliberately compromised. Not by accident. Not by negligence. By design.

The trunk line clicked again. Another voice, another connection forming in the dark.

Ruth reached over and closed the circuit, letting the call route through. Her hands were steady now. The fear had settled into something harder, something that looked like purpose.

Tomorrow, the police would return. And Ruth would have more than questions to give them.
