Chapter 14

Marjorie's Slip

────────────────────────────────────────

Marjorie Finch stood in the office doorway, her apron still on, as if Ruth had caught her between calls. The fluorescent corridor light behind her cast her face in shadow, but Ruth could see the tension in her shoulders, the way her fingers twitched at her sides.

“Supervisor Calloway? You asked to see me?”

Ruth set down her pen and gestured to the chair across from her desk. “Close the door, please. This won’t take long, but it’s best kept between us for now.”

The hum of the exchange floor seeped through the walls, a constant reminder of the work continuing beyond the supervisor’s office. Marjorie stepped inside and pulled the door shut with a soft click. She moved to the chair with careful steps, as though navigating a room full of broken glass.

“I need to clarify a few details from your statement,” Ruth said, keeping her voice level. “For the police report. Routine procedure, nothing more.”

Marjorie’s eyes flickered. “Of course. Anything to help.”

Ruth opened the folder on her desk, revealing the fault log page she had stolen from the technical office. The stolen page sat beneath her fingertips, the unlisted extension number circled in red pencil. She had discovered it only yesterday, tracing the anomaly through hours of cross-referencing. No one outside the technical staff should have known it existed.

“Let’s go back to the night in question,” Ruth said. “You stated you overheard a call while working the late shift. A trunk call that struck you as unusual.”

Marjorie nodded, her hands folded in her lap. “That’s right. Bad business, I called it. The tone of it.”

“Can you tell me exactly where you were when you heard this call?”

“In the operators’ room, near the east bank of boards. I was covering for Irene, who’d gone on break.” Marjorie’s voice carried the practiced rhythm of a woman who had repeated this story before. “The line was open, and I caught fragments. A man’s voice, clipped. Professional. Speaking to someone about arrangements.”

Ruth watched her carefully. “And you didn’t recognize the voice?”

“I told the police, I didn’t. Just another trunk call to my ear.” Marjorie’s fingers tightened around each other. “We handle dozens of them a night, Supervisor. They blur together.”

“But this one stood out.”

“The tone did. Urgent, but controlled. Like someone giving orders who expected them followed.” Marjorie glanced at the green-shaded lamp on Ruth’s desk, then back to her hands. “I mentioned it to you because it felt wrong. That’s all.”

Ruth let the silence stretch. The hum of the switchboards below filled the gap, a low vibration that seemed to pulse through the floorboards. She studied Marjorie’s face, searching for the telltale signs of deception she had learned to recognize over years of supervising operators. The slight tightening around the eyes. The careful control of breathing.

“You said the call came through the standard trunk line,” Ruth said slowly. “A routine connection.”

“That’s right.”

“But you also mentioned the caller seemed to know the exchange procedures. The routing patterns.”

Marjorie’s chin lifted slightly. “I know how it sounded. Like someone testing the system, maybe. Or exploiting it.”

Ruth tapped her finger against the ledger entry, just below the red circle. She had not yet mentioned the unlisted extension to anyone outside the technical office. The discovery was hers alone, a thread she had pulled from the fabric of falsified records. “Tell me more about what you heard. The specifics.”

“I’ve told you everything I remember.” Marjorie’s voice carried an edge now, defensiveness creeping in around the margins. “Fragments. A man’s voice. Something about timing and confirmation. I didn’t write it down.”

“You didn’t think to report it immediately?”

Marjorie’s face colored. “I mentioned it to you the next morning. That’s reporting, isn’t it?”

“After the fact. After the fault had already been logged and the record altered.”

The accusation hung in the air. Marjorie’s mouth opened, then closed again. Her throat worked as she swallowed.

“I don’t know anything about altered records, Supervisor. I’m an operator. I connect calls. I don’t touch the logs.”

Ruth leaned forward, letting the lamp light fall across the ledger. “I’m not accusing you of anything, Marjorie. I’m trying to understand the sequence of events. When exactly did you first become aware that something was wrong with the call routing that night?”

“When I heard the call itself. That’s when.”

“And you didn’t discuss it with anyone else on the floor?”

Marjorie’s eyes darted to the door, then back. “We talk, of course. The girls share observations. It’s how we stay sharp.”

“What exactly did you share?”

“Just that there was a strange call. Bad business, I said. Nothing specific.”

Ruth nodded slowly, making a note on her pad. The scratch of her pen filled the quiet. “So no one else on the floor would have known the details of what you heard.”

“I suppose not. Not from me, anyway.”

Ruth stopped writing. Her pen hovered above the page. “What do you mean, ‘not from you’?”

Marjorie’s fingers twisted in her lap. “I’m just saying, gossip travels. You know how it is. Someone overhears something, passes it along. By the time it reaches the third ear, it’s half fiction.”

“So the call details might have spread through gossip.”

“Possibly. Probably. That’s how the exchange works, Supervisor. We’re all connected, in more ways than one.”

Ruth let the observation settle. The constant hum from below seemed to grow louder, pressing against the walls. She looked down at the ledger again, at the circled extension number that had no business appearing in a public routing log.

“Tell me about the private booth,” she said.

Marjorie blinked. “What?”

“The private booth. The one near the supervisor’s station. You’ve used it before, I assume.”

“Of course. For sensitive calls. Client negotiations, that sort of thing.”

Ruth watched her carefully. “Were you using it that night?”

“No. I told you, I was at the east boards, covering for Irene.”

“So you never accessed the private booth extension.”

Marjorie’s brow furrowed. “Why would I? I was on the floor, not in the booth.”

Ruth tapped the ledger again, her finger resting on the circled number. “I never mentioned any extension, Marjorie. I asked about the private booth itself.”

The silence that followed was absolute. Even the hum of the exchange seemed to recede, leaving only the sound of Ruth’s own breathing. Marjorie’s face had gone pale, the color draining in a slow wave from her cheeks to her throat.

“I…” Marjorie’s voice faltered. “I must have misunderstood.”

“Misunderstood what? I asked about the booth. You answered about an extension.”

“I assumed you meant the private line. The one we use for executive calls.”

Ruth’s pen remained frozen above the page. “There is no private line for executive calls in that booth. It’s a standard connection point. The extension you referenced doesn’t exist in any public directory.”

Marjorie’s hands flew to her mouth. “I didn’t… I must have heard it somewhere.”

“Heard what? The extension number?”

“No, I mean…” Marjorie’s words tumbled over each other. “The girls talk. Someone must have mentioned it. A private line, a special connection. I thought everyone knew.”

Ruth felt the cold certainty settle in her chest. “No one knew, Marjorie. That extension was unlisted. Hidden. I discovered it myself yesterday, on a stolen page from Alec Dunbar’s fault log. So tell me: how did you know to reference it just now?”

Marjorie’s chair scraped against the floor as she pushed back. Her eyes were wide now, darting between Ruth and the door. “I made a mistake. A slip of the tongue. I don’t know anything about any hidden extension, I swear.”

“Then why did you say it?”

“I don’t know!” Marjorie’s voice rose, cracking on the last word. “I heard something, maybe. Gossip. Someone mentioned a private line in the booth, and I assumed that’s what you were asking about.”

Ruth stood, her hands flat on the desk. “Who mentioned it? Give me a name.”

“I can’t. I don’t remember. It’s all just chatter, bits and pieces.”

“You expect me to believe that a specific detail about an unlisted extension made its way to you through casual gossip?”

Marjorie’s shoulders hunched. She looked smaller now, diminished. “It’s the truth, Supervisor. I swear on my mother’s grave.”

Ruth moved around the desk, positioning herself between Marjorie and the door. “Let me tell you what I think happened. You knew about that extension because someone told you about it. Someone who had access to technical records. Someone who needed you to know what to listen for on the night of that call.”

Marjorie’s breath came in shallow gasps. “You’re wrong. I’m just an operator. I connect calls. That’s all.”

“Then why are you so frightened?”

“I’m not—“ Marjorie’s voice broke. She pressed a hand to her chest. “I don’t want trouble, Supervisor. I came to you in good faith. I reported what I heard.”

“You reported fragments. Conveniently vague fragments that kept you clear of any direct involvement.”

“Because I’m not involved!”

Ruth took another step forward. “Then explain how you knew about the extension.”

Marjorie’s face crumpled. For a moment, Ruth thought she might cry. Instead, the other woman straightened her spine and lifted her chin.

“I heard it from one of the other girls. That’s all I’ll say. I won’t name names.”

“You’re protecting someone.”

“I’m protecting myself. And the rest of the operators who don’t deserve to be dragged into whatever mess you’re investigating.”

Ruth studied her face, searching for cracks in the facade. Marjorie’s jaw was set, her eyes bright with unshed tears. Whatever else she might be hiding, her loyalty to the other operators seemed genuine.

“This isn’t over, Marjorie. I’ll need that name eventually.”

“You’ll have to compel me, then. I’ve said all I’m going to say.”

Marjorie stood abruptly, her chair scraping again against the floor. She moved toward the door, then stopped, her hand on the knob.

“I’m sorry, Supervisor. Truly. I didn’t mean to cause trouble. I thought I was helping.”

“Helping who?”

Marjorie didn’t answer. She pulled the door open and slipped through, letting it close behind her with a soft click.

Ruth stood alone in the office, the hum of the exchange filling the silence. She looked down at the ledger on her desk, at the circled extension number that had no business being known to an operator. Marjorie’s slip replayed in her mind. That extension in the private booth. A casual reference to information that should have been impossible for her to possess.

Ruth moved back behind her desk and sat heavily in her chair. Her teacup sat near the edge, the tea long gone cold. She picked it up, feeling the chill seep into her fingers.

Gossip, Marjorie had claimed. Bits and pieces passed among the operators. But Ruth knew how information traveled on the exchange floor. It flowed through networks of trust and obligation, carried by women who depended on each other for survival. A detail like an unlisted extension wouldn’t spread by accident. It would be planted deliberately, shared with purpose.

Who would have told Marjorie? And why?

Ruth set down the cup and pulled the fault log closer. The forgery in her own handwriting stared back at her, the familiar loops and curves that weren’t quite right. Someone had studied her penmanship, learned its rhythms. Someone with access to her records, her reports, her daily notations.

Alec Dunbar had that access. But Alec was too obvious, his resentment too raw. He wouldn’t have involved an operator like Marjorie. He worked alone, kept his secrets close.

Unless he hadn’t worked alone.

Ruth thought about the networks on the exchange floor. The alliances and rivalries that shifted like currents beneath the surface. Marjorie had claimed she was protecting herself, protecting the other operators. But which operators? And from what?

The missing trunk call to Terence Bolt still haunted the edges of the investigation. Ruth had traced its absence through the ledgers, following the gap where a record should have been. Someone had erased that call, scrubbed it from existence. And they had used Ruth’s own handwriting to cover their tracks.

Violet Sykes had admitted to stealing from the tea fund, a petty crime that paled beside the forgery. But Violet’s desperation had felt real, her fear genuine. Ruth had believed her when she denied creating the forged page. Someone else had the skill, the opportunity.

Marjorie’s slip about the extension suggested she knew more than she claimed. But knowledge wasn’t the same as guilt. She could have overheard something, caught a fragment of conversation not meant for her ears. Or she could have been part of it from the beginning.

Back at her supervisor’s office desk, Ruth’s finger traced the edge of the ledger. The green lamp cast a circle of light across the page, illuminating the careful forgery. She had stolen this page to keep it from being destroyed, but she still didn’t know what it truly meant.

The knock on her door made her jump.

“Supervisor? Are you there?”

It was one of the night operators, her voice muffled through the wood.

Ruth gathered the papers into a folder and slid them into her drawer. “One moment.”

She stood, smoothing her skirt, and moved to the door. The exchange floor stretched beyond, rows of switchboards humming with activity. Operators sat at their stations, cables in hand, connecting voices across the city and beyond.

The night operator stood in the corridor, her face pale. “There’s been another incident, Supervisor. A call came through, and the line went dead. The operator said she heard something strange before the connection broke.”

“What kind of something?”

“Voices. Two of them, arguing. And then nothing.”

Ruth’s chest tightened. “Which line?”

“The private booth extension. The one near your station.”

The same extension Marjorie had referenced. The one that shouldn’t have been active at all.

“I’ll handle it,” Ruth said. “Return to your post.”

The operator nodded and hurried away. Ruth stood in the corridor, listening to the hum of the exchange. The sound that had always seemed so steady, so reliable, now felt uncertain. A facade covering secrets she was only beginning to uncover.

She thought of Marjorie’s face as she fled the office, the panic in her eyes. The loyalty she claimed to feel for the other operators. The name she refused to give.

Gossip as evidence. Information passed between women who trusted each other. A network hidden in plain sight.

Ruth walked toward the private booth, her heels clicking against the floor. The door stood slightly ajar, the light inside switched off. She pushed it open and stepped into the cramped space.

The telephone sat on a small shelf, its receiver in place. But the connection light flickered, a weak pulse of green that shouldn’t have been there. Someone had used this line recently, within the last hour.

Ruth picked up the receiver and held it to her ear. Static crackled, then cleared. In the background, she could hear the faint echo of another connection, a line that hadn’t fully disconnected.

Two voices. Arguing. That was what the operator had said.

Ruth strained to listen, but the echo faded, leaving only the empty hum of an open line. She replaced the receiver and stared at the telephone. Someone had made a call from this booth, using an extension that didn’t officially exist.

Marjorie had known about this extension. Had she used it? Or had she merely heard about it from someone else?

Ruth stepped out of the booth and closed the door behind her. The exchange floor stretched before her, a maze of switchboards and cables, of women working in the half-light. Any one of them could be hiding something. Any one of them could be part of the network that had forged her handwriting and erased a trunk call.

She returned to her office and sat at her desk. The cold teacup still sat near the edge. She picked it up again, feeling its weight.

Marjorie’s knowledge could have come from gossip. Women talking in the break room, sharing fragments over tea. But the specificity of her slip suggested something more deliberate. Someone had told her about the extension, had trusted her with that information.

Or someone had used her.

Ruth set down the cup and opened her drawer. The stolen fault log lay inside, its secrets still unreadable. She needed more information, more evidence. She needed to trace the network, to find the source of the forgery.

The technical archive held the original records, the ones that hadn’t been altered. If she could access them, she might find the missing trunk call, the one to Terence Bolt that had been scrubbed from existence.

Ruth stood and gathered her things. The archive was in the basement, a locked room that required supervisor clearance. She had the key, had used it before for routine audits.

But this wouldn’t be routine. This would be a descent into the hidden heart of the exchange, where records lived and died at the whim of whoever controlled them.

She thought of Violet’s theft, of Alec’s silent threats, of Marjorie’s panicked flight. Each piece of the puzzle pointed in a different direction. Each thread led to another knot.

Ruth opened her office door and stepped into the corridor. The hum of the exchange followed her, a constant reminder of the work that never stopped. Below her feet, the archive waited, dark and silent.

She walked toward the stairs, her mind turning over the question that had haunted her since the beginning. Who had forged the ledger page? And what fatal call were they trying to erase?

The answer lay somewhere in the records, buried beneath layers of falsification. And Ruth would find it, no matter what it cost.

She descended the stairs, her hand trailing along the wall. The fluorescent lights flickered, casting shadows that shifted with each step. The basement door loomed ahead, its lock gleaming in the artificial light.

Ruth pulled the key from her pocket and slid it into the mechanism. The tumblers clicked, and the door swung open, revealing the darkness beyond.

She stepped inside, letting the door close behind her. The archive stretched before her, rows of shelves reaching into the shadows. The air smelled of dust and paper, of secrets preserved in silence.

Ruth found the switch and flipped it. The overhead lights buzzed to life, illuminating the space in harsh white. She moved between the shelves, her eyes scanning the labels.

Fault logs. Call records. Routing sheets. Everything the exchange had recorded in the past decade, preserved for audit and review.

She found the section she needed and began to pull files, her hands working quickly. The original fault log for the night in question should be here, untouched by forgery. If she could find it, she could compare it to the stolen page and see what had been changed.

The files were arranged by date, each one labeled in careful script. Ruth found the folder for the relevant week and pulled it from the shelf.

But when she opened it, she found only blank pages.

Someone had been here before her. Someone had removed the original records and replaced them with nothing.

Ruth stared at the empty folder, her breath caught in her throat. The forgery wasn’t just a single page. It was a systematic erasure, a scrubbing of history that reached deeper than she had imagined.

She closed the folder and returned it to the shelf. Her hands were steady, but her mind raced. Whoever had done this had access to the archive, had known exactly what to remove.

A supervisor. A technician. Someone with clearance and knowledge.

Someone who knew her handwriting well enough to mimic it.

Ruth turned and walked back toward the door. The hum of the exchange seemed distant here, muffled by layers of concrete and steel. But she could still feel it, a vibration in the soles of her feet.

She stepped out of the archive and locked the door behind her. The corridor stretched ahead, empty and silent.

Ruth thought of Marjorie’s face, the panic in her eyes. The slip about the extension that had revealed more than she intended.

Gossip as evidence. Or something more deliberate.

Ruth walked back up the stairs, her mind turning over possibilities. Marjorie had claimed she was protecting herself, protecting the other operators. But protection could mean many things. It could mean silence, complicity, or fear.

It could mean that Marjorie had been in the room when the forgery was planned.

Ruth reached the ground floor and paused. The exchange hummed around her, a living thing composed of voices and cables and secrets.

She would find the truth. She had to.

The alternative was to let the forgery stand, to let whoever had erased that trunk call win.

And Ruth Calloway had never been one to surrender.

She turned toward the main hall—and stopped.

Her hand remained on the newel post, knuckles whitening. The realization had been there all along, waiting. Marjorie had said “that extension in the private booth” with the ease of long familiarity. Not “an extension” or “some private line.” That extension. Specific. Definite.

As if she’d known it existed long before Ruth had ever pulled the fault log.

Ruth’s pulse quickened. She forced her hand to release the railing, made herself walk toward the supervisor’s desk with measured steps. Inside, her thoughts raced.

Marjorie had needed to deflect. The panic in her eyes hadn’t been surprise—it had been the fear of saying too much. She’d overplayed her hand, tried to steer Ruth away from something by offering just enough to seem cooperative.

But cooperation from someone frightened was never innocent.

Ruth reached her desk and opened the drawer. The stolen fault log page lay where she’d hidden it, its edges soft from handling. She traced the line where the trunk call had been recorded, then erased. Someone had wanted this call buried. Someone with access, with technical skill, with the authority to alter records without question.

Alec Dunbar had that access. But so did others.

She thought of Marjorie fleeing the office, the way she’d clutched her cardigan, the excuse tumbling out—“I must check my board”—when no board needed checking at this hour.

Ruth slipped the page into her bag. The Technical Archive stayed open late on Thursdays. If she could match the routing sequences to the personnel logs, she might find who else had been in the room that night.

She walked toward the exit, her heels clicking against the floor.

Behind her, the exchange hummed on, indifferent.
