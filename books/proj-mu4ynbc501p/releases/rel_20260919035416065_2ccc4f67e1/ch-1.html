Chapter 1

The Tampered Ledger

────────────────────────────────────────

The ledger drawer slid open with a sound like a tomb.

Ruth Calloway stood in the vault’s yellow light, her hand still on the iron pull, and listened to the silence that followed. The vault was a narrow concrete cell at the back of the Manchester Central Telephone Exchange, shelved with ledgers bound in cloth and leather, each one spanning a month of trunk calls, local connections, line faults, and supervisor’s notes. The air smelled of dust and old paper and the particular metallic cold of a room that never saw sunlight.

She reached for the February ledger. It sat between January and March on the second shelf, spine facing outward, the embossed letters faded to a whisper. Her fingers closed around the cloth cover. It was heavier than she expected, or perhaps the hour made everything heavier. Two in the morning had a way of pressing down on ordinary objects until they acquired the density of evidence.

The police request had come through at half past one. A telephone request, routed through the night supervisor’s line, from a detective whose name she had written on the duty board in red chalk: Det. Sgt. Hadley, City Police, requesting the call ledger for the night of February 11th, the night Councillor Gerald Pryce had been found dead in his study. A routine request. The kind that came perhaps twice a year, when a call placed through the exchange became a thread in some larger fabric of inquiry. Ruth had handled a dozen of them. You pulled the ledger, you confirmed the relevant pages, you noted the request in the incident book, and you made the volume available for collection in the morning. Procedure. Clean and simple as a ruled line.

She carried the ledger out of the vault, locking the door behind her with the brass key she kept on a chain at her belt. The corridor back to her office ran along the rear wall of the switchboard hall, where the six night operators sat at their positions under the low-wattage ceiling lights, backs curved, headsets on, hands moving with the automatic precision of women who had connected ten thousand calls and could connect ten thousand more without thinking. Through the glass partition that separated the corridor from the hall, Ruth could see the amber glow of the switchboard lamps, the cords hanging like dropped threads, the constant small motions of plugs being lifted and replaced.

She paused at the duty board. The night’s assignments were chalked there in her own handwriting, each operator assigned to her trunk group or local panel. At the bottom, she had noted the police request in red. Her handwriting on the board was the same handwriting that filled the ledger, the same handwriting that signed the supervisor’s column at the foot of every page: R. Calloway, Night Supervisor.

A door opened behind her. Marjorie Finch stepped out of the tea cupboard with a cup in one hand and a biscuit in the other, her headset pushed back from her dark hair. She was a slight woman, quick-fingered, the kind who noticed everything and said half of it.

“Ruth,” Marjorie said, glancing at the ledger under Ruth’s arm. “Is that the police request, then?”

“It is.”

“Routine, is it?” Marjorie’s eyes lingered on the ledger’s spine, then on Ruth’s face. She bit the biscuit. “Only it’s the second time they’ve asked for something this week. First the fault sheets, now the whole ledger.”

“The ledger doesn’t lie, Marge. Only people do.” Ruth shifted the ledger’s weight against her hip. “It’s a straightforward retrieval. They want the record for the eleventh, I’ll have it ready for morning collection.”

“Oh, I’m not one to talk, but if you were to ask me…” Marjorie trailed off, sipping her tea. Her gaze flicked to the red chalk note on the duty board, then back. “It’s just that when they start pulling ledgers out of the vault at two in the morning, it doesn’t feel routine. That’s the long and short of it.”

Ruth held the other woman’s look for a moment. Marjorie was right, of course. A trunk call at that hour was not a coincidence. It was a footprint. But Ruth had no intention of saying so.

“Get back on your board, Marge. Line four was queuing when I came through.”

Marjorie gave a small, knowing nod and retreated through the door, and Ruth heard her settle back into the rhythm of the switchboard hall, the click of plugs, the murmur of connection.

In her office, Ruth set the ledger on the desk beneath the green-shaded lamp and opened it to February 11th. The pages were ruled in faint blue lines, each row a minute, each column a line or trunk group. The entries were written in the operators’ various hands, some round and careful, some hurried and slanting. Ruth’s own entries as supervisor appeared in the margins and at the foot of each page, initialled where she had verified a fault, countersigned where she had approved a trunk release. She knew these pages. She had written on them.

She turned to the hour in question. The police had specified the period between 1: 00 AM and 2: 00 AM on the twelfth, the window during which Pryce was believed to have died. Ruth found the page. The entries were there, in the proper hands, the times running down the left margin in the sequence she expected. She read them. Trunk calls to London, a local to a Salford number, two incoming trunk calls from Leeds, a fault noted on line six at 1: 22. All correct. All accounted for.

Her finger moved down the column. Then it paused.

She felt it before she saw it. A faint difference in the paper under her fingertip, a smoothness where there should have been the slight tooth of the original page. She pressed gently. The paper gave, just barely, with the particular softness of a surface that had been erased and rewritten. Not chemically erased. Mechanically, with a rubber, the kind of careful rubbing that smoothed the fibres flat without tearing them.

She bent closer under the lamp. The green light pooled on the page. The entries for the crucial hour were written in ink that matched the original. The handwriting was hers. Not approximately hers. Exactly hers. The slight forward lean, the way her ‘t’ crossed high, the particular shape of her ampersand. She had supervised this page. She had signed it at the bottom, in the supervisor’s column, with the signature she used on every page: R. Calloway, the ‘C’ beginning with its characteristic loop.

The loop.

Ruth’s breath stopped.

She stared at the ‘C’ in Calloway. The ink was identical. The stroke weight was identical. The pen had been held at the same angle she held hers, with the same pressure on the downstroke. But the loop did not close. Her ‘C’ always closed. It was a habit she had carried since grammar school, a defect her penmanship teacher had tried to correct and failed. The loop of the ‘C’ swept upward and around, and the tail curved back to meet the top of the stroke, forming a small, almost imperceptible seal. It was a scar in her handwriting, a flaw so intimate that no one who had not studied her signature line by line would know it existed.

The loop on this page failed to close. The tail curved back and stopped, a hair’s breadth short of the top stroke. The gap was invisible to a casual reader. Under the lamp, with her face inches from the paper, Ruth could see the tiny crescent of white where the ink should have met itself.

Someone had forged her handwriting. Someone had erased the original entries for the crucial hour, rewritten them in a perfect imitation of her hand, and signed her name with a forgery so exact that only this one, private, intimate failure gave it away.

The Scar.

She sat back. The hum of the exchange pressed against the frosted glass of her office door. The green lamp made a small world of her desk, the ledger, her hands lying flat on the open page. She tasted the stale cold tea at the back of her throat.

Her mind moved through the implications the way it moved through a switchboard: methodically, connection by connection. If the page had been rewritten, then the original entries for that hour had contained something someone needed to erase. She turned back to the entries as they now stood. Trunk calls to London. A local to Salford. Two incoming from Leeds. A fault on line six.

She knew the exchange’s trunk groups. She knew which lines ran where, which operators handled which groups, and she knew, because she had been on duty that night, that the pattern of calls was wrong. Not wrong in an obvious way. Wrong in the way a familiar room looks wrong when one piece of furniture has been moved three inches to the left.

The local call to Salford. She stared at it. The number was written in her forged hand: a Salford exchange prefix she did not recognise as one of the night’s regular callers. She pulled the exchange directory from the shelf behind her desk and looked it up. The number belonged to a residential address in Pendleton. She did not know who lived there. But the prefix, she realized, was the same prefix listed in the personnel file for Terence Bolt, the councillor’s driver.

A call had been placed from the exchange to Bolt’s home between 1: 15 and 1: 40. That call was not in the ledger now. The forged entries had replaced it with the Salford number written as a local call, stripped of its trunk routing, its timing shifted to fill the gap.

Someone had called Terence Bolt from the Manchester Central Telephone Exchange in the hour Councillor Pryce died, and someone had then erased that call and rewritten the page in Ruth’s handwriting to hide the fact that it had ever been placed.

She closed her eyes. The exchange hummed. Somewhere in the hall, a plug clicked into a jack. A voice murmured a number. The ordinary sounds of the night shift, the sounds she had trusted for eleven years.

She could report this. She could telephone the detective back, tell him the ledger had been tampered with, hand the whole matter to the police and let them pull her operators off the board one by one for questioning. She could stand aside and let the system she upheld do its work.

She thought of Marjorie’s face at the duty board. The quick eyes, the knowing look. She thought of the six operators on the night shift, women who depended on these jobs, women who had no union, no savings, no margin for suspicion. She thought of what the police would assume when they learned the supervisor’s own handwriting had been used to forge a page in a murder inquiry. They would not look for the gap in the loop. They would look at Ruth.

She opened her eyes. She noted the time of the missing call on a slip of scrap paper and folded it into her pocket. Then she closed the ledger, locked it in her desk drawer, and sat in the green light with the taste of stale tea in her mouth and the hum of the exchange pressing against the frosted glass of her office door, and she understood that the world she had built, the orderly, sealed, humming world of the night shift, was no longer what she had believed it to be. The system she upheld had been violated from within. The handwriting was hers, but she had not written it.

And somewhere in the building, in the hours between midnight and six, someone was still keeping a secret that had been written in her hand.

She would find them herself.
