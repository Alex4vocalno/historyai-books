Chapter 22

Catching Terence in His Own Voice

────────────────────────────────────────

Ruth’s fingers traced the cold brass of the test jack, waiting for the footsteps that would either vindicate or ruin her. The maintenance bay hummed with the low frequency charge of dormant relay racks, the air thick with ozone and machine oil. Somewhere beyond the door, Marjorie was making the call that would bring Terence Bolt into this room. If he came. If he believed the story about the security test on the new monitoring line. If he did not sense the trap waiting in the shadows of the equipment racks.

Marjorie had been terrified but resolute when she dialled the number. Ruth had watched from the gallery as the operator’s hand trembled only slightly, her voice steady as she delivered the scripted message. A routine security check. The chief engineer wanted Terence to assist with testing the new line. Could he come to the exchange within the hour? Marjorie had ended the call and looked up at Ruth, her face pale in the green glow of the switchboard lamps. He said yes. He was on his way.

That had been forty minutes ago.

Ruth adjusted the recording equipment one final time, checking that the spools were properly threaded and the pickup microphone positioned to catch every word spoken in the bay. The unlisted extension they had discovered, the one Marjorie had referred to in her panicked slip, now sat connected to the test jack through a hidden bypass. Everything was in place. The fault log page Ruth had stolen lay folded in her skirt pocket, its forged entries waiting to be matched against the sequence she was about to witness.

The door handle turned.

Ruth’s pulse spiked, but her hands remained steady on the equipment housing. She stepped back from the workbench, arranging herself in the posture of a supervisor conducting a routine technical verification. Marjorie materialised at her elbow, having slipped in through the maintenance corridor moments before. The operator’s breathing was shallow, her eyes fixed on the door.

Terence Bolt entered the maintenance bay with the unhurried confidence of a man who believed he belonged wherever he chose to be. He wore his usual work clothes, the cap tucked under one arm, his expression one of professional readiness. His gaze swept the room, taking in Ruth, Marjorie, the equipment racks, the workbench with its array of testing tools. He showed no sign of alarm.

“Supervisor Calloway,” he said, his voice carrying the flat courtesy of a subordinate addressing a superior. “Marjorie said you needed assistance with a line test.”

“Thank you for coming on short notice, Terence.” Ruth kept her tone businesslike. “We’ve installed a new monitoring extension for the trunk lines, and I need someone with technical experience to verify the connection sequence. The chief engineer wants documentation that the line is secure before we bring it online.”

Terence nodded slowly, his eyes moving to the test jack on the workbench. “A monitoring extension. For which trunk route?”

“The northern corridor. The one that services the council exchanges.” Ruth watched his face carefully, looking for any flicker of recognition. “Given the sensitivity of those calls, we need to ensure the line cannot be compromised.”

“Of course.” Terence set his cap on the workbench and rolled up his sleeves with the practiced motion of a man preparing for routine work. “What sequence do you need me to run?”

Ruth gestured to the test panel. “We’ll simulate an incoming trunk call routed through the monitoring extension. You’ll insert the test plug, verify the connection, then disconnect and re-insert to confirm the line stabilises properly. Standard fault-check procedure.”

“Standard procedure,” Terence repeated. He moved to the workbench, his broad shoulders blocking Ruth’s view of the panel. “Shall I begin?”

“Please. I’ll document each step for the record.” Ruth picked up a clipboard, though her real documentation was the recording spooling silently in the cabinet behind her. “Marjorie will monitor the extension output.”

Marjorie stepped to the secondary panel, her hand hovering over the dials. Ruth had briefed her on exactly what to watch for, the specific anomaly that would reveal whether Terence knew more than he should about this line.

Terence lifted the test plug, its brass tip catching the dim emergency lighting. He examined it with a technician’s eye, turning it between his fingers. Ruth’s chest tightened. This was the moment. The test sequence she had described was deliberately wrong, a subtle error in the standard procedure that any genuine technician would question. But the forged fault log contained that same error, logged with precision. If Terence made the correction without being prompted, he would prove he had written that entry.

“Ready when you are,” Ruth said.

Terence aligned the plug with the jack. His finger hovered over the opening, and Ruth found herself holding her breath. He inserted the plug with a decisive click, waited the standard three-count for the connection to stabilise, then began to pull it out.

Then he stopped.

His hand remained frozen on the plug, his head tilting slightly as though listening to something only he could hear. The silence in the maintenance bay stretched taut as a wire.

“Something wrong?” Ruth asked, keeping her voice neutral.

Terence pulled the plug free and turned to face her. His expression had shifted, the professional mask slipping to reveal something harder underneath. “The sequence you described. Disconnect and re-insert to confirm stabilisation.”

“That’s correct.”

“That’s not standard procedure for a monitoring line test.” Terence’s voice remained level, but an edge had entered it. “Standard procedure is to hold the connection for five seconds, then disconnect once. Re-insertion is only required for fault isolation, not routine verification.”

Ruth felt the trap closing, but not in the direction she had planned. “The chief engineer specified this sequence.”

“Did he.” Terence’s gaze moved from Ruth to Marjorie, then to the equipment cabinet where the recorder hummed. His eyes narrowed. “Or did you specify it, Supervisor Calloway?”

Marjorie made a small sound of distress. Ruth stepped forward, placing herself between Terence and the door. “What makes you ask that, Terence?”

“Because I’ve worked exchanges before. I know fault procedures.” Terence set the plug down on the workbench with careful deliberation. “And because I know what you’ve been doing, Ruth. The questions you’ve been asking. The records you’ve been pulling. You think I haven’t noticed?”

Ruth’s throat tightened, but she forced herself to hold his gaze. “I don’t know what you mean.”

“The ledger. The fault log.” Terence’s voice dropped, the flat courtesy replaced by something cold. “You’ve been looking for something. And now you’ve brought me here, to a room with recording equipment, to run a test sequence that matches an entry you think I wrote.”

The silence that followed was deafening. Ruth could hear her own heartbeat, the hum of the relay racks, Marjorie’s rapid breathing. The trap had sprung true—Terence had reached for the plug on the workbench before he caught himself, his hand stopping mid-motion, and she saw the exact moment recognition flooded his face.

“You’re very clever,” Terence said, his tone shifting to something intimate and threatening. “But not clever enough. You thought I’d just follow your script, make the correction, give you your proof. Did you really think I wouldn’t recognise my own procedure?”

Ruth’s mind raced. The confidence in his voice, the certainty—it could only come from one place. He knew she had no witnesses, no official backing. This was her private investigation, conducted without authorisation, and if he exposed her first, she would be the one facing disciplinary action.

“What are you saying, Terence?” Ruth kept her voice steady despite the tremor in her chest.

“I’m saying we can help each other.” Terence moved closer, his bulk filling the space between her and the door. “You stop this investigation. You return whatever records you’ve taken. And I forget that the night supervisor was conducting unauthorised surveillance on exchange personnel.”

“And if I don’t?”

Terence smiled, a thin expression that did not reach his eyes. “Then I report you. Theft of exchange property. Improper investigation. Harassment of staff. How long do you think you’d keep your position?”

Ruth glanced at Marjorie, who stood frozen at the secondary panel. The operator’s eyes were wide with fear, but something else flickered there too. A question. A choice.

“Terence.” Ruth turned back to face him, squaring her shoulders. “You’re right. I did bring you here to trap you. And you’re right that I don’t have official authorisation for this investigation. But there’s something you haven’t considered.”

“What’s that?”

“The recording equipment is still running.” Ruth gestured to the cabinet behind her. “Every word you’ve just said, every threat, every admission that you recognised the procedure, it’s all on that spool. Including your offer to help me cover it up.”

Terence’s smile vanished. He lunged for the cabinet.

“Marjorie, now!” Ruth shouted.

Marjorie’s hand came down on the bypass switch, rerouting the recording through a secondary spool hidden in the maintenance corridor. At the same moment, Ruth grabbed Terence’s arm, trying to pull him back from the equipment. He shook her off with a violent twist, sending her stumbling against the workbench. Tools clattered to the floor, the noise echoing in the confined space.

“You think a recording will save you?” Terence’s voice had lost all pretence of civility. He yanked open the cabinet door, reaching for the primary spool. “This is exchange property. I’ll take it, and who’s going to believe you? A supervisor who stole records, who set up a false test, who entrapped a colleague on private equipment?”

Ruth scrambled to her feet, her hip striking the corner of the workbench. Pain flared through the old scar, a sharp reminder of another fall, another moment when the ground had rushed up to meet her. She had been a child then, the bicycle skidding on wet pavement, the shame of the wound she had hidden from her parents. The secret she had kept for decades.

“You already know about secrets, don’t you, Terence?” Ruth’s voice came out breathless but hard. “The ones people keep. The ones they’ll do anything to protect.”

Terence paused, his hand on the spool. “What are you talking about?”

“The fault log entry. The one you wrote, in my handwriting.” Ruth pressed on, though every instinct screamed at her to stop. “You didn’t just forge a technical record. You forged it to cover something else. A call. A specific call that went through this exchange on a night you were here after hours.”

Terence’s face went still. The silence stretched between them, broken only by the hum of the equipment and the distant sounds of the exchange floor above.

“How do you know about that?” His voice was barely audible.

“I know because Marjorie heard it.” Ruth gestured to the operator, who stood rigid by the secondary panel. “She heard the call you made. The one you erased from the ledger. The one that connects to a trunk route that shouldn’t exist.”

Terence’s gaze moved to Marjorie, and something shifted in his expression. The calculation gave way to a dawning realisation, a reassessment of the pieces on the board. “She told you.”

“She told me enough.” Ruth stepped forward, ignoring the tremor in her legs. “A call to a councillor. Made on an unlisted extension. A call about blackmail.”

Terence’s hand dropped from the spool. He turned to face Ruth fully, his posture changing from aggression to something more complex. A man caught, but not yet defeated. A man weighing his options.

“You don’t understand what you’re dealing with,” he said quietly.

“Then explain it to me.”

Terence was silent for a long moment. His eyes moved from Ruth to Marjorie to the equipment, calculating something that Ruth could not read. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its edge, replaced by a weary resignation.

“The call was made on behalf of someone else. Someone powerful. I was just the instrument.” He straightened his uniform, smoothing the fabric with automatic precision. “A councillor was being pressured. Threatened with exposure if he didn’t vote a certain way on a development contract. The call was to deliver that threat.”

“Blackmail,” Ruth said flatly.

“Political negotiation, if you want the polite term.” Terence’s mouth twisted. “I made the call from the unlisted extension because it couldn’t be traced. Then I altered the fault log to show an outage during that period, so there’d be no record of any call going through. Standard procedure, as I’m sure you’ve worked out.”

“And the ledger page? The one in my handwriting?”

Terence met her gaze. “I needed a supervisor’s authorisation for the fault log entry. I’d seen your handwriting often enough, studied the forms you signed. It wasn’t difficult.”

Ruth felt the weight of the confession settling over her, the answer to weeks of questions and sleepless nights. The forgery was not random. It was not a technical error or a bureaucratic mistake. It was a deliberate erasure, a crime committed to cover another crime, committed by a man she had worked alongside for years.

“Who asked you to make that call, Terence?” Ruth’s voice was steady now, the fear replaced by cold purpose. “Who’s the blackmailer?”

Terence shook his head slowly. “I can’t tell you that.”

“You’ve already confessed to forgery and obstruction. What more have you got to lose?”

“More than you know.” Terence’s eyes held something that might have been fear, or might have been something darker. “The people involved don’t leave loose ends. If I name them, I’m not just losing my job. I’m losing everything.”

“Then let the law decide how to handle them.”

“The law?” Terence laughed, a hollow sound. “Half the council is in their pocket. The police take orders from people who take orders from them. There is no law for this, Ruth. There’s only survival.”

Ruth heard footsteps in the corridor outside. The officers she had arranged, waiting for her signal. She had known this might happen, known she might need backup if Terence became violent. She had not expected him to surrender so easily, to confess so readily. A man who gave up this quickly was either truly broken, or he was playing for time.

“The recording has your confession,” Ruth said. “Whatever happens next, that evidence exists. You can’t erase it now.”

Terence glanced at the cabinet, then back at Ruth. For a moment, she thought he might try again for the spool, might try to fight his way out. Instead, he stepped back from the workbench, raising his hands in a gesture of surrender.

“I know when I’m beaten.” His voice was flat, the professional mask sliding back into place. “Do what you have to do.”

Ruth moved to the door and opened it. Two uniformed officers stood in the corridor, their expressions alert. She had briefed them earlier, explained that she might need assistance with a suspect in a forgery case. She had not told them the full scope of what she suspected, only that a technician had been altering records and might resist arrest.

“Terence Bolt,” Ruth said, her voice carrying the formal weight of her authority. “You’re detained on suspicion of falsifying official exchange records and obstructing a police investigation.”

The officers entered the bay, flanking Terence with practiced efficiency. He did not resist as they secured his wrists, his face impassive, his eyes fixed on some middle distance. Ruth watched, the recording spool still turning in the cabinet behind her, the weight of what she had done settling onto her shoulders.

“Supervisor Calloway.” One of the officers addressed her, a man with graying temples and a careful expression. “We’ll need to take him to the station for processing. You’ll need to come as well, to give your statement.”

“Of course.” Ruth glanced at Marjorie, who still stood by the secondary panel, her face pale but composed. “Marjorie, stay here. Secure the equipment. Don’t let anyone into this bay until I return.”

Marjorie nodded, her voice a whisper. “Be careful.”

Ruth followed the officers and their prisoner into the corridor, the fluorescent lights casting harsh shadows on the walls. Terence walked between his captors without speaking, his posture erect, his gaze forward. A man resigned to his fate, or a man calculating his next move.

At the end of the corridor, Ruth paused, looking back at the maintenance bay door. Inside that room lay the evidence of her investigation, the recording that would convict Terence, and the operator who had helped her set the trap. Marjorie, who had overheard a blackmail call and said nothing. Marjorie, who had been too afraid to speak, too afraid of losing her job, too afraid of what might happen if the powerful people Terence served learned what she knew.

Ruth understood that fear now. She felt it herself, sitting heavy in her chest. The people Terence had protected were still out there, still powerful, still dangerous. A confession from a technician was not the same as justice. A forged ledger was not the same as accountability.

But it was a beginning.

The officers led Terence through the exchange floor, past the night operators who watched with curious eyes, past the switchboards that hummed with the constant traffic of the city’s conversations. Ruth followed, her mind already turning to the statement she would give, the evidence she would present, the steps she would need to take to protect herself and Marjorie from whatever came next.

Terence paused at the main entrance, turning to face her one last time. The officers allowed the moment, their grip on his arms loosening slightly.

“You think this ends here,” Terence said quietly, his voice pitched for her ears alone. “But you’ve only just begun. They’ll come for that recording. They’ll come for the girl. And when they do, you’ll wish you’d taken my offer.”

Ruth met his gaze without flinching. “I’ll take my chances.”

Terence smiled, a thin expression that held no warmth. “Everyone does, until they don’t get any more chances to take.”

The officers led him out into the night, the heavy door closing behind them with a dull thud. Ruth stood in the entrance, the exchange humming around her, the weight of the recorded confession heavy in her thoughts. She had done what she set out to do. She had caught the forger, extracted a confession, secured the evidence. The ledger page in her pocket, the one with her forged handwriting, was no longer a threat hanging over her head. It was proof of a crime, a piece of a larger puzzle she had only begun to understand.

But Terence’s words echoed in her mind, settling into the hollow space behind her ribs. They’ll come for that recording. They’ll come for the girl.

Ruth turned back toward the maintenance bay, her steps measured, her posture erect. She was a supervisor, and she had a job to do. The night shift was not over. The exchange still hummed with its cargo of voices and secrets, the wires carrying conversations she would never hear, connections she would never see. Somewhere in that web of lines and switches, the truth about the larger conspiracy waited, patient and dangerous.

She had caught one man. But the web was bigger than Terence Bolt, and it was still waiting, still watching, still ready to close around anyone who got too close.

Ruth’s hand went to her pocket, feeling the folded page beneath her fingers. The scar on her hip throbbed with the memory of the fall, the old wound reminding her that some marks never fully healed. She had survived this long by being careful, by being methodical, by following the procedures that kept the exchange running and the records straight.

Tonight, she had broken every rule she knew.

Tomorrow, she would have to decide whether it had been worth it.

The maintenance bay door stood ahead of her, Marjorie waiting inside, the recording spool still turning in its cabinet. Ruth straightened her shoulders, smoothed her skirt, and walked forward to meet whatever came next.
