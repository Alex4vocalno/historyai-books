Chapter 2

A Simple Question

────────────────────────────────────────

Ruth’s fingers were still cold from the ledger’s binding as she waited by the coat hooks. The chill had settled into the joints of her hands, that particular stiffness that came from gripping something too tightly for too long. She pressed her palms flat against her skirt to warm them. The fabric was slightly damp. Everything in the locker room was slightly damp. The wool coats on their pegs, the metal of the lockers, even the air itself, which sat heavy with the smell of floor polish and wet gabardine.

The page was still behind her eyes. She could not stop seeing it. The loops of her own handwriting, familiar as her breathing, marching across the entry lines in perfect order. Except the signature. The tail of the R in her name curved wrong. It hooked left where she always hooked right, and the difference was so small that anyone else would have missed it. She had not missed it. The handwriting was hers, but she had not written it. Someone had sat at her desk, taken her pen, and reproduced her hand well enough to fool a police inspector. Well enough to almost fool her. The system she upheld, the careful architecture of times and lines and signatures that made the exchange run, had been violated from within.

She had not reported it. That fact sat in her chest like a stone she had swallowed whole. The police had asked for the ledger. She had retrieved it. She had opened it. She had seen the forgery. And instead of picking up the telephone and calling the station, she had closed the ledger and put it back in the drawer and walked to the locker room to wait for Marjorie Finch.

Marjorie came in carrying her handbag and a folded copy of the Evening Chronicle, which she tucked under her arm as she began unbuttoning her cardigan. She moved with the unhurried confidence of a woman who believed the world operated on gossip and kindness in equal measure. Her hair was pinned neatly for the shift, though a single strand had escaped at the temple and curled against her cheek in the damp air.

“Marjorie,” Ruth said.

“Oh! You made me jump.” Marjorie hung her cardigan on her peg and patted it flat. “I didn’t expect to see you back here. I thought you’d be in the office with the, you know, the police business. Important business. Marge heard it was to do with the councilman. The one who passed.”

“It is to do with the councilman, yes.”

“Well.” Marjorie set her handbag on the bench and sat beside it. Her fingers found the hem of her uniform skirt and began smoothing it across her knees, a habitual motion, the kind that gave her hands something to do while her mind worked. “That’s the long and short of it, then. Sad business. Very sad.”

Ruth leaned against the locker behind her. The metal was cold through her blouse. She could feel the ridges of the ventilation slats pressing into her shoulder blade. “I need to ask you something, Marge. Routine. About ledger security.”

Marjorie’s hands stopped moving. The pause lasted perhaps half a second before they resumed, but Ruth caught it. The rhythm had broken and reformed, like a stitch dropped and picked up.

“Ledger security?” Marjorie tilted her head. Her expression was open, curious, perfectly pleasant. “Oh, I wouldn’t know about that. That’s your department, isn’t it? You and the logbooks. Marge just connects the calls.”

“You’re being modest. You notice more than you let on.”

“Well.” A small smile. The skirt-smoothing continued. “I’m not one to talk, but if you were to ask me, I’d say the system works fine. Always has. You run a tight ship, Ruth. Everyone says so.”

“I appreciate that.” Ruth kept her voice level. She had rehearsed this conversation in her head during the walk from the office, framing it as procedural concern, a supervisor reviewing protocols. The words needed to sound ordinary. “The thing is, I’m reviewing access protocols for the logbook cabinet. Who has keys, who’s been in the office during off hours. Standard review.”

“Of course. Standard.” Marjorie nodded, but her gaze had drifted to the window where the fog pressed grey against the glass. She was not looking at Ruth when she answered, which was unusual. Marjorie Finch made a point of looking at people when she spoke to them. It was part of her currency. “I wouldn’t know about keys. You have the only set, don’t you? For the cabinet?”

“I do.”

“Well, then.” Marjorie spread her hands. “There you are. If you have the only key, then Marge can’t help you. I wouldn’t know who else could get in.”

The locker room felt smaller than it had a moment ago. The damp wool smell was closer, thicker. Ruth shifted her weight against the locker and the metal rang dully, a sound like a muted bell. Somewhere in the exchange, a trunk call was connecting. She could hear the relay clicks through the wall, steady and purposeful. The building never stopped. Even at this hour, it breathed.

“There’s a spare key,” Ruth said. “In the engineer’s toolkit. For maintenance access.”

Marjorie’s hands went still again. This time the pause was longer. She looked down at her lap. When she spoke, her voice had dropped half a register.

“Oh. That key. Yes, I suppose there would be, wouldn’t there. For the engineers.”

“You’ve seen it used.”

“I wouldn’t say I’ve seen it used, exactly.” Marjorie reached up and tucked the loose strand of hair behind her ear. Her fingers were not steady. “I wouldn’t say that at all.”

Ruth waited. The relay clicks continued. Water dripped somewhere, a tap left running in the lavatory down the corridor.

“What would you say, Marge?”

Marjorie looked at the window again. The fog sat there like a wall. “Well, I shouldn’t say. It’s not my place, and Marge doesn’t like to cause trouble for anyone. But if you’re doing a review, and if it’s routine, then I suppose it wouldn’t hurt to mention.” She smoothed the skirt again, pressing the fabric flat with both palms. “Alec was here awfully late. That night. The night you’re asking about, I mean. The night of the councilman.”

Ruth did not move. She kept her shoulder against the locker and her hands at her sides. She could feel her fingernails pressing into her palms through the fabric of her skirt.

“Alec Dunbar.”

“Well, yes. I noticed because his toolkit was out. On the bench by the trunk rack. I came back from the lavatory around half eleven and there he was, crouched by the rack with his screwdrivers laid out. I thought it was odd because the trunk lines had been quiet all evening. No faults reported. Nothing in the fault log. So why he’d be servicing the rack at that hour, I couldn’t say.” Marjorie paused. “I didn’t ask. Marge minds her own business.”

“But you noticed.”

“I notice things.” Marjorie met her eyes then, and Ruth saw something she had not expected. Not fear, not guilt, but calculation. Marjorie Finch, for all her flutter and gossip, was choosing her words with surgical care. “A body notices, working nights. You see things. People forget you’re there. The operators are at their boards, the engineers are under the racks, and nobody looks at Marge because Marge is just connecting calls.” She smoothed the skirt one final time. “That’s the long and short of it.”

Ruth studied her. Marjorie held the gaze, which told Ruth more than the evasion had. Whatever else Marjorie Finch was, she had decided to give Ruth this much. The question was why.

“Thank you, Marge.”

“Routine, like you said.” Marjorie stood and picked up her handbag. “Marge hopes it was helpful.”

Ruth watched her leave. The door swung shut and the locker room was empty and cold and smelled of floor polish and damp wool and something else underneath, something metallic, the ghost of the ink she had handled. She stood there for a long moment, her back against the locker, her hands pressed flat.

Then she walked to the supervisor’s office.

The room was small and too warm. A single desk lamp threw a circle of yellow light across the blotter, leaving the corners in shadow. The exchange hummed through the walls, relays and trunk connections firing in their endless rhythm. Ruth sat in her chair and pulled the engineers’ access log from the shelf beside the desk.

The log was a green cloth-bound book, smaller than the call ledger. Engineers signed in and out by date, noting the equipment they serviced and the duration of their work. It was a maintenance record, nothing more. Nobody scrutinized it. Nobody had reason to.

Until now.

Ruth opened the log to the date in question. The page was filled with entries in various hands. The day shift engineers had signed in at seven, eight, quarter past eight. Routine work. Line testing, rack maintenance, a fault on the Salford junction. Their entries were brief and practical.

She found Alec Dunbar’s entry near the bottom of the page. The handwriting was cramped and precise, the letters small. The ink was blue-black, the same shade used in the call ledger. He had signed in at twenty-two hundred hours. Out at twenty-three forty-five. The notation read: “Trunk rack inspection, bay 3.”

Forty-five minutes. On a quiet night with no reported faults. Ruth’s index finger rested on the entry. She could feel the slight indentation of the pen on the page, the faint groove where pressure had been applied.

He had been in the building. He had been at the trunk rack. He had signed out at twenty-three forty-five, which meant he was present during the window when the call to the councillor’s residence would have been logged. The call that was now erased. The call whose absence had left a perfect forgery in its place.

Ruth closed the log. She sat in the heat of the desk lamp with her hands on the cloth cover and listened to the exchange breathe. She had not called the police. She had not reported the forgery. She had interrogated a colleague under the pretence of a routine review. And now she was sitting alone with an access log she had no authority to examine for investigative purposes, reading an entry that pointed toward a man who was not here to defend himself.

She had crossed a line. The realization arrived not as a feeling but as a fact, simple and irreversible. The keeper of the clock had stepped outside the clock’s mechanism. She was no longer the supervisor reviewing her records. She was a woman with a secret, pursuing a suspicion she could not share, building a case she had no mandate to build. If anyone discovered what she was doing, the forgery would be the least of her problems. She would be the one who had tampered with an investigation. She would be the one who had withheld evidence.

Ruth opened the log again and read the entry once more. The cramped letters. The blue-black ink. The trunk rack inspection that served no apparent purpose.

Alec Dunbar was an engineer. He had technical access to the trunk lines, to the switchboard racks, to the physical infrastructure of the exchange. He would have a key to the logbook cabinet in his toolkit—or access to one. He had been present at the hour the call was erased.

But the forgery was in her hand. Not his. Not a rough approximation. A precise, studied reproduction of Ruth Calloway’s handwriting, complete with the specific way she crossed her t’s and dotted her i’s. An engineer with a toolkit and a limp could have access. He could have opportunity. But could he reproduce a supervisor’s signature closely enough to pass inspection?

The question sat in the circle of lamplight like an object she could not identify. She stared at the name in the access log, at the blue-black ink that matched the ink in the ledger, and the question formed clearly, coldly, in the silence of the office.

Did he have the skill, or did someone guide his hand?

The fog pressed against the window. The exchange hummed. Ruth sat in the too-warm office and did not move.
