Chapter 3

Scrutinizing the Pages

────────────────────────────────────────

The green desk lamp cast a forensic circle of light onto the open ledger, illuminating the perfect loops of her own handwriting—that weren’t hers.

Ruth had not moved from this position in forty minutes—not since Marjorie’s deflection sent her to the access log, not since Alec’s name surfaced in the late entries, and she realized she had crossed a line by investigating alone. The shift had ended, the day staff had trickled home, and still she sat. Now the exchange hummed around her in its night-shift register: the drone of the ringer racks, the muffled bark of a distant operator connecting a local call, the rhythmic click of relay banks settling into their work. She heard none of it. Her world had contracted to the circle of light, to the cream-colored page and the columns of figures inscribed upon it in a hand that wore her skin.

Alec Dunbar’s name still sat behind her eyes like an afterimage. She had stared at it in the access log until the letters seemed to rearrange themselves into a question: Did he have the skill, or did someone guide his hand? She had closed that log, replaced it on the shelf, and walked back to her desk in a fog of quiet, ominous certainty. Now she sat with the evidence of the crime laid out before her, and the question had not followed her. It had been replaced by something more urgent. Someone had written this page in her hand. The forgery was nearly perfect—the slant of the letters, the particular way her ‘f’ crossed its bar high and to the right, the slight leftward lean of her numerals. But perfect things do not exist in nature, and what is made by hand can be unmade by hand.

She would find the flaw. She had to find it.

Ruth opened her personal log to the corresponding date and laid it beside the ledger. Her log was a different creature—smaller, bound in dark green cloth, its pages dense with her own shorthand. Where the ledger was the public face of the exchange’s night, her log was its private conscience: not just times and line numbers but her own observations, the small jottings that made the difference between a record and a memory. She had always trusted it absolutely. The ledger could be rewritten by a forger. Her log was hers alone.

She began a line-by-line comparison, working from the top of the page. The magnifying glass was an old friend, its brass rim worn smooth by years of use. She moved it slowly down the ledger column, matching each entry against her log. Times. Line numbers. Durations. Call types. The glass revealed the texture of the ink, the slight pooling where the nib had pressed harder on a downstroke, the tiny variations that distinguished one night’s writing from another’s.

The first fifteen entries matched. Her log and the ledger agreed on every particular, as they should. Ruth felt the familiar satisfaction of order confirmed—a small, maternal pride in the system she had built and maintained for nine years. Her ledger, her shift, her girls. The records were clean because she kept them clean.

Entry sixteen: 11: 14 p. m., trunk call, line 7, duration two minutes. The ledger entry matched her log. She moved on.

Entry seventeen: 11: 19 p. m., local call, line 3, duration one minute. Matched.

Entry eighteen. Ruth stopped.

The glass hovered over the entry. 11: 22 p. m., trunk call, line 9. The duration column showed a figure that made her lean closer. There was a blotch there—a small, dark pool of ink where the pen had lingered too long, or pressed too hard, or been lifted and set down again. The blotch obscured the digit partially, but Ruth could read what was beneath it: a ‘2’. Two minutes. Her log said the same. 11: 22, trunk, line 9, two minutes.

She should have moved on. Everything agreed. But the blotch kept her.

Ruth tilted the glass, catching the light at a different angle. The ink in the ledger was standard-issue—Waterman’s blue-black, the same bottle she had used since 1953. It dried at a predictable rate. A natural blotch, the kind that happened when a hand slipped or a nib caught the paper’s grain, had a particular shape: irregular, feathered at the edges where the ink wicked into the fibre. This blotch was different. It was round. Compact. The edges were too clean, as if the ink had been deposited deliberately, a small well of it laid over an existing mark and allowed to pool. It did not look like an accident. It looked like a correction.

Her heart shifted into a higher gear. She held the glass steady and leaned until her face was inches from the page, until she could see the way the paper fibres had been disturbed beneath the blotch. There was a ghost there. A faint impression, barely visible even under magnification, of a different numeral beneath the ink. The original mark had been made, then covered. Someone had written a digit, decided it was wrong, and pooled ink over it rather than scratching it out. A scratched correction would have drawn attention. A blotch looked like nothing. A blotch looked like carelessness.

Ruth sat back. Her thumb, smudged with ledger dust from turning pages, pressed down on the rim of the magnifying glass as she held it over the entry. The circle of distorted light isolated the blotch and its ghost. She could not read the original numeral with certainty. It might have been a ‘3’. The shape was wrong for a ‘1’ or a ‘7’—too round in the lower curve, too wide. But she could not swear to it.

What she could swear to was this: someone had corrected a duration figure on this entry. The call at 11: 22 p. m. on line 9 had been logged, then altered. And in her personal log, the same call sat in her own hand, unaltered: two minutes.

She stared at the two entries side by side. The ledger said two minutes. Her log said two minutes. But the ledger’s ‘2’ sat on top of a buried numeral, and the buried numeral was not a ‘2’.

The trunk record.

The thought arrived with the force of a physical push. The trunk record was the machine’s own log—printed automatically by the trunk-line equipment, a mechanical tally of every connection made and its duration, down to the second. It lived in the archive room, in a heavy bound volume that no one consulted unless there was a dispute. It was the final authority. The ledger was human. The trunk record was not.

Ruth stood. Her legs ached; she had been sitting too long. She gathered her personal log under her arm and walked through the exchange, past the sleeping rows of switchboards, past the ringer racks with their semaphore of pending calls, through the narrow corridor that led to the archive. The air changed as she entered—the warmth of the lamp and the desk giving way to a metallic chill. The shelving units were steel, industrial, holding their contents at the temperature of the building’s bones.

She found the volume for February and pulled it from the shelf. It was heavy, bound in dark cloth, its pages filled with the precise, typed grid of the trunk recorder. She opened it to the relevant date and ran her finger down the column until she found it.

11: 22 p. m. Line 9. Trunk call. Duration: three minutes.

Ruth’s finger stopped. She pressed it against the typed numeral, as if pressure could change it. Three minutes. The machine said three. Her log said two. The ledger said two, but the ledger’s ‘2’ was a lie laid over a buried truth, and the buried truth was a ‘3’.

The call had lasted three minutes. Her log was wrong.

She stood in the archive’s cold air, holding both records open, the discrepancy between them a physical thing—a minute of time that existed in the machine’s memory but not in hers. She had logged two minutes. She was certain she had logged what she remembered. But the machine did not remember. It recorded. And the record said three.

The cold closed around her. She felt it in her hands first, then in her chest, then in the slow, sickening vertigo of a woman who has built her authority on the proposition that her records are perfect and has just watched that proposition crack.

She turned back to the trunk record and traced the entry’s neighbours. 11: 19, local call, line 3, one minute—this matched her log. 11: 25, trunk call, line 4, four minutes—this matched as well. The entries on either side of the 11: 22 call were correct in her log. Only this one was wrong. Only the call that someone had blotted and corrected in the ledger.

The forgery had a target. Whoever had rewritten the ledger page in her handwriting had not been covering their tracks at random. They had been after this call specifically—the three-minute trunk call on line 9 at 11: 22 p. m. They had rewritten the entire page to disguise the alteration of a single entry. And they had written the false duration in her hand, so that if the forgery was discovered, the discrepancy would point not to them but to her. Her log confirmed the false figure. Her own record made her the corroborating witness to the lie.

Ruth closed the trunk record slowly. The weight of it settled in her hands, heavier than it had been a moment ago. She carried both volumes back to her desk and sat down under the lamp’s circle again. The ledger lay open to the blotted entry. Her log lay open to the matching one. The trunk record’s typed ‘3’ burned in her memory.

One minute. The call had lasted three minutes, but every record she controlled said two. That missing minute was not an error. It was an excavation—a minute that had been deliberately cut out of the night, buried under ink and false handwriting, and left for her to find or to miss. She had missed it once. She would not miss it again.

But the finding was worse than the missing. Because if her log was wrong about this call’s duration, then her log was fallible. And if her log was fallible, then the system she had built—her system, her records, her authority—had a crack running through its foundation. She had always believed the ledger could be tampered with but her personal log could not. The ledger was public. The log was hers. Now she held proof that her log agreed with a forgery, and she could not say whether she had been mistaken or whether someone had reached into her log as well.

A sound behind her. Ruth turned.

Violet Sykes stood in the aisle between the switchboard bays, her coat buttoned to the throat, her face pale and creased with the particular exhaustion of someone who had not slept. She was not supposed to be here. Her shift had ended hours ago.

“Violet.” Ruth’s voice came out flat. She closed the ledger, instinctively, the way one covers a wound.

“I forgot my gloves,” Violet said. She held them up—woollen, grey, the fingers curled in on themselves. “I thought I’d left them at the board.”

She was looking at Ruth with an expression that Ruth recognised: the careful blankness of someone trying not to see what was in front of them. Ruth’s desk, the open log, the magnifying glass, the trunk record lying beside the ledger. The evidence of an investigation that was not authorised and that Ruth had told no one about.

“You didn’t log off at the right time last night,” Ruth said. She did not know why she said it. Perhaps because Violet was standing there with her careful blankness and her forgotten gloves, and Ruth needed to hear someone else’s voice confirm that the night had happened, that the records were real, that the minute she had lost was not a figment of exhaustion.

Violet’s brow creased. “I’m sure I… I don’t really remember. The lines were bad that night. There was a fault on—“ She stopped. Her eyes went to the closed ledger. “Why does it matter?”

“It matters because a trunk call at 11: 22 was logged for two minutes and lasted three. That’s not a fault, Violet. That’s a footprint.”

“A footprint.” Violet repeated the word as if it were in a foreign language. “Could it have been a mistake? You could have miscounted. The lines were bad, and you were—“

“I don’t miscount.” Ruth heard the edge in her own voice and pulled it back. She was not angry at Violet. She was angry at the minute, at the ink, at the buried ‘3’ that she could still see when she closed her eyes. “I’m not asking you to remember. I’m asking you to think. Was there anything unusual about that call? Line 9, 11: 22. Trunk.”

Violet’s face tightened. She clutched her gloves against her chest, the wool compressed between her fingers. “I don’t really remember. I told you. The lines were bad and there was—there was a lot of interference, and I connected it and I logged it and that was all.”

“You logged it for two minutes.”

“I logged what I saw.”

“And what you saw was two minutes.”

Violet said nothing. The silence between them was filled by the exchange’s hum, the patient machinery of connection and relay that went on regardless of what anyone remembered or failed to remember. Ruth looked at her and saw what she had seen in a hundred operators over nine years: the face of someone who knew more than they would say, not from malice but from the small, human instinct to protect oneself from consequence. She had seen it in herself, ten minutes ago, when she had closed the ledger before Violet could see it open.

“Go home, Violet.”

“I just want to find my—“

“Your gloves are at the board. Third position, left side.” Ruth had noticed them on her way back from the archive. She noticed everything. That was the problem. She noticed everything, and she had still logged two minutes for a three-minute call.

Violet retrieved her gloves without another word. The door to the exchange floor closed behind her with a soft, pneumatic thud.

Ruth sat alone. The lamp hummed. The ledger lay closed on her desk, and beside it her personal log, open to the entry that agreed with the forgery. She stared at her own handwriting—the familiar slant, the high-crossed ‘f’, the leftward lean—and for the first time in nine years, she did not trust it.

The forgery had targeted a specific call. That much was clear. The entire page had been rewritten to bury the alteration of a single duration figure, to shave one minute off a three-minute trunk call and make every record agree that the call had been shorter than it was. Someone had needed that minute to disappear. The question was no longer whether the forgery was intentional. The question was what had happened in the minute that someone had killed.

And the question beneath that, the one that sat in Ruth’s chest like a stone: if her log was wrong about this call, what else was it wrong about? The system’s flaw did not run through the ledger alone. It ran through her. Through her hand, her memory, her authority. The forger had known this. They had used her own fallibility as their cover.

She picked up the magnifying glass one final time and held it over her personal log, over the entry that read ‘2’ in her own steady hand. The glass revealed what it always revealed: the texture of her own penmanship, the slight groove where the nib had pressed into the paper, the ink dried true and undisturbed. No blotch. No correction. No ghost.

She had written ‘2’ and believed it. The machine had recorded ‘3’ and known it. Between those two numbers lay a minute of stolen time, and Ruth understood now that finding it did not make her the investigator. It made her the scene of the crime.
