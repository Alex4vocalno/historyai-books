Chapter 19

The Chivalrous Cover

────────────────────────────────────────

The maintenance bay held its breath in the amber glow of emergency lighting. Relay racks stood in silent rows along the walls, their copper guts exposed like anatomical displays in a medical lecture hall. The air tasted of ozone and machine oil, sharp enough to coat the tongue. A single fluorescent tube buzzed and flickered overhead, casting shadows that jumped and settled with arrhythmic patience.

On the workbench near the door, someone had left a half-disassembled rotary dial, its internal springs coiled like sleeping serpents. A grease rag draped over a stool had dried to a stiff crescent. The concrete floor showed the evidence of traffic: scuff marks, a single boot print in what might have been leaked hydraulic fluid, and near the far corner, a chair pulled out from a small metal desk as though its occupant had risen in haste.

Drawers stood slightly open. A pen had rolled off the edge and rested against the leg of a filing cabinet. The metal-bound fault log lay closed on the desk’s corner, its brass clasp catching the light. The room smelled of secrets kept too long in enclosed spaces.

Ruth stepped inside and let the heavy door close behind her. The latch engaged with a soft, final click.

Alec Dunbar sat on a stool at the far end of the bay, his back to her, shoulders curved inward. He was examining a schematic spread across a workbench, though his pen had not moved in the time it took her to cross half the distance between them. The fluorescent light caught the thinning hair at his crown, the grease stains on his shirtsleeves.

“You’re here late,” she said.

He did not turn. “So are you.”

“I need to talk to you about the fault log.”

Now he turned, slowly, on his stool. His face was unreadable in the half-light. “You want a story, read a paperback. I give you facts.”

Ruth reached into her pocket and withdrew the folded page she had taken from the records cupboard. She had studied it for the better part of an hour, comparing the ink pressure, the slant of the letters, the tiny imperfections that distinguished a genuine entry from a fabricated one. The timestamp was wrong. The outage duration had been altered by exactly eleven minutes. And the technician’s initials, entered in a hand that matched Alec Dunbar’s own registry signature, confirmed what her investigation had been circling for days.

She placed the page on the bench between them, smoothing it flat with her palm.

“The outage on February fourteenth,” she said. “The trunk line to Manchester Central. You logged it at 11: 47 p. m., duration of six minutes. But the automatic fault recorder shows the line went dead at 11: 36 and stayed down for seventeen minutes.”

Alec’s eyes dropped to the paper. A muscle twitched along his jaw.

“The ledger doesn’t lie,” Ruth continued. “Only people do. And you, Alec, have been lying about this entry since the night it was made.”

He picked up his pen, turned it between his fingers, set it down again. The movement was deliberate, a way of buying time.

“You’ve been checking my work,” he said. Not a question.

“I’ve been checking everyone’s work. That’s my job.”

“Is it?” He looked at her now, and something in his gaze shifted. The flatness was still there, but beneath it she detected a current of something else. Weariness, perhaps. Or resignation. “Or have you been looking for something specific?”

Ruth did not blink. “I’m looking for the truth.”

“The truth.” He exhaled through his nose. “You know what truth looks like in a place like this? It looks like whatever the supervisor writes in the official record. It looks like whatever keeps the equipment running and the payroll on time. Administrative truth. That’s what we trade in.”

“And technical truth? What does that look like?”

Alec’s mouth twisted. “Technical truth is what’s written in the fault recorder. The machines don’t care about payroll. They don’t care about keeping up appearances. They mark the time, the duration, the line number, and they don’t ask permission.”

“Then why did you alter the log?”

The silence stretched. The fluorescent tube buzzed. Somewhere in the relay racks, a connection clicked, a sound like a fingernail against glass.

Alec reached for the fault log on the corner of his desk. He opened it, turned to a page near the middle, and slid it toward her. The entry was there, in his own hand, recording the outage at the falsified time.

“Tell me why,” Ruth said.

“You already know why.”

“I know what the evidence shows. I want to hear it from you.”

He looked at her for a long moment. Then his shoulders dropped, the rigid set of his spine loosening as though something internal had given way.

“She came to me,” he said quietly.

Ruth felt a stillness settle in her chest. “Who?”

Alec turned away, staring at the relay racks. “Marjorie. Marjorie Finch. She came to me that night, after everyone else had gone. She was… upset.”

“Upset how?”

“Crying. Proper tears. Her makeup running, her hands shaking.” He shook his head slowly. “A proper lady like her, weeping in the corridor. What was I supposed to do?”

Ruth’s fingers went slack against the edge of the bench. The name hung in the air between them. Marjorie Finch. The operator with the careful voice and the neat uniform. The woman Ruth had questioned, had considered, had set aside as a minor player in the larger conspiracy.

“What did she ask you to do?”

“She said she’d been late. Not just late, but late enough that it would show on the attendance register. Late enough that if anyone cross-referenced the timing with a fault on her board, there would be questions.”

“So she asked you to create a fault.”

“Not create. Log.” Alec’s voice hardened. “She said if there was a recorded outage on her trunk line during the time she wasn’t at her station, no one would notice her absence. The call records would show the line was down. She’d have an excuse.”

“And you agreed.”

He turned back to her, and for the first time, Ruth saw something vulnerable in his expression. Not guilt, exactly. Something closer to shame.

“She was frightened,” he said. “Not just worried about her job. Frightened. There’s a difference.”

“What was she frightened of?”

“I don’t know.” He spread his hands. “She wouldn’t say. Just that she couldn’t lose her position, couldn’t have anyone looking too closely at her movements that night. She begged me, Ruth. Actually begged.”

Ruth stared at the falsified entry. The ink had faded slightly, the paper had yellowed at the edges. A small lie, in the scope of things. A few minutes of time, a line of ink in a metal-bound book. But the consequences rippled outward, touching everything.

“You realize what you’ve done,” she said. “By altering that record, you’ve made it impossible to trust anything in the log. Every entry becomes suspect. Every technician becomes a potential conspirator.”

“I know.” His voice was flat again, the vulnerability retreating behind the mask. “I didn’t think about that. I wasn’t thinking about the integrity of the system. I was thinking about her face. The tears. The way she kept saying she couldn’t lose this job.”

Ruth let out a slow breath. The ozone smell seemed thicker now, coating the back of her throat.

“The night Councillor Fairley died,” she said. “February fourteenth. That’s the night you made this entry.”

Alec went still.

“A trunk call came through that night,” Ruth continued. “A call that should have been recorded, that should have appeared in the ledger. But there’s a gap. A missing entry. And you created a false fault to cover a different gap in the records.”

“I told you why I did it.”

“You told me part of why. But Marjorie Finch wasn’t just late that night, was she? She was somewhere she shouldn’t have been. Somewhere that would have put her near the switchboard when that call came through.”

Alec’s jaw tightened. “I don’t know anything about a call.”

“But you know she was afraid. You said so yourself. Not just afraid of losing her job. Afraid of what?”

He looked away again. The fluorescent light flickered, casting his face in shadow for a moment before the glow steadied.

“She said something,” he muttered. “When she was pleading with me. She said she’d overheard something she wasn’t supposed to hear. Something that frightened her.”

Ruth’s pulse quickened. “What did she overhear?”

“I don’t know. She wouldn’t tell me. Just that she needed to disappear for a while, needed her absence to be explainable, needed there to be a reason no one could find her at her station during a particular window of time.”

“A particular window.”

“Eleven thirty-six to eleven fifty-three.” He recited the times as though they had been branded into his memory. “The real outage window. The one I was supposed to cover with the false entry.”

Ruth closed her eyes for a moment. The missing trunk call. The call that should have been recorded but wasn’t. The call that someone had gone to great lengths to erase from the official record.

And Marjorie Finch had been absent from her station during exactly that window.

“You didn’t ask what she overheard,” Ruth said.

“I didn’t want to know.” Alec’s voice was bitter now. “The less I knew, the better. I told myself it was just a personnel matter. A woman worried about her job, nothing more. I told myself I was being… chivalrous. Helping a lady in distress.”

“And now?”

“Now I wonder if I’ve been a fool.” He picked up his pen again, turned it over, set it down. “I’ve been watching you investigate. Watching you pull at threads. And I’ve been asking myself what I’ve gotten myself into.”

Ruth studied him. The slump of his shoulders, the defeat in his posture. He had been protecting Marjorie Finch, but not out of any grand conspiracy. Out of something older, simpler, more foolish. A sense of obligation to a woman in tears.

“You realize you’ve implicated yourself in falsifying official records,” she said. “That’s a serious offense. Potentially criminal.”

“I know.”

“And you realize I could report this. Bring it to the administration, let them decide how to handle it.”

“I know.” He met her gaze. “But you haven’t yet.”

“No.”

“Why?”

Ruth considered the question. The truth was complicated. She needed Alec. He was a technician, someone with access to the equipment, the records, the inner workings of the exchange. If she turned him in now, she would lose a potential source of information. And more than that, she would lose a chance to understand what Marjorie Finch had actually overheard.

“Because I think there’s more to this than a falsified fault log,” she said. “I think you stumbled into something larger than you understood. And I think you want to help me untangle it.”

Alec laughed, a short, humorless sound. “Help you. After everything I’ve done to obstruct you.”

“You’ve been protecting yourself. Protecting Marjorie. But now you see that the protection was misplaced. That whatever she was afraid of, it’s connected to something worse than a late arrival.”

She reached into her bag and withdrew the thermos she had brought from her desk earlier that evening. The tea inside had gone lukewarm, but it was still drinkable. She unscrewed the cap, poured a measure into the cup, and slid it across the bench toward him.

“Drink,” she said. “And then tell me everything you remember about that night. Every detail. What Marjorie was wearing. What she said, exactly. Where she went after she left you.”

Alec stared at the cup as though it were a foreign object. Then, slowly, he reached for it. His fingers closed around the metal. He raised it to his lips and drank.

When he lowered the cup, some of the tension had left his frame. Not all of it, but enough that he no longer looked like a cornered animal.

“She was wearing her uniform,” he said. “But the collar was undone. I’d never seen her with an undone collar. She was always so precise, so proper. But that night, she looked… disheveled. Like she’d been running.”

“Running from what?”

“I don’t know. She came to the technical office, found me at my desk. She was crying, trying to keep her voice down. She said she needed my help. Said she’d made a terrible mistake, stayed somewhere too long, lost track of time.”

“Did she say where she’d been?”

“No. But she kept looking over her shoulder. Like she expected someone to come through the door after her.”

Ruth filed that away. The behavior suggested fear of pursuit, not just fear of discovery. Someone had frightened Marjorie Finch, and that fear had driven her to seek out Alec Dunbar’s help.

“What time was this?”

“Almost midnight. I was about to leave when she came in.”

“And you altered the fault log at her request.”

“Yes. I backdated the entry, made it look like the line had been down earlier. Covered her absence.”

“Did she say anything else? Anything about what she’d overheard?”

Alec hesitated. His gaze drifted to the relay racks, the shadows pooling between them.

“She said one thing,” he said slowly. “Just before she left. She said, ‘They’ll kill me if they know I heard it.’”

Ruth felt a chill move through her. “Kill you? She used that word?”

“She did.” Alec’s voice was quiet. “I thought she was being dramatic. You know how people are when they’re upset. Everything becomes life and death. I didn’t take it literally.”

“But now?”

“Now I’ve read the papers. Seen what happened to Councillor Fairley. And I wonder.”

Ruth stood in silence for a moment. The weight of the revelation settled on her shoulders. Marjorie Finch had overheard something, something that had put her in fear for her life. And that something was connected to the missing trunk call, the falsified fault log, and the death of a city councillor.

“You should have come forward,” she said.

“I know.”

“You should have told someone what she said.”

“I know.” Alec’s grip tightened on his cup, knuckles whitening. “But what was I supposed to do? Walk into the supervisor’s office and say, ‘One of the operators thinks she overheard something that might be connected to a murder’? They’d have laughed me out of the building. Or worse, they’d have asked questions I couldn’t answer.”

Ruth understood. The system did not reward those who spoke out of turn. It punished them. Alec had been caught between his instinct to help a frightened woman and his fear of the consequences. In the end, he had chosen the path of least resistance, and that choice had led him here, to a maintenance bay at midnight, confessing to a crime he hadn’t fully understood.

“I need your help,” she said.

Alec looked up. “My help?”

“You have access to the technical records. The fault recorders, the line schematics, the equipment logs. Things I can’t get to without raising suspicion.”

“What are you asking?”

“I’m asking you to be my eyes inside the technical office. When I need information, you’ll provide it. When I need records pulled, you’ll pull them. And when the time comes, you’ll testify to what you’ve told me tonight.”

He stared at her. “And in exchange?”

“In exchange, I don’t report what I know about the falsified log. Not yet. Not until we’ve gotten to the bottom of this.”

“And after? When it’s all over?”

Ruth met his gaze steadily. “That depends on what we find. And on how helpful you are.”

The fluorescent light flickered again. In the brief darkness, Ruth heard Alec exhale, a long, shuddering breath.

“Alright,” he said. “Alright. I’ll help you.”

“Good.”

She reached out and picked up the falsified log page, folding it carefully and returning it to her pocket. Evidence. Proof. A piece of the puzzle that would help her understand the larger picture.

“One more thing,” she said. “The unlisted extension. The one that doesn’t appear on any official schematic. Do you know anything about it?”

Alec’s expression flickered. “What extension?”

“The one that shouldn’t exist. The one that might have been used to route a call without leaving a trace.”

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, slowly, “There are… rumors. Among the technicians. About a line that runs through the old junction room. A line that was supposed to be decommissioned years ago but never was.”

“Who uses it?”

“I don’t know. But the maintenance schedules for that room are… irregular. Someone goes in there regularly, but it’s never on the official roster.”

Ruth filed that away. The unlisted extension. Another thread to pull.

“Thank you, Alec.”

She turned to leave, her hand on the door.

“Supervisor Calloway.”

She paused, looked back.

Alec sat on his stool, the cup of cold tea still in his hands. He looked older than he had when she entered. Older and smaller.

“Be careful,” he said. “Whatever Marjorie heard, whatever she was running from, it scared her badly enough to make her beg. And she’s not the type to beg.”

Ruth nodded once. Then she opened the door and stepped out into the corridor.

The hallway stretched before her, lit by the same flickering fluorescents. The hum of the switchboards followed her as she walked, a constant reminder of the machinery that underlay every conversation, every connection, every secret passed through the wires.

She had what she needed. Alec Dunbar had confessed, not to conspiracy, but to foolishness. A chivalrous cover for a frightened woman. And that woman, Marjorie Finch, had overheard something that had put her in fear for her life.

The ticket in Ruth’s pocket felt heavier now. The night Councillor Fairley died. The night everything had started to unravel.

She had a new name to investigate. A new thread to pull. And somewhere in the tangle of wires and records, the truth was waiting.
