Chapter 17

Reconstructing the Blackmail

────────────────────────────────────────

Marjorie’s footsteps had faded down the corridor, leaving Ruth alone in the cramped records cupboard with the truth that was now a weapon she could not legally use. The silence pressed against her ears, heavier than the constant hum of the switchboards beyond the wall. She stood motionless, her hand still resting on the shelf where Marjorie had replaced the ledger, the spine of the fault log digging into her palm. Terence Bolt. The name turned over in her mind like a stone in a current. He had driven the councillor. He had made the call that never appeared in any record. And someone in this building had helped him do it.

She slipped the log back into its proper place on the shelf, her fingers moving by habit though her thoughts raced far ahead. The forgery in her own hand still burned in her memory. The missing trunk call. The altered entry. Someone had gone to extraordinary lengths to erase a single connection, and that erasure pointed to a conspiracy far more dangerous than a technician falsifying overtime.

Ruth stepped out of the records room and pulled the door shut behind her. The corridor stretched ahead, lit by the green glow of the exit signs and the faint amber of the night-duty lamps. Her heels clicked against the linoleum, each step echoing too loudly in the stillness. She needed to understand the mechanics. The how. Marjorie’s confession had given her the who and the why, but the physical evidence still pointed to someone inside the exchange, someone who knew the systems intimately enough to bypass every safeguard.

She needed Alec Dunbar.

The thought sat bitter in her throat. Alec, who had obstructed her at every turn. Alec, who knew about the scar she had never told a soul about. Alec, whose handwriting she had studied for hours, searching for the telltale pressure points of forgery. But Alec was also the only person in this building who understood the trunk routing system well enough to explain how a call could vanish without a trace.

She found him in the technical office, as she had expected. He sat hunched over a schematic spread across his workbench, a pen clutched in his fingers, his solid shoulders casting a shadow across the paper. The smell of hot valves and machine oil hung thick in the air. He did not look up when she entered.

“Dunbar.”

His pen paused mid-stroke. A long moment passed before he raised his head, his face unreadable in the low light. “Supervisor Calloway.” His voice was flat, clipped. “Working late again?”

“I need you to walk me through something.” She crossed to his bench and placed the fault log between them, open to the altered entry. “This outage time. Show me what it means in physical terms. Not the paperwork. The actual equipment.”

Alec’s eyes dropped to the page. Something shifted in his expression. A tightening around his jaw, a flicker of what might have been professional offense. He set down his pen with deliberate care.

“You’re asking me to explain my own system?”

“I’m asking you to explain how someone could place a trunk call to an unlisted extension without it being logged.” She kept her voice level, though her pulse beat hard against her collar. “The entry shows a line outage at two-seventeen. But the call Marjorie overheard came through sometime after two. That means the outage was fabricated to cover the routing.”

Alec stared at the log for a long moment. When he looked up, his eyes had changed. The defensiveness was still there, but beneath it, something else. A cold, analytical interest.

“You think someone bypassed the trunk register.”

“I think someone had to. A call that doesn’t exist in the ledger had to go somewhere. Show me where.”

He studied her face, searching for something. Whatever he found there seemed to satisfy him. He rose from the bench, his movements slow and deliberate, and reached for his flashlight.

“Follow me.”

He led her out of the technical office and down the service corridor, past the relay racks that hummed with dormant connections. The building felt different at this hour, stripped of the chatter of operators and the click of connecting calls. Only the machines remained, their constant low vibration thrumming through the walls like a heartbeat.

Alec stopped at a junction box set into the wall, half-hidden behind a rack of spare cables. He pried open the panel with a practiced motion, the metal groaning in protest, and shone his flashlight into the tangle of wires within.

“This is the main patch panel for trunk routing,” he said, his voice dropping to a murmur that barely rose above the hum of the equipment. “Every long-distance call passes through here before it hits the register. The register logs the time, the destination, the duration. It’s automatic. Infallible.”

“Nothing is infallible.”

“Legally, no.” He reached into the panel and traced a specific wire, his finger moving along the sheathing until it reached a junction point. “But physically? There’s a bypass. It’s not in any manual. It’s not supposed to exist. But the engineers who built this system left themselves a back door.”

He shifted the flashlight beam, and Ruth saw it. A small jack set apart from the others, its metal glinting coldly in the artificial light. Dust had settled around its edges, undisturbed for years.

“This one.” Alec pointed directly at it. “It bypasses the trunk register entirely. You patch a line directly from the incoming trunk to any internal extension, and the call never touches the logging system. It’s invisible.”

Ruth stared at the jack. Her hand moved without conscious thought, her finger hovering over the metal contact point. This was where it had happened. The fatal connection. Terence Bolt’s voice flowing through the wires, bypassing every safeguard, reaching the councillor’s private line without leaving a single trace.

“How would someone know this existed?”

Alec’s face hardened. “They’d have to know the system. Really know it. Not just the operating procedures. The architecture. The bones.”

“Who knows about it?”

“I know about it.” His voice was flat, but a muscle jumped in his jaw. “The senior technicians. The ones who trained under the original installation crew. Maybe six people in this building.”

“Including you.”

“Including me.” He met her gaze without flinching. “But I didn’t do this, Ruth. I had no reason to. Whatever Bolt wanted from the councillor, it had nothing to do with me.”

She wanted to believe him. The logic of it pushed against her suspicion. If Alec had been the inside accomplice, he would never have shown her the bypass. He would have obfuscated, deflected, maintained his stonewall. Instead, he had handed her the key to the entire puzzle.

“Show me the rest,” she said. “Show me how the call would have been routed from this jack to the councillor’s extension.”

Alec nodded, a sharp, economical motion. He moved down the corridor, and she followed, her mind racing ahead of her feet.

The maintenance corridor branched into a narrower passage, lined with junction boxes and cable conduits. Alec’s flashlight beam picked out the dust motes swirling in the stagnant air. He stopped at another panel, this one marked with a faded warning label.

“The councillor’s line is unlisted,” he said, pulling the panel open. “But it still has to connect to the internal network. The bypass routes through this junction. If someone patched the trunk line directly here—“ He traced the path with his light. “The call would ring through without ever hitting the public exchange. No operator intervention. No logging. Just a direct connection.”

Ruth examined the junction. The cables were neat, bundled with wire ties, but one bundle sat slightly askew. She reached out and touched it. The plastic was warm.

“It’s been used recently.”

Alec leaned closer, his brow furrowing. “That shouldn’t be possible. The silent alarm would have sounded.”

“Silent alarm?”

He straightened, his face going pale in the half-light. “There’s a security protocol. If anyone accesses the internal network from an unregistered point, an alarm triggers at the supervisor’s station. It’s supposed to be foolproof.”

Ruth’s stomach dropped. “I never saw an alarm that night.”

“No.” Alec’s voice had gone quiet, his earlier composure cracking. “You wouldn’t have. Not if someone disabled it first.”

The implications settled over her like a weight. “An operator.”

“Someone with access to the supervisor’s station. Someone who knew the alarm codes.” Alec turned to face her, his eyes hard with something that looked almost like sympathy. “I can show you how the call was placed. But I can’t show you who turned off the alarm. That’s not in my system, Ruth. That’s in yours.”

They walked back to the technical office in silence. Ruth’s mind churned through the possibilities, sorting and discarding, building and rebuilding the sequence of events. Terence Bolt had arrived at the exchange, presumably with inside knowledge. He had accessed the patch panel, placed the call, spoken to the councillor. But the alarm had been disabled before he ever touched the equipment. Someone on the inside had prepared the way.

At the office door, Alec paused. He looked at her with an expression she couldn’t quite read, a mixture of professional respect and something darker.

“I didn’t forge that entry,” he said quietly. “I didn’t know about the call until you started asking questions. But I know what it looks like when someone’s been in my system. And whoever did this knew exactly what they were doing.”

Ruth nodded slowly. “I believe you.”

The words surprised her as much as they seemed to surprise him. His eyebrows lifted slightly, the first crack in his unreadable facade.

“That’s a change.”

“You showed me the truth.” She met his gaze. “The ledger doesn’t lie. Only people do. You could have kept this hidden. You chose not to.”

Alec’s mouth twitched, not quite a smile. “Don’t make me regret it.”

She left him there and made her way back to her own office, her footsteps echoing in the empty corridor. The door stood slightly ajar, just as she had left it. Inside, the desk lamp cast a warm circle of light across the cluttered surface. The internal duty roster hung on the wall, five names printed in neat columns.

She sat at her desk and stared at the roster. Five operators on duty that night. Five women she had trained, supervised, trusted. One of them had betrayed everything the exchange stood for. One of them had let Terence Bolt into the system, had silenced the alarm, had covered her tracks with a forgery so perfect it bore Ruth’s own handwriting.

Her gaze drifted to the corner of the desk, where a collection of teacups sat waiting to be returned to the canteen. Each one bore the faint imprint of lipstick, the dregs of tea long since cooled. She had shared tea with these women countless times. She had listened to their complaints, their hopes, their small dramas. She had believed herself their protector.

Now she would have to become their investigator.

The thought settled into her chest, cold and heavy. The moral arbiter, forced to judge her own. The supervisor, forced to suspect her subordinates. The protector, forced to confront the possibility that protection had been misplaced.

She reached for the roster and pulled it from the wall, laying it flat on the desk. Five names. Five faces that swam in her memory. One of them had sold the exchange’s integrity for reasons Ruth could not yet fathom. One of them had stood at the supervisor’s station, fingers moving over the alarm controls, silencing the safeguard that should have caught Terence Bolt in the act.

The question was no longer how the call had been made. That much was clear now. The patch panel bypass, the disabled alarm, the forged entry. The mechanics were laid bare, exposed to the light of Alec’s flashlight.

The question now was far more dangerous. Which of her operators had opened the door?

Ruth folded the roster and tucked it into her desk drawer, alongside the stolen fault log page that had started everything. Her hands were steady now, the earlier shake gone, replaced by a cold, clear purpose. She would find the accomplice. She would reconstruct the sequence, minute by minute, until the gaps in the record revealed the truth.

And when she did, she would have to decide what mattered more: the system’s integrity, or the woman behind the betrayal.

The night stretched ahead of her, long and silent, filled with the hum of machines and the weight of the truth she now carried. Somewhere in this building, the answer waited. She would find it. She had no choice.

The ledger had been returned to its shelf, its secret shared between Ruth and the forger. But the silence in the records room had followed her here, resonating with the weight of her complicity, her suspicion, her dread.

She turned off the desk lamp and sat in the darkness, listening to the building breathe around her. Tomorrow, she would begin again. Tomorrow, she would watch her operators more closely than ever before.

Tomorrow, she would find out which of them had let the fox into the henhouse.

But for now, in the small hours before dawn, she let herself feel the full weight of what she had become: a woman who knew too much, with no one to tell, and a choice ahead that would cost her more than she could bear to calculate.

The exchange hummed on, indifferent to her turmoil. The machines had no conscience. They simply recorded, connected, transmitted.

Only people lied.

And somewhere in the darkness, the liar waited.
