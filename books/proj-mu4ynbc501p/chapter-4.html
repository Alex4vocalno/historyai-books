Chapter 4

The Driver's Story

────────────────────────────────────────

The smell of petrol and polish hit Ruth before she saw the black car gleaming under a single bare bulb. The bulb hung from a wire strung across the garage ceiling, swaying in a draught she could not locate, throwing a circle of yellowish light that moved across the car’s bonnet like a slow searchlight. The vehicle was a Humber Super Snipe, recently washed, still wearing a film of moisture along its chrome runner boards. A man stood beside it with a chamois leather draped over one forearm, rubbing the nearside wing in small, obsessive circles. He did not look up when Ruth’s shoes scraped on the concrete floor.

She had walked from the exchange, three blocks south through a thin evening rain that had stopped ten minutes ago but left the pavements dark and reflective. The cold had settled into her knuckles. She carried no briefcase, no official paperwork. Her notebook was in her coat pocket, small enough to close one hand around. The missing trunk call had brought her here, a line erased from the ledger that should have recorded a connection made to this man’s number on the night in question. Someone had removed it. The page was in her handwriting, but the entry was not hers. She had not written it. The forgery was precise enough to pass inspection, and that precision was what frightened her, because it meant the forger knew her hand.

The garage was a long, low building of brick and corrugated iron, divided into three bays. Two were empty. The third held the Humber and the man who tended it. Along the back wall, a workbench held an array of tins and brushes arranged by size. A clock hung above the bench, its face grimy, its second hand sweeping with a faint tick audible between the drips from the car’s undercarriage. The floor was swept clean. Oil stains had been scrubbed into pale shadows. Everything in the room spoke of a man who controlled his environment with an attention that bordered on the compulsive.

Terence Bolt turned then, and Ruth saw his face properly for the first time. He was fifty, built like a retired boxer gone slightly to seed. Broad shoulders, a thick neck, a permanent squint that deepened as he registered her presence. His hair was salt-and-pepper, cropped short. He wore a worn leather driving coat that smelled of petrol and pipe smoke even from six feet away. His knuckles were scarred, and his right thumb moved over a deep callus on his palm, the kind a gear stick leaves after decades of use.

“Mr. Bolt,” Ruth said. “I’m from the exchange. I wonder if I might have a word.”

“I drive,” he said. “I don’t ask questions.”

“I’m not asking you to ask questions. I’m asking you to answer a few.”

He set the chamois leather down on the wing mirror, folding it once, then again, so the edges aligned. His eyes went to the clock and came back. She noted the glance. He had measured the time she had already taken.

“Office,” he said, and jerked his chin toward a door in the back wall.

The office was a windowless room with a desk, two chairs, and a calendar on the wall showing a photograph of a lake that was two months behind. A gas heater in the corner ticked as its cools expanded. Bolt pulled out the chair behind the desk and sat. Ruth took the other chair, which was wooden and lower than his by three inches, a deliberate arrangement she recognized from interview rooms in police stations. She did not ask for a cushion.

“I want to ask about your evening, the night of the fourteenth,” Ruth said. She opened her notebook to a blank page. She had already decided not to mention the ledger, the missing entry, or the forgery. She would ask about movements, not evidence. “Routine inquiry. Nothing formal.”

“Routine,” Bolt repeated. His right hand lay on the desk. His index finger began to tap the edge, a slow, regular beat. “What do you want to know?”

“Start from when you finished your last run.”

“Dropped the councillor off at the house. Quarter past ten. Maybe twenty past. He went in. I drove the car back here.”

“What route?”

“Oxford Road. Straight through. No detours.”

“And then?”

“Left the car in the bay. Wiped it down. Locked up.”

“What time did you lock up?”

“Twenty to eleven. Maybe quarter to.”

His finger tapped the desk. Tap. Tap. Tap. The rhythm did not change. Ruth watched the movement, the way his fingertip rose and fell with the regularity of a metronome. She had seen men tap like this before. Some did it to steady themselves. Others did it because they had practised what came next so many times that the words had acquired their own tempo.

“How did you get home?”

“Bus.”

“Which bus?”

His eyes went to the clock again. Second time in four minutes. She counted.

“The 11: 05 from Piccadilly,” he said. “You can set your watch by it.”

The rhythmic tapping matched the cadence of his delivery. Each phrase landed on a tap. Dropped the councillor. Quarter past ten. Wiped it down. The 11: 05. Ruth let her pen rest against the page without writing. The story was too clean. Not wrong, not impossible, but assembled with the care of a man who knew exactly what he needed to prove and had arranged every detail to prove it.

“From Piccadilly,” she said. “That’s a walk from here.”

“Ten minutes. I know the timing.”

“Did anyone see you on the walk?”

“I didn’t take attendance.”

His thumb found the callus on his palm again. He rubbed it in a slow circle. The gesture was unconscious, a habit worn into the muscle by years of changing gears in traffic. Ruth noted the clean fingernails, the polished skin. Then she saw what lay beneath: a permanent dark grime under each nail, ground in too deep for soap. Hands that looked clean at a distance but were not, not really. Hands that had been scrubbed for presentation.

“What stop did you get off at?”

“Wilmslow Road. The one by the park.”

“And you were home by what time?”

“Half past eleven. Quarter to twelve at the latest.”

She wrote nothing. The pen stayed where it was. Bolt watched her not writing, and his finger stopped tapping for the first time since he had started talking. The silence held. The gas heater ticked. From the garage, a drip struck the concrete floor.

“I’ve given you what you asked,” Bolt said. “I drove. I locked up. I took the bus. That’s the evening.”

“I’m not asking you to remember,” Ruth said. “I’m asking you to think. Was there anything between locking up and reaching Piccadilly? Anyone you spoke to. A phone call. A stop along the way.”

His jaw tightened. The squint deepened into something closer to suspicion. “I don’t make calls. I don’t carry a phone. I drive, I go home.”

“You’re certain no one contacted you that evening.”

“I just told you. No calls.”

She held his gaze. He held hers. The gas heater clicked off, and the sudden absence of its sound made the room feel larger and colder. Ruth closed her notebook. The dry rustle of the pages was the only sound between them.

“Thank you, Mr. Bolt. That’s all I need for now.”

He stood when she stood, a courtesy that belonged to his training rather than his temperament. At the door, she paused. The black Humber sat in its bay, gleaming under the swaying bulb. The chamois leather was still folded on the wing mirror, edges aligned.

“One more thing,” she said. “The 11: 05 from Piccadilly. Does it run every night?”

“Every night. You can set your watch by it,” he said again, and the repetition landed like a fingerprint pressed twice into the same spot of wet ink.

Ruth walked back through the rain-damp streets to the exchange. The cold had tightened her fingers, and she fumbled her key into the lock, gripping the ring with her whole fist before the catch turned. The building was empty at this hour. The switchboard sat in its amber light, the rows of jacks quiet, the cords hanging still. Her desk lamp cast a cone of yellow across the blotter, the inkwell, the stack of fault logs she had not yet filed. Dust motes turned in the lamplight. The wall clock ticked with a dry, measured sound that filled the room the way water fills a glass, from the bottom up.

She pulled open the bottom drawer of her desk. The bus timetables were stored there, a bundle of flimsy pamphlets held together with an elastic band that had perished and cracked. She fanned through them until she found the Piccadilly routes. The paper was thin, the print small, the fold lines so worn they split when she opened the sheet flat.

She ran her finger down the column of times. The 11: 05 was there. It existed on paper, scheduled, printed, authoritative. Piccadilly Gardens to Wilmslow Road, via Oxford Road, seven stops, twenty-two minutes. She traced the route with her fingertip, reading each intermediate stop. The bus passed through St. Peter’s Square, then All Saints, then turned south along Oxford Road past the university buildings. Stop four was the Royal Infirmary. Stop five was the Whitworth Art Gallery. Stop six was a stretch of road with no landmark listed, only a notation that read “request stop.” Stop seven was Wilmslow Road, the park, the place Bolt had named.

She read the listing twice. The 11: 05 existed. The route matched his account. The timing was consistent with his claim of arriving home between half past eleven and quarter to twelve. On paper, the alibi was complete. It had a time, a route, and a destination. It was the kind of alibi that could be checked, and Bolt had offered it with the confidence of a man who expected it to be checked and welcomed the attempt.

But Ruth’s finger had stopped on stop six. The request stop. The stretch of road with no shelter, no lit storefronts, no landmark. A bus on a quiet Saturday evening with light rain and a half-empty route would slow there, or pass without stopping if no one stood. And if no one stood, the bus would reach Wilmslow Road two minutes early, perhaps three. Which meant a man who claimed to have arrived home by half past eleven might have arrived home by twenty past, and the seven minutes between the two were unaccounted for.

Seven minutes. Enough time for a phone call from a public box. Enough time to receive an instruction. Enough time to walk to a second location and be somewhere other than where the alibi placed him.

She stared at the timetable. The print was small and precise, the kind of type that admitted no ambiguity. The 11: 05 from Piccadilly existed. It ran every night. You could set your watch by it. But a schedule was a promise made by an institution, not a fact observed by a person. Bolt had built his evening on the promise, and Ruth could not yet tell whether the foundation was stone or sand.

The wall clock ticked. Dust turned in the lamplight. Ruth pressed her fingertip to the glass of the timetable, holding the 11: 05 in place, and read the route listing a third time. The request stop sat there on the page, unremarkable, a small notation between two more significant landmarks. It was the kind of detail a man rehearsing a story might not think to account for, because it was too small to matter. It was also the kind of detail that could unmake him.

She folded the timetable along its worn creases and set it on her desk beside the lamp. The paper caught the light and cast a faint shadow across the blotter. She would need to verify the actual running time, not the scheduled one. She would need to ride the bus herself, on a similar evening, in similar weather, and count the minutes between Piccadilly and Wilmslow Road.

But that was tomorrow’s work. Tonight, she sat with the alibi and its flaw, and with the deeper question that had followed her from the garage. Bolt had been precise about the bus. He had been precise about the route. He had been precise about the time. And a man who is precise about everything is either innocent or has spent a long time constructing the appearance of it.

The handwriting in the ledger was hers, but she had not written the entry. That contradiction had gnawed at her since she first discovered it, and now Bolt’s precision gave it new teeth. A man who rehearsed every detail of his alibi might also think to forge a ledger entry in a switchboard operator’s careful hand—or find some way to ensure she would never question what was written there. The trunk call to his number had existed, and someone had erased it. Bolt denied receiving any call. One of them was lying. The ledger did not lie. Only people did.

Ruth turned off the lamp. The room went dark except for the amber glow of the switchboard and the pale square of the window where streetlight pressed through the glass. She sat for a moment in the half-dark, listening to the clock, and then she locked the door behind her and walked out into the wet street with the timetable folded in her coat pocket and the shape of Bolt’s alibi turning in her mind like a key in a lock that might or might not fit.

She unfolded the timetable under the next streetlamp, the paper soft and worn from use, creased into quarters. The 11: 05 from Piccadilly stood in neat type: Wilmslow, Handforth, Gatley, Cheadle. A sensible route. A local service. She traced the line with her finger and felt the first cold doubt settle.

Bolt had told a human story. He had spoken of the rain, the empty platform, the driver who had nodded him aboard. He had mentioned the woman with the shopping basket and the boy on the bicycle who had nearly missed his stop. Small details. Convincing details. The kind that lodged in memory because they carried the texture of real life.

But the timetable offered no room for such things. It listed only times and places, indifferent to passengers and their small dramas. The 11: 05 existed. Its route was fixed. And somewhere in the gap between the schedule and Bolt’s story, Ruth felt the shape of a lie.

She had expected to close the matter tonight. Bolt had seemed to want the same—his hands folded on the table, his answers ready, his eyes steady and patient. He had presented himself as a man grateful for the chance to be cleared. And perhaps he was. Perhaps the story was true.

But a man who constructs an alibi from a bus timetable has either caught that bus or studied the schedule with care. The woman with the shopping basket. The boy on the bicycle. Convenient witnesses, if they existed. More convenient still if they did not.

Ruth refolded the timetable and pressed it flat against her chest. The rain had stopped. The street shone black and empty. She would need to walk that route herself, ride that bus, sit in the seat Bolt had described and see what the driver remembered. She would need to find the woman, find the boy, or find the absence where they should have been.

The timetable went into her pocket. The doubt stayed where it was.
