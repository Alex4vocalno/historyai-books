Chapter 20

Marjorie's Confession

────────────────────────────────────────

The maintenance bay hummed with the low frequency drone of dormant relay racks, a sound Ruth had stopped hearing years ago, though now it pressed against her eardrums like a warning. Alec Dunbar’s words still echoed in the narrow space between them, hanging in the air with the smell of solder and ozone. What she might have overheard. Not her job. Something far more dangerous.

Ruth stood motionless, her hand still resting on the edge of the metal workbench where Alec’s fault log lay open. The stolen page burned against her palm inside her skirt pocket. She could feel its sharp corner pressing into her thigh, a reminder of how far she had already stepped outside procedure. Alec watched her with those flat, calculating eyes, his solid shoulders hunched in that permanent posture of a man protecting something hidden.

“You’re telling me,” Ruth said slowly, her voice level despite the tremor in her chest, “that Marjorie Finch let someone use her extension.”

“I’m telling you nothing, Supervisor.” Alec’s tone remained clipped, precise. He picked up a schematic and rolled it with deliberate care. “I’m merely recalling what I observed during a routine maintenance check. A technician notices things. Cable routing. Unusual patch patterns. Operators who stay past their shift to chat with friends.”

The implication settled over Ruth. Friends. Terence Bolt had been a friend to many in the exchange, with his easy smile and his way of making every operator feel seen. She had watched him charm his way past security, past protocol, past good sense.

“And you covered for her,” Ruth said. “The fault log. The line outage you falsified. That wasn’t for Bolt at all.”

Alec’s pen paused mid-stroke. When he looked up, his expression remained unreadable, but something flickered behind his eyes. Not guilt. Something closer to resignation.

“I covered a fault that needed covering. The reason mattered less than the result.”

“The reason always matters.” Ruth heard her own voice harden. “The ledger doesn’t lie. Only people do. And you, Alec, have been lying since the moment I first questioned you.”

He set down the pen. The metal-bound fault log sat between them, a record of deception that Ruth now understood had been pointing in the wrong direction all along. She had been so certain the falsified entry was evidence of Bolt’s accomplice. She had built her investigation around that assumption, chasing a conspiracy that had never existed.

“A woman feared losing her position,” Alec said quietly. “I made a choice. You can interpret that as you wish.”

Ruth’s mind raced through the implications. If Marjorie had let Bolt use her extension, then the unlogged trunk call, the forged ledger page, the entire elaborate cover-up, all of it traced back to a single moment of misguided kindness on the switchboard floor. A favor for a friend. A family emergency, Bolt had probably claimed. And Marjorie, generous, lonely Marjorie, had believed him.

She thought of the scar on her hip, the one Alec had weaponized against her in this very room just hours before. A childhood accident. A bicycle. A secret she had carried for decades, now exposed and useless as leverage. Alec had invaded her privacy to protect himself. But he had also, in his calculated way, handed her the truth.

“If Marjorie enabled that call,” Ruth said carefully, “then she enabled whatever followed. She’s not just a witness. She’s an accomplice.”

Alec’s mouth twisted, not quite a smile. “That would depend on what she knew. And what she chose not to ask.”

Ruth turned away from the workbench, her heels clicking against the concrete floor. The maintenance bay stretched before her, a corridor of silent equipment and shadowed corners. Somewhere beyond these walls, the night operators were minding their boards, routing calls across the city, oblivious to the weight pressing down on their supervisor’s shoulders.

She needed to find Marjorie. She needed answers. And she needed them before the night shift ended and the morning supervisor arrived to discover what Ruth had stolen, what she had concealed, and how far she had strayed from the orderly procedures that had defined her career.

The tea cupboard was tucked behind the supervisor’s office, a cramped space that smelled of loose leaves and damp linoleum. Ruth found Marjorie there, standing with her back to the door, her hands wrapped around a porcelain cup that steamed in the cool air. Two cups sat on the narrow counter, freshly poured and untouched.

“Supervisor.” Marjorie’s voice was too bright, too quick. She didn’t turn around. “I was just making tea. Would you like—“

“I need to speak with you.” Ruth stepped inside and closed the door behind her. The lock clicked, a small sound that seemed to echo in the tight space. “About Terence Bolt.”

Marjorie’s shoulders stiffened. The steam from her cup curled upward, dissipating against the low ceiling. For a long moment, neither woman moved. Then Marjorie set down her cup with a trembling clatter and turned to face Ruth.

Her eyes were red-rimmed, her face pale beneath the harsh fluorescent light. She looked older than her forty-three years, the lines around her mouth deeper than Ruth remembered. This was not the Marjorie who gossiped by the switchboard, who rolled cigarettes with steady hands and traded rumors like currency. This was a woman who had been carrying something heavy for far too long.

“I don’t know what you mean,” Marjorie said. But her voice cracked on the last word.

Ruth reached into her pocket and withdrew the folded page she had taken from Alec’s fault log. She smoothed it flat against the counter, beside the untouched cups of tea. The entry glared up at them, the falsified line outage that had started everything.

“Alec Dunbar falsified this record,” Ruth said. “Not for Bolt. For you.”

Marjorie’s gaze dropped to the page. Her hands, hanging at her sides, began to twist the fabric of her apron. Ruth had seen that gesture before, on operators who had made mistakes they couldn’t undo. It was the posture of someone calculating how much had been discovered, and how much could still be hidden.

“I don’t know what Alec told you,” Marjorie began, “but he’s mistaken if he thinks—“

“He told me you let Terry use your extension.” Ruth kept her voice level, professional, though her heart was pounding against her ribs. “He told me you were afraid of losing your job. And he told me that what you overheard that night was far more dangerous than any late arrival.”

The color drained from Marjorie’s face. Her mouth opened, closed, opened again. No sound came out.

“I’m not asking you to remember,” Ruth continued, her tone hardening. “I’m asking you to think. Think about what you did. Think about what Terry Bolt used that extension for. And think about what it means that someone went to extraordinary lengths to erase any record of that call.”

Marjorie slumped against the counter. Her hand found the second cup of tea, lifted it, set it down again without drinking. The steam continued to rise, curling around her fingers like a ghost.

“He said it was an emergency.” Marjorie’s voice was barely above a whisper. “Family trouble. His sister, he said, was in trouble, and he needed to reach her without going through the main board. Without the call being logged.”

Ruth felt the words land like stones in her chest. Of course. The trunk call that had never appeared in any official record. The call that had been placed through a patch panel bypass, with the silent alarm disabled, routed through an operator’s extension to avoid detection. An inside job, as Ruth had suspected. But not the kind she had imagined.

“And you believed him.”

“He was my friend.” Marjorie’s voice cracked. “He’d been coming ‘round for years. Always with a smile, always with a kind word. He remembered my birthday. He asked about my mother in hospital. He made me feel…” She trailed off, her eyes glistening. “He made me feel seen.”

Ruth thought of the ledger in her desk drawer, the forged page in her pocket. She thought of Violet Sykes and her petty theft, of Alec Dunbar and his quiet intimidation, of all the small compromises that accumulated like dust in the corners of this building. Everyone here had secrets. Everyone had reasons to look the other way.

But this was different. This was not a stolen ticket or a falsified fault log. This was a call that had enabled blackmail. Perhaps worse.

“What did you overhear, Marjorie?”

Marjorie’s hands trembled as she reached into her apron and withdrew a cigarette. Her fingers, usually so steady, fumbled with the match. The first strike failed. The second caught, flaring briefly before she cupped it to the tip. Tobacco shreds spilled onto the linoleum, a brown scatter against the grey, and for a moment Ruth watched them drift like dead leaves.

“I didn’t mean to,” Marjorie said. The smoke curled around her words. “He told me to step away. Said it was private, family business. I went to make tea, just like tonight. But the extension…” She took a shaky breath. “The extension has an open circuit when the receiver is off the hook. I heard voices. Not clearly, just fragments.”

Ruth leaned forward. “What fragments?”

“A name. Someone important, I think. Someone with power.” Marjorie’s eyes darted to the door, as if someone might be listening. “And then Terry’s voice, saying something about evidence. About payment. About making sure no one ever found out.”

The words settled over Ruth like a weight. Blackmail. The call had been blackmail, placed through Marjorie’s extension, routed past every safeguard the exchange had built. And Marjorie, kind, lonely Marjorie, had enabled it without knowing.

“Did you tell anyone?”

Marjorie’s laugh was hollow. “Who would I tell? That I let a man use my extension for an unlogged call? That I heard something I shouldn’t have?” She shook her head. “I was terrified. When I heard about the murder, when I realized what Terry might have done…” Her voice broke. “I thought if I stayed quiet, if I kept my head down, it would all go away.”

Ruth looked at the woman before her. Marjorie Finch, who had worked the switchboards for twenty-two years. Who knew every operator’s birthday, every supervisor’s preference, every piece of gossip that passed through these walls. Who had let a charming man with a warm smile use her extension for a call that had ended in blood.

The fault log made sense now. Alec had falsified the line outage not to protect Bolt, but to protect Marjorie. Old-fashioned chivalry, he had called it. Or perhaps something more pragmatic. A favor owed, a debt that might one day be called in.

But the forgery in Ruth’s handwriting, the ledger page that had been fabricated to look like her own work, that remained a mystery. Someone had covered the call’s origin, and someone had framed Ruth for the cover-up. Alec had done neither, apparently. Which meant there was still another player in this game.

“You need to tell me everything,” Ruth said. “Every detail. Every word you remember. I can’t protect you if I don’t know the full truth.”

Marjorie looked up, her eyes wet. “Protect me? Supervisor, I don’t see how anyone can protect me now. I let a murderer use my extension. I kept quiet when I should have spoken. If the police find out…”

“They may still.” Ruth’s voice was gentle but firm. “But I need to understand what happened before I decide what to do next.”

Marjorie stared at her for a long moment. The cigarette burned forgotten between her fingers, ash dropping to the floor. Then, slowly, she nodded.

“Terry came to me that night. Late, past the main shift. He had a way of appearing when the floor was quiet, when only the night operators were at their boards. He asked if he could use my extension. Said it was urgent, a family matter, and he couldn’t go through official channels.” Marjorie’s voice steadied as she spoke, as if the act of confession was releasing something she had held too tight. “I didn’t think. I just… I let him in. I stepped away, like he asked. And I heard what I heard.”

“Did he see you afterward?”

Marjorie shook her head. “I don’t think so. I stayed in the tea cupboard until he left. By the time I came out, he was gone. But the next day, when I heard about what had happened, about the murder…” She shuddered. “I was so frightened. I thought if anyone found out I’d let him in, they’d blame me. They’d say I was part of it.”

Ruth absorbed this. The picture was clearer now, though pieces still refused to fit. Terence Bolt had used Marjorie’s extension to place a blackmail call. Someone had disabled the silent alarm and bypassed the main board. Someone had forged a ledger page in Ruth’s handwriting to cover the gap. And someone had killed Terence Bolt before he could reveal what he knew.

But who? Alec claimed innocence of the forgery, and his story checked out. Violet had her own secrets, but petty theft and alibi fabrication hardly pointed to murder. Marjorie was a victim of her own kindness, not a conspirator.

There was still a piece missing. A connection Ruth couldn’t yet see.

“What will you do?” Marjorie asked. Her voice was small, pleading. “Supervisor, I have nowhere else to go. This job is all I have. If you report me…”

Ruth looked at the woman before her. Marjorie Finch, who had made a terrible mistake. Who had believed a charming man’s lies and let him use the switchboard for murder. Who was now standing in a tea cupboard, trembling, waiting for Ruth to decide her fate.

The rules were clear. A supervisor’s duty was to the exchange, to the integrity of the records, to the truth. Ruth had built her career on those principles. The ledger doesn’t lie. Only people do.

But she had also lied. She had stolen a fault log page, concealed evidence, and investigated outside official channels. She had her own secrets now, her own compromises. And she had seen what happened when those compromises accumulated, when good people looked the other way for too long.

“I need time,” Ruth said finally. “I need to think.”

Marjorie’s face crumpled with relief. “Thank you. Thank you, Supervisor. I swear, I didn’t know what he would do. I swear on my mother’s grave.”

Ruth held up a hand. “I’m not promising anything. I’m only saying I need to understand the full picture before I decide.” She picked up the forged fault log page and folded it back into her pocket. “Stay here. Finish your tea. And Marjorie?” She met the woman’s eyes. “Don’t talk to anyone. Not Alec. Not Violet. Not until I say.”

Marjorie nodded quickly. “Of course. Whatever you say.”

Ruth turned and unlocked the door. The corridor outside was empty, the switchboard floor humming with its eternal drone. She stepped out, leaving Marjorie alone with two untouched cups of tea and the weight of her confession.

The main exchange hall stretched before Ruth, rows of silent switchboards standing like sentinels in the dim light. The night operators were at their stations, plugging and unplugging connections, their voices low and rhythmic as they routed calls through the sleeping city. None of them looked up as Ruth passed. None of them knew what she carried in her pocket, or what she had just learned in the tea cupboard.

She walked slowly, her heels clicking against the linoleum, her mind turning over the pieces of the puzzle. Marjorie had let Bolt use her extension. Alec had falsified the fault log to cover her mistake. But the ledger forgery, the page in Ruth’s own handwriting, that remained unexplained. Someone else had been involved. Someone with access to personnel files, someone who knew about Ruth’s past, someone who had the skill to forge her hand.

The maintenance bay door appeared on her left. She paused, looking at it, thinking of Alec inside with his schematics and his secrets. He had given her Marjorie’s involvement, but he had held back something else. She could feel it. The way he had watched her, the careful way he had phrased his confession. There was more he wasn’t saying.

But that was a question for another moment. For now, Ruth had what she needed. The call’s origin point. The method of placement. The reason for the cover-up.

She continued walking, past the technical office, past the records cupboard, past the coat room where Violet Sykes had once cornered her with threats and desperation. The building seemed different now, its familiar corridors shadowed with new meaning. Every operator she passed might be hiding something. Every record might be false. Every connection might lead somewhere she didn’t want to go.

Her office door stood ajar. She pushed it open and stepped inside, closing it behind her. The desk drawer where she had hidden the page she’d torn from the maintenance ledger seemed to hum with accusation. She crossed to it, pulled it open, and stared at the papers inside.

The forgery stared back at her. Her own handwriting, duplicated with uncanny precision. A trunk call that had never existed, recorded by a hand that had never written it.

Ruth thought of Marjorie’s tears. Of Alec’s quiet threats. Of Violet’s desperate lies. Of all the people in this building who had compromised themselves, step by step, until the compromises became a path they couldn’t leave.

She had a choice now. She could take everything she knew to the police. She could report Marjorie, confront Alec, expose Violet, and let the consequences fall where they may. It would be the right thing. The proper thing. The thing a good supervisor would do.

Or she could wait. She could use what she knew to set a trap. She could find out who had forged that ledger page, who had killed Terence Bolt, and who had pulled strings from the shadows. She could uncover the full truth, even if it meant bending more rules, even if it meant becoming something she had never intended to be.

The telephone on her desk sat silent. She could pick it up right now and call the police. She could end this.

Ruth looked at the instrument. Then, slowly, deliberately, she closed the desk drawer without touching it.

Not yet. There was still more to learn. And somewhere in this building, someone was watching, waiting for her to make a mistake.

She would not give them that satisfaction.

Ruth turned off her office light and stepped back into the corridor. The night shift had hours left to run, and she intended to use every one of them.
