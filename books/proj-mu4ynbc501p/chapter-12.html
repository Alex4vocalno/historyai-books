Chapter 12

Violet's Broken Timetable

────────────────────────────────────────

Ruth’s thumb leaves a grey smudge on the library’s leather-bound timetable. The binding crackles under her grip, and she eases her hold, conscious of the silence pressing against her from all sides. The exchange’s reference library sits at the end of the corridor past the night operators, a cramped room lined with technical manuals, bound directories, and archived logs nobody ever consults. A single green-shaded lamp pools light across the reading stand. The air smells of dry paper and old dust, and the building’s constant hum seems distant here, muffled by the weight of forgotten records.

She has come here alone. After Alec’s whispered revelation about the scar on her hip, she had walked out of the technical office on unsteady legs, the stolen fault log page hidden in her blouse. Her desk drawer had seemed too obvious a hiding place, her handbag too risky to carry through the corridors. So she had folded the page flat against her sternum, felt its edge dig into her skin with every step, and made her way to the one room in the building where nobody would think to look for her.

But the page is not why she is here now.

Violet Sykes had given her an alibi. A simple, verifiable story about taking the late bus home on the night the councillor died. Ruth had accepted it at face value, grateful for one less thread to pull. But Alec’s knowledge of her secret had shaken something loose in her thinking. If someone could know that, what else might they know? What other small lies had she let pass because they came from trusted faces?

She opens the timetable to the relevant page. The leather cover falls back under its own weight, and the paper crackles in the silent library. City Bus Services, Winter Schedule. The print is small and dense, arranged in columns by route number and departure time. She runs her finger down the page, smudging newsprint onto her skin, looking for Route 23.

Violet had been specific. She had mentioned the number more than once, as if repetition could make it true. The eleven-fifteen from the terminus, she had said. I caught it just in time. The driver can vouch for me.

Ruth’s nail stops on a blank space where a ‘23’ should be.

She blinks. Looks again. The column heading reads “Route 23 — City Center to Western Suburbs.” But below it, where the departure times should list in neat half-hour intervals, there is a gap. The last entry reads “21:45.” The next entry reads “06:15.”

No eleven-fifteen. No late service at all.

Her breath fogs the glass of the reading lamp as she leans closer. The timetable is clear. Route 23 does not run past nine forty-five in the evening. The last bus leaves the terminus nearly two hours before Violet claimed to board it.

Ruth sits back. The wooden chair creaks beneath her. Her mind runs through the implications, cold and methodical. Violet could have made a mistake. She could have confused the route number, the time, the direction of travel. But she had been so certain. She had offered the driver as a witness, a man who would remember her face on a late run that does not exist.

The lie is not casual. It is constructed. Deliberate.

Ruth closes the timetable and runs her palm across the cover, feeling the leather grain under her fingers. She thinks of Violet’s face that morning after the councillor’s death was reported. Pale, drawn, hands wrapped around a cup of tea as if it were a lifeline. Ruth had interpreted that as shock. Now she wonders what else it might have been.

She stands, her legs stiff from sitting too long, and tucks the timetable under her arm. The library door opens onto the corridor, and the building’s hum returns, the distant clatter of plugs and the murmur of operators’ voices. Ruth walks toward the canteen, her heels clicking against the linoleum. Her reflection passes in the dark windows of the switchboard room, a woman in a skirt and blouse carrying a leather-bound book, her face set in an expression that shows nothing.

The canteen sits at the back of the ground floor, a narrow room with a serving hatch and a cluster of round tables. At this hour, mid-afternoon between shifts, it is nearly empty. A few operators sit near the window, their voices low, their spoons clinking against cups. The smell of stewed tea and damp wool hangs in the air.

Violet Sykes sits alone at the far table, her back to the wall, her hands wrapped around a cup of something that has long gone cold. She is a slight woman, dark-haired, with a habit of tucking her chin when spoken to. She looks up as Ruth approaches, and something flickers across her face. Recognition, then something else. Wariness.

“May I sit?”

Violet nods, a small, quick motion. Ruth pulls out the chair opposite and sets the timetable on the table between them. The leather cover faces upward, giving nothing away.

“You look tired,” Ruth says.

“Long shift.”

“I need to ask you something.”

Violet’s hands tighten around her cup. “About the other night?”

Ruth studies her face. The dark circles under her eyes. The way her gaze keeps flicking to the timetable and away again. “You told me you caught the eleven-fifteen bus home. Route 23.”

“That’s right.”

“The driver would remember you.”

Violet’s chin tucks. “He might. I don’t know. I didn’t speak to him.”

Ruth opens the timetable. She turns to the relevant page and slides it across the table, orienting it so Violet can read. Her finger points to the blank space in the column.

“There is no eleven-fifteen on Route 23. The last bus leaves at nine forty-five.”

Silence. The canteen’s background noise seems to recede, leaving only the two of them at the table. Violet stares at the timetable. Her lips part, then close. Her spoon clinks once against the side of her cup, then lies still.

“I don’t understand,” she says.

“The schedule is clear. There is no late service on that route. There hasn’t been for years, according to this edition. So the bus you claimed to take could not have existed.”

Violet’s face has gone pale. The color drains from her cheeks, leaving her skin waxen under the canteen’s fluorescent lights. Her fingers tremble against the cup’s rim.

“I must have… I thought…”

“You were specific, Violet. Route 23. Eleven-fifteen. You offered the driver as a witness. That is not a casual mistake. That is a story.”

The words hang between them. Violet’s eyes dart to the other tables, checking if anyone has heard. The operators near the window continue their quiet conversation, oblivious.

“I didn’t…” She stops. Swallows. Her voice drops to a whisper. “Please. Supervisor Calloway. Ruth.”

Ruth waits. She does not soften her expression. She does not reach out or offer comfort. She sits in silence and lets the weight of the lie do its work.

“I was wrong about the number,” Violet says. “I get confused sometimes. The routes, the times. It all blends together.”

“You don’t seem confused now. You seem frightened.”

Violet’s jaw tightens. For a moment, Ruth sees something flash behind her eyes. Defiance, perhaps. Or calculation. Then it is gone, replaced by a look of weary surrender.

“I didn’t take the bus.”

“Where were you?”

Violet shakes her head. A small, tight motion. “It doesn’t matter.”

“It matters to me. It matters to the police, when they ask why you lied about your whereabouts on the night a councillor died.”

“They won’t ask.”

“They might. And if they do, what will you tell them?”

Violet’s hands have gone still around her cup. The trembling has stopped. Her face settles into a mask that Ruth recognizes. It is the same expression she sees in the mirror some mornings, the look of someone holding too much inside.

“I was somewhere else,” Violet says. “That’s all.”

“Where?”

“I can’t say.”

Ruth leans forward. Her voice drops to match Violet’s whisper. “You gave me a false alibi. You looked me in the face and told me a story you knew was untrue. Do you understand what that means? Do you understand what I have to do now?”

Violet’s eyes meet hers. There is fear there, yes. But there is also something else. A kind of desperate resolve.

“You have to do what you think is right.”

“I think right is finding the truth. I think right is understanding why someone would lie about where they were on a night when a man died and a call went missing from my records.”

Violet flinches at that. The mention of the missing call. Her gaze drops to the table, to the timetable, to her own hands wrapped around the cold cup.

“I didn’t have anything to do with that.”

“Then tell me where you were.”

“I can’t.”

“Why not?”

Violet’s lips press together. A thin line. She shakes her head again, and Ruth sees the decision settle in her frame. She will not speak. Not now. Not here.

Ruth sits back. The wooden chair creaks. She looks at Violet, really looks, and sees not the girl she has worked beside for three years, the quiet operator with the ready smile and the careful hands. She sees a stranger. A woman with secrets that run deep enough to risk her job, her reputation, perhaps more.

“You need to understand something,” Ruth says, keeping her voice level. “I trusted you. I defended you when the shift rosters came up short. I gave you the benefit of every doubt because I thought you were honest. Because I thought I knew you.”

Violet’s chin trembles. “You do know me.”

“I know a version of you. Apparently not the real one.”

The words land harder than Ruth intended. Violet’s eyes glisten, but she does not cry. She blinks rapidly and presses her lips tighter together.

“I’m sorry,” she whispers.

“Sorry for lying, or sorry for being caught?”

Violet does not answer.

Ruth picks up the timetable and closes it with a soft thud. The canteen’s background noise seems to rush back, the clink of cups and murmur of voices filling the space where silence had been. She stands, the chair scraping against the floor.

“If you decide you want to tell me the truth,” Ruth says, “you know where to find me. But do not wait too long. The truth has a way of coming out, and I would rather hear it from you than from someone else.”

She walks away without looking back. Her heels click against the linoleum, each step measured and precise. The canteen door swings shut behind her, and she is in the corridor again, the timetable under her arm, the weight of what she has learned pressing against her chest.

Violet Sykes lied. One of her own operators, a woman she had trusted, had constructed a false alibi and offered it without hesitation. And she refused to explain why.

Ruth’s mind turns to the other names on her shift list. Alec Dunbar, already caught falsifying records, already revealed as someone who knows secrets he should not know. Violet Sykes, with her impossible bus ride and her stubborn silence. How many others? How deep does this run?

She passes the switchboard room and glances through the window. The operators sit at their posts, plugs in hand, faces lit by the glow of the boards. They look innocent. They look ordinary. But so did Violet, until an hour ago.

The corridor stretches ahead of her, lined with doors that lead to offices and storage rooms and the technical archives. Somewhere in this building, the truth is waiting. A forged page. A missing call. A dead councillor. And now, a woman who would rather risk everything than admit where she was.

Ruth thinks of the stolen fault log page hidden against her chest. She thinks of Alec’s quiet voice, intimate and threatening, describing a scar she had never spoken of to anyone. She thinks of the ledger page forged in her own handwriting, a perfect imitation that still haunts her dreams.

The forger knows her. The forger has access to her work, her records, her past. And the forger is still here, walking these corridors, sitting at these desks, waiting for Ruth to make a mistake.

She reaches her desk and pulls out her chair. The drawer slides open, and she slips the timetable inside, next to her reference sheets and her private notes. The fault log page stays where it is, pressed against her skin, a secret she will keep until she understands who she can trust.

Which is no one.

The realization settles in her stomach like cold tea. She cannot trust Alec. She cannot trust Violet. She cannot trust the system that was supposed to keep these records honest. All she has is herself, her memory, and her ability to see patterns where others see only noise.

She opens her notebook to a fresh page and begins to write. Violet Sykes. Route 23. No late service. Alibi false. Refused to explain. She writes quickly, her pen scratching against the paper, capturing the facts before they can blur or fade. Below that, she adds: Alec Dunbar. Fault log altered. Knows about the scar.

Two names. Two lies. Two threads in a web she does not yet understand.

The building hums around her, the constant sound of the exchange at work. Calls connect and disconnect. Operators speak in measured tones. The machinery of communication grinds on, indifferent to the small human drama unfolding in its shadow.

Ruth closes her notebook and slides it into the drawer. Her reflection looks back at her from the dark window, a woman with tired eyes and a set jaw. She does not look like someone who has just discovered that her investigation has turned inward, that the forger might be one of her own.

She looks like someone who has known it all along.

The shift change comes at six. Ruth stays at her desk as the day operators file out and the night shift arrives, their faces familiar and strange in the half-light of the corridor. She watches them pass, wondering what secrets they carry. Who else has lied? Who else is hiding something?

Violet does not appear. She must have left early, slipped out through the back entrance while Ruth was making her notes. Or perhaps she is still in the canteen, sitting with her cold tea, trying to decide what to do next.

It does not matter. Ruth has time. She has patience. And she has something Violet does not: the knowledge that her alibi has collapsed, and the certainty that the truth will come out eventually.

She rises from her desk and walks toward the technical office. The corridor is quieter now, most of the staff gone, the exchange settling into its night rhythm. Her footsteps echo against the linoleum, a steady beat that matches the hum of the relay racks.

Alec Dunbar’s door stands closed. The light is off inside. He has gone home, or perhaps he is working late in some other part of the building. Ruth pauses outside, her hand on the frame, listening to the silence beyond.

She thinks of the fault log page against her skin. She thinks of the missing call, the one that should have been recorded but was not. The call that someone wanted to erase so badly that they forged her handwriting to cover its absence.

Terence Bolt. The councillor’s driver. The name surfaces in her memory, unbidden. A missing trunk call to Terence Bolt existed. That fact has been sitting in her notes for days, a thread she has not yet pulled. Why would someone call the driver on the night the councillor died? And why would that call need to disappear?

She turns away from Alec’s door and continues down the corridor. The technical archives are two floors below, in a basement room lined with metal shelves. The secure records are kept there, the original logs that cannot be altered without a key and a supervisor’s signature.

Her key is in her pocket. Her signature is her own.

The stairwell is dark, the light above the landing flickering in an irregular rhythm. Ruth descends, her hand trailing along the railing, her footsteps echoing in the enclosed space. The air grows cooler as she goes down, carrying the scent of concrete and old paper.

The archive door stands at the end of a short hallway. It is heavy, metal-bound, with a small window set at eye level. Ruth peers through the glass, but the room beyond is dark, empty of movement.

She unlocks the door and steps inside. The fluorescent lights flicker on, casting a harsh white glow across the metal shelves. Row after row of bound volumes, each labeled with a year and a department. Fault logs. Call ledgers. Personnel records. The raw material of the exchange’s memory.

Ruth walks to the section marked “Fault Logs — Current Year” and pulls out the most recent volume. The cover is plain, black, unmarked. She carries it to the reading table in the corner and sets it down.

The stolen page burns against her chest. She pulls it out now, unfolding it carefully, smoothing the creases with her palm. The entry for the night in question is there, the time altered, the handwriting a perfect match for her own.

But the original record should still exist, somewhere in this archive. A duplicate, perhaps. Or a carbon copy that was never altered. If she can find it, she can prove the forgery.

She opens the bound volume and begins to search.

The pages turn beneath her fingers, each one a record of faults and repairs, line outages and service calls. The entries are neat, orderly, written in various hands. She finds Alec’s handwriting, the precise script of a man who takes pride in his work. She finds her own entries, the familiar loops and curves of her signature. She finds the entries of other technicians, other supervisors, a chorus of voices preserved in ink.

And then, on the page corresponding to the night the councillor died, she finds something else.

A gap. A torn edge where a page should be.

Ruth stares at it. The paper has been ripped out, cleanly, as if someone used a straightedge to remove it. The surrounding pages are intact, their entries undisturbed. But the page that should contain the fault log for that night is gone.

Someone got here before her. Someone with access to the archives, knowledge of the records, and a reason to destroy the evidence.

She thinks of Alec, his quiet presence in the technical office, his hand on the metal-bound log. She thinks of Violet, her pale face in the canteen, her refusal to explain. She thinks of the other names on her shift list, the faces she passes every day.

The forger is still here. And they are ahead of her.

Ruth closes the volume and returns it to the shelf. Her mind races, sorting through possibilities. If the original record is gone, she will need another source. The call ledger, perhaps. The switchboard logs. Somewhere in this building, the truth still exists.

She walks back to the door and steps into the hallway. The lights flicker behind her as she leaves, the archive returning to darkness.

The corridor stretches ahead, long and empty. At the far end, a door stands slightly ajar, a sliver of light spilling through the gap. Ruth pauses. That door leads to a storage room, rarely used, filled with old equipment and forgotten files. It should be locked.

She moves toward it, her footsteps silent on the concrete floor. The light from the gap is steady, not flickering. Someone has turned on a lamp inside.

She reaches the door and pushes it open.

The room beyond is small, cramped, the walls lined with metal shelving. In the center, a desk has been pulled from the corner, its surface covered with papers. A lamp sits on one side, casting a warm pool of light across the documents.

And there, standing with her back to the door, is Violet Sykes.

She does not turn at the sound of the door. She stands frozen, her hand on a file folder, her shoulders rigid.

Ruth speaks first, her voice calm.

“I thought you went home.”

Violet’s breath catches. A small sound in the quiet room.

“I forgot something.”

“What did you forget?”

Violet turns slowly. Her face is pale, her eyes wide. In her hand, she holds a file folder, its contents hidden against her chest.

“Nothing important.”

Ruth steps into the room, letting the door close behind her. “Let me see.”

Violet shakes her head. A small, tight motion.

“Give me the folder, Violet.”

“I can’t.”

Ruth moves closer. The lamp casts strange shadows across the shelves, the old equipment looming like silent observers. She can see Violet’s hands trembling, the folder clutched against her.

“If you do not give it to me,” Ruth says, “I will have to assume the worst.”

Violet’s eyes meet hers. There is fear there, and desperation, and something else. Something that looks almost like resolve.

“You do not understand,” Violet says. “None of this is what you think.”

“Then explain it to me.”

“I can’t. Not yet.”

Ruth holds out her hand. “The folder.”

For a long moment, neither of them moves. The building hums around them, the distant sound of the exchange at work. Then, slowly, Violet extends her arm and places the folder in Ruth’s hand.

Ruth opens it.

Inside is a single sheet of paper, a photocopy of a call ledger entry. The time stamp reads 23:47. The extension number is one she does not recognize. And the destination is listed as a trunk call, routed to a number that makes her breath catch.

Terence Bolt. The councillor’s driver.

The missing call. It exists after all. Not erased, but hidden.

Ruth looks up at Violet. The operator’s face is a mask of exhaustion and fear.

“Where did you get this?”

“I can’t tell you.”

“You had this all along. You knew about the call, and you said nothing.”

“I was trying to protect—“

She stops. Her mouth closes with an audible click.

“Protect who?”

Violet shakes her head. Her jaw is set, her eyes bright with unshed tears.

Ruth folds the paper and places it in her pocket, next to the stolen fault log page. The weight of evidence is growing, pressing against her, demanding answers she does not yet have.

“You will tell me,” she says. “Eventually. You will tell me everything.”

Violet’s chin trembles. “I know.”

Ruth turns and opens the door. The corridor beyond is dark, the light at the far end seeming very far away. She steps out, leaving Violet alone in the cramped room with the lamp and the shelves and the weight of her silence.

The door closes behind her with a soft click.

Ruth walks back toward the stairs, her footsteps steady, her mind clear. The forger is still ahead of her. The secrets are still buried. But now she has something she did not have before.

A name. A number. A call that should not exist.

And the certainty that Violet Sykes knows more than she will admit.

The night shift has begun. The exchange hums around her, indifferent to the small human drama unfolding in its shadow. Ruth climbs the stairs, her hand on the railing, her eyes fixed ahead.

The truth is waiting. And she will find it, no matter how many lies stand in her way.
