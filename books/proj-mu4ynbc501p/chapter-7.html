Chapter 7

Pressure from the Police

────────────────────────────────────────

Violet’s tears had dried to salt tracks by the time Ruth made it back from the vault, but the final demand notice lay crumpled in the girl’s pocket like a stone she couldn’t swallow. Ruth had offered what comfort she could—a hand on the shoulder, a murmur about advance wages tomorrow—but her mind was already elsewhere, turning over the forgery in the ledger, the missing call to Terence Bolt, the question that had haunted her since she first recognized her own counterfeit signature.

She had barely settled at her desk when the front bell announced a visitor.

Rain dripped from the hem of a dark gabardine coat onto the clean linoleum, pooling in a spreading stain. Detective Sergeant Miller stood in the doorway of her office without invitation, his hat in one hand and his eyes moving across the room with the practiced assessment of a man who catalogued exits before he sat down.

“Miss Calloway.” His voice carried the flat authority of official business. “I hope we’re not interrupting your morning.”

Ruth rose, her fingers instinctively moving to straighten the papers on her desk. The ledger sat in the center, its leather cover closed, the forged page hidden somewhere in the middle like a wound beneath a bandage. “Detective Sergeant Miller. This is unexpected.”

“Is it?” He crossed to the chair opposite her desk without waiting to be asked, lowering himself with the deliberate weight of a man who intended to stay. His coat left a damp outline on the upholstery. “We requested the call records for the night of Councillor Hammond’s death. You provided a summary. I’m here for the full ledger.”

The ledger. Of course. The police had every right to examine the original record. Ruth had known this moment would come—had been dreading it since she first opened the drawer and saw her own handwriting staring back at her from a page she had never written.

She sat slowly, keeping her hands flat on the desktop where he could see them. “The summary was complete, Detective Sergeant. I checked it myself.”

“Then you won’t mind if I check it again.” Miller’s smile didn’t reach his eyes. He had the look of a man who had seen too many liars to be fooled by a calm exterior. “The Councillor’s death is raising questions at the highest levels. His driver claims he made a call that night—a call we can’t find in any record.”

Terence Bolt. The name sent a jolt through Ruth’s chest. She had seen it in the ledger—or rather, she had seen where it should have been. A trunk call, placed late on the night of the councillor’s death, connecting the exchange to a number that now existed only in the negative space of a forged page.

“I reviewed the records thoroughly,” Ruth said, and the words felt like glass in her throat. “There was no call from Mr. Bolt logged that night.”

Miller tilted his head, watching her. “No call. Or no call that was recorded?”

“I beg your pardon?”

“The distinction matters, Miss Calloway. A call that wasn’t made is one thing. A call that was made but not recorded—“ He let the sentence hang, unfinished. “That would be a very different matter. That would suggest someone at this exchange had something to hide.”

The hum of the switchboard filtered through the wall, a constant electric murmur that Ruth had worked beside for fifteen years. It sounded different now. Hostile. She could feel Miller’s gaze like a pressure against her skin.

“Our records are accurate,” she heard herself say. “I supervise every entry myself.”

“Every entry.” Miller leaned forward, his elbows finding her desk. “That’s quite a claim. Six operators on the night shift, isn’t it? Do you watch every hand that touches the log? Every correction, every amendment?”

Ruth’s throat tightened. He was circling something, and she couldn’t tell if he knew what he was hunting or if he was simply flushing the brush to see what broke cover.

“I review the final ledger each morning,” she said carefully. “Any irregularities would be noted and investigated.”

“Investigated by you.” Miller’s tone carried a dangerous lightness. “And reported to whom? The telephone board? The police? Or do these irregularities simply… disappear?”

The ledger sat between them, its secrets locked inside. Ruth could feel the weight of the forged page pressing against her from within, a silent accusation. If she opened the book now, if she turned to the relevant hour and showed Miller what she had found, she could unburden herself. She could tell him about the handwriting, the missing call, the evidence that someone had tampered with the record.

And she could watch him turn that suspicion on Violet, on Marjorie, on Alec Dunbar—on every vulnerable operator who had been in the exchange that night.

“I’m not sure what you’re implying, Detective Sergeant, but my staff are exemplary. If there’s an error in the record, it would be an honest mistake, not—“

“An honest mistake.” Miller cut her off, his voice sharpening. “Let’s talk about honest mistakes, shall we? Terence Bolt was seen entering this exchange at eleven-forty on the night of the Councillor’s death. He was seen leaving at twelve-fifteen. He claims he came to place an urgent call—a call concerning his employer’s safety—and that the call was connected. The number he claims to have dialled has since been disconnected, but the timing aligns precisely with the window during which Councillor Hammond’s death was discovered.”

Ruth’s hands were cold. She pressed them flat against the desk, willing them to steady.

“And yet,” Miller continued, “when we examined the ledger you provided, there was no record of any call placed at that time. Not from Bolt. Not from anyone. That hour is simply… blank.”

Blank. No, not blank—rewritten. The original entries scraped away and replaced with careful forgeries in Ruth’s own distinctive hand. Someone had taken the time to reconstruct the hour without Bolt’s call, and they had done it so skillfully that only Ruth’s intimate knowledge of her own pen-strokes had revealed the deception.

Miller reached into his coat and withdrew a folded document, which he smoothed open on the desk between them. A witness statement, official police header, the words blurred slightly where rain had touched the paper.

“Bolt’s alibi for the time in question. He was here, in this exchange, witnessed by two of your operators. They signed this statement.” He tapped the paper with one thick finger. “So either Bolt is lying about placing the call, or your ledger is lying about receiving it. Which is it, Miss Calloway?”

The trap had closed. Ruth could feel its teeth in the air pressing around her. If Bolt was telling the truth—and the witness statements suggested he was—then the ledger was wrong. And if the ledger was wrong, then someone had made it wrong. Someone with access, someone with skill, someone who knew Ruth’s handwriting well enough to replicate it.

Someone on her staff. Someone she was supposed to protect.

“I would need to examine the original statement,” Ruth said slowly, buying time. “The operators who signed it—were they identified?”

“Miss Sykes and Mr. Dunbar. Violet Sykes and Alec Dunbar. Do they work under your supervision?”

Violet. Alec. Both of them in the exchange that night, both of them witnesses to Bolt’s presence, and now one of them—perhaps—responsible for erasing the very call they had seen him place.

“Yes,” Ruth managed. “They’re under my supervision. If they signed a statement, they must have believed it to be true.”

“They believed Bolt was here. They watched him enter and leave. But the call he claims to have made—“ Miller’s eyes narrowed. “That call doesn’t exist. So either your operators are mistaken about what they witnessed, or someone removed the record after the fact.”

Ruth’s pulse beat in her temples. She could feel the lie forming before she had consciously chosen it, rising through her like something inevitable.

“The ledger was complete when I reviewed it,” she said. “I found no irregularities.”

The words fell into the silence between them. Miller’s expression didn’t change, but something behind his eyes shifted—a sharpening, a confirmation.

“No irregularities,” he repeated. “You’re certain of that.”

“Completely.” Ruth held his gaze, forcing her voice to remain steady. “If Mr. Bolt was in the exchange that night, as the statements claim, then any call he placed would have been logged. I can only conclude that no such call was made. Perhaps his memory is confused. Perhaps he’s protecting someone.”

“Protecting someone.” Miller sat back, his fingers lacing across his stomach. “An interesting theory. But Bolt has no reason to lie. He’s the one who reported the discrepancy. He’s the one who insists the call exists.”

“Then perhaps he’s mistaken about the timing. Perhaps he intended to place the call and was interrupted—“

“He was seen at the switchboard, Miss Calloway. By two witnesses. For over twenty minutes.”

Twenty minutes. Enough time to place a call, certainly. Enough time for something to be recorded in the ledger—something that was now gone, erased and replaced with Ruth’s own forged handwriting.

She thought of Violet’s red-rimmed eyes, the final demand notice in her pocket. She thought of Alec’s careful hands, his technical precision, his quiet presence in the exchange long after the other operators had gone. She thought of Marjorie, who watched everything and said nothing, who had been at the exchange longer than anyone.

“I can only tell you what the record shows,” Ruth said, and each word was a brick in a wall she was building around herself. “The ledger is accurate. I reviewed it personally. If Mr. Bolt’s call isn’t there, it’s because it was never placed.”

Miller studied her for a long moment. The rain had stopped outside, but the light through the window remained grey and diffuse, the color of secrets.

“You’re staking a great deal on that ledger,” he said finally. “Your reputation. Your position. The integrity of this entire exchange.”

“I’m staking nothing except the truth.”

“The truth.” He rose, his chair scraping against the floor. “I’ve been a detective for eighteen years, Miss Calloway. I’ve heard a lot of people talk about truth. Most of them were lying. The ones who weren’t—“ He paused, collecting his hat from the desk. “The ones who weren’t usually had more to say. More to explain. More to show.”

He moved toward the door, then stopped, turning back with the heavy deliberateness of a man delivering a final blow.

“We’ll be back. With a warrant for the original ledger, not just your summary. And when we examine it—really examine it—we’ll find out what’s missing and why.” His smile was thin, humourless. “If I were you, I’d start thinking about who might have tampered with the record. Because when we find the forgery—and we will—anyone who helped hide it will be treated as an accessory.”

The door closed behind him with a soft click. Ruth sat motionless, her hands still flat on the desk, her breath shallow.

She had lied. She had looked a detective in the eye and told him the ledger was accurate, knowing it wasn’t. She had protected the forgery—and by extension, the forger—for reasons she couldn’t fully articulate even to herself.

The ledger still sat before her, leather-bound and silent. Ruth reached for it, her fingers trembling slightly as she opened the cover and turned the pages. The entries flowed past in neat columns—times, numbers, durations, operators’ initials—until she reached the hour in question.

The forged page stared up at her. Her own handwriting, or a perfect imitation of it. The entries were mundane, routine, unremarkable. But where Bolt’s call should have been recorded, there was nothing. A gap that someone had carefully filled with false entries, obscuring the evidence of a call that Terence Bolt swore he had made.

Ruth closed the ledger and pressed her palms against her eyes.

The door opened without a knock.

“Ruth?” Marjorie Finch stood in the doorway, a china cup in each hand. Steam rose from both, carrying the bitter scent of overbrewed tea. “I saw the detective leave. Thought you might need this.”

Ruth dropped her hands, straightening in her chair. “Marjorie. I didn’t hear you come up.”

“I’ve learned to walk softly. It’s a useful skill in an exchange—people say the most interesting things when they don’t know you’re listening.” Marjorie set one cup on the desk, then settled into the chair Miller had just vacated. Her eyes moved across the desk, pausing on the closed ledger, then returning to Ruth’s face. “He seemed intense. The detective.”

“He had questions about the Councillor’s death. Routine follow-up.”

“Routine.” Marjorie’s voice carried a lightness that didn’t match her eyes. She took a slow sip of her tea, watching Ruth over the rim. “I’m sure it was all very routine. Police visiting the exchange, demanding records, questioning the supervisor—that’s an ordinary Tuesday, isn’t it?”

Ruth wrapped her hands around the warm cup, letting the heat seep into her cold fingers. “Is there something you needed, Marjorie?”

“Needed? No, nothing specific. I just thought—“ Marjorie paused, her gaze drifting to the desk again. Something caught her attention, some detail Ruth couldn’t see. “Alec left something of his on your desk. Earlier, before all the excitement. I noticed it when I came in.”

Ruth followed her gaze. A slim folder had been pushed to the edge of the desk, half-hidden beneath a stack of routing slips. Alec’s fault log—a technical record of line problems and equipment failures, maintained separately from the main ledger.

She hadn’t noticed it before. In the chaos of Miller’s arrival, in the pressure of maintaining her composure, she had missed it entirely.

“I’m sure it’s just routine maintenance notes,” Ruth said, reaching for the folder. “Alec often leaves his reports for my review.”

“Of course. Routine.” Marjorie rose, taking her untouched tea with her. “Though I’ve noticed routine has a way of becoming complicated, lately. Things that should be simple—records, calls, entries—suddenly aren’t.” She paused at the door, her back to Ruth. “Whatever the detective wanted, I hope you were able to satisfy him. Men like that don’t like leaving unsatisfied.”

The door closed behind her, the click of the latch sharp in the silence.

Ruth sat alone, the cooling tea in her hands, Alec’s fault log on the desk before her. She opened it with careful fingers, scanning the entries for the night in question.

Most of the log was what she expected—line tests, equipment checks, minor faults logged and resolved. But near the bottom of the page, in Alec’s precise engineering hand, a notation caught her eye.

Extension 147 — unlisted routing — line test required.

Extension 147. Ruth’s breath caught. The exchange didn’t have an extension 147. The numbering system ran from 100 to 145, with the final numbers reserved for administrative lines that had never been assigned.

She read the entry again, her heart beating faster. An unlisted extension, appearing in Alec’s fault log on the very night that a call had been erased from the ledger. A call that Terence Bolt swore he had made, and that someone had gone to great lengths to hide.

The unlisted extension could be nothing—a clerical error, a test number, a ghost in the system. But it could also be something else. A line that wasn’t supposed to exist, connected to a call that wasn’t supposed to have happened.

Ruth closed the fault log and sat back in her chair.

Miller would return with a warrant. He would examine the ledger, and he would find the forgery. When that happened, everyone in the exchange would fall under suspicion—Violet, Marjorie, Alec, and Ruth herself. The lie she had told would be exposed, and she would have no defense.

Unless she found the truth first.

The unlisted extension was a thread. If she could pull it—trace the routing, identify the number, understand why it had been hidden—she might find her way to the forger. And if she could find the forger, she could understand what had really happened to Terence Bolt’s missing call.

Ruth rose from her desk, the fault log tucked under her arm. The switchboard hummed beyond the wall, oblivious to the danger gathering around them all.

Somewhere in this exchange, someone had forged her handwriting. Someone had erased a call that might have changed everything. And someone—Ruth was increasingly certain—was watching her, waiting to see what she would do next.

The afternoon stretched ahead, grey and uncertain. But for the first time since she had opened the ledger and seen her own counterfeit signature staring back at her, Ruth had something more than fear. She had a direction. She had a question.

She moved toward the filing cabinet, her fingers already sorting through the possibilities of who might have access to her logbook, who might have learned to mimic the particular slant of her R’s and the tight loop of her T’s. The list was shorter than she would have liked.

“Ruth?”

She started, nearly dropping the fault log. Marjorie Finch stood in the doorway, her cardigan buttoned wrong and her reading glasses pushed up into the grey wisps of her hair. She was carrying a mug of tea, steam curling upward in the stale air.

“You’ve been at that desk for two hours without so much as a stretch,” Marjorie said, stepping inside and letting the door fall shut behind her. “I thought you might need this.” She set the mug down on the corner of Ruth’s desk, her eyes lingering on the ledger tucked under Ruth’s arm.

“Thank you.” Ruth’s voice came out steadier than she expected. “I was just—“

“Checking the fault logs.” Marjorie’s smile didn’t reach her eyes. “You’ve always been thorough, Ruth. It’s what makes you good at this job. But sometimes…” She paused, her gaze drifting toward the wall that separated them from the switchboard. “Sometimes being too thorough can be dangerous.”

The words hung in the air between them.

Ruth tightened her grip on the log. “What do you mean?”

Marjorie busied herself straightening a stack of message slips that didn’t need straightening. “I’ve been here twenty-three years. Long enough to see things. Long enough to know that some calls don’t get recorded, and some recordings don’t get kept.” She glanced back at Ruth, something unreadable in her expression. “Terence Bolt wasn’t the first, you know. Important man, important connections. Calls go missing all the time when the right people want them to.”

Ruth’s pulse quickened. “You knew about the Bolt call?”

“I knew enough to keep my head down.” Marjorie’s voice dropped. “And I’m telling you this as a friend, Ruth—someone who’s watched you work, watched you care about doing things right. Be careful what questions you ask. The wrong person might answer.”

She moved toward the door, then hesitated, her hand on the knob. For a moment, Ruth thought she would say more—something that might explain the knowing look in her eyes, the way she had lingered over the fault log. But Marjorie only sighed.

“The forger,” she said quietly, not turning around. “Whoever they are, they’ve had practice. I’ve seen that handwriting before. Just not in your logbook.”

Then she was gone, leaving Ruth alone with the cooling tea and the weight of everything unspoken.
