Chapter 9

One Page, All the Risk

────────────────────────────────────────

The fault log lay open on Alec Dunbar’s desk, the incriminating entry glowing under the green-shaded lamp. The technical office held its breath in the minutes before action. Dust motes drifted through the cone of light, suspended like evidence itself, each particle a witness to what the room had seen.

The metal binding of the log caught the lamp’s reflection, throwing a thin blade of brightness across the cluttered surface where Alec’s pen sat capped and aligned precisely with the log’s edge. A soldering iron cooled in its stand, the smell of hot metal and flux still thick enough to taste. Relay racks hummed along the wall, their mechanical hearts clicking in the secret language of the exchange, routing voices through copper veins to destinations across the city.

The window overlooking the fire escape had fogged with the heat of the room, condensation tracing irregular paths down the glass like maps of nowhere. A tea mug stood sentinel near the log’s edge, the tea inside gone cold, a film forming on its surface. The overhead bulbs flickered once, twice, casting shadows that jumped and settled. Every surface bore the marks of territorial occupation. A crumpled worksheet, a half-eaten biscuit, a jar of screws with the lid left off.

Alec’s domain, his kingdom of paper and metal, and somewhere in this ordered chaos, the lie he had committed to record. The fault log’s pages fanned slightly in the draft from the door, and the crucial entry for 13:47 on February 14th sat there, waiting. The time stamp, the fault code, the technician’s initials. A.D. The letters looked innocent enough. But Ruth knew now that innocence was a costume, and records were only as honest as the hands that wrote them.

She moved before she could think herself out of it.

The door clicked shut behind her, and she was alone in Alec’s territory. Her heart knocked against her ribs, a frantic bird trapped in a cage of bone. She had perhaps four minutes. Alec had gone for tea, his whistling fading down the corridor toward the break room, and Violet had been stationed at the switchboard, her back to the technical office door. Ruth’s hands shook as she reached for the log.

The metal binding was cold under her fingers. She lifted it, feeling its weight, the density of accumulated days and documented faults. The pages whispered as she turned them, February’s entries scrolling past, each line a small truth or a small lie. She found the fourteenth. Her thumb found the crease of the crucial page, and she paused, listening. The relay racks clicked. The overhead bulb buzzed. No footsteps.

She pulled the page free.

The paper tore with a sound like a held breath releasing, the fibres parting along the perforated edge. The tear was clean, almost surgical. She had the entry in her hand now, the evidence, the proof that someone had altered the outage time with a single, precise forgery. The fault log entry showed the falsified time that created a gap in the record. And now she held the physical manifestation of that alteration.

She reached into her pocket and withdrew the blank page she had prepared, cut to match, perforated along one edge. She slotted it into place, her fingers working with a precision that belied her racing pulse. The substitution took three seconds. The log looked undisturbed. She closed the binding and returned it to the exact position on Alec’s desk, the green lamp still throwing its cone of light across the cover.

Ruth exhaled. The stolen page went into her pocket, pressed flat against her thigh, the paper warm now from her body heat. She was a thief. The realization settled in her chest alongside the evidence. She had just committed a sackable offence, possibly a criminal one. The GPO did not take kindly to employees who tampered with official records, even when those records were themselves tampered with.

She turned toward the door.

Footsteps. Not from the corridor. From the stairwell.

Ruth froze, her hand still extended toward the door handle. The footsteps were heavy, deliberate, the gait of a man who knew exactly where he was going and why. Alec’s gait. She could see the shadow now, moving across the frosted glass of the door panel, and then the door was opening, and Alec Dunbar stood in the threshold, his tea still steaming in his mug.

He stopped when he saw her.

His eyes moved from her face to the desk, to the fault log, to the desk again, cataloguing, calculating. His expression remained flat, unreadable, the controlled mask of a man who had learned to keep his thoughts submerged. The steam from his mug curled upward, dissipating in the close air of the office.

“Supervisor.” His voice was clipped, the syllables bitten off. “Forgot my pen.”

He moved past her, close enough that she could smell the tea on his breath, the staleness of his clothes. He reached for the desk, his pen, his fingers brushing the fault log’s cover. Ruth watched him, her pulse visible at her throat, her hands clasped behind her back to hide their trembling.

Alec straightened, pen in hand. His gaze swept the desk one more time, and Ruth saw it catch on the log, on the edge of the pages, on the slight irregularity where her substitution sat. He frowned. The expression was brief, a flicker, gone before it could fully form.

He turned to her. “You’ve been busy.”

The words hung in the air, weighted with suspicion. Ruth felt the stolen page against her leg, a burning presence, a secret that might consume her.

“Routine check,” she said. Her voice came out level, professional. “Verifying the February entries against the master ledger. Standard procedure.”

Alec’s eyes narrowed. He knew something was wrong. She could see it in the tension of his jaw, the way his fingers tightened around his pen. But he couldn’t name it, couldn’t point to the theft without revealing why the page mattered.

“Standard procedure.” He repeated her words, tasting them. “And? Everything in order?”

The question was a trap. If she said yes, she accepted his falsified record. If she said no, she revealed that she knew.

“Discrepancies,” she said. “Nothing that can’t be resolved.”

She moved toward the door, her heels clicking on the linoleum. Alec stepped aside, but his body blocked half the doorway, his solid shoulders creating a barrier. She had to turn slightly to pass, and as she did, she felt his gaze on her back, on her pocket, on the line of her coat where the stolen page pressed against the fabric.

“Supervisor.” His voice stopped her at the threshold. “The log. Have you… tidied up my files? Made any adjustments?”

The question was too pointed, too deliberate. He knew. Or he suspected. And his suspicion was a weapon, even without proof.

“Your files are your domain, Alec. I wouldn’t presume.”

She didn’t turn around. She walked out, her pace measured, her heels striking the floor with deliberate rhythm. The corridor stretched ahead, linoleum shining under the overhead lights, and she didn’t let herself run.

The washroom was empty.

Ruth locked the door behind her and leaned against the sink, her breath coming in short gasps. The tap dripped, a steady metronome counting the seconds she had been a thief. She pulled the stolen page from her pocket and flattened it against the porcelain, her fingers tracing the edges, the creases, the ink.

The entry for 13:47, February 14th. Fault code L-7. Line outage, duration forty-seven minutes. Technician initials: A.D.

She had studied this entry before. She knew what it was supposed to mean. A legitimate fault, a legitimate repair, a legitimate gap in service. But now, with the page in her hands, she saw something she had missed before.

The time stamp.

The entry recorded the fault beginning at 13:47. But the ledger, the call ledger, showed a trunk call terminating at 13:52. Five minutes after the supposed outage began. If the line was truly down at 13:47, no call should have connected at 13:52.

Unless the fault log was wrong.

Unless the outage had been backdated, the fault recorded later than it occurred, creating a cover for the erased call.

Ruth’s mind raced. The ledger page was forged in her handwriting. She hadn’t written it. Someone had copied her hand, imitated her script, created a false record under her name. And Alec had falsified the fault log to match, creating a technical explanation for a gap that shouldn’t exist.

A trunk call to Terence Bolt. That was the call that had been erased. She remembered the name from the original ledger, the destination that had nagged at her memory. Terence Bolt. The councillor. The man whose death had brought the police to the exchange, requesting records.

She stared at the stolen page, at the neat columns, at Alec’s initials. The evidence was in her hands now, but what could she do with it? Take it to the police? Admit that she had stolen it, that she had tampered with official records herself? The irony was bitter. To expose a forger, she had become one.

A knock at the door.

Ruth went still, her fingers tightening around the page. She did not move.

“Ruth? Are you in there?”

Violet’s voice, muffled through the wood.

Ruth composed herself. She folded the page, slipping it back into her pocket, and opened the door.

Violet stood in the corridor, her expression anxious, her hands twisting the cord of her headset. She looked past Ruth into the washroom, as if checking for secrets.

“I’ve been looking for you,” Violet said. “Alec’s asking questions.”

Ruth kept her face neutral. “What kind of questions?”

“About the February logs. About whether anyone’s been in his office.” Violet’s voice dropped. “He’s angry. I can tell.”

“Let him be angry. He has no right to question my movements.”

Violet’s eyes widened. She stepped back, her headset cord tangling at her waist. “I didn’t mean to imply—“

“You didn’t.” Ruth softened her tone. “I’m tired, Violet. It’s been a long day.”

Violet nodded, but her gaze lingered on Ruth’s pocket, on the line of her coat where the folded page pressed against the fabric. “Is everything alright, Ruth? You seem… different.”

“Everything is fine. Go back to the board. I’ll be at my desk.”

Violet hesitated, then nodded again and retreated down the corridor. Ruth watched her go, the girl’s silhouette shrinking under the overhead lights, and then she turned and walked the other way.

Her office waited at the top of the stairs. The door was closed, the lamp on her desk still burning, the forged ledger still lying where she had left it. She crossed to her stationery drawer and pulled it open, her fingers finding the packet of personal paper she kept there, cream-colored sheets for personal correspondence.

She slipped the stolen page inside, between two blank sheets, and closed the drawer.

The evidence was hidden now, but the weight of it remained. She had crossed a line. The ledger doesn’t lie, she had always believed. Only people do. But now she was one of them. A person who lied, who stole, who tampered with the records she was supposed to protect.

She sat at her desk and stared at the forged ledger, at the entries she hadn’t written, at the call that had been erased. A trunk call to Terence Bolt. A councillor. A dead man.

The police had requested the records. They would see the ledger, the fault log, the gaps. They would see what Alec wanted them to see, what the forger wanted them to see. Unless Ruth could prove that the records were lies.

But proving it meant confessing her own crime. It meant admitting that she had stolen evidence, that she had broken the rules she was sworn to uphold.

She thought of Alec’s question in the technical office. Have you tidied up my files? The accusation beneath the words. He knew something was wrong. He would watch her now, monitor her movements, guard his domain with renewed vigilance.

And she would watch him. The game had changed. She was no longer just a supervisor conducting an audit. She was a participant, a conspirator, a thief with stolen evidence hidden in her drawer.

The overhead bulb flickered. Outside her window, the city hummed with its million connections, voices traveling through copper and air, secrets passed from mouth to ear. The exchange held them all, every call a thread in the web, and somewhere in that web, a single thread had been cut.

A trunk call to Terence Bolt. A dead councillor. A forged ledger in her handwriting. A fault log falsified to cover the gap.

Ruth touched the drawer, feeling the paper inside. She had taken the first step. She didn’t know if it led toward justice or toward a trap of her own making. But she couldn’t turn back now.

The ledger doesn’t lie. Only people do.

And she had become one of them.

The drawer slid open with a soft rasp. Ruth’s fingers found the folded page, the stolen evidence pressing against her palm like a live coal. She had done it. The theft was complete.

But as she withdrew her hand, she noticed the edge of the replacement page she had slipped into the fault log—corner slightly askew, the metal binding not quite seated. Anyone looking closely would see the disturbance. Anyone who knew what to look for.

The office door clicked open.

“Mrs. Hartley wants the morning reports.” A clerk stood in the doorway, clipboard in hand, eyes already drifting toward her desk. Toward the drawer she hadn’t fully closed.

“Just a moment.” Ruth slid the drawer shut with her hip, the stolen page burning against her thigh inside her pocket. Her voice held steady. Her hands did not.

The clerk collected the papers and left, but the moment lingered. Someone had been in the technical office earlier. Someone had watched her movements, noted the time she spent at the fault log. The theft was done, but it was not unseen.

Alec Dunbar’s face surfaced in her memory—those flat eyes, that deliberate stillness when she had handed him the log. He had counted the pages. She was certain of it. He had counted them, and he had said nothing.

Which meant he was waiting.

Ruth pressed her hand to her pocket, feeling the stolen page crinkle beneath her fingers. The forgery sat in the official record, her own handwriting documenting a lie. If Alec spoke, if he brought this to management, she would lose everything—her position, her reputation, any chance of discovering who had made that call to Terence Bolt.

But she had the original now. The truth, hidden in her pocket.

The question was whether it would save her or destroy her.
