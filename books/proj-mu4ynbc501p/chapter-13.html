Chapter 13

A Plea and a Threat

────────────────────────────────────────

Ruth’s finger, pale against the newsprint, pointed to the bus timetable entry that didn’t exist. The 11: 47 service to Milchester had been cancelled for three weeks due to bridge repairs on the Upper Road. She had verified it twice with the transport office that morning, her voice steady as she asked about routine scheduling matters while her other hand trembled against the receiver. The ledger doesn’t lie. Only people do. And Violet Sykes had lied about where she was the night the fault log was altered.

The Reference Library smelled of old paper and furniture polish, a quiet tomb of records that Ruth had commandeered for this confrontation. She had asked Violet to meet her here under the pretense of reviewing shift coverage, but the younger woman’s face had gone grey the moment she saw the timetable spread across the reading table.

“I don’t understand,” Violet said, her voice thin. She wore the same cardigan she always wore, the blue one with the frayed cuff, and her hands kept pulling at the sleeves as if she could make them longer. “Why does it matter? It was just a call.”

Ruth slid the timetable closer to Violet’s side of the table. The cancellation notice was circled in red pencil, the ink still fresh from Ruth’s own hand. “The 11: 47 bus you claimed to take home that night. It wasn’t running. There was no service on that route from the twelfth of October through the end of the month.”

Violet’s mouth opened and closed. A strand of hair had come loose from her bun and hung against her cheek, and she didn’t brush it away. “I’m sure I… I don’t really remember. Could it have been a different bus? The lines were bad that night. Everything was confused.”

“I’m not asking you to remember. I’m asking you to think.” Ruth kept her voice level, the supervisor’s voice she had perfected over years of managing operators and handling complaints. But beneath it, something harder was forming. “Think about which bus you actually took. Or if you took one at all.”

The fluorescent light overhead buzzed, casting its flat, clinical glare across the table. Violet’s face looked washed out under it, her eyes too bright, her lips pressed together in a thin line. The silence stretched between them, filled with the distant hum of the exchange floor beyond the library walls.

“I made a mistake,” Violet whispered. “That’s all. I must have mixed up the times. It was late, and I was tired, and I just said the first thing that came into my head when you asked.”

“A trunk call at that hour is not a coincidence. It’s a footprint.” Ruth tapped the timetable. “And neither is a lie about something as simple as a bus. You told me you left on the 11: 47. You were specific about it. You said you remembered because the driver was short with your change. Now you’re telling me you mixed up the times. Which is it?”

Violet’s hands had stopped pulling at her sleeves. They lay flat on the table now, palms down, fingers slightly spread. Ruth noticed a small stain on the cuff of the cardigan, something dark, maybe tea or ink. The younger woman’s breathing had grown shallow, her chest barely moving beneath the worn wool.

“You don’t understand,” Violet said. “You think you know everything because you have your books and your records. But some things aren’t in the books.”

“Then tell me.” Ruth leaned forward, her elbows on the table. “Tell me what isn’t in the books. Tell me where you really were that night, because right now, the only explanation that fits the facts is that you were here. In the exchange. When you said you weren’t.”

“I wasn’t!” The words came out too loud, and Violet glanced toward the door, checking if anyone had heard. Her voice dropped to a harsh whisper. “I wasn’t here. I swear it. I never touched the fault log or the ledger. I don’t even know how to alter those records. You know that. You’ve seen my penmanship. I can barely fill out a chit without smudging it.”

It was true. Ruth had graded Violet’s operator tests herself, remembering the shaky letters, the crossed-out corrections, the ink spots where Violet’s nervous hand had pressed too hard. The forgery in the ledger was precise, controlled, almost mechanical in its consistency. It mimicked Ruth’s own hand, but it was too steady, too deliberate, lacking the natural variation of genuine writing. Whoever had done it had skill and patience.

But Violet was still lying.

“Then where were you?” Ruth pressed. “If you weren’t on the bus and you weren’t here, where? Give me something I can verify. Give me a witness, a receipt, anything. Because right now, your alibi has collapsed, and you’re the only person who had both access and motive to falsify that record.”

Violet’s eyes filled with tears. They didn’t spill over, just sat there, glassy and red, making her look younger than her twenty-three years. “I can’t.”

“Why not?”

“Because I’ll lose my job.” Violet’s voice cracked. “I’ll lose everything. Please, Supervisor Calloway. You have to believe me. I didn’t forge anything. I would never. I know what’s at stake. I know what those records mean.”

Ruth studied her, searching for the telltale signs of deception she had learned to recognize over years of supervising operators. The averted gaze, the fidgeting hands, the rehearsed phrases. Violet had all of them, but beneath the obvious nervousness, there was something else. A desperation that seemed too raw, too unguarded for someone who had successfully executed a forgery.

“What did you do?” Ruth asked, her voice softer now. “What could be worse than being suspected of falsifying official records? Because whatever it is, I promise you, the police will be less forgiving than I am.”

The word hung in the air between them. Police. Violet flinched as if Ruth had struck her.

“It was just money,” Violet whispered. “Just a little. I needed it, and I took it, and I’ve been paying it back bit by bit, and no one would have noticed if you hadn’t started asking all these questions.”

Ruth blinked. “Money?”

“From the tea fund.” Violet’s hands came up to cover her face, her voice muffled through her fingers. “The petty cash box in the supervisor’s office. I had a key because I was on the refreshment committee. I only took a pound. Just once. I was going to put it back before the quarterly audit. I swear I was.”

The admission landed like a stone dropping into still water. Ruth felt the ripples spreading through her understanding, rearranging the facts she had assembled. Theft. Violet had committed theft, petty and desperate, the kind of small crime that ruined lives not because of its magnitude but because of its discovery.

“That’s why you lied about the bus,” Ruth said slowly. “You weren’t here, but you weren’t where you said you were either. Where were you really?”

Violet lowered her hands. Her cheeks were wet now, the tears finally spilling over. “With someone. A man. He’s married, and his wife would… Please, you can’t tell anyone. I know I stole, and I know I lied, but I didn’t forge anything. I didn’t touch the ledger or the fault log. I barely know how those systems work.”

Ruth absorbed this, fitting it into the framework of what she knew. Violet had motive to lie, but not motive to forge. She had opportunity to access the records, but not the skill to alter them undetectably. And her confession, while damning, had the ring of truth precisely because it was so small, so pathetic, so unworthy of the larger crime.

“I need proof,” Ruth said. “Not of the theft. I believe you about that. I need proof that you weren’t involved in the forgery. Something that shows you couldn’t have done it, or someone who can confirm your whereabouts.”

Violet’s expression shifted. The tears were still wet on her cheeks, but something else had entered her eyes. A calculation, quick and sharp, there and gone in an instant.

“I can’t give you that,” she said. “But I can give you something else.”

“What?”

“Insurance.” Violet reached into her cardigan pocket and withdrew a crumpled piece of paper. She smoothed it on the table, her movements slow and deliberate, and Ruth saw that it was a one-pound note, worn soft from circulation. Violet placed it on the table between them like an offering. The Queen’s portrait was smudged with what looked like a fingerprint, a tiny, desperate seal on their mutual ruin.

“I took this from the tea tin,” Violet said. “On the night in question. And you know how I know the exact date? Because that was the night you took something too.”

Ruth went very still. “What are you talking about?”

“The ledger page.” Violet’s voice had steadied, the desperation replaced by something harder, more controlled. “The one that’s missing from the fault log. You have it, don’t you? You took it the same night I took this. And you’ve been carrying it around ever since, trying to figure out who forged it.”

The silence that followed was absolute. Ruth could hear her own heartbeat, feel the blood rushing in her ears. She had been so careful. She had waited until the night shift, until the corridor was empty, until she could slip the page from the binder without anyone seeing. She had hidden it in her desk drawer, beneath the routine paperwork, telling herself it wasn’t really theft because it was evidence, because she needed it, because the truth mattered more than procedure.

But procedure was everything. And she had broken it.

“How do you know?” Ruth asked, her voice barely above a whisper.

“I saw you.” Violet’s hand rested on the pound note, her fingers splayed across the Queen’s face. “I was coming back from the toilet, and I saw you at the records cabinet. You took the page and hid it in your desk. I didn’t think anything of it at the time. I thought maybe you had a reason, supervisor’s business. But then you started asking questions, and I realized you were investigating. And I thought, if you can take evidence from a police inquiry, maybe you’re not as honest as everyone thinks.”

“I took it to find the truth.”

“And I took this because I needed it.” Violet’s voice was flat now, stripped of its earlier pleading. “We’ve both done something wrong, Supervisor. The question is whether either of us can afford to report the other.”

Ruth stared at the crumpled note. In the stark electric light of the library, she could see every detail: the worn edges, the creases from being folded and unfolded, the faint shadow of Violet’s fingerprint across the portrait. It was such a small thing. One pound. A trivial sum that could end a career, destroy a reputation, send someone tumbling from the precarious respectability of employment into the abyss of scandal.

But the ledger page was worse. Because it wasn’t just evidence. It was obstruction. If the police found out she had removed it, if they discovered she had been conducting an unauthorized investigation, withholding information, tampering with the very records she was supposed to protect, she would lose everything. Not just her job. Her credibility. Her standing. Her word.

“The ledger doesn’t lie,” Ruth said, but her voice had lost its edge. “Only people do. And we’ve both lied, haven’t we?”

Violet nodded, a single, sharp movement. “So what happens now?”

Ruth thought about the options. She could report Violet’s theft, but Violet would report hers. She could deny everything, but Violet had seen her, and there was no guarantee the girl wouldn’t talk out of spite or desperation. She could try to reason with her, appeal to her conscience, but conscience was a luxury that people in trouble couldn’t afford.

The truth was, she needed Violet. Not as a friend or an ally, but as a silent partner in the investigation. Because if Violet hadn’t forged the ledger, then someone else had. Someone with technical skill, someone who knew the systems, someone who had been in the exchange that night and had the knowledge to mimic Ruth’s handwriting well enough to fool casual inspection.

That someone was still out there. And if Ruth was going to find them, she couldn’t afford to be exposed.

“We keep each other’s secrets,” Ruth said slowly. “You don’t tell anyone about the ledger page, and I don’t report the missing pound. We forget this conversation happened.”

“And the investigation?” Violet asked. “What about that?”

“That’s my concern. Not yours.” Ruth gathered the timetable and folded it into a precise rectangle, her fingers working automatically while her mind raced. “You’ve told me where you were and what you did. I believe you about the forgery. But if I find out you’re lying about any of it, if I discover you had any part in altering those records, this agreement ends. Do you understand?”

Violet’s shoulders sagged with relief. “I understand. I swear, I never touched the fault log. I wouldn’t know how.”

Ruth stood, tucking the timetable into her skirt pocket. The library felt smaller now, the walls pressing in, the hum of the exchange floor beyond seeming louder. She had come here expecting to corner a suspect, to extract a confession, to solve a puzzle. Instead, she had become an accomplice.

She thought of Alec Dunbar, his flat voice, his precise threats. She thought of the scar he had mentioned, the childhood bicycle accident that no one was supposed to know about. He had invaded her privacy, weaponized her past, and now she was doing the same to Violet. The parallel was not lost on her.

“There’s one more thing,” Ruth said, pausing at the door. “The man you were with that night. The married one. Does he have any connection to the exchange?”

Violet’s face flushed. “No. He’s not involved in any of this. He’s just… he’s nobody.”

“Make sure it stays that way.” Ruth opened the door and stepped into the corridor. The switchboards hummed in the distance, the operators’ voices a distant murmur beneath the electric drone. “And Violet? If anyone asks, we were reviewing shift coverage. Nothing more.”

She didn’t wait for a response. Walking back toward her desk, she felt the weight of the stolen page in her drawer, the timetable in her pocket, the secrets she now carried like stones. The investigation had turned inwards, just as she had feared. Violet was a thief and a liar, but she wasn’t the forger.

Which meant someone else was.

Ruth thought of Alec Dunbar’s hands, steady and precise as he worked on the relay racks. She thought of his knowledge of her past, the quiet threat in his voice when he had cornered her in the corridor. She thought of the technical skill required to alter a fault log without leaving traces, to mimic handwriting with mechanical precision.

But Alec wasn’t the only one with that skill. The exchange employed half a dozen technicians, each one trained in the same systems, each one capable of the same work. And beyond them, there were supervisors, administrators, anyone with access to the records and knowledge of how they functioned.

Ruth reached her desk and sat down heavily. The drawer with the stolen page was inches from her hand. She pulled it open and stared at the folded paper, the evidence she had risked everything to obtain.

The handwriting was hers, but it wasn’t. The strokes were too even, the pressure too consistent, the letters too perfectly formed. It was her signature, reproduced with the precision of a printer rather than a human hand. Whoever had done this had studied her writing, practiced it, learned its rhythms and quirks.

Or they had been taught.

She closed the drawer and leaned back in her chair, her gaze drifting across the exchange floor. Operators sat at their switchboards, cables in hand, voices repeating the same phrases over and over. The routine was hypnotic, comforting in its predictability. But beneath the surface, something was wrong. A thread had been pulled, and the fabric was unraveling.

Violet was a decoy, whether she knew it or not. The theft, the lie, the alibi that collapsed under scrutiny, all of it was too convenient, too obvious. Someone had wanted Violet to look guilty. Or someone had wanted Ruth to waste her time chasing a petty thief while the real forger remained hidden.

The question was whether Violet had been chosen deliberately or simply seized an opportunity. Her theft provided a perfect cover for the forgery, a ready-made suspect with motive and opportunity. But if Violet was telling the truth, she hadn’t known about the forgery until Ruth started asking questions.

Which meant someone else had known about her theft. Someone who had watched and waited and used her weakness as a shield.

Ruth thought of Alec Dunbar again, his unreadable face, his careful words. He had been in the exchange late that night, he had access to the records, he had the technical skill to alter them. And he knew things about Ruth that he shouldn’t, secrets from her past that could only have come from someone who had been watching her for a long time.

But Alec wasn’t the only one watching. The exchange was full of eyes, operators and technicians and administrators, each one seeing a fragment of the whole. Ruth had thought herself invisible, a supervisor who observed without being observed. Now she realized how wrong she had been.

She opened the drawer again and took out the stolen page. The paper felt thin and fragile between her fingers, a single sheet that contained the weight of everything she didn’t know. She needed to examine it again, to look for the details she might have missed, the pressure marks and ink blots that could reveal the method of its making.

But not here. Not where anyone could see.

Ruth stood and tucked the page into her bag, beneath her lunch and her compact mirror. The Technical Archive was two floors up, a locked room that only supervisors could access. There, surrounded by original records and backup ledgers, she could study the forgery in private.

She had become a criminal to catch a criminal. The irony was not lost on her. But as she walked toward the stairwell, her heels clicking on the linoleum, she felt a cold certainty settling in her chest.

The ledger didn’t lie. And somewhere in its pages, in the ink and the paper and the careful lies, the truth was waiting to be found.
