Chapter 10

Scarred by Childhood

────────────────────────────────────────

Ruth slapped the stolen fault log page onto Alec Dunbar’s desk, the paper crackling under the harsh desk lamp.

The sound cut through the stagnant air of the technical office, sharp as a switchblade. Alec didn’t flinch. He sat with his solid shoulders hunched over a schematic, his back to the fogged window and the fire escape beyond. The smell of hot metal and ozone hung thick between the relay racks, and somewhere in the building, the dormant switchboards hummed their constant, low-frequency drone.

“You’ve been busy,” Ruth said. Her voice came out level, professional, exactly as it should. But beneath the surface, something cold and hard had settled in her chest.

Alec set down his pen. He turned with deliberate slowness, his taut spine straightening as he regarded her with that unreadable expression she had come to despise. The desk lamp threw a hard cone of light across the metal-bound fault log, illuminating the precise, damning alteration she had uncovered.

“Supervisor Calloway.” His voice was flat, clipped, each syllable bitten off with resentful precision. “To what do I owe this intrusion?”

“You know exactly why I’m here.”

She tapped the stolen page with one fingernail. The paper was warm from being folded inside her blouse, pressed against her sternum as she’d walked through the corridor past the night operators. Her heart had been hammering then. Now it beat with a cold, steady rhythm.

“The fault log entry for thirteenth forty-seven,” she said. “The line outage during the Bolt trunk call. You altered it.”

Alec’s gaze dropped to the page. His expression didn’t change. He might have been reading a grocery list.

“That’s a serious accusation.”

“It’s not an accusation. It’s a fact.” Ruth leaned forward, planting both hands on the edge of his cluttered desk. Schematics and spare parts shifted under her weight. “The original entry showed a four-minute outage window. Four minutes, Alec. Exactly long enough for a trunk call to be rerouted without a trace. But this log, the one you submitted, shows a two-minute window. A two-minute window that conveniently eliminates any record of interference.”

“You want a story, read a paperback.” Alec’s voice remained flat, but something flickered behind his eyes. “I give you facts.”

“The facts are that you falsified an official record. The facts are that you were in this exchange late on the night in question. The facts are that someone made a trunk call to Terence Bolt disappear, and you helped them do it.”

Silence stretched between them. The hum of the building seemed to grow louder, a collective heartbeat of clicks and connections flowing through walls they couldn’t see.

Alec picked up his pen again. He turned it between his fingers, the metal catching the light.

“You stole that page.”

Ruth blinked. “What?”

“That page.” He nodded toward the fault log. “That’s not your copy. That’s the original from the technical archive. You removed it from a secure file without authorization.” His voice remained level, almost bored. “That’s theft, Supervisor Calloway. That’s a firing offense. Possibly criminal, depending on how the administrators feel this week.”

Ruth felt a flush of heat rise to her cheeks, but she kept her voice steady. “I’m conducting an internal investigation into records tampering. I have full authority to examine any document in this exchange.”

“Do you?” Alec raised an eyebrow. “I wasn’t informed of any investigation. Were the administrators informed? The police liaison?” He set the pen down with a soft click. “Because from where I’m sitting, this looks like a supervisor with a personal obsession, stealing documents to manufacture evidence against a technician she doesn’t like.”

“I don’t need to manufacture anything. The alteration is right there in front of you. The ink matches your pen. The pressure matches your hand.”

“Technical truth.” Alec shrugged his solid shoulders. “I log faults. Sometimes I make corrections. Sometimes entries need updating based on system diagnostics that come in after the fact. That’s standard procedure.”

“A four-minute outage doesn’t become a two-minute outage through diagnostics.”

“It does when the initial reading comes from a faulty relay sensor.” Alec leaned back in his chair, his gaze finally meeting hers directly. “You’re not a technician, Ruth. You don’t understand how the systems work. You see a number in a log and you think it’s holy scripture. But numbers change. Readings get refined. That’s the job.”

Ruth stared at him. The audacity of it, the sheer bureaucratic smugness, made her want to reach across the desk and shake him. But she held herself still. Her hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the last hour, and she wasn’t about to let them start again.

“The ledger doesn’t lie,” she said. “Only people do.”

Alec’s mouth twitched. Not quite a smile. Something colder.

“You really believe that, don’t you?” He tilted his head, studying her like a specimen under glass. “You think if you line up all the papers in the right order, the truth will just pop out like a jack-in-the-box. Clean. Simple. Satisfying.”

“I think someone in this exchange helped erase a phone call that matters to a police investigation. I think that someone sits in this room.”

“Think all you want.” Alec spread his hands wide, a gesture of false openness. “But you’d better think about your own position first. Because right now, the only person I see breaking rules is you.”

Ruth took a slow breath. She had expected this, the denial, the deflection, the bureaucratic counterattack. What she hadn’t expected was how prepared he was, how smoothly he turned her evidence into her own liability.

She tried a different angle.

“A trunk call at that hour is not a coincidence,” she said. “It’s a footprint. Someone made that call disappear, and they needed technical access to do it. Access that you have.”

“Half the technical staff has that access.”

“But only one of them was here late that night. Only one of them altered the fault log.”

Alec said nothing. His face remained blank, a mask of professional neutrality, but Ruth sensed something shifting beneath it. A tension in his jaw. A calculation behind his eyes.

She pressed forward.

“I’m not asking you to remember. I’m asking you to think.” She straightened, crossing her arms. “Whatever they paid you, or promised you, or threatened you with, it won’t be enough. When the police start asking questions, and they will, they’ll start with the records. And when they find the same discrepancies I found, they’ll come to you. You can tell them what you know now, while it still counts as cooperation. Or you can wait until it looks like obstruction.”

The words hung in the air. The hum of the switchboards seemed to grow quieter, as if the building itself was listening.

Alec didn’t move. Then, slowly, he leaned forward. His elbows came to rest on the desk, his hands clasped loosely in front of him. The desk lamp cast shadows upward across his face, hollowing his cheeks, turning his unreadable expression into something almost skeletal.

“You’re right about one thing, Ruth.”

She waited.

“This isn’t about the ledger.” His voice had changed. The flat, clipped professionalism was gone, replaced by something quieter. Something intimate. “You’re not here because you care about some trunk call. You’re not here because of the police, or the records, or the system.”

“I’m here because someone committed a crime, and you’re the most likely suspect.”

Alec’s lip twitched again. This time, it was definitely a smile. A small, cold thing that didn’t reach his eyes.

“Most likely.” He let the words sit. “That’s what you said. Most likely. Not certain. Not proven. Just a feeling you have, backed up by a stolen page and a lot of assumptions.”

“I have evidence.”

“You have paper.” Alec’s voice dropped lower, barely above a whisper. “But paper doesn’t know anything about you, does it? It can’t tell me where you were last Thursday night. It can’t tell me what you think about when you can’t sleep. It can’t tell me about the things you’ve hidden, even from yourself.”

Ruth felt a prickle at the back of her neck. The cold that had settled in her chest began to spread.

“What are you talking about?”

Alec leaned farther forward. The light from the desk lamp caught the metal rim of his glasses, obscuring his eyes for a moment.

“How’s that scar on your hip, Ruth?”

The words landed like a slap.

She didn’t move. Didn’t breathe. For a single, suspended moment, the world stopped turning.

“The one from the bicycle when you were twelve.”

The technical office, the smell of ozone, the hum of the switchboards, all of it fell away. There was only Alec’s voice, soft and precise, and the sudden, visceral burn of a wound that had healed decades ago.

Ruth’s hand moved before she could stop it. Not to her hip, but to the edge of the desk, gripping it hard enough to whiten her knuckles. She felt the phantom ache of gravel embedded in skin, the hot pulse of blood soaking through a torn dress, the sound of her mother’s voice calling from an open window.

No one knew about that scar. No one alive. She had never told a soul at the exchange. She had never mentioned it to friends, to colleagues, to anyone. The accident itself was a childhood memory, buried so deep she barely thought of it anymore.

And Alec Dunbar, a technician she barely spoke to, knew exactly where it was.

“I asked you a question.” Alec’s voice remained low, intimate, a thread of sound that carried perfectly in the dead air. “How is it?”

Ruth’s throat worked, but no words came. Her heart, which had been beating with cold, steady purpose, now slammed against her ribs. The edges of her vision seemed to darken.

“Get out.”

The words came from somewhere far away. Alec’s voice had returned to its flat, clipped register. He picked up his pen and turned back to his schematic, dismissing her as easily as closing a door.

“Take your stolen paper with you. And think carefully about your next step. Because if you come at me again, with accusations or investigations or anything else, I won’t just defend myself with technical justifications. I’ll defend myself with everything I know.”

Ruth stared at the back of his head. The solid shoulders, the taut spine, the absolute stillness of a man who had just played his hand and won.

She picked up the fault log page. Her fingers were trembling now, the paper shaking visibly as she folded it and tucked it back into her blouse. The warmth of her body had left it, and it felt cold against her skin.

She walked out of the technical office without another word.

The corridor stretched before her, linoleum gleaming under the fluorescent lights. She moved without thinking, her heels clicking a rhythm that seemed too loud in the empty hallway. Her breath came shallow, catching in her chest like something physical.

She reached the main exchange floor and stopped.

The switchboard bays rose in rows before her, tall cabinets with their blinking lights and dangling plugs, the tools of connection that bound the city together. The night shift operators worked in silence, their hands moving with practiced efficiency, connecting calls and logging records and maintaining the endless flow of information that kept the world turning.

None of them looked up. None of them noticed her standing there, one hand pressed against a cold metal cabinet, the other hovering at her side.

Ruth leaned against the switchboard bay. The metal was cool through her blouse, grounding her in the present. But her mind was spinning, racing through the implications of what had just happened.

Alec knew about the scar. Alec knew about a childhood accident that she had never spoken of, that no one at the exchange could possibly know about. Which meant either he had access to her past in ways she couldn’t imagine, or someone had told him.

Someone who knew her secrets.

The thought sent a fresh wave of cold through her. She thought of the forged ledger page, the one in her own handwriting, the one she hadn’t written. She thought of the missing trunk call to Terence Bolt, the councillor’s death, the police investigation that was circling closer every day.

And she thought of Alec Dunbar, sitting in his cluttered office with his schematics and his soldering iron and his knowledge of things he should never have known.

The familiar sounds of the exchange surrounded her. The click-click of operators’ plugs, the faint hum of current through the lines, the distant smell of floor polish and tea from the break room. But the sounds felt different now. They felt like surveillance. Every click was a record being made. Every hum was a line being tapped.

She had started this investigation to find the truth. She had stolen the fault log page because she believed the evidence would speak for itself. But now she understood, with a chill that went deeper than bones, that the truth was not a simple thing.

The truth was that someone had forged a record in her handwriting. The truth was that someone had erased a phone call that might have mattered. The truth was that Alec Dunbar knew about a scar on her hip that no one should have known about.

And the truth was that she was no longer the hunter.

She was the prey.

Ruth straightened slowly. Her hands were still shaking, but she pressed them flat against the switchboard cabinet until the trembling subsided. She took a long, slow breath, filling her lungs with the smell of hot Bakelite and ozone.

She would not let him see her break. She would not let anyone see it.

But she needed to think. She needed to understand how deep this went, who else might be involved, what other secrets might be lurking in the shadows of her own past.

The missing trunk call to Terence Bolt. The forged ledger in her handwriting. Alec’s late-night presence in the exchange. And now this, the scar, the bicycle, the childhood memory that should have stayed buried.

It was all connected. It had to be. The only question was how.

Ruth pushed away from the switchboard bay and walked toward her office at the top of the stairs. Her heels clicked against the linoleum, each step deliberate, each breath measured. She passed an operator in the hallway, a young woman with dark circles under her eyes, who nodded a tired greeting.

Ruth nodded back. Her face was a mask of professional calm. No one would have guessed that her heart was still pounding, that her hands wanted to shake, that she could still feel the phantom burn of a scar she hadn’t thought about in years.

She reached her office and closed the door behind her. The lamp on her desk cast a warm circle of light across the blotter, the pencil tray, the empty space where the forged ledger should have been.

She sat down heavily and pulled the fault log page from her blouse. She unfolded it and smoothed it flat on the desk, staring at the altered entry without really seeing it.

The ledger doesn’t lie. Only people do.

But people had lied to her. People had forged her handwriting and stolen her secrets and used them as weapons. The system she had trusted, the records she had believed in, had been hacked from the inside.

And now she had to decide what to do next.

She could go to the police. But what would she tell them? That she had stolen evidence? That a technician knew about her childhood scar? They would laugh her out of the station, or worse, they would start asking questions she couldn’t answer.

She could go to the administrators. But Alec had been right about one thing. Her unauthorized investigation, her theft of documents, her accusations without proof, all of it could be turned against her. She could lose her job, her reputation, everything she had built.

Or she could keep going. She could find out who had forged the ledger in her handwriting. She could trace the missing trunk call to its source. She could uncover whatever connection Alec Dunbar had to the councillor’s death.

But that would mean stepping deeper into the shadows. It would mean risking more exposure, more vulnerability, more secrets dragged into the light.

Ruth looked at the fault log page one more time. The altered entry stared back at her, a small lie that hid a larger one.

She thought of her mother, calling from an open window all those years ago. She thought of the bicycle, the gravel, the blood soaking through her dress. She thought of how quickly a childhood could change, how suddenly safety could turn to danger.

She would find out who had done this. Not just for the system’s integrity, and not just for the truth.

She would find out because someone had invaded her private history, and she would not let that stand.

Ruth folded the page again and tucked it into her desk drawer, beneath a stack of routine reports. Then she stood, smoothed her skirt, and walked to the door.

The exchange floor hummed on, indifferent to her fears. But somewhere in that hum, in the endless flow of calls and connections, a thread was waiting to be pulled.

And Ruth Calloway intended to pull it until the whole thing unraveled.

She made it three steps onto the exchange floor before the feeling crept in.

At first, it was just the normal awareness of being observed—the night supervisor visible to everyone, her heels marking her progress across the polished floor. But as she passed the first row of switchboards, something shifted. The operators who glanced up didn’t look away quickly enough. Their eyes lingered, tracking her movement.

Ruth slowed, her hand drifting to the edge of a cold metal bay. The hum of connections, the clicks and pulses that had always been her rhythm, now seemed to press against her from all sides. Every light on every board felt like an eye. Every wire, a nerve ending carrying information about her—where she walked, what she knew, how badly her hands were shaking.

She thought of Alec’s voice, flat and certain: I know about the scar.

Who else knew? Who else had been watching, cataloguing, filing away pieces of her life she thought she’d buried?

A operator at Bay Seven turned her head, following Ruth’s path. Further down, two technicians paused their conversation as she approached, resuming only after she’d passed. Or had they? The noise of the floor made it impossible to tell if their voices had ever stopped.

Ruth’s throat tightened. She pressed her palm flat against the switchboard housing, feeling the cold metal bite into her skin. This place had been her refuge. The order of it, the predictable dance of connections. Now it felt like a trap she’d walked into willingly, every wall lined with ears.

She needed to move. To get back to her desk, her drawer, her evidence. But the distance seemed impossibly far, and every operator between here and there was a potential witness—maybe worse, a potential reporter.

Ruth swallowed hard and made herself step forward. The exchange floor hummed on, and somewhere in that hum, she felt the weight of unseen attention settling across her shoulders like a hand.
