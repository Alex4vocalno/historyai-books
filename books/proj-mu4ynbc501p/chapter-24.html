Chapter 24

A Choice for Violet

────────────────────────────────────────

Ruth closed the ledger, her fingers tracing its spine. The echo of the last disconnected call lingered in the empty exchange, a quiet question hanging in the air. Outside her office window, the afternoon light had begun to fail, casting long amber stripes across the rows of silent switchboards. In three hours, the night shift would arrive, and with them the ceaseless hum of connections made and broken. But for now, the building held its breath.

She had spent the morning reviewing the tea fund accounts, comparing deposit slips against the petty cash ledger. The discrepancy was small at first, just a few shillings here and there, easily mistaken for poor bookkeeping. But when Ruth traced the pattern back through six months of records, the numbers began to tell a different story. Seven pounds, twelve shillings, gone in increments small enough to escape notice. And every withdrawal bore the same telltale signature: a falsified initial, a date shifted by a day, a receipt that corresponded to no purchase.

The thief had been careful. But careful was not the same as invisible.

Ruth drew a slow breath and reached for the telephone on her desk. Her hand trembled slightly as she dialed the internal extension for the operators’ floor. When the connection clicked through, she kept her voice level.

“Please send Violet Sykes to my office.”

The line went silent. Ruth replaced the receiver and folded her hands on the desk, waiting.

She had known Violet for four years. The girl had come to the exchange at nineteen, all nervous energy and eager smiles, desperate to prove herself. Ruth had trained her personally, watching her grow from a fumbling apprentice into one of the most reliable operators on the floor. Violet had a gentle way with difficult callers, a patience that Ruth sometimes envied. She had defended Violet when others questioned her competence, had covered for her minor mistakes, had believed in her when she insisted she could do better.

And now this.

The door opened without a knock. Violet stood in the threshold, her face pale, her hands clasped before her. She wore the standard operator’s uniform, the navy dress freshly pressed, the white collar stiff and proper. But her eyes betrayed her. They darted around the office, landing on the ledger, then on Ruth’s face, then on the ledger again.

“You asked to see me, Supervisor?”

Ruth did not rise. She gestured to the chair across from her desk. “Sit down, Violet.”

The girl obeyed, lowering herself into the seat with rigid movements. Her knees pressed together, her back straight, she looked like a student awaiting a reprimand. Perhaps she was.

For a long moment, Ruth simply studied her. The afternoon light caught the edge of Violet’s fair hair, turning it golden. She looked younger than her years in this light, younger and more fragile. Ruth felt a tightness in her chest, a pressure she could not name.

“I’ve been going through the accounts,” Ruth said finally. Her voice came out steady, professional. “The tea fund deposits don’t match the withdrawals.”

Violet’s throat moved. A small swallow, barely visible. “I wouldn’t know about that, Supervisor. I handle the switchboard, not the accounts.”

“Someone has been taking money, Violet. Small amounts, spread over months. Seven pounds, twelve shillings in total.”

The color drained from Violet’s face. Her lips parted, but no words came.

“The withdrawals were recorded with falsified initials,” Ruth continued. “Receipts were invented. Dates were altered. Whoever did this knew the system well enough to cover their tracks.” She paused, letting the silence stretch. “They knew the system because they work here.”

Violet’s hands tightened in her lap. Her knuckles whitened. “I don’t know what you’re implying.”

“I’m not implying anything. I’m telling you.” Ruth opened the ledger and turned it toward Violet, pointing to a row of figures. “These entries match your shift schedule. The dates align with your days on the floor. And the handwriting, while disguised, has certain characteristics that I’ve seen before.” She looked up, meeting Violet’s eyes. “I trained you, Violet. I know how you form your letters.”

The girl’s composure cracked. Her lower lip trembled, and she looked away, her gaze falling to the desk surface. “It wasn’t… I didn’t…”

Ruth waited. The clock on the wall ticked through the seconds, each one loud in the quiet room.

“I needed the money.” The words came out in a whisper. Violet’s voice shook. “I didn’t mean to take so much. I thought I could pay it back before anyone noticed.”

“Seven pounds is not a small sum.”

“I know.” Violet’s hands came up to her face, covering her mouth. Her shoulders hunched forward. “I know it isn’t.”

Ruth felt something shift inside her. The anger that had been building all morning, the sense of betrayal, the disappointment in someone she had trusted, all of it began to lose its edge. In its place came a cold, hollow feeling. “Why?”

Violet shook her head, tears gathering at the corners of her eyes. “It doesn’t matter.”

“It matters to me.”

“My mother.” The words broke free like water through a dam. Violet’s voice cracked, and a sob escaped her throat. “She’s been ill. The doctors, the treatments, they cost more than we have. I tried everything. I took extra shifts, I sold what I could, but it wasn’t enough. The bills kept coming, and she kept getting worse, and I didn’t know what else to do.”

Ruth’s hands stilled on the desk. The tightness in her chest became a sharp pang. “Your mother has been ill?”

“For almost a year now.” Violet wiped at her eyes with the back of her hand, smearing the tears across her cheek. “She doesn’t know about this. She thinks I’ve been picking up extra work to save for a wedding. She would be ashamed if she knew. She’s always told me that honesty is the only thing we can afford to keep.”

The words hung in the air between them. Ruth thought of her own mother, long gone now, but still present in the small lessons she had passed down. The ledger doesn’t lie. Only people do. She had said that to Violet once, years ago, when the girl had made a mistake in the call records and tried to hide it. Ruth had corrected her, guided her, taught her the value of accuracy.

And now Violet had broken that trust. Not out of greed, not out of malice, but out of desperation.

Ruth closed the ledger slowly. Her fingers lingered on the cover, feeling the worn leather beneath her touch. “You know what this means.”

Violet nodded, fresh tears spilling down her face. “I’ll be dismissed. Reported. They’ll call the police.”

“They could.” Ruth kept her voice even. “Theft is a criminal offense. The exchange has policies, procedures. I could have security escort you out within the hour.”

Violet’s shoulders sagged. She looked small in the chair, diminished, her uniform hanging loose on her thin frame. “I understand.”

Ruth studied her for a long moment. She thought about procedure, about the rules she had spent her career upholding. She thought about the integrity of the records, the trust placed in her as supervisor. She thought about the other operators, the ones who would see Violet dragged away, who would whisper and speculate and wonder if they might be next.

Then she thought about Violet’s mother, alone in some cramped flat, waiting for news of her daughter, unaware that the money paying for her medicine had been stolen.

Ruth opened the drawer of her desk and withdrew a blank sheet of paper. She placed it on the desk between them, then reached for a pen.

“Here is what is going to happen.” She spoke carefully, each word measured. “You will sign a letter of resignation, effective immediately. You will cite personal reasons, family obligations, nothing more. You will pack your belongings and leave the exchange quietly, without scene or announcement.”

Violet’s head snapped up. Her eyes widened, red-rimmed and glistening. “Supervisor?”

“You will repay the money you took. Not all at once, but in installments, as you can manage. You will send payments to the exchange’s general fund, care of me. I will ensure they are recorded properly.”

“I don’t… I don’t understand.”

Ruth met her gaze steadily. “I am giving you a choice, Violet. You can accept this arrangement and leave with your dignity intact, or you can force me to follow procedure. But if you choose the latter, I want you to understand something.” She leaned forward slightly. “I take no pleasure in destroying a young woman’s future. But I will not compromise the integrity of this exchange for anyone. Not even you.”

Violet stared at her. Her mouth opened and closed, soundless. Then, slowly, understanding dawned in her eyes. “You’re letting me go.”

“I am offering you a chance to go quietly. To protect your mother from the truth. To keep this mistake from defining the rest of your life.” Ruth pushed the blank paper toward her. “But only if you take responsibility. Only if you promise to make this right.”

The pen sat on the desk between them. Violet reached for it with a trembling hand. Her fingers closed around the barrel, and she drew the paper closer, steadying herself with a visible effort.

“Thank you.” The words came out hoarse, barely audible. “Thank you, Supervisor.”

Ruth did not respond. She watched as Violet bent over the paper and began to write. The scratch of the nib filled the silence, punctuated by the soft tick of the clock. The afternoon light shifted, casting longer shadows across the desk.

When Violet finished, she pushed the paper back toward Ruth. The letter was brief, formal, the handwriting shaky but legible. A single tear had fallen onto the corner of the page, smudging the ink where Violet had signed her name.

Ruth read the letter once, then again. She folded it carefully and placed it in the drawer beside the ledger. “You have thirty minutes to collect your things. I will inform the floor supervisor that you have resigned for personal reasons. You will not discuss this matter with anyone. Is that clear?”

“Yes, Supervisor.” Violet rose from the chair on unsteady legs. She smoothed her uniform, wiped her face with her sleeve, and took a breath that seemed to steady her. “May I… may I say goodbye to anyone?”

“That is your choice. But I would advise discretion.”

Violet nodded. She turned toward the door, then paused, her hand on the frame. “Supervisor Calloway?”

Ruth looked up.

“I know I don’t deserve your kindness. But I want you to know… I will never forget this.” Her voice cracked on the final word. “I will make it right. I swear I will.”

“Go, Violet. Before I change my mind.”

The girl left, closing the door softly behind her. Ruth sat alone in the office, the silence pressing in around her. She looked at the closed ledger, then at the drawer where she had placed the resignation letter. A choice made. A rule broken. A debt that would never be fully repaid.

She rose from her desk and moved to the window. The exchange floor spread out below, rows of switchboards standing like silent sentinels in the fading light. A few operators remained at their stations, finishing their shifts, their movements automatic and unhurried. Near the far wall, Violet crossed toward the locker room, her steps slow, her head bowed. She did not look back.

Ruth watched as the girl disappeared through the doorway. A moment later, she reemerged, carrying a small cardboard box filled with the meager contents of her locker. A photograph, a cardigan, a few personal items. The sum total of four years at the exchange.

The other operators glanced up as Violet passed. Some offered tentative waves, confused by her sudden departure. Others simply watched, their curiosity evident, their questions unasked. Violet moved through the room with her head down, making no eye contact, speaking to no one. She reached the main exit and pushed through the heavy door.

Outside, the Manchester sky had darkened to gray. A fine drizzle misted the air, softening the edges of the buildings, blurring the streetlamps into halos of pale light. Violet stepped onto the pavement and paused, looking up at the sky, letting the rain fall on her face.

Then she began to walk.

Ruth watched from the window until the girl’s figure disappeared around the corner, swallowed by the city’s maze of streets and shadows. The weight of what she had done settled over her, heavy and complex. She had broken the rules she had spent her career defending. She had let a thief walk away unpunished. She had compromised the integrity of the exchange for the sake of compassion.

And she would do it again.

The ledger still sat on her desk, its spine worn, its pages filled with the careful records of connections made and broken. Ruth thought about all the calls that had passed through this building, all the voices carried on lines of copper and wire. Lives intersecting briefly, then separating, leaving nothing behind but faint impressions in the records.

Violet’s call had ended. But Ruth’s was not yet finished.

She turned back to her desk and opened the drawer. The resignation letter lay beside the stolen fault log page, the one she had taken weeks ago, the one that had started all of this. The handwriting on that page was not Violet’s. It belonged to someone else, someone whose motives were far darker than a daughter’s desperation.

Alec Dunbar had falsified records too. He had covered a line outage for Marjorie Finch, had altered the fault log for reasons he claimed were chivalrous. But Ruth had seen something else in his eyes when he spoke of Marjorie’s fear, something that hinted at deeper secrets. What had the girl overheard that frightened her so badly? What call had passed through the exchange that night, unrecorded, untraceable, buried beneath layers of forgery and silence?

Ruth touched the edge of the stolen page. The paper was cool beneath her fingertips, its surface smooth and unmarked save for the precise rows of technical notation. Somewhere in those numbers lay the truth about Terence Bolt, about the trunk call that had vanished from the official record, about the murder that had set everything in motion.

Violet’s theft had been small, personal, born of love and desperation. But the forgery Ruth now investigated was something else entirely. It was calculated, professional, designed to erase a connection that someone did not want discovered.

Ruth closed the drawer. The clock on the wall marked the hour, and somewhere in the building, a telephone began to ring.
