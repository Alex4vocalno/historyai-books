Chapter 15

The Emperor in the Age of Print

How do you save a story from being loved to death? Not from neglect, but from the affectionate, distorting squeeze of collective memory—from the embellishments, the contradictions, the outright inventions that accumulate like dust on a cherished heirloom passed through too many hands. The answer is not to tell it less, but to fix it in a form that resists change.

By 1915, the legend of Emperor Norton was precisely such an heirloom. It had survived the fire, but it was smoky, handled, its edges softened by decades of retelling. To preserve it for a century that would have no living witnesses, you needed to press it between the pages of something more permanent than recollection. You needed to freeze it in print.

And so, in the December 11, 1915, issue of The Saturday Evening Post, one of the most widely circulated magazines in America, there it was: a feature article titled “The Emperor of the United States.” This was not a local squib or a humorous filler. It was a signal.

The process of institutionalization—the rescue of a fluid oral tradition by the formal machinery of historical and literary culture—had found its first major national distributor. The Post was a weekly institution that defined middle-class taste and curiosity from coast to coast. Its pages carried serialized novels, political commentary, and advertisements for national brands. To feature in its pages was to enter a canon of American oddities and archetypes.

The article’s author, Idwal Jones, presented Joshua Abraham Norton not as some vague local character, but as a defined historical figure. He recounted the rice corner, the bankruptcy, the proclamation in the Bulletin. He described the imperial inspections, the accepted currency, the respectful police. The tone was warm, slightly wistful for a vanished San Francisco, but the facts were presented as settled history. This was not a story being told over a bar; this was a story being archived for a national audience.

Jones was performing a crucial act of translation: packaging a local San Francisco idiosyncrasy as a species of American folklore.

In his hands, and in the hands of journalists like him, Norton became a type: the Harmless Eccentric Monarch, a figure who could sit comfortably in the national imagination alongside Johnny Appleseed or Casey Jones. The emphasis was always on the city’s charming tolerance—the “kindness” of our thesis—but now that kindness was framed as a civic achievement, a point of pride to be advertised to the rest of the country. San Francisco, these articles implicitly argued, was not just a boomtown that got rich; it was a sophisticated city that knew how to play.

This journalistic project was the first of three parallel lines of work that, in the 1910s and 1920s, hardened the Emperor’s story into a reproducible narrative. The generation that had actually tipped its hat to Norton on Montgomery Street was dying off. The city they had known was gone, replaced by a faster, steelier metropolis rising from the ashes. The legend, if it were to endure, could no longer be an oral tradition. It had to become literature. It had to become history.

A cohort of writers—journalists, early civic chroniclers, feature writers—took up the task almost simultaneously, not by coordination but by a shared cultural instinct. They became the mechanics of canonization, building the machinery that would standardize the Emperor’s story and insert it irrevocably into the official mythology of San Francisco.

First, the national journalists. Their work was export and branding. After Jones’s 1915 piece, other major publications followed suit. Writers for Collier’s, The American Mercury, and travel magazines discovered Norton as a perfect symbol of San Francisco’s purported whimsy and tolerance. They smoothed out the rough, peculiar edges of the oral tales into a cohesive, repeatable anecdote. The messy complexity of the man—his bankruptcy despair, his political anger, his precise management of his imperial persona—was often sanded down into a simpler fable of a dreamer humored by a good-natured town.

Yet, even in this smoothing, a crucial service was rendered: they established a baseline plot. The rice speculation, the imperial proclamation, the loyal subjects, the majestic funeral—this sequence became the agreed-upon arc.

It was a story that could be told in Philadelphia, Chicago, or New York without needing local footnotes. In making Norton a national figure, they ensured his local survival; a story interesting to America would not be forgotten by San Francisco.

The second line of work was even more vital: the early civic historians. These were writers compiling the first formal popular histories of the city in the aftermath of the 1906 disaster. Their books were not dry academic treatises; they were foundational texts for a city reconstructing its identity, meant for the parlor bookshelf.

Writers like John Bruce, or the teams behind volumes like San Francisco: The Story of a City, made a conscious decision: Emperor Norton was not a sidebar. He was a chapter. He belonged in the narrative of the city’s growth alongside figures like the last Mexican alcalde, José de Jesús Noé, and the engineers who built the new water systems. This was a profound promotion. It moved Norton from the category of “amusing anecdote” to “essential character.”

In these histories, the legend was given context and gravity. The historians often reached back into the oral tradition, but they filtered it through a lens of documentation and sober judgment. They tried to reconcile conflicting birth dates—noting the silver plate on his coffin suggested 1815—and they presented his proclamations not just as jokes, but as political commentary. They treated his reign as a social phenomenon worthy of analysis. By embedding him in the chronological flow of the city’s story—after the Gold Rush, before the Big Earthquake—they gave him historical weight. He became a landmark in time, not just in space.

A resident or a newcomer reading these books in the 1920s would absorb a lesson: to understand San Francisco, you had to understand its Emperor. This was canonization in the most literal sense—inclusion in the accepted list of works that define a culture.

The third parallel effort came from local newspapers and publishers who acted as re-circulators and amplifiers.

The San Francisco dailies, particularly the Chronicle and the Call, would periodically reprint stories about Norton, especially on anniversaries of his death or on slow news days. But now, they often drew from these new, stabilized print sources rather than from fading memories. They reprinted excerpts from Jones’s Post article or summarized chapters from the new city histories.

Local publishing houses also began to include Norton tales in anthologies of California humor or collections of San Francisco lore. Each reprinting was another stamp of institutional approval, another instance of the story being validated by an official-seeming source.

This ecosystem—national magazines, local histories, and republishing newspapers—formed a virtuous circle of reinforcement. A detail printed in a 1922 history would be cited by a magazine writer in 1925, which would then be echoed in a 1928 newspaper column. Through this repetition, certain versions of events solidified as standard. The wilder rumors of the oral age were pruned away or relegated to footnotes.

One popular story that had circulated for decades suggested Norton was the secret son of Emperor Napoleon III of France, his South African origin a ruse to avoid persecution. The new print chroniclers sometimes mentioned this tale, but they did so with a wink or a dose of historical reality-check, noting the chronological improbability (Napoleon III would have been only eleven at the time of Norton’s birth). They presented it as an example of the myth-making he inspired, not as a plausible fact. In this way, the print culture didn’t just preserve the legend; it disciplined it. It drew a line, however fuzzy, between the documented Emperor and the mythical one.

Now, you might object: isn’t this just proof that Norton was merely a tolerated eccentric, a harmless delusion indulged by a frontier city to burnish its own myth of tolerance? Weren’t the real mechanisms of power—capital, politics, law—operating unchanged and unchallenged by his pantomime? And isn’t this print canonization just the sentimental recycling of that feel-good myth for a modern audience? Look at what these writers actually did. They didn’t treat him as a joke.

They treated him as a historical actor. They analyzed the social contract his “reign” represented. In their accounts, the police salute was not just a cute bit; it was a conscious civic choice. The acceptance of his scrip was not just charity; it was a symbolic economy. By taking him seriously enough to include him in formal histories, they argued that his story was part of the real mechanism of the city. It was part of San Francisco’s social power grid—a circuit that ran on recognition and collective consent rather than statute.

These writers were documenting an alternative system of legitimacy that had actually functioned for twenty-one years. In codifying it, they were preserving not just a quirky tale, but evidence of that system’s existence and endurance. They were proving that the city’s choice of kindness was not passive tolerance, but active, creative participation.

The collective work of this ensemble—Jones, Bruce, the newspaper editors, the anthology compilers—created something durable: a paper trail that could outlast memory. The legend no longer lived primarily in the minds of old-timers.

These writers operated within a broader cultural moment that prized local color and regional distinctiveness as antidotes to a homogenizing national modernity. As standardization swept through industry and mass media began to shape a more uniform American consciousness, cities like San Francisco actively cultivated their unique historical personalities as a form of civic capital. The codification of Emperor Norton was part of this project of self-definition. He became a trademark of San Francisco’s exceptionalism, a proof that its history was not merely a linear progression of commerce and disaster but was enriched by a human peculiarity that more sober metropolises lacked. This was not accidental nostalgia; it was a strategic curation of the past for present purposes. The historians and journalists were, in effect, building a usable past—a past that could explain and justify the city’s contemporary reputation for eccentricity and tolerance.

The process of repetition across this print ecosystem had a subtle but profound disciplinary effect. When a detail appeared in both Idwal Jones’s Saturday Evening Post article and John Bruce’s city history, it gained the weight of consensus. This consensus then guided subsequent tellings. For instance, the poignant detail of Norton’s funeral being paid for by the Pacific Club membership solidified as fact through such cross-citation, even though earlier oral versions sometimes credited other benefactors. The print record began to function as its own authority, a closed loop of verification where one published source corroborated another. This created a stable core narrative that was resistant to challenge simply because it was so widely disseminated in respectable venues. The legend was not just being preserved; it was being standardized, much like a manufactured good, ensuring that every retold version contained the same essential components.

Local newspapers played a crucial role in this standardization by acting as daily tutors for the public memory. Their periodic republications of Norton stories—often on the anniversary of his death in January—were less about breaking news and more about ritual commemoration. These articles served as mnemonics for a citizenry increasingly composed of newcomers who had no direct link to the 19th-century city. A 1923 piece in the San Francisco Chronicle might not only recount the familiar beats of the story but also explicitly reference its source: “As recounted in last year’s popular history San Francisco: The Story of a City, Emperor Norton’s proclamations revealed a curious foresight…” This practice did more than remind; it educated readers on where to find the “official” version, directing them from the ephemeral newspaper to the more permanent book on the shelf. The newspaper column became both a lesson and a bibliography.

This institutionalization also required navigating the tension between documentation and enchantment. The early civic historians felt a professional obligation to fact-check where possible, yet they were also storytellers crafting an engaging civic mythos. Their treatment of Norton’s proclamations exemplifies this balancing act. They would often transcribe the text of an edict dissolving Congress or ordering a bridge built, presenting it as a primary document worthy of archival notice. Simultaneously, they would frame these documents not as evidence of insanity but as symbolic protests, interpreting them through a lens of social commentary. In doing so, they performed a critical alchemy: they transformed ephemeral street theater into lasting political critique, granting Norton’s performance an intellectual gravity that pure oral anecdote often lacked. They made him a thinker, not just a character.

The collective effort of these actors created a feedback loop of legitimacy. A story deemed worthy of inclusion in a weighty city history gained stature; that stature then made the story more attractive for inclusion in national magazines; national coverage further validated its importance for local readers and historians. This loop ensured that Norton’s place in the narrative was not precarious but continually reinforced. By the late 1920s, omitting Emperor Norton from an account of San Francisco would have seemed an oversight, not a choice. He had become obligatory, a required stop on any tour through the city’s past. His legend was now part of the city’s curricular heritage, something to be learned rather than merely remembered.

Yet, this very success contained an inherent tension.

It lived in libraries, on magazine racks, in footnoted chapters. It was now citable. It was now verifiable. A high school student in 1930 could write a term paper about Emperor Norton using sources that their teacher could check.

This is the moment when anecdote becomes artifact. The consequence of all this machinery was a paradox. The Emperor Norton story was saved from the vagaries of love and forgetfulness. It was given a stable, authoritative form. But in being set in print, it also became static. The vibrant, living tradition of the saloon tale, where every teller might add a new flourish, was effectively over. The story was now official. It had an approved version.

This was not a loss so much as a necessary metamorphosis. The myth had to become history to survive into an age of mass media and disappearing eyewitnesses. By the early 1930s, the job was largely complete. The Emperor had been successfully institutionalized within the literary and historical culture of early twentieth-century San Francisco.

He was no longer a ghost of the old city; he was a documented citizen of its past, a character in its foundational saga. The printed canon provided a fixed portrait, framed and hung in the gallery of the city’s memory. That portrait, however clear and stable, was waiting for new eyes. It was waiting for a medium that could take that fixed image and set it in motion again, for a generation that learned its stories not just from pages, but from flickering light and projected sound. The canon was ready. The next era would decide what to do with it.
