Chapter 3

The Proclamation in the Bulletin (September 1859)

The paper was cheap, the ink still smelling faintly of the press. The San Francisco Bulletin for Saturday, September 17, 1859, was eight pages of the usual civic noise. The front page carried shipping news and political reports from the East.

Inside, advertisements vied for attention: Dr. D.B. Smith’s Magnetic Oil for rheumatism, the Union Iron Works offering steam engines, a notice for the steamer Golden Age departing for Panama.

On page four, tucked into a column headed “Miscellaneous Items,” between a note about a new city ordinance on garbage and another about a missing shipment of boots, appeared a notice of a different order. It was set in the same modest typeface as everything else, granted no special headline, and began with a simple, staggering declaration:
“At the peremptory request and desire of a large majority of the citizens of these United States, I, Joshua Norton, formerly of Algoa Bay, Cape of Good Hope, and now for the last 9 years and 10 months past of San Francisco, California, declare and proclaim myself Emperor of these United States.”

The man who emerged from the wreckage in 1858 was physically the same Joshua Norton, but he was stripped of every anchor that had defined him: his wealth, his profession, his social standing, and his identity as a pillar of the commercial community. For over a year, he had been a ghost in the city he helped build, moving through streets lined with buildings whose foundations he had financed, past wharves where ships he had once owned shares in now disgorged cargo for other men’s accounts. He lived in a rented room at the Eureka Lodgings on Commercial Street, a far cry from the fine hotels and clubrooms of his prime.

The city, meanwhile, had not paused in its frantic growth. It was a place of constant becoming, where fortunes were made and lost between breakfast and supper, where the very ground seemed unstable, both literally—with its planked streets over shifting sand—and socially. It was a city that understood ruin intimately, which is perhaps why it could look upon a ruined man and see something other than just failure.

By the autumn of 1859, the political air was thick with a different kind of instability. The Union was tearing itself apart over slavery. The Dred Scott decision two years prior had shredded any pretense of national compromise. John Brown was gathering his forces for the raid on Harpers Ferry that would ignite in October. In San Francisco, the newspapers were full of it: the debates, the threats of secession, the palpable fear that the great American experiment was cracking at its core.

For a man like Norton, who had become completely discontented with what he considered the inadequacies of the legal and political structures of the United States, the spectacle of a nation potentially dissolving itself through political failure would have been more than an abstraction. It would have felt like a confirmation. If the system could destroy a solid merchant on a technicality of contract and market timing, what could it not do? If the representatives of the people in Congress could not steer the ship of state away from the rocks, then what good were they? He had been brooding on this for some time.

In July of that same year, Norton had issued a brief manifesto addressed to the “Citizens of the Union.” It was a trial balloon, a sketch of grievances. It outlined in the broadest terms the nation’s political maladies and hinted that a remedy, however drastic, might be required. It went nowhere, lost in the summer news. But it proved he was thinking in terms of national addresses, in proclamations. He was practicing the form.

So, on or just before September 17, Joshua Norton left his lodgings, walked up Commercial Street, and turned onto Montgomery. The Bulletin’s office was at 145 Montgomery Street, in the heart of the newspaper district. He would have passed brokers’ offices, assayers, saloons, and the new, imposing Mills Building.

He carried with him a piece of paper. We do not know if it was neatly penned or hastily scrawled. We know only what appeared in print. He entered the office, a busy place of clattering telegraphs and scratching pens, and presented his document. He did not, it seems, demand an audience with the editor.

He likely handed it to a clerk at the counter who collected such items for the “Miscellaneous” column. It was processed as correspondence.

Here lies the first critical pivot. The editor on duty, or the clerk, read it. They had a choice. They could have dismissed it as the ramblings of a known bankrupt, a sad joke. They could have filed it in the wastebasket. They could have printed it with a mocking editorial comment. They did none of these things. They typeset it verbatim and slotted it into the paper as if it were any other notice from a citizen.

This was not an act of charity. It was an act of publication, granting the words the legitimacy of the public record. It was the inaugural loop of what we might call recursive legitimacy: the act of treating a claim as legitimate makes it slightly more legitimate, which invites further acts of recognition. The proclamation did not stop with its opening salvo. Having declared himself Emperor, Norton I got straight to work.

“And in virtue of the authority thereby in me vested,” the text continued, “I do hereby order and direct the representatives of the different States of the Union to assemble in Musical Hall, of this city, on the 1st day of February next, then and there to make such alterations in the existing laws of these States as may ameliorate the evils under which the country is laboring, and thereby cause confidence to exist, both at home and abroad, in our stability and integrity.”

This was breathtaking in its bureaucratic audacity. He did not ask. He ordered and directed. He specified a location: Musical Hall, a real venue on Bush Street used for concerts and lectures. He set a date: February 1, 1860. He gave them an agenda: fix the laws to cure the national evils and restore confidence. He was summoning a rump Congress to San Francisco under imperial decree. Then came the hammer.

“And I do hereby further order and direct the immediate dissolution of Congress; and also order and direct that all laws enacted by said Congress shall remain in full force and effect until otherwise ordered and directed by myself.”

With these two sentences, Norton performed a complete constitutional coup in print. He dissolved the existing federal legislature and simultaneously ensured continuity of governance by preserving all its laws—provisionally, subject to his future review. It was a masterstroke of pseudo-legal framing.

He invoked “the authority thereby in me vested,” a phrase that pointed back to his opening line about the “peremptory request… of a large majority of the citizens.” His authority, he claimed, flowed from a popular mandate. The people were dissatisfied; they desired a new sovereign; he was merely answering their call. He was not seizing power; he was accepting a duty.

This is where the document transcends mere eccentricity. Its power lies in its perfect mimicry of legal and political form. It reads like a cross between a royal patent and a municipal ordinance.

It uses the language of sovereignty (“We do hereby order and direct”) with the specificity of a city council meeting notice. It acknowledges existing reality (“the existing laws”) while asserting the power to change it. It is calm, procedural, and utterly insane.

And because it is so perfectly formatted, it becomes hard to dismiss as mere raving. Raving is incoherent. This was coherent. It was a blueprint for an alternative government, filed in the public square.

Consider what it weaponized. First, it weaponized popular discontent. By 1859, “a large majority of the citizens” probably were fed up with Congress. Norton was channeling a real sentiment into his fictional mandate. Second, it weaponized San Francisco’s self-image. The order for representatives to assemble in this city flattered local pride. It placed San Francisco at the center of national renewal, a notion that played well in a boomtown that still saw itself as the future capital of the Pacific world. Third, and most crucially, it weaponized the formalities of public life.

In a young city struggling to establish order—with its volunteer fire departments, its vigilance committees that had taken over policing just years before, its ad-hoc governance—the performance of order was itself a valued commodity. Norton offered a performance of supreme order. He was not chaotic; he was issuing decrees. He was not violent; he was using the press. This was performative sovereignty in its purest inaugural act.

Authority would derive not from arms, nor inheritance, nor election, but from the consistent, public performance of the imperial role, starting with this text. The performance sought ratification. The newspaper’s decision to print it without comment was the first flicker of that ratification.

Why did the Bulletin do it? The paper was a serious, Republican-leaning daily, not a scandal sheet. The most plausible explanation is that in the context of 1859 San Francisco, the notice fit a pattern. The city papers were full of grandiose claims, speculative puffery, and advertisements that promised miracles. The line between earnest ambition and entertaining folly was famously thin.

Norton’s proclamation could be read as a particularly sharp piece of political satire—a Swiftian modest proposal to replace a broken Congress with a single, sane emperor. It could be read as a cry for help. Or it could be read as a simple statement of fact from a man who believed it. The Bulletin’s genius was to not choose a reading. By printing it straight, they allowed all readings to coexist. They made it a public fact and let the public decide what to do with it.

The document also cleverly sidestepped the charge of treason. He declared himself Emperor “of these United States,” not in place of them. He dissolved Congress but kept its laws. He was not overthrowing the republic; he was restructuring its faulty machinery from within, by popular demand. It was a correction, not a revolution. This legalistic hair-splitting made it more palatable, more of a thought experiment than a call to arms. Now, the strongest counter-argument is obvious and must be met head-on.

One could say Norton was merely a tolerated eccentric, a harmless delusion indulged by a frontier city to burnish its own myth of tolerance. The real mechanisms of power—capital, politics, law—operated unchanged and unchallenged by his pantomime. His “reign” was a sideshow, and San Francisco’s embrace was just civic vanity, a way to feel broad-minded and colorful.

The facts begin to answer this in the text itself. Look at what the proclamation does not do. It does not ask for money. It does not demand personal loyalty oaths. It does not threaten violence. It addresses a civic problem—political dysfunction—with a civic solution: a new assembly under new leadership. Its concerns are public stability, confidence at home and abroad, the amelioration of evils. These are the concerns of a merchant, a city builder. This is not the manifesto of a hermit; it is the edict of a man who, however broken, still thought in terms of systems, credit, and public order. The pantomime, if it is one, is meticulously aimed at the city’s own self-interest.

Furthermore, the act of publication itself changed the landscape. Before September 17, Joshua Norton was a private failure. After September 17, he was a public figure with a published platform. The performance now had a script. Anyone could read it. Anyone could refer to it. It existed outside his head.

This moved him from the realm of private delusion into the realm of public discourse. Tolerance is passive; discourse is active. The city did not just tolerate him; it began, through this act of publication, to engage with him. The path from his lodgings to the newspaper office was short. The path from the newspaper column to the city’s consciousness was instantaneous.

By that Saturday evening, as copies of the Bulletin were read in saloons, boarding houses, and counting rooms across San Francisco, Joshua Norton ceased to be a failed merchant. He was now Norton I., Emperor of these United States. He had performed the foundational act. He had minted his new currency of authority.

The coin was struck; its value would be determined entirely by what people were willing to give in exchange for it.

Would they salute? Would they laugh? Would they ignore him? Would they use his name to sell souvenirs? The proclamation did not predict these outcomes. It merely set them in motion.

It created a vacancy—the role of Emperor—and then filled it with one very specific, very serious-looking man. It was an open invitation to a social game, with rules implied by its own formal language.

In the days that followed, the notice did not disappear. It was not followed by an editorial retraction or an apology for a hoax. It sat in the back issues, a matter of record. Norton himself now possessed a tangible proof of his new station: a newspaper clipping.

He could show it. He could refer to it. His next move would be critical. Would he retreat, as if shocked by his own audacity?

Or would he behave as if the proclamation were true—as if Congress were indeed dissolved and representatives were expected in February? The city held its breath without knowing it was holding its breath. A wire had been tripped. A new reality, however thin and performative, had been declared into the noisy air of San Francisco. It waited now for a response. The response would not come from Washington, D.C. It would come from the bartenders, the policemen, the newspaper editors, and the streetcar conductors of San Francisco. They would decide, through a thousand small choices, whether this was a joke to chuckle at once and forget, or the beginning of a story they wanted to tell about themselves. The paper had been published. The words were loose in the world. The emperor had issued his first decree. There was no going back.
