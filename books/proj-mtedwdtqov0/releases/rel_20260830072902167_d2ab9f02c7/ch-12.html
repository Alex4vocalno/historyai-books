Chapter 12

The Currency of a Kingdom (1880-1889)

The steel engraving plate, cool and smooth as a tombstone, lay clamped in the bed of the Washington hand press. Around it, the small back room of Bradley & Co., Stationers, on Montgomery Street held the close, metallic smell of ink and the sharper tang of the acid used to clean the rollers.

It was late February 1880. The great black crepe of the public funeral had been taken down from the City Hall columns weeks before, but here, in this cramped space lit by a single gas jet, a different kind of interment was underway.

The proprietor, a practical man whose name history did not trouble to record, carefully inked the plate. It bore not text, but an image: the stern, bearded profile of Joshua A. Norton, crowned by a beaver hat with a faded military cockade.

With a practiced pull on the lever, he brought the platen down with a solid thump. The first impression of the first commercial print run was made. He peeled the sheet off. There, in crisp black lines, was the Emperor, reproduced.

The sheet would be cut into dozens of individual cartes-de-visite, the calling-card photographs of the era. For a nickel or a dime, any San Franciscan could now own a portable piece of the passing pageant.

This was not an act of memorialization in the poetic sense; it was an act of manufacture. The raw material of a life—a face, a story, a peculiar sovereignty—was being fed into the mechanical press and stamped out by the hundred.

The kindness economy, having settled its accounts with the man in a spectacular display of communal capital, was discovering that capital could be liquefied, minted into new coin. Where there was collective affection, a market was quick to follow.

This was the quieter, more enduring work that began the moment the funeral carriages turned off onto the quiet side streets.

The magnificent public performance was over. The 30, 000 mourners had gone home. Now came the curation of the ghost, and the first step was inevitably commercial. Bradley’s cartes-de-visite were the opening bid.

The photograph he copied was not new; it was a studio portrait taken years earlier when the Emperor had graciously consented to sit for the camera. In life, such an image was a curio, a snapshot of the town character you might chuckle over.

In death, mass-produced and sold for small change, it transformed into a relic. It allowed a citizen to possess, in tangible form, what they had already participated in collectively. The transaction was simple: you paid a few cents, and in return you received not just a picture, but a token of your membership in a shared civic story. You were buying proof that you belonged to the city that had played the game.

The industry of Emperor Norton was born not from grief, but from opportunity. And the opportunities multiplied with swift, unromantic logic.

Almost in tandem with the photographs, another artifact from Norton’s reign began its lucrative second life: his imperial bonds.

During his twenty-one-year rule, these handsomely engraved certificates—issued in denominations from fifty cents to ten dollars and signed with his looping “Norton I”—functioned as physical tokens of the kindness economy. Restaurateurs and saloon-keepers accepted them for his modest meals and drinks, treating them as polite fictions: IOUs from a sovereign whose treasury was his city’s goodwill. They were social currency, valued for the interaction they represented rather than any monetary worth. His death in January 1880 triggered a radical revaluation. The bonds became instantly priceless as unique mementos—scraps of paper that had been in his pocket, touched by his hand—and simultaneously worthless as financial instruments, for their sole issuer and guarantor was gone. This paradox made them ideal for commodification. Within months, printers produced reproductions openly marketed as souvenir certificates, “facsimiles of the Original Imperial Debt.”

They served two markets: the barkeep who had once honored a bond could now frame a replica as a trophy of his own good citizenship, and the tourist or newcomer could purchase, for a quarter, a ready-made piece of San Francisco folklore. The original signed bonds became cherished heirlooms behind barroom glass; the reproductions became a flood of affordable, standardized nostalgia. The fragile, living performance of the kindness economy was thus stabilized, packaged, and offered for sale.

Why did this happen with such speed and lack of sentimentality? Because San Francisco in 1880 was an adolescent metropolis in urgent need of its own founding myths. The raw, roaring boomtown of’49 was a generation past. The city now had central banks, gaslit streets, horsecars, and a population pushing a quarter-million. It was building universities, grand hotels, and an opera house. It was, in other words, growing up and growing self-conscious.

A mature city requires foundation tales—stories that explain its character to itself and to the world.

Its official history was one of brutal ambition: financial panics and audacious comebacks, vigilante committees and political graft, racial violence along the docks and in the alleys. It was a history of hard power and harder money, true enough, but not a history that warmed the heart or sold the city as a place of particular virtue.

The story of Emperor Norton offered a counter-myth. It was a tale not of crushing failure, but of eccentric elevation. It showcased San Francisco not as a machine that broke its dreamers, but as a community that could crown one—if only in spirit. It replaced the archetype of the lone prospector striking it rich with a more complex, more appealing archetype: the community lifting up the man who had lost everything.

Here was a foundational folktale that emphasized tolerance, wit, and collective benevolence. It suggested that amidst the famous gold-rush avarice, there had also been a unique capacity for play, for granting dignity. This was a useful myth for a city seeking respectability and a distinctive identity.

But folktales must be collected, curated, and canonized to endure. The marketplace, sensing this unspoken civic demand, became the chief curator. The process advanced logically from artifact to anecdote.

If photographs and bonds commodified his image, the city’s newspapers undertook the systematic commodification of his story. Throughout 1880 and the years that followed, the San Francisco Chronicle, the Bulletin, the Call, and others regularly published retrospective sketches and “reminiscences” of the Emperor.

These pieces were not news; they were acts of canonization. Journalists scoured civic memory, gathering oral anecdotes that had circulated for decades like a kind of municipal gossip: the time he inspected new sewer lines with the gravity of a field marshal reviewing fortifications; the time he was said to have dissolved an angry mob by issuing a decree on the spot; the time he levied a “tax” on a wealthy bank president, who paid it with a laugh and a gold piece.

These stories were set in type. Print is a fossilizing agent. An oral tale is fluid, changing with each teller, embellished or trimmed.

Once set in newspaper columns, a story becomes fixed. By committing these anecdotes to print, journalists performed a crucial editorial function: deciding which stories were “true” enough—meaning emblematic enough—to repeat. They selected moments that best captured the desired essence: harmless, dignified, whimsically authoritative. They created a standardized biography from the chaotic raw material of lived experience.

In these printed retrospectives, narrative shaped memory carefully. The story of his catastrophic bankruptcy in the rice market was no longer a tale of mental collapse and ruin; it became a whimsical prelude, the necessary fall before his glorious invented rise. His poverty was rendered quaint, his decrees charmingly absurd rather than disturbingly unmoored. The potential tragedy sanded away, leaving only polished comedy and benevolence—a secular saint of good humor.

This was not conspiracy but convergence: commerce meeting civic identity. Newspapers sold copies by feeding a palpable public appetite for nostalgic content.

In doing so, they solidified a shared memory that inherently flattered the city itself. Every reprinted bond in a shop window and every published anecdote in the morning paper served this dual purpose: it generated profit for a printer or publisher, and it reinforced San Francisco’s desired self-image as a uniquely kind and clever place. The legend was not emerging organically from the misty past; it was being actively, deliberately constructed in real time, its bricks made of newsprint and souvenir cardstock.

Consider the process, on a vastly different scale and with opposite intent, that would unfold in another empire decades later. After a catastrophic war, Japan’s Emperor Hirohito would be compelled to explicitly renounce the quasi-official claim that he was an arahitogami—an incarnate divinity. That was the deliberate, political dismantling of a state-imposed myth.

In San Francisco in the 1880s, the mechanism was inverted and benign: a city was deliberately assembling a myth for social cohesion and commercial benefit.

It was bottom-up canonization: printers and journalists drove it rather than priests or ministers; its scripture composed of humorous column inches and salable souvenirs; its doctrine one of inclusive laughter. This construction project had its own internal logic and chronology. By the mid-1880s, the legend had achieved stable, marketable form: standardized anecdotes; fixed iconic image; souvenir bonds as staple of tourist trade. Emperor Norton had become safe packaged symbol of “Old San Francisco,” touchstone for local pride that cost nothing in political capital and asked only for a smile. His story grew useful beyond selling newspapers or picture cards—it became rhetorical device, benchmark of city character. Orators at civic banquets invoked his name as proof of San Francisco’s big-hearted uniqueness; guidebooks included paragraphs on “Reign of Norton I” as essential local color.

The legend became a tool for smoothing over the city’s rougher edges. When confronted with evidence of its harshness—the anti-Chinese riots that had marred the late 1870s, the ruthless inequalities of its economy—a defender could point to the Emperor. See? the gesture implied. We are also the city that supported this man for twenty-one years. Our heart is in the right place. His myth acted as a moral counterweight, an anecdotal shield against less flattering narratives.

This functional, utilitarian aspect of the legend is crucial to understanding its rapid solidification. A city’s memory is not a passive archive; it is an active tool.

San Francisco was using Norton, or rather the carefully curated version of him, to do cultural work. He helped bridge the gap between the wild frontier past and the aspirational cosmopolitan present. He provided a continuous thread of harmless eccentricity that made the city’s turbulent history seem more colorful, less brutal.

This process embedded Emperor Norton into San Francisco’s daily social fabric more deeply than stable legend alone could achieve. Reproduced bonds and cartes-de-visite became conversational totems—physical prompts for retelling now-standardized anecdotes. In saloons across town above bars hung framed facsimile bonds no longer mere souvenirs but invitations: regulars regaled newcomers with tales like that imperial audit concluding always with toast to city good nature.

The utility of this packaged legend extended beyond casual storytelling into the realm of civic rhetoric and self-justification.

As the city grappled with the violent aftermath of the anti-Chinese riots and the stark divisions of the ongoing “Sandlot” labor agitation, the figure of Emperor Norton was increasingly invoked as a symbol of what San Francisco could be at its best.

When a newspaper editor or a politician wished to appeal to the city’s professed higher character, they might contrast present strife with the remembered harmony of Norton’s “reign.” The implied argument was potent: if we could collectively sustain this benevolent fiction for two decades, surely we possessed the innate generosity to solve our real problems.

This was, of course, a profound simplification, using the sanitized myth to paper over complex social fractures. Yet it demonstrated how thoroughly the manufactured legend had become a functional tool in the city’s ideological toolkit, a ready-made emblem of unity that was wielded to admonish, to inspire, or to deflect.

He was a symbol that everyone could agree on—businessmen and laborers, natives and newcomers—because his story demanded nothing more than a nod of recognition. In a place often divided by class, race, and labor strife, the Emperor was neutral territory, a monarch who asked no taxes and levied no armies, only a little respectful play-acting.

By the close of the 1880s, this process of commodification and canonization was complete. The raw material of Joshua Norton’s life had been thoroughly processed. The man had been transformed into the symbol.

The living, breathing, sometimes hungry individual who walked the streets was now fully encased in the protective lacquer of legend—a legend that was reproducible, marketable, and useful. You could buy his image, read his standardized stories, and cite his reign as proof of your city’s special spirit.

The kindness economy had successfully securitized its greatest asset. It had taken the immense, intangible capital of public affection revealed at the funeral and issued shares in it, shares that sold briskly on the open market of civic identity.

The consequence was a legacy that was now stable, portable, and ready for export. The Emperor Norton of San Francisco’s local memory was no longer a fragile story dependent on eyewitnesses who would themselves die off. He was a set of products and printed tales. He could survive the passing of the generation that had known him. He could be picked up, examined, and reinterpreted by people who had never seen his solemn patrol along Montgomery Street.

The city had finished building its favorite ghost. It had given him a durable form, one that could withstand time and travel. The currency of his kingdom—the bonds, the images, the anecdotes—was now solid, negotiable tender in the economy of local myth.

It sat in shop windows and on library shelves, waiting for the next stage of its journey, when it would leave the city limits and enter the broader, more unpredictable currents of the national imagination.
