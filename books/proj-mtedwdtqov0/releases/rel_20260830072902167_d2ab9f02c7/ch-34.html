Chapter 34

A Throne in the Public Imagination

The final column is still being tallied. But the first entry in that ledger was written in black crepe and flowers on a January afternoon in 1880.

Nearly a third of the city of San Francisco—some 30, 000 souls—formed a silent, somber river along the sidewalks. They watched a hearse drawn by two white horses carry the body of Joshua A. Norton from the mortuary chapel to the Masonic Cemetery.

It was a sovereign’s funeral for a sovereign without a state. The police who had always saluted him served as his honor guard. The businesses that had honored his scrip closed their doors. The newspapers that had printed his proclamations wrote his obituaries in the tone reserved for departed heads of state. For a few hours, the fiction was made flesh, and then it was lowered into the ground.

What do you do with a throne when the king is dead, and the throne never existed in the first place?

One hundred and forty-five years later, in the second decade of the 21st century, a different kind of petition was circulating in that same city. It was not for a funeral, but for a naming.

A civic advocacy group, The Emperor Norton Trust, campaigned to have the newly constructed eastern pedestrian walkway on the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge officially designated “The Emperor Norton Tunnel.” They submitted proposals, gathered signatures, and made their case to regional transportation commissions.

Here was no mourning for a lost past, but an active attempt to engrave the Emperor’s name onto the infrastructure of the present. The bridge, that supreme symbol of tangible, engineered connection, might carry the name of a man whose entire empire was built on intangible, communal consent.

The contrast is stark: 1880’s massive, final tribute to a vanished persona, versus 2025’s forward-looking effort to institutionalize his memory in steel and concrete. One closed a book. The other insists the story is still a living, usable text. Why does this happen?

Why does a bankrupt rice merchant who declared himself emperor not fade into a footnote of local oddities, but instead grow in stature, his story retold and repurposed for generations? The easy answer is that it’s a charming tale. But America has no shortage of charming tales that end up preserved in amber, visited only by antiquarians.

The harder, more significant answer is that Joshua Norton’s story persists because it accidentally put its finger on a perennial American nerve: the raw, often uncomfortable space between the authority that is and the authority we choose to believe in.

After the last mourner left the cemetery in 1880, San Francisco faced that question in its most immediate form. The Emperor was gone. The performance was over.

What remained was the memory, and memory is malleable. The first instinct was preservation, a kind of gentle embalming. Norton became a safe, nostalgic curio of “Old San Francisco,” a relic of the city’s rough-and-tumble, individualist youth. He was filed alongside tales of gold rush bandits and Barbary Coast saloons—colorful, harmless, and definitively past.

This was the era of civic boosterism, especially after the 1906 earthquake and fire demanded a new founding myth for a rebuilt city. The 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition presented a gleaming, modern metropolis; Norton served as a quaint, pre-modern counterpoint, proof of the city’s colorful character.

But this packaging could not fully contain the story’s inherent voltage. To treat Norton merely as a harmless eccentric was to ignore the subversive core of his two-decade reign. He had issued edicts dissolving the U.S. Congress. He had commanded armies to disband. He had created currency that people accepted. These were not the acts of a mere fool; they were a mirror held up to the solemn rituals of actual power, and the reflection was both hilarious and deeply questioning.

The story kept escaping its nostalgic cage. The first major jailbreak was academic. In 1923, Robert Ernest Cowan published a serious article on Norton in the California Historical Society Quarterly. This was not a whimsical feature in a newspaper, but a scholarly treatment with footnotes and research.

Cowan’s work granted the Emperor something crucial: historical ballast. He was no longer just a “character,” but a subject, a figure whose impact could be studied and analyzed. This academic anchor made him portable. He could be moved from the shelf labeled “Local Lore” to the shelf labeled “American Studies.” Biographies followed, most notably Allen Stanley Lane’s Emperor Norton, Mad Monarch of America in 1939. Published on the eve of a world war that would feature very real, very dangerous emperors, Lane’s book framed Norton’s benign sovereignty as a poignant alternative—a monarchy of pure imagination, devoid of tanks or secret police.

This is where we hit the first major “why.” Why did scholars start taking a paper emperor seriously?

Because his story formalized a suspicion that runs through American history: that the trappings of authority are often just that—trappings. That legitimacy is sometimes a collective agreement to pretend. The founding of the republic itself was an act of magnificent fiction-making.

The Declaration of Independence was adopted unanimously by the Second Continental Congress on July 4, 1776, and its first public readings occurred simultaneously at noon on July 8 in Philadelphia, Trenton, and Easton. It was a proclamation that created a new political reality through words, ratified by the delegates’ act of high treason against the Crown.

A group of colonies declared themselves sovereign states, constituting a new nation by an act of will and words on parchment. They had to fight a war to make it stick, but the initial act was one of proclamation.

Norton’s 1859 proclamation in the San Francisco Bulletin was a farcical echo of that foundational national gesture. He held up a distorting funhouse mirror to the nation’s own origin story, and in the reflection, people saw something strangely familiar.

This is not to equate the American Revolution with the reign of Emperor Norton. The stakes were worlds apart. But the underlying mechanism of creating authority through declaration and consensus—that was a shared thread.

Consider an earlier crack in the edifice of British imperial authority in America: the Gaspee Affair. In June 1772, American patriots, including John Brown, burned a British warship that had been vigorously enforcing unpopular trade regulations. It was an act of violent defiance against a symbol of legal power deemed illegitimate by the local community. The affair was investigated for possible treason, but no one was ever convicted.

Local sentiment protected the perpetrators. The royal government had the legal authority, but on the ground, in the waters of Rhode Island, a different consensus about legitimacy had formed—one that sanctioned the destruction of the Crown’s property. It was a raw, violent preview of the revolution to come, demonstrating how legal fictions crumble when popular consent withdraws.

Norton’s story is the peaceful, inverted twin of this incident. Instead of withdrawing consent from a powerful authority (burning the Gaspee), San Francisco actively bestowed consent upon a powerless one (saluting Norton). Both acts, centuries apart, highlight the same volatile substrate: authority is not monolithic. It is negotiated, sometimes violently, sometimes with a tip of the hat and a smile. Norton’s enduring appeal lies in his story capturing that negotiation in its most benign and theatrical form.

As America changed through the tumultuous 20th century, the Norton myth proved endlessly adaptable, a symbolic tool kit for whatever crisis of legitimacy was at hand. In the post-war boom, he could be a reminder of simpler, more individualistic times.

But by the 1960s and 70s, with the establishment under siege from counterculture and anti-war protests, Norton was resurrected as a patron saint of non-conformity.

Here was a man who had opted out of the commercial and political systems entirely and built his own reality with the city’s collusion. For a generation skeptical of all institutions, his reign looked less like madness and more like a brilliant, peaceful hack of the system.

Robert Altman’s 1975 film Nashville, a sprawling critique of American politics and celebrity culture, didn’t feature Norton directly, but it swam in the waters his story had helped to chart. The film’s landscape is filled with characters whose authority is performative, whose legitimacy is derived from audience belief—from country stars to political operatives. It presents an America where reality is often just an agreed-upon fiction, a theme Norton’s life had enacted on the streets of San Francisco a century earlier.

The Emperor had become an archetype: the un-corrupt sovereign whose power exists only in the space between performance and audience belief.

Which brings us to the most modern “whys.” Why does his name appear now in debates about mental health policy? Why do advocacy groups invoke him when discussing homelessness?

Because his treatment by the city poses a profound, challenging historical question: What is our obligation to someone whose reality does not align with our own? San Francisco’s 19th-century answer was remarkable: not incarceration, not ridicule (at least not cruel ridicule), but a dignified incorporation. They met his fiction with their own, creating a protective social fiction around him. In an era where mental illness is often criminalized or medicalized into anonymity, Norton’s story stands as evidence of a different possibility—one rooted in communal care rather than institutional control. It’s a hard model to replicate, but its existence is a permanent rebuke to indifference.

And why the campaigns to name bridges and towers after him? The effort to dub the Bay Bridge walkway “The Emperor Norton Tunnel” is about more than historical homage. It is an attempt to literally build his alternative model of legitimacy into the city’s official geography.

It is to say that the man who walked these streets with imaginary armies at his back deserves a permanent place on the map next to the mayors and governors who wielded actual power. It is a civic argument in steel and signage: that legitimacy born of kindness and collective play is as worthy of memorialization as legitimacy born of elections or force.

This returns us to the central tension. The strongest counter-argument against all this is simple: Norton changed nothing. He was a tolerated eccentric, a pet delusion of a frontier town. The real mechanisms of power—capital, politics, law—churned on unaffected by his pantomime. The banks foreclosed on others, the police arrested other citizens, Congress continued to meet (and dissolve) without his decree. His was a sideshow, not a challenge.

But this view mistakes the nature of his challenge. His reign was not a political revolution but a social and imaginative one. He did not alter laws; he exposed their contingency.

He did not redistribute wealth; he demonstrated that value could be assigned by consensus to worthless paper scrip.

His challenge was to the monopoly of seriousness. In a nation increasingly obsessed with industrial progress, financial rigor, and legal formality, Norton carved out a public space for a different kind of value—the value of humor, of theater, of compassionate play.

He proved that a community could, in full awareness, sustain a shared fiction not because it was true, but because the act of sustaining it made them a kinder, more interesting community. The city’s power structures did operate unchanged around him. But their operation was now watched by an audience that had also, for twenty-one years, participated in crowning an emperor.

That experience subtly changed the audience. It taught them that authority could be a game they all played, not just a force that acted upon them. That lesson is subversive in the deepest sense.

Once you have collectively pretended to obey an emperor you invented, you can never quite look at a real mayor, governor, or president with the same unquestioning awe. You have seen behind the curtain, because you helped sew it.

So the final reckoning of the paper crown? The ledger is not closed because the central American conflict it illustrates is not closed.

The tension between the rigid fictions of law and the fluid fictions of popular consent is the story of the republic itself. We see it in every protest movement that claims a moral authority beyond statute, in every populist campaign that rails against “the elites,” in every community that rallies around an underdog in defiance of official odds.

Norton’s story is a benign, distilled concentrate of that eternal struggle. San Francisco’s decision in 1859 to play along was not an endpoint. It was a genesis. It took a local man’s personal catastrophe and alchemized it, through thousands of individual choices over twenty-one years, into a national parable.

They bequeathed not just a funny story to history, but a tool for thinking about power, sanity, charity, and belonging.

When 30, 000 people turned out for his funeral, they were not just mourning a man. They were mourning, and simultaneously celebrating, their own remarkable creation.

They had built a sovereign from spare parts—bankruptcy notices, newspaper ink, theatrical uniforms, and an infinite supply of sidewalk salutes. In doing so, they proved that the most durable empires are not those carved by swords or bought with gold, but those built, laugh by laugh and kindness by kindness, in the shared space of the public imagination.

That empire has no borders and no expiration date. Its currency is still accepted wherever people wonder, just for a moment, what would happen if we all decided to play along.

The best-loved monarch in American history remains a bankrupt rice merchant who declared himself Emperor of the United States. He deserves not our pity, but our salute. The Emperor is dead. Long live the Emperor.
