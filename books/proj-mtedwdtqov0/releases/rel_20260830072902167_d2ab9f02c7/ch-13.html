Chapter 13

The Emperor in the National Imagination (1889-1906)

The cartoonist’s pencil hovered over the illustration board, then descended in a swift, sure line. It carved out the silhouette of a hat—an old-fashioned beaver hat with a distinct, ragged plume. A few more strokes suggested a worn coat, a pair of canine shapes at heel.

The artist sat back in his chair, the gaslight hissing overhead in the Chicago Inter-Ocean’s art room late in 1890. On another sheet lay his first drawing: the bristling mustache, the spiked helmet, the rigid posture of Kaiser Wilhelm II. That one was easy; the German Emperor’s aggressive posturing was a daily reference point.

This second figure required a different touch. It needed to be immediately familiar to readers who had never been west of the Mississippi, yet devoid of menace.

The caption was already written: “A Study in Imperial Styles.” He labeled the first “The Real Article.” For the shabbier, gentler figure, he wrote: “The American Imitation.” He did not need to write the name.

By 1890, the outline of Emperor Norton was becoming a national glyph. This was the moment of translation.

The local story, freshly minted into the tidy currency of San Francisco myth in the 1880s, was being stamped with a new, broader denomination. It was being reshaped, repurposed, and sent into circulation far beyond the city limits.

The cartoon’s joke was gentle, almost affectionate: America, it suggested, produced its emperors as harmless parodies, not threatening autocrats. But in that repurposing lay a subtle theft. The Emperor was no longer solely San Francisco’s favorite ghost; he was becoming a malleable symbol for a changing America, a vessel for commentary on everything from populist fervor to media sensationalism to the very idea of what it meant to be American in the Gilded Age.

This chapter charts that maturation. It follows the centrifugal force that spun Joshua Norton’s story out from its civic center into the wild, less controlled orbits of national fascination. In the two decades after his local canonization—after the biographies, the photographs, and the sentimental reminiscences had fixed his image in the Bay Area—his legend escaped.

It was appropriated and reshaped by forces that had never saluted him on Montgomery Street. The claim here is simple: San Francisco’s specific, sustained act of kindness, that twenty-one-year performance of playing along, did not just create a local wonder. It yielded a robust national artifact. And in doing so, it proved that the mechanisms of his “reign”—the social currency, the performed legitimacy—could travel. They could mean something even to people who had never seen him, because what they recognized in his story was a reflection of their own bewildering time.

The first and most prolific agents of this nationalization were not cartoonists but wordsmiths. In the 1890s, the anecdote about Emperor Norton became a staple of syndicated columns and humor magazines read from New York to St. Louis. Publications like Puck, Judge, and The Wasp trafficked in political satire and social jokes, and Norton’s tale was perfect raw material.

Writers and editors plucked it from the growing reservoir of print and embellished it to fit their needs—to fill a column inch, to land a punchline, to illustrate a point about human folly.

The core facts often blurred in transit. His disastrous rice gambit in late 1852, which played out poorly and ultimately forced him to declare bankruptcy in 1856, was sometimes rendered as a vague “speculative loss.” His solemn proclamation in the Bulletin, signed “Norton I., Emperor of the United States,” was frequently condensed into a single comic headline: “BANKRUPT DECLARES HIMSELF EMPEROR.” The delicate, sustained reciprocity of his daily life—the honored scrip, the salutes, the free meals—was boiled down to a simpler formula: the city “humored” its “harmless lunatic.”

But it was in the inventions and exaggerations that the legend truly flexed its new muscles. These were not mistakes; they were creative amendments. One popular recycled tale had Norton issuing a decree to formally abolish the Democratic and Republican parties for causing too much trouble. Another had him commanding the Army to clear Congress out of the Capitol building so it could be turned into a public marketplace. These were folk inventions, adding political satire to his résumé long after the fact. They made him a vehicle for widespread frustration with the partisan gridlock and corruption of the era.

The most persistent fabrication, which sprouted in Eastern papers in the mid-1890s, was that Emperor Norton was secretly supremely wealthy and was merely feigning poverty as an eccentric experiment. This twist perfectly served the Gilded Age’s obsessive anxiety about wealth and appearance. It transformed him from a man who had failed in the marketplace into a mysterious millionaire who had transcended it—a fantasy that resonated deeply in an age of sudden fortunes and crushing panics.

These stories had a self-perpetuating energy. A joke published in New York would be picked up by a paper in Cleveland, then adapted by a traveling lecturer, and eventually find its way back to California in a slightly altered form.

The Emperor’s story became a game of telephone played across a continent. With each retelling, it became less about the specific man, Joshua Norton, and more about the abstract type: “The American Emperor.” He was a blank slate upon which different anxieties and amusements could be written.

Which brings us back to the political cartoon, the most potent visual tool of the age. By the 1890s, Emperor Norton’s image had become a standard piece of shorthand in the editorial artist’s kit. He was a safe symbol of imperial pretension precisely because his was so benign. To contrast him with Kaiser Wilhelm, as the Chicago cartoon did, was to make a point about American exceptionalism: we might have our ambitions and our pomp, but ours were laughable, not lethal. When cartoonists wanted to jab at the expansionist foreign policy of presidents like Benjamin Harrison or William McKinley, they sometimes depicted those policies as “Norton-esque” proclamations—grandiose but fundamentally silly. The legend proved politically ambidextrous.

In one notable cartoon from the heated 1896 election cycle, the fiery populist Democrat William Jennings Bryan was shown wearing Norton’s plumed hat, issuing “decrees” about free silver to a crowd of farmers. The message was clear: Bryan’s radical economic proposals were as delusional and self-proclaimed as an emperor’s edicts. Here, Norton’s symbol was wielded as a conservative weapon against populist change.

In another drawing from the same period, the bloated power of railroad monopolies was represented by a giant figure in an emperor’s robe, whose face was a fusion of a robber baron’s and Norton’s. Now the symbol critiqued entrenched capital. It was a neutral, absurdist lens through which to view any concentration of power that seemed unearned or presumptuous—whether that power came from a radical mob or a corporate boardroom.

This was a profound transformation. In life, Norton’s proclamations—about dissolving Congress or building a bridge—were gentle counter-programming to real power. In national cartooning, his image became a tool for engaging with that real power directly.

San Francisco had accepted his performance as an alternative to the hard currencies of politics and finance. The national press now used his performance as a metaphor to dissect those very currencies. The local kindness had been metabolized into national critique.

The final stage of this journey from local curiosity to national folk hero was physical. It happened on the stages of lyceums and vaudeville houses. Traveling lecturers, those purveyors of “chalk talks” and illustrated essays on everything from the Holy Land to the habits of microbes, began to include the tale of the Emperor of the United States in their repertoires. He was a perfect subject: visually distinctive, rich with anecdote, and terminally odd. A lecturer could hold up a lantern slide of his photograph, tell a few stories about his bonds and his dogs, and get easy, knowing laughs from Boston to Omaha. He was presented as a “quaint relic of the Wild West,” a charming paradox from the frontier that proved America could produce anything, even its own harmless monarchy. Vaudeville comedians did him one better.

They didn’t just talk about him; they impersonated him. A standard bit involved a performer coming on stage in a ragged coat and a fake plume-hat to deliver “Imperial Decrees” about the day’s news—the high price of beef, a newfangled bicycle craze, the latest scandal. It was gentle, topical humor that relied on the audience already knowing the reference. The fact that it worked from coast to coast was the ultimate proof of concept.

Emperor Norton had achieved that rare status: a nationally recognized inside joke. By the turn of the century, the process was complete. The story that had been so carefully curated and commodified in San Francisco in the 1880s had flown the coop. It existed independently, in the air of American culture. You could find it in a schoolboy’s joke almanac published in Philadelphia. You could see it referenced in a socialist pamphlet in Milwaukee critiquing capitalist royalty. You could hear it invoked from a stage in St. Louis.

This nationalization answered, in a way, the strongest counter-argument against Norton’s significance: that he was merely a tolerated eccentric, a harmless delusion indulged by a frontier city to burnish its own myth of tolerance, while the real mechanisms of power—capital, politics, law—operated unchanged and unchallenged by his pantomime.

Look at what happened after his death, the counter-argument would say. The railroads still monopolized, the politicians still grafted, the banks still called in their loans. Nothing changed.

But that misses the point entirely. The “real mechanisms of power” were not challenged by his pantomime in life, true. They were, however, persistently annotated by his legend in death. His story became a tool people used to think and talk about those very mechanisms. When a cartoonist used Norton’s image to mock a politician or a monopoly, he was engaging with power through the lexicon San Francisco had created. The city’s act of kindness had generated a unique form of social capital—a shared story of performative legitimacy—that proved transferable and durable.

It outlasted the man and became a piece of the national cultural toolkit. The pantomime didn’t stop the machine of Gilded Age America, but it gave people a recognizable, humorous frame through which to watch it grind away. It allowed them to name its absurdities without having to fully confront its violence.

There is an irony here that Joshua Norton, that most local of sovereigns, would have appreciated. His story achieved its broadest sovereignty only when it was completely divorced from his physical person and his city. The legend was now a disembodied citizen of the national imagination. It could live anywhere.

But that very disembodiment created a vulnerability. A symbol that lives everywhere lives nowhere in particular. Its roots in a specific place, with specific people making specific choices—the saloonkeeper honoring the scrip, the policeman giving the salute—were what gave it substance and emotional weight. Those roots were its tether to reality. As the 20th century began, the Emperor Norton story floated freely over the country, a popular ghost with no fixed address.

The mechanisms that propelled Norton’s story into national circulation were themselves products of the Gilded Age’s transforming media landscape. The rise of newspaper syndicates and wire services meant that a humorous item published in a San Francisco paper could be telegraphed to subscribing editors in New York, Chicago, or Atlanta within days. These editors, perpetually in need of filler material to pad their ever-expanding columns, found in the Emperor a reliably strange and adaptable figure. He was not merely copied; he was processed.

The syndicate system acted as a cultural distillery, stripping the story of its local nuances and concentrating it into a potent, portable essence of “quaintness” or “absurdity.” This industrial repackaging ensured his legend’s wide distribution but also accelerated its detachment from fact. The Emperor became less a historical person and more a standardized character in a national repertoire of American oddities, filed alongside tales of Paul Bunyan or Johnny Appleseed, yet distinct in his urban, faux-regal charm.

This national appropriation also reflected a deeper cultural anxiety about authority itself. In an era defined by the colossal power of industrial trusts, the bitter strife of labor wars, and the populist thunder of figures like Bryan, the figure of a benign, pretend emperor served as psychological relief. He represented authority stripped of its coercive power, sovereignty without consequence.

When cartoonists or writers contrasted Norton with the Kaiser or with domineering railroad magnates, they were engaging in a form of symbolic disarmament. By rendering real threats in the idiom of Norton’s harmless pageantry, they momentarily made those threats seem manageable, even laughable.

This was not an act of trivialization but one of cognitive framing—a way for a public grappling with overwhelming concentrations of power to process them through a familiar, less terrifying lens. Norton’s legend provided a safe vocabulary for critique in a period when direct criticism could carry substantial economic or social risk.

The traveling lecturers and vaudeville performers who enacted his story completed this cycle of abstraction. On stage, Norton was transformed into pure performance, a costume to be donned and a set of mannerisms to be mimicked. This physical dissemination cemented his folk-hero status but at a cost: it replaced lived memory with theatrical imitation. For audiences in Baltimore or Des Moines, “Emperor Norton” was no longer a man who had walked specific streets; he was a convention, a stock type like the Yankee rube or the city slicker. His San Francisco became not a real place with a complex social contract but a mythic “Bagdad-by-the-Bay,” a whimsical backdrop for his antics. This was the final stage of nationalization: the localization of a delocalized symbol.

Its survival depended on memory and retelling, not on lived experience. This set up a coming contradiction. What happens to a ghost when its haunted house is destroyed? What becomes of a national symbol whose entire reason for being, whose origin story of civic kindness, is tied to a physical place that could cease to exist?

The story faced no immediate threat in 1905. It seemed more robust than ever. But its strength was now of a different, more fragile kind. It was the strength of an echo, not of a source.

The test would not be one of relevance or popularity. The joke books and vaudeville stages proved he had both.

The test would be one of existential continuity. Could a story born from and sustained by the streets of one city survive if those streets themselves were obliterated? If the shop windows that displayed his bonds were shattered, if the libraries that held his biographies were ash, if the very pavement he walked was swallowed by fire and earthquake?

The Emperor Norton legend, now a confident, disembodied national symbol, floated into the new century. Ahead lay its first great trial not by opinion, but by fire and earth.
