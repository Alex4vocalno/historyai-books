Chapter 8

The Sovereign in the Street (1872-1875)

The policeman’s hand came up in a crisp salute, his eyes fixed on the familiar figure in the faded military coat. The gesture was automatic by now, a small ritual performed dozens of times a week along the beats of Montgomery Street or the Embarcadero.

But on this afternoon in 1872, the salute was not merely a courtesy. It was a shield.

Behind the Emperor, a knot of men had gathered. They were not regulars. Their clothes were too new, their laughter too loud and pointed. One of them, emboldened by whiskey or the anonymity of a crowded sidewalk, had reached out and plucked at the braid on Norton’s epaulette. “Hey there, King,” he’d slurred. “Where’s your crown?” The Emperor had stopped, turning with a solemnity that seemed to magnify the shabbiness of his uniform. He did not engage the taunt; he inspected it, as he might inspect a poorly maintained public drain. His silence was more unnerving than any retort. It drew more of the crowd, some amused, some uneasy. That was when Officer Patrick Riordan arrived.

He did not push through the gathering with his club raised. He simply stepped into the space between the Emperor and his hecklers, turned his back to the men, and brought his hand to his cap. The salute was performed with exacting formality.

In that single motion, Riordan communicated several things to the crowd: that this man was recognized by the city’s authority, that his dignity was under official protection, and that the joke, if it continued, would now be on them. He held the salute for a beat longer than usual, then turned, his expression neutral but his posture firm.

“Move along, gentlemen,” he said, not to the Emperor, but to the men. The message was clear. You may laugh privately, but you will not disrupt the public performance. The crowd dispersed, grumbling. The Emperor nodded gravely to Riordan, as one sovereign acknowledging the dutiful service of another, and continued his inspection walk.

This was sovereignty in the street. Not proclaimed from a balcony, but enforced in the friction of the sidewalk.

The grand, nation-focused edicts of the late 1860s—the dissolution of Congress, the command to build a bridge across the Bay—had receded like a tide. What remained was not less powerful, but more intimate.

From 1872 into 1875, Emperor Norton’s reign underwent a subtle but definitive transformation. His authority became less a matter of theatrical decree printed in the Pacific Appeal and more a function of daily, civic care—a network of silent agreements that clothed, fed, transported, and protected him. The city was no longer just playing along; it was actively sustaining. And in doing so, it was cementing him not as a mascot, but as a peculiar and beloved civic institution.

His power now resided in the salute of a policeman, the reserved seat at the theater, and the credit line at Martin’s Restaurant. To understand this shift, you have to look at the mechanics. An emperor, even a performative one, has a household.

His was not funded by a civil list voted by Parliament, but by what we might call the Kindness Economy—an informal, non-monetary system of value exchange based on dignity, care, and social inclusion. This economy expanded in these years, drawing in more participants.

Take his uniform. It was his essential regalia, the visual proof of his office. The blue military coat with its gilt epaulettes grew threadbare, faded by sun and rain. A tailor on Commercial Street took it in, patched it, and brushed it, never presenting a bill.

This was not charity in the pitying sense; it was a transaction within the Kindness Economy. The tailor paid in cloth and labor; he received in return the prestige of being the Imperial Tailor, a story for his customers, and the satisfaction of upholding a civic symbol they all recognized.

The same exchange operated at restaurants like Martin’s, where Norton took his meals. He would eat, sometimes with a chosen companion, and upon finishing, would solemnly issue a scrip note—an imperial bond—to cover the cost.

Ferry captains waved him aboard the boats to Oakland or Sausalito without a ticket. Streetcar conductors nodded him to a seat. Theaters—Maguire’s Opera House, the California Theater—kept a seat open for him, often in a box. If he appeared, the performance was blessed with imperial approval. If he did not, the empty seat was a reminder of his potential presence, a ghost of sanction. These managers weren’t just humoring a crank; they were buying into the theater of it, and the theater was good for business. And then there were his decrees. After naming the Pacific Appeal, a Black-owned newspaper, as his official “imperial organ” in December 1870, Norton had a reliable mouthpiece. Through May 1875, the paper published some 250 of his proclamations. But read them in sequence and the shift is clear. The dizzying national ambitions fade.

In their place comes the minutiae of municipal governance. He inspects. He audits. He comments. He walked the city with the diligence of a foreman, his cane or umbrella serving as a pointing stick. He examined the condition of sidewalks and cable cars, the state of repair of public property, and the appearance of police officers.

The failure to treat his adopted home city with appropriate respect was the subject of particularly stern edicts. His most famous—and likely apocryphal—edict from this period perfectly captures this hyper-local focus: a decree that anyone using the abominable nickname “Frisco” would be fined a sum of twenty-five dollars. It was a defense of his city’s honor, a matter of civic branding. The nation’s Congress might be beyond his reach, but a city’s slang was not.

This was his statecraft now: a daily street-level audit. And for it to work, the audited had to accept the auditor. Most did. For San Franciscans who had lived with him for over a decade, Norton was a fixture, as natural as the fog.

His inspections were a form of benign attention. To have the Emperor notice your well-swept doorstep or your properly functioning streetlamp was a small point of pride. He was a mirror that reflected back to the city a version of itself as orderly, worthy, and under gentle supervision.

But here we must pause to answer the skeptic—the voice that says this was all just tolerance for a harmless delusion, a civic joke that left the real mechanisms of power untouched. It’s a reasonable charge. The railroads were still built by Collis Huntington’s money, city hall was still run by politicians, and the law was still enforced by men like Officer Riordan. Norton’ proclamations did not change tariffs or arrest murderers. So what was the point?

The point was in the doing. The Kindness Economy was a real mechanism, operating in parallel. It created a different set of rules and rewards. When a tailor mended a coat for free, he opted out of a cash transaction for a social one.

When a policeman saluted, he momentarily subordinated official authority to a performative sovereignty. These were conscious choices that diverted resources and recognition into a separate stream. This economy didn’t replace the cash economy or political power; it existed in its gaps, and by filling them, it changed the atmosphere in which power operated. It made the city’s public life softer, more whimsical, and more cooperative. To dismiss it as mere indulgence is to miss how deeply its rituals were woven into daily commerce and civic identity. It wasn’t that power was unchanged; it was that San Francisco had collectively decided to fund and protect a small, alternative court where the currency was kindness.

The problem, of course, is that kindness requires consensus. The Kindness Economy had a fundamental vulnerability: it required universal, or near-universal, buy-in. Its currency was belief, and belief can be broken by a single skeptic who refuses to play along. The city was growing, filling with newcomers every month off the transcontinental railroad or clipper ships. Not all of them had read the script.

The threat was always there, simmering beneath the ritual salutes and free meals. It was the threat of the literal mind, the eyes that saw only a dirty old man in a funny coat talking to himself.

The encounter Officer Riordan managed was a common type of skirmish. For every protective policeman, there might be a drunk from the mining counties who saw an easy target for mockery. For every restaurateur who honored the scrip, there might be a new shopkeeper who saw only worthless paper and shooed him away.

The police intervention is so revealing because it shows the city’s institutions consciously choosing to uphold the fiction. The force had history with Norton; he had once granted an Imperial Pardon to an officer named Barbier. The reciprocal salute became custom.

But in that moment with Riordan, the custom became operational policy. The officer did not arrest the hecklers for disturbing the peace—they hadn’t quite crossed that line. Instead, he used a symbol—the salute—to redefine the situation.

He drew a line between the Emperor and the crowd, marking one as sanctioned and the other as disruptive. He enforced the social contract physically. Why would a police force, tasked with real crime in a booming city, bother?

Because Norton’s sustained performance was now seen as a public good. In a city growing increasingly fractious, his was a unifying pageant. The mid-1870s saw sporadic violence in the poorer districts, often directed at Chinese immigrants. Demonstrations flared into riots; there were fatalities. While the large-scale sandlot rallies led by Denis Kearney were still a few years off, the social tinder was dry. Resentment, unemployment, and racial hatred were real currents in the city’s life.

Against this backdrop of potential chaos, Emperor Norton’s orderly patrols were a relief. His sovereignty was absurd but peaceful. His decrees were whimsical but never called for violence. His entire persona rejected the raw, transactional strife of politics and class warfare. Protecting him was, in a subtle way, protecting a version of San Francisco that preferred wit to wrath, theater to terror.

He was a walking antidote to the city’s own darker impulses. So the city made its choice. It would fund the Emperor through its Kindness Economy. It would feed him at Martin’s, clothe him at the tailor’s, seat him at the theater, ferry him across the Bay. In return, he would provide his service: he would be the symbol.

He would walk the streets as a living reminder that authority could be gentle, that rules could be ridiculous yet respected, and that a community could agree on a shared story simply because it made life more interesting and more civil. By 1875, this symbiosis was deep and resilient. The U.S.

Census of 1870 had already captured the official paradox. It listed a 50-year-old Joshua Norton at 624 Commercial Street. His occupation: “Emperor.” And in a bureaucratic footnote, the census taker had added the clinical diagnosis: “insane.” The formal record acknowledged both his claimed title and society’s verdict on his mind. San Francisco, in its daily actions, chose to engage only with the former. The diagnosis was paperwork; the title was lived reality.

The mechanics of this system extended beyond merchants and restaurateurs to include the city’s transport workers, who integrated the Emperor into the very rhythm of public transit. Ferry hands on the Oakland run knew to steady the gangplank a moment longer for his deliberate, stately boarding, while cable car conductors developed a practiced pause at certain corners, a silent inquiry answered by his grave nod if he wished to ascend. These were not grand gestures but small, operational accommodations, the unspoken protocols of a shared civic routine. They transformed Norton from a pedestrian curiosity into a passenger with standing, his passage facilitated by a workforce that saw his presence as a part of their daily duty, a living landmark on their route.

This protective consensus found its most formal expression in the police department, where the casual salute evolved into an understood policy. Officers like Riordan were not merely indulging a whim; they were upholding a civic asset. The force’s institutional memory held the Emperor’s earlier proclamations of pardon and appreciation, creating a legacy of reciprocal respect. By the mid-1870s, intervening to deflect mockery was less about humoring an eccentric and more about maintaining a public order that now included his dignified passage as one of its visible signs. The police, in their blue uniforms, became the ceremonial guard for an army of one, their authority lending concrete support to the fiction of his sovereignty whenever that fiction was threatened by disbelief or malice.

Yet this very protection highlighted his underlying vulnerability. The Kindness Economy was a social edifice, magnificent in its way, but built on the shifting sands of collective sentiment and economic stability. For every tailor who repaired his coat, there was a calculation—tacit or conscious—about the value of the story against the cost of the thread. The free seat at the theater was a marketing expense, the complimentary ferry passage a negligible loss leader. These transactions flourished in the climate of a booming post-Gold Rush city, where a little whimsy could be afforded and where Norton’s long tenure had educated a generation in their roles.

He was securely enmeshed. The web of care held him aloft. The Kindness Economy had more participants than ever—theaters, tailors, ferry companies, newspapers all part of a silent consortium that kept the performance running.

But webs are not walls; they are flexible, sensitive to tremors. This economy that clothed and fed him was robust, but it was not immune to larger shocks. It depended on the prosperity and goodwill of its participants—the tailors, restaurateurs, theater managers. Their ability to extend credit was a function of their own financial health. And as Norton made his rounds in 1875, inspecting sidewalks with imperial concern, a different kind of pressure was building far to the east. In banks and railroads and wheat fields, threads were beginning to fray. The web that held the Emperor of the Street was strong. It was woven from local affection and civic pride. But it was about to be tested by a force that respected no proclamations, no salutes, and no scrip: the simple, brutal fact of a nation running out of money.

The Kindness Economy could thrive in good times, when a free meal or a mended coat was an easy gesture. The question waiting in the wings was what happened when times were no longer good, when every dollar counted, and kindness became a luxury fewer could afford.
