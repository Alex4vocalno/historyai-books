Chapter 27

The Monarch in the Mirror of Modernity (1915-1934)

The smell was different before the fire. It wasn’t just the coal smoke and horse manure of any growing city, or the salt-tang off the bay. It was the particular, close odor of a man who had not bathed in a week, layered over with the mothball scent of a military greatcoat worn in all seasons. It was the faint, sour trace of cheap boarding-house meals on a breath that spoke decrees to anyone who would listen.

To walk past Joshua Norton on Montgomery Street in the 1880s was to receive a full, unvarnished sensory report on the reality of his sovereignty. His uniform was fraying at the cuffs. The beaver hat was stained. The eyes could be piercing, but the hands were often dirty. The currency he issued was accepted in certain saloons, but you would not have wanted one of those hand-scrawled notes in your pocketbook for long; they smelled of him, too.

This was the emperor in his kingdom: a living, breathing, sometimes pungent fact, complicating the clean air of Gilded Age ambition with the stubborn, indigestible reality of human poverty maintained by collective will. He was a transaction, not a symbol. Every salute from a policeman, every free meal from a restaurateur, every honored scrap of scrip was a specific, local negotiation between a man’s need and a city’s willingness to pretend. It was kindness, yes, but it was kindness with sweat on its brow and grime under its nails.

Now, open a different book. The year is 1923. The place is a clean, well-lit study, probably smelling of good paper and pipe tobacco. The man is Robert Ernest Cowan, a respected librarian and bibliographer for the prestigious firm of Taylor, Nash & Taylor. Before him are not soiled scraps, but carefully arranged documents. His work is not sustenance but curation. He publishes a piece titled “The Proclamations of a Monarch.” In it, the decrees are transcribed neatly, the dates verified, the odd spellings preserved with scholarly care.

The man who issued them is framed no longer as a civic pet or a psychiatric case study, but as something new: a “patron saint” of San Francisco. The odor of authenticity—the complicated, difficult smell of real life—has been carefully washed away. What remains is a portrait, sanitized and framed for hanging in the hall of civic memory.

The question posed by the mere existence of Cowan’s 1923 work is not what Emperor Norton was, but why, two decades after his death and nearly two decades after the city that knew him had been reduced to ash, someone felt the need to repackage him in this way. The myth, tested in total ruin, was now unbreakable. And it stood ready, simplified and potent, for those who would next seek to define not just a city’s past, but its place in the modern national imagination.

Between 1915 and 1934, that is precisely what happened. This was not an organic flowering of folk memory. It was a campaign.

The transformation of Emperor Norton from a fading local memory into a national cultural archetype was a deliberate, piece-by-piece reconstruction, driven by writers, journalists, and civic boosters who stumbled upon a startling discovery: in an America becoming uniformly corporate, anxiously modern, and grimly efficient, the story of the bankrupt emperor was a potent antidote. They found they could use him. The kindness once shown to a man was now being repackaged as a civic brand and a national parable. The process operated like an assembly line in reverse, taking a complex, hand-hewn artifact and smoothing it into a mass-produced totem.

The engine of this transformation was an evidence chain, each link forging a new, more polished version of the legend.

It began in the columns of newspapers that had survived the fire or sprung up in the new city. In the immediate aftermath of 1906, nostalgia was a raw, desperate need. Remembering Norton was part of remembering home, a tactile connection to a physical world that was gone. But as the decade turned, the tone shifted.

The city was rebuilding at a furious pace. The new San Francisco rising from the ashes was one of broader streets, steel-reinforced concrete, and ambitious civic projects like the Hetch Hetchy water system, overseen by city engineer Michael O’Shaughnessy (1864–1934). It was modern, powerful, and increasingly impersonal.

In this environment, the reminiscences became less about mourning a lost individual and more about promoting a surviving ideal. Feature writers, needing copy that was colorful yet uncontroversial, seized on him. He was safe. He was funny. He belonged uniquely to San Francisco and offended no important interest.

A 1915 piece might recall his antics with a chuckle; by 1918, another was hinting at a deeper meaning—a symbol of the city’s unique tolerance. The newspapers were creating a demand for a usable past, a past that could distinguish bustling new San Francisco from any other booming American metropolis. Norton’s story was local color that could be bottled.

This soft-focused journalism created a demand for the “real” story, which in turn created an opportunity for men like Robert Ernest Cowan.

Cowan was not a poet or a fabulist; he was a collector and an archivist. His authority came from his meticulousness.

By gathering and publishing the proclamations in 1923, he did something crucial: he moved Norton from the realm of oral anecdote into the realm of documented fact. He provided the canon.

But in his framing—that “patron saint” label—he also performed the alchemy. He took documents that were, in their original context, often petitions for basic respect or bizarre political demands, and sanctified them. He presented not a needy man, but a whimsical prophet. The gritty reality documented in his collection was subtly subverted by the graceful, appreciative context in which he placed it. The scholar’s rigor gave the booster’s interpretation an unassailable foundation.

Cowan’s work signaled that Norton was now a subject worthy of formal study, not just fond gossip.

Cowan’s work acted as a catalyst. It gave other writers permission to escalate the claim. If a serious bibliographer called Norton a patron saint, then a popular historian could call him a philosopher-king.

Journalists on the East Coast, always hungry for a quirky “California story,” now had a reputable source to cite. Norton began to appear in syndicated columns not as a mere oddity, but as an exemplar of something America was supposedly losing: individuality, humor, communal grace. He was held up in contrast to the assembly line, the time clock, the sprawling, anonymous metropolis.

The 1920s were a decade of profound anxiety wrapped in glittering prosperity. Americans were moving to cities in record numbers, working for vast corporations like Ford or U.S. Steel, and consuming identical national products. The era bred a nostalgia for a supposedly simpler, more personal past. The refashioned Norton fit this national mood perfectly.

In this narrative, San Francisco’s act of playing along was no longer just a local idiosyncrasy; it was a lost national virtue, a lesson in how to be human in the machine age. Writers used him to gently critique modernity itself.

Here was a man who created his own value outside the banking system, who commanded loyalty without a police force, who ruled without taxes or elections. In an age of standardization, he was gloriously non-standard.

This version of the story carefully omitted the fact that his alternative economy relied entirely on the surplus of the very capitalist system he seemed to transcend. That omission was the point.

The boosters—those men charged with selling the new San Francisco to investors and tourists—found this refashioned Norton invaluable. Their city needed an identity that was not solely based on commerce, earthquake resilience, or engineering marvels. They needed soul. Norton, the archetype now being polished by writers, provided it ready-made.

He became the star of what we might now call “content marketing.” Speeches to chambers of commerce would include a nod to the emperor as proof of the city’s creative spirit and humane heart. Guidebooks began to feature his story as essential local color.

The message was clear: here was a city so confident, so creatively wealthy, that it could afford to support its own imaginary monarch. It reframed charity as patronage, and collective humoring as sophisticated performance art. The booster’s logic was potent: other cities had skyscrapers and banks; only San Francisco had an emperor.

This process systematically airbrushed out the complications. The Norton of this new legend was never truly hungry. His poverty was a charming aesthetic choice, not an economic fact. His mental state was “eccentricity,” never illness. The civic transaction—the salutes, the honored scrip, the free meals—was recast as a purely joyful festival of consent, stripped of its underlying mechanics of pity and obligation. The collective decision to sustain him was now portrayed as a purely aesthetic choice, a civic whim.

This cleansing made the story palatable for national consumption. A story about a smelly, possibly mad pauper kept alive by charity was sad and potentially disturbing. A story about a whimsical character embraced by a sophisticated city was delightful.

The most telling success of this campaign was how it answered, without ever directly addressing, the strongest counter-argument against taking Norton seriously: that he was merely a tolerated eccentric, a harmless delusion indulged by a frontier city to burnish its own myth of tolerance, while the real mechanisms of power—capital, politics, law—operated unchanged and unchallenged by his pantomime. The refashioned legend embraced that critique and spun it into gold. Yes, the boosters and writers implicitly agreed, the real mechanisms were unchanged. That was precisely the point! The genius of San Francisco was that it allowed this beautiful, useless pantomime to exist alongside the serious business of power.

It proved the city had a soul separate from its ledger books. Norton wasn’t challenging capitalism; he was providing a vacation from it. He wasn’t subverting politics; he was offering a satire that made politics seem less grim. His power was not an alternative to real power, but a decorative complement to it. This made the story safe for consumption in boardrooms and literary clubs.

It transformed what could be seen as a troubling collective engagement with madness into a heartwarming tale of civic whimsy. The counter-argument was neutralized by absorption. The legend conceded everything the skeptic claimed and called it a virtue. The chain of evidence—from nostalgic columnist to meticulous collector to syndicated journalist to civic booster—closed the loop.

By the late 1920s, the product was complete. Emperor Norton was no longer a person remembered by some aging San Franciscans. He was a standardized character, a set of talking points. He symbolized tolerance, whimsy, and individuality. He was proof of San Francisco’s special character. He was an American folk hero whose moral was about kindness over efficiency.

You could measure the success of this refashioning campaign in the sheer volume and reach of its output. Dozens, then hundreds, of articles across the country carried the tale. Mentions appeared in popular national magazines like The American Mercury and The Saturday Evening Post. He was included in anthologies of American humor and oddities.

He became a reference point that required no explanation for a certain class of reader—a shorthand for San Franciscan quirkiness. More concretely, you could see it in the uniform way his story was now told. The anecdotes were the same—the currency, the police salutes, the funeral—but the framing was always the same, too: smiling, wistful, elevating. The complicated man had been successfully replaced by the useful archetype. The campaign had not just revived memory; it had manufactured a new one.

The final, perhaps inevitable, pressure point arrived as this polished legend fully escaped the custody of the city that created it. It became a cultural commodity, available for anyone’s use. By the early 1930s, with the Great Depression deepening a sense of national crisis, Norton’s story was being used to sell more than just San Francisco; it was being used to sell nostalgia for a pre-modern America, for community, for simplicity. He was ripe for appropriation by anyone feeling alienated by the speed, scale, and now collapse of modern life.

The legend, now national property, began to generate its own consequences. It created an expectation. If San Francisco was the city of Emperor Norton, then it had to keep performing that role, living up to its own marketed mythology of tolerance and eccentricity. The curated philosophy had become a cage of identity, a set of standards the real city would now be judged against.

And so, by 1934, the emperor stood immortal in a mirror not of his own making. His reflection showed not the needy man in the soiled coat, but whatever the viewer needed to see: a saint for boosters, a parable for journalists, a solace for the modern anxious soul.

He had been launched into the American imagination as a finished product, a myth sealed for delivery. The next handlers of his legacy would not need to dig through ashes or interview aging witnesses. They would only need to unpack the crate. The contents were already labeled: one durable American myth, slightly used, ideal for periods of anxiety.

The price of this success was that the real Joshua Norton, the man who lived and breathed and needed, was now buried under twenty years of deliberate interpretation, inaccessible beneath the clean, weightless monument he had become.
