Chapter 4

Griggs's Last Round

The door to the lodging house opened on a smell of damp wool and boiled cabbage, and Ada Quill stepped into a hallway so narrow her shoulders nearly brushed both walls. Varney’s warning still sat behind her teeth—don’t make trouble over a misplaced decimal—but her own ledger was in her satchel, and the ledger did not trade in decimals. It traded in facts.

Three lamps on Commercial Road, logged as lit on a Tuesday by a man whose Tuesday sheet placed him on Cable Street. She also carried the Tuesday sheet itself, folded flat against the ledger’s spine, and the scrap of paper on which she had copied the Cable Street entry two days before—the three items distinct in her mind as three columns that must reconcile. Either the record was wrong or the man was lying, and she had spent four years ensuring the record was never wrong.

The stairs groaned under her boots. A gas bracket on the first landing flickered with an orange tongue that threw shadows across peeling wallpaper. She climbed to the second floor and found the door she wanted—number six, paint flaking at the edges, a strip of light at the threshold. She knocked twice, precisely, and waited.

Shuffling inside. A chair leg scraped against boards. Then a low, compressed voice from behind the door: “Who’s there?”

“Miss Quill, Mr. Griggs. From the Gas Light and Coke Company. I need to speak with you about your Tuesday round.”

A long pause. Footsteps crossed the room. The scrape of a bolt. The door opened three inches, and Griggs’s face appeared in the gap—a ruddy, veined face gone pale under the eyes, the heavy jowls drawn tight. He looked past her into the hallway before he looked at her.

“I already told the police everything. They said it’s done.”

“I am not the police, Mr. Griggs. I am the clerk who transcribed your sheet, and I need five minutes.”

He stared at her. His thick fingers, grimed at the knuckles, gripped the door’s edge. Then he stepped back and let her in.

The room was small enough that a man could touch both walls if he stretched his arms. A single candle burned on a table beside an unmade cot, its flame swaying in the draught from the door. Rain ticked against the window, which looked out onto a blank brick wall three feet away. A leather apron hung from a nail, and the small scissors and tallow tin sat on the table beside a chipped cup half-full of cold tea. The air was thick with damp and the residual tallow smell that clung to every lamplighter.

“Sit, then,” Griggs said, and lowered himself onto the cot rather than take the single chair. He folded his hands between his knees. His forearms were still thick, but the fingers would not stay still.

Ada placed her own ledger on the table and opened it to the relevant page. Beside it she laid the Tuesday sheet—Griggs’s own marks, rough and blunt—and the scrap of paper bearing the Cable Street entry she had copied two days before. Three documents, three hands. She set her pen beside them, uncapped. “You were assigned the Whitechapel southern section that night. Cable Street to Penfold Street. Is that correct?”

“You know it is.”

“I do. But the ledger shows three lamps on Commercial Road logged as lit under your initials. Commercial Road is not within your section.”

Griggs looked at the page. His jaw worked beneath the skin, the muscles bunching and releasing. “I didn’t write that. I didn’t light those lamps. I told the police the same thing.”

“I believe you, Mr. Griggs. But I need to understand how the entry came to exist. If you did not walk Commercial Road that night, did you see it at all? Even in passing?”

“I don’t go near Commercial Road. My round takes me south.”

“Then who logged the entry?”

“How should I know? I’m a lamplighter, not a clerk. You’re the one with the books.” His voice rose, then compressed again, as if he had caught himself. He glanced at the window. The rain streaked the grimy glass, and beyond it the brick wall was featureless and dark.

Ada studied the entry. The handwriting was not hers, but it was clean, practiced, consistent with house style. Someone who knew the company’s forms had written it. She looked at Griggs’s hands, still working between his knees, the left one missing the tip of its little finger.

“Mr. Griggs, I am not here to accuse you of anything. I am establishing the sequence of entries. Every fact must be accounted for. You were on Cable Street. The entry says Commercial Road. Something does not fit, and I need your help to find where the gap is.”

“I’ve told you what I can.”

“You’ve told me where you weren’t. I am asking what you saw.”

He breathed heavily through his nose. The candle guttered, throwing long shadows up the wall behind him, and for a moment his face was unreadable. Then he leaned forward, and his hands came apart.

“I saw them.”

Ada’s pen stopped above the paper. “You saw the lamps on Commercial Road lit?”

“I finished my round on Cable Street and was walking back along Penfold to the depot. You can see Commercial Road from the corner of Penfold, if you look north. Three lamps. Burning steady, not flickering—properly lit, with the gas on full.”

“At what time?”

“Half-eleven, near as I reckon. The church clock had just gone.”

Ada wrote the time in the margin of the Tuesday sheet, beside the existing entry. The ink dried quickly on the rough paper. “You saw three lamps lit on Commercial Road at half-eleven, but you did not light them yourself.”

“I did not. I was on Cable Street all evening. When I saw those lamps, I’d finished my round and was walking in.”

“Did you report it?”

Griggs’s hands went still. The change was sudden—he had been fidgeting, working his fingers, shifting on the cot, and now he was rigid, his shoulders drawn up and his chin lowered. He looked at the window again.

He told her then, haltingly, that he had reported it to his foreman first thing the next morning—three lamps burning on Commercial Road and no one from the yard had lit them. The foreman had told him to put it in writing, and he had.

“And what came of the report?”

“Nothing. For a week, nothing. Then a gentleman from the office came to see me.”

Ada set her pen down. “Which office?”

“He didn’t say. Coat and hat, clean boots. Had a way of talking that was very precise—he asked the same questions you’re asking now, only slower.”

“What did you tell him?”

“What I’ve told you. I saw the lamps. I didn’t light them. I reported it.” Griggs’s voice had dropped to nearly a whisper. “He wrote it all down in a little book, very neat. Then he said I should forget about it. Said the entries were a clerical matter and would be corrected.”

“And have they been corrected?”

“You’re looking at the ledger. You tell me.”

Ada looked at the page. The entry was still there, in the same clean hand, unaltered. Whatever the gentleman from the office had intended, the record remained exactly as it had been. She thought of the handwriting—practiced, consistent, someone who knew the forms—and of the roster, which placed Griggs on Cable Street, and of the three lamps burning on Commercial Road at half-eleven, lit by no one from the yard.

“Mr. Griggs, since that visit, have you noticed anything unusual?”

He didn’t answer immediately. His eyes moved to the window again, and his right hand came up to the side of his face, rubbing the jaw as if trying to push something back. The candle flame bent in a draught, and the shadows on the wall shifted.

“I walk my round same as always. Cable Street, Penfold, back to the depot. But there’s been a man. Twice now, standing at the corner of Penfold when I come through. Just standing there, not moving, not talking to anyone. I pass him and he watches me go. Then he’s gone.”

“Could you describe him?”

“Average. Dark coat. I didn’t see his face proper, not either time.” He swallowed. “I’m not a man given to fancies, Miss Quill. But I know when I’m being watched.”

Ada wrote nothing down. She looked at Griggs—at the way he sat hunched on the cot, his heavy frame drawn in on itself, his eyes still fixed on the window. The candle was burning low, the wax pooling around the tin base, and the room had grown dimmer. She could smell the tallow on his hands and the damp in the walls, and underneath both, something stale and human that came from living in a room this size for too long.

“You said the gentleman from the office told you to forget about it.”

“He did.”

“Did he give a reason?”

“Same as the police. Clerical error. Said someone entered the wrong route number and the lamps were probably lit by a man from another yard who didn’t log it properly.”

Ada considered this. It was reasonable. A man from another yard could have lit the Commercial Road lamps and failed to record them, while someone at the depot, seeing them lit, entered the work under Griggs’s initials by mistake. The explanation covered the facts. But it did not cover the gentleman from the office arriving a week later to ask slow, precise questions and then leaving the entry uncorrected. It did not cover the man standing at the corner of Penfold Street in the dark.

“Mr. Griggs, when you saw the lamps on Commercial Road, was there anything about them that struck you as unusual? The brightness, the color, the position?”

“They were lit proper. Nothing strange about the lamps themselves. Only that they were lit at all, when no one from our yard had the call to light them.”

“And you’re certain no one from another yard would have been on Commercial Road that night?”

“I don’t know what other yards do. I know what our yard does. Commercial Road isn’t our section. It’s never been our section.”

Ada paused. She had been about to close the ledger, but the entry was still open on the table—the clean handwriting, her own ledger, Griggs’s initials beside lamps he swore he never lit. She had walked here with three documents and a question. She had the answer now, but not the one she had expected. She had not yet shown Griggs the entry itself, had not put the proof before him and watched his face as he read it.

“Mr. Griggs, before I go, I want you to look at something.” She turned her own ledger to face him, tapping the entry with her pen. “Read that. Your initials. Your route number. Commercial Road.”

Griggs leaned forward. His eyes moved across the line. Then his jaw tightened, and his hands began to tremble against his knees—not anger, not cold, but the fine vibration of a man watching something he cannot explain.

“I didn’t write that,” he said.

“You did not light those lamps.”

“I did not. But they were lit. I saw them burning when I came round. I thought—“He stopped. His gaze went to the lodging-house window, then back. He told her, his voice barely above breath, that he had reported it to the foreman, and the foreman had brought him in, and there had been a gentleman there—not the foreman, not anyone from the yard. The man had called himself “from the office,” but he had known things—Griggs’s route, his times, which lamps he had checked twice. He had asked questions Griggs could not answer. Then he had told Griggs to forget what he had seen.

“I haven’t forgotten,” Griggs said. “And I think he knows that.”

Ada closed the ledger slowly. The trembling in his hands had not stopped. He was not lying about the lamps. He was lying when he said he would be fine.

She had spent four years trusting that every discrepancy had a clerical explanation—a mislaid sheet, a wrong initial, a man who forgot his round. But this was not a discrepancy. It was a door someone had propped open and then sealed shut behind them, and Griggs had walked through it without knowing, and now the man who propped it was watching to see what he would do next.

She stood, tucked her own ledger under her arm, and looked once more at his room’s window. The glass was dark, streaked with rain. Beyond it the blank brick wall offered nothing. But the window itself—the way his eyes kept returning to it—told her enough.

“Thank you, Mr. Griggs. I may need to speak with you again.”

“I’d rather you didn’t.”

“I understand. But if the entry is to be corrected, I need to establish the facts properly, and you are the only witness I have.”

He looked at her then, fully, for the first time since she had entered the room. His eyes were bloodshot, and the lines around them were deep. “Witness,” he said. “That’s a word for the courts.”

“It is a word for the record, Mr. Griggs. Nothing more.”

She picked up the ledger and moved toward the door. As she reached for the handle, Griggs spoke again, quietly, from the cot.

“There are eyes on my round, Miss Quill. I’ve been lighting lamps in this city for eleven years, and I’ve never been afraid of the dark. But I don’t go looking over my shoulder for sport. Something’s wrong, and I think you know it as well as I do.”

Ada paused with her hand on the door. She did not turn around. “I know that the ledger contains an entry that cannot be accounted for by the roster or by your testimony. That is what I know. Everything else remains to be established.”

She opened the door and stepped into the hallway. Behind her, she heard Griggs rise from the cot, heard his heavy tread cross the floor, and then the door closed and the bolt scraped home.

The stairwell was darker than before. The gas bracket on the landing had been turned down, and she descended by feel, one hand on the wall, the satchel pressed against her hip. The damp-wool smell was stronger on the ground floor, and the cabbage had gone cold. She pushed through the front door and out onto the street.

The rain had stopped. A thin mist hung between the buildings, and the cobblestones gleamed under the light of a single gas lamp at the corner. Somewhere to the south, a horse clopped steadily away, and the sound faded until the street was quiet. Ada stood on the step and breathed in coal smoke and river air.

She walked. The satchel was heavy against her side, and the ledger’s weight inside it had taken on a different quality. It was no longer a book of entries to be transcribed and filed. It was a record of something that had happened—three lamps lit by an unknown hand, witnessed by a man who was now afraid to walk his own route, questioned by a gentleman from an office that had left the error standing in the books. The entry was not a mistake. It was a fact someone had placed there deliberately, in a hand trained to match the company’s forms, and no one had corrected it.

She turned onto the main road. A hansom cab passed, its wheels hissing on the wet stones, and she stepped aside to let it go. The driver did not glance down. She walked on, past shuttered shopfronts and the dark mouth of an alley where a cat moved between bins. The mist was thickening, and the gas lamps along the pavement burned with halos of pale gold.

Griggs had seen the lamps. That was the fact she had come for, and she had it. But the fact sat inside a larger shape she could not yet see—the gentleman from the office, the man at the corner of Penfold Street, the entry left uncorrected. She did not know what connected them. She did not know who had written the entry, or why, or what profit there could be in three lit lamps on Commercial Road. But she knew, with the cold certainty of a woman who had spent four years verifying records, that an uncorrected entry was not an oversight. It was a decision.

The Gas Light and Coke Company clerking office was dark when she reached it. She let herself in with her key and stood in the doorway, the Thames visible through the frosted window as a faint grey line beneath the black sky. The desk was as she had left it, the filing cabinets closed, the gas bracket light extinguished. She set the satchel on the desk and took out her own ledger, the Tuesday sheet, and the scrap of paper with the copied Cable Street entry, and laid them side by side.

She opened the ledger to the entry. The clean handwriting stared up at her, each letter formed with care. She compared it to the roster—Griggs’s assignment, in a different hand, to Cable Street. She compared it to the Tuesday sheet—Griggs’s own marks, rough and blunt, recording his times. Three documents, three hands, and only one of them told a story that could not be true.

Ada picked up her pen. She did not correct the entry. Instead, she wrote at the bottom of the page, in her own precise hand: Entry verified against roster and lamplighter’s testimony. Lamps witnessed lit at half-eleven from Penfold Street corner. Lamplighter denies lighting. Entry uncorrected as of this date.

She closed the ledger, capped the pen, and placed everything back in the satchel. The office was silent. Through the frosted window, the Thames moved in the dark, and somewhere across the water a factory whistle blew—a long, flat note that died in the mist.

Ada stood in the empty office. Griggs’s face was still with her—the pallor, the hands working between his knees, the eyes turning again and again to the window. There are eyes on my round. He had not said that lightly. Eleven years on the same route, and the man was afraid.

She left the office and locked the door behind her. The street was empty, the gas bracket above the company entrance burning low. She stepped out onto the pavement, where the mist pressed close and the lamps along the road threw their halos into nothing. She walked home with the satchel pressed tight against her side, and the ledger inside it pressed back—a secret that someone had gone to trouble to plant, and that a man was now terrified to have noticed.

Griggs had seen the lamps. He had reported them. A gentleman from an office had come to ask questions and then told him to forget. A man had stood in the dark on Penfold Street, watching. And the entry remained in the books, clean and deliberate and uncorrected. Ada walked through the mist and the halos of pale gold, and for the first time in four years of ledgers and rosters and times and initials, she felt the weight of a fact she could not account for pressing against her ribs like a stone.
