Chapter 17

Confronting Finch

The fog pressed its wet palm against the window of Ada’s lodging. Rook’s question still sat between them, unanswered. She had not answered because the answer was obvious: the lamplighters’ yard had been quiet when they returned from Beckton, quiet the way a street goes quiet when someone is listening from behind a shutter. She had found wet footprints on the corridor tiles outside her room—fresh, unexplained, too large for any of the other lodgers.

She closed the door, locked it, and turned the key twice. Rook stood by the window, not looking out, keeping his body angled away from the glass. Grease from the valve gallery still blackened the creases of his knuckles. He wiped them on a rag without seeming to notice he was doing it.

“We cannot go back to Beckton,” he said.

“No.”

“Someone saw us. Or someone saw the valve.”

“Someone saw us,” Ada said.

She set her satchel on the table and opened it. The notebook with copied meter readings lay on top, and beneath it the Tuesday sheet, and beneath that her own ledger with its careful columns of dates and figures. She had organized the materials on the train back from Beckton, arranging them in the order she would need. The order mattered. Facts presented out of sequence lost their cumulative force. She had learned that from her father’s disgrace—the hearing had jumped from one accusation to the next without establishing a chain, and the panel had been confused, and confusion had served the other side.

Rook folded the rag and put it in his coat pocket. “What will you do?”

“I am going to speak to Finch.”

“Tonight?”

“Tomorrow morning. At his office, before the regular clerks arrive. At half past seven he is alone. He arrives early because he does not trust the night porter to light the stove.”

Rook studied her for a moment. “You are certain he is involved.”

“His nervousness began the moment I first asked about the October fourteenth entry. A clerk who has made an honest error does not perspire when the error is pointed out. He corrects it. He was not correcting. He was calculating how much I already knew.”

“And now you know about the bypass.”

“Now I know about the bypass.” She paused. “And so does whoever was watching us tonight.”

Rook pulled his coat tighter. The gas lamp at the end of the corridor cast a thin line of light under her door, and it flickered as someone passed through the hall. Neither of them moved. The footsteps continued past and faded down the stairwell.

“Be careful,” Rook said.

“I will be methodical.”

He left without another word. She listened to his boots on the stairs and then to the street door closing below. The lock on her door stayed turned.

She spent the next two hours arranging her evidence. The notebook with the copied meter readings from Beckton lay open to the page showing the Whitechapel district main. The figures were in her own hand, transcribed from the meter room’s brass dials. Beside them she placed the lamplighters’ ledger entries for the corresponding dates. The two sets of numbers did not agree. The ledger reported more gas consumed than the meter recorded as delivered. Gas had left the works, passed through the district main, and arrived at lamps that were not lit—or rather, at lamps logged as lit while the actual gas went elsewhere, through a pipe that branched off the main before the meter could register it.

The bypass. She and Rook had found it in the valve gallery: a secondary pipe joined to the Whitechapel district main with crude fittings and a hand-operated valve. The valve had been open. Gas had been flowing through it, into a conduit that ran beneath the retort house floor and out toward the river wall, where it presumably connected to some private line. They had not been able to follow it further. The sound of footsteps on the gallery grating had driven them back.

She closed the notebook and placed it in the satchel alongside her own ledger and the Tuesday sheet. Griggs’s name and route assignment were recorded in the lamplighters’ ledger. Griggs had been assigned to the Whitechapel southern section, but on the night of October fourteenth he had been sent along another route. He had not been on Commercial Road. The ledger entry recording three lamps lit on Commercial Road that night was not his entry. Someone had written it in his name, in a hand that was not his, and the gas that passed through those three lamps—or through the bypass beneath them—had been unrecorded.

Griggs had insisted the ledger was wrong. His written denial of the entry was a matter of record. He had denied lighting the lamps. He had insisted, and one week later he was in the canal.

She slept fitfully and woke at six. The fog had thinned overnight but still clung to the streets in grey rags. She dressed in her dark wool, pinned her hair, and checked that the ink stain on her right forefinger was dry—it was always dry, but the check was habitual. She collected the satchel and walked to the company offices.

The building was quiet at that hour. The night porter sat dozing in the entrance hall and did not look up as she passed. The stairs to the second floor smelled of gas and furniture polish, a combination she had always associated with the company’s offices. Finch’s door was closed. A line of light showed beneath it. She knocked once, firmly.

“Come.”

She entered and closed the door behind her.

Finch sat behind his desk, his pen in his hand, a cup of tea steaming at his elbow. The office was small and orderly, the walls lined with shelves of bound records. A clock on the mantelpiece ticked steadily. The polished surface of the desk reflected the gaslight from the wall fixture, and the whole room smelled of warm brass and lemon oil.

“Miss Quill.” He did not put down the pen. “You are early.”

“I am here at a time when we can speak privately, Mr. Finch.”

“I see.” He set the pen in its tray and leaned back. His face was composed, the face of a man who has practiced composure. “What is it you wish to speak about?”

Ada opened her satchel and drew out the notebook with the copied meter readings. She placed it on the desk, open to the page showing the Whitechapel district figures. Beside it she laid the lamplighters’ ledger, open to the October fourteenth entry. She did not speak yet. She let him look.

Finch looked at the two open pages. His eyes moved from one to the other. He did not touch either book.

“You have been busy,” he said.

“The meter readings from the Beckton works do not correspond with the lamplighters’ ledger for the Whitechapel district. Over a period of six weeks, the deficit is consistent. Gas is recorded as consumed by lamps that were not lit. The gas left the works, but the district meter shows less than the ledger claims.”

“A discrepancy is not an accusation. It is an invitation to re-check one’s sums.”

“I have re-checked them. The sums are correct. I copied the meter readings myself from the brass dials in the meter room. The readings are mechanical. They do not make errors.”

Finch picked up his tea and drank. The cup clinked against the saucer when he set it down. “Meter mechanisms fail. Dials stick. You are a clerk, Miss Quill, not an engineer. You are not qualified to pronounce on the reliability of a mechanical instrument.”

“Mr. Rook is qualified. He was with me.”

The cup stayed in Finch’s hand. He held it suspended an inch above the saucer. The tea inside tilted slightly, and a drop spilled onto the polished wood. He did not wipe it away.

“You took an engineer to the Beckton works,” he said. His voice was measured. “On what authority?”

“On the authority of a dead lamplighter whose own written denial said the ledger was wrong.”

Finch put the cup down. The saucer rattled. “Griggs was a drunkard who fell into a canal.”

“Griggs was not on Commercial Road on October fourteenth. He was assigned to a different route that night. The ledger entry recording three lamps lit on Commercial Road was written in his name, but not by his hand. I have compared the letter forms. The terminal G is different. Griggs formed his terminal letters with a tail. The writer of the October fourteenth entry did not.”

Finch stood. His chair scraped the floor. He came around the desk and stood in front of her, closer than was proper. She could smell the tea on his breath and something else, something sour, the smell of a man who has not slept well.

“You are making very serious allegations, Miss Quill.”

“I am establishing a sequence of verifiable facts. The meter readings, the ledger entries, the route assignment, and the handwriting comparison are all documented in my notes. I am not making allegations. I am presenting data.”

“Data.” He said the word as though it had a bad taste. “You present data to me as though I were the one who had fabricated these entries.”

“Did you?”

The question hung between them. Finch’s hand went to his collar. He loosened it a fraction, then seemed to realize what he was doing and dropped his hand to his side.

“This is absurd,” he said. “You have no evidence of any such thing. You have a set of copied numbers and a theory about handwriting. The police have already investigated Griggs’s death and found it to be accidental. The company has accepted the finding. If you continue to pursue this, I will have no choice but to review your fitness for your position.”

“The valve in the Beckton valve gallery,” Ada said. “The secondary pipe joined to the Whitechapel district main, with a hand-operated valve. Mr. Rook and I found it on the night of October twenty-seventh. The valve was open. Gas was flowing through it into a conduit beneath the retort house floor. The conduit runs toward the river wall. The pipe is not on any company distribution map. It is an unauthorized tap on the district main, installed after the meter.”

Finch had gone still. His face had changed. The composure was still there, but thinner now, stretched over something underneath. His eyes moved to the desk, to the drawer on the left side, and then back to her.

“You went into the valve gallery,” he said quietly.

“I did.”

“At night. Without authorization.”

“The authorization is the discrepancy. A clerk’s duty is to the integrity of the record.”

“A clerk’s duty is to her superiors.” His voice was sharp now, the composure cracking at the edges. “You have exceeded your authority at every stage. You have conscripted an engineer into your private inquiry, trespassed in company works, and copied restricted meter records. Any one of these is grounds for dismissal.”

Ada did not move. She stood with her hands at her sides, the satchel open on the desk before her, the evidence laid out in its careful order. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked.

“Mr. Rook witnessed the bypass,” she said. “He can identify the valve by its position in the gallery. Third tier, east wall, between the Whitechapel and Limehouse district junctions. The fittings are crude. The pipe is iron, not the brass used in standard company installations. It was installed by someone with access to the gallery but without access to the company’s standard materials, or by someone who did not want the installation to match company specifications.”

Finch sat down. Not in his chair behind the desk, but on the bench against the wall, the one where visitors waited. He sat heavily, as though his legs had decided for him. He put both hands on his knees. His palms left dark patches on the fabric of his trousers.

“You do not understand what you have done,” he said.

“Then tell me.”

He looked at the door. Then at the window. Then at the drawer of his desk. His right hand moved to his pocket and came out with a small brass key. He held it between his thumb and forefinger, turning it slowly.

“I did not write the October fourteenth entry,” he said. “But I knew it had been written. I knew because the man who wrote it told me. He told me because I am the one who keeps the records, and the records had to match.”

Ada watched the key turning in his fingers. The clock ticked. The tea on the desk was cooling, and a skin had formed on its surface.

“Who wrote it?”

Finch did not answer that. He stood again and went to the desk. He put the key into the lock of the left drawer. His hand trembled. The key scraped against the keyhole, and he had to steady it with his other hand. The lock clicked. In the quiet office, the sound was sharp and definite, like a bone snapping in the silence.

He pulled the drawer open and drew out a ledger. It was smaller than the company’s standard volumes, bound in dark cloth that had once been green. The cover was stained—tea, she thought, and something else, a darker blotch that might have been ink or might have been something she did not want to name. The corners were soft with handling. He set it on the desk and pushed it toward her.

“Open it,” he said.

She did. The pages were ruled in pencil, not printed. The columns were labeled by hand. Date, district, meter reading, diverted volume, recipient. Each entry was in Finch’s handwriting. The first entry was dated July third. The last was October twenty-first. Three months of systematic, careful records. The diverted volumes were modest at first—a few hundred cubic feet per week—and then grew. By September, the weekly diversion was in the thousands. The recipient column contained initials, not names. She did not recognize them.

“I was instructed to keep this record,” Finch said. His voice had dropped, barely above a whisper. “I had no choice.”

Ada looked at the columns. “The recipient initials. Who do they refer to?”

Finch pressed his lips together and shook his head. “I do not know all of them. I was told what to write, not why.”

“You are the chief clerk. You keep every other record in this office. You expect me to believe you did not ask.”

“I learned not to ask.”

Ada looked up from the ledger. Finch stood on the other side of the desk. His face was grey. The sour smell was stronger now, and she realized it was fear.

“Griggs found the bypass,” she said.

Finch nodded, once. “He was doing routine maintenance on the lamps. He found the tap. He reported it to me. I told the man who had it installed.” He stopped. His jaw tightened. “Griggs had also told the night inspector, and the night inspector would have checked the records.”

“And then Griggs died.”

Finch’s hand went to his face. He pressed his fingers against his temple, hard, as though trying to push something back inside. “I did not—“He stopped. He took a breath. “I did not touch Griggs. I did not order anyone to touch Griggs. But I was told it would be handled. And then it was handled.” His voice dropped further. “And then he was in the canal.”

Ada closed the second ledger. Her fingers were steady. The ink stain on her forefinger pressed against the stained cloth cover, and she was conscious of that contact—the clean mark of her trade against the soiled record of his.

“You are telling me you were coerced.”

“I am telling you I had no choice. You have not seen the men who came to this office and told me what to write, nor have you seen what they did to Bill Hobbs. You have not—“He stopped himself. He pressed his lips together and looked at the door again.

“What did they do to Bill Hobbs?”

Finch shook his head. “Bill will not talk to you. Bill will not talk to anyone. I am the only one who kept a record, because I could not bear it otherwise. I could not bear that the company’s books should be false and no honest account exist anywhere. I kept that ledger because someone, someday, might need it. And now you are here, and I am giving it to you, and I do not know whether that makes me brave or whether it makes me a fool.”

Ada picked up the ledger. It was heavier than it looked. She put it in her satchel alongside her own notebook, her ledger, and the Tuesday sheet. The satchel was full now, the strap tight across the flap.

“The men who coerced you,” she said. “Are they inside the company?”

Finch looked at her. His eyes were red, and the skin beneath them was dark and swollen. He opened his mouth and then closed it. He turned to the window and stood with his back to her.

“Take the ledger and go,” he said. “Do not come back to this office. Do not write my name on anything. If you are going to use what is in that book, use it somewhere other than here.”

She fastened the satchel and slung it over her shoulder. The weight pulled against her hip. She walked to the door and put her hand on the handle.

“Mr. Finch.”

He did not turn from the window.

“Griggs denied lighting those lamps. The ledger entry says he lit them. The ledger entry is false. You have just confirmed that it is false. I want you to say it.”

Finch’s shoulders moved. He turned his head, not fully, just enough that she could see the edge of his face, the jaw set tight, the eye wet.

“The ledger entry is false,” he said. “Griggs did not light those lamps. He was not on Commercial Road that night.”

Ada opened the door and stepped into the corridor. The gas lamp at the far end hissed in its fixture. She pulled the door shut behind her, and the click of the latch was small and final.
