Chapter 18

The Secret Ledger

The brass key sat in Finch’s palm, slick with sweat. His fingers closed around it twice before they steadied enough to find the keyhole.

Ada stood on the far side of the desk, her notebook open to the page where she had transcribed the Beckton meter readings for October — the ones she and Rook had copied in the meter room after dark, pressing close to the brass housings with a penlight clamped between her teeth. The numbers were precise. The numbers were impossible. Gas leaving the works at a volume the district consumption ledgers did not account for, the gap steady for months, a channel of unrecorded fuel flowing somewhere beneath the streets.

Finch turned the key. The lock gave with a flat click that carried in the quiet office. The single gas lamp on the corner of his desk threw yellow light across the polished oak, and the drawer slid open on oiled runners, soundless, as though it had been opened many times before.

“Sit down, Miss Quill.”

“I would rather stand.”

He pulled a ledger from the drawer. Bound in black leather, darker than the company’s standard brown, slimmer — perhaps a third the thickness of the quarterly distribution records. When he set it on the desk between them, it landed with a weight that did not match its size. The cover was scuffed at the corners. The spine had cracked along a line that showed white beneath the dye, opened and closed and opened again across many months.

Ada did not touch it. She looked at Finch. His collar was loose, the knot of his tie pulled away from his throat, and his face shone under the lamp. The careful authority she had known for three years — the measured voice, the tidy desk, the man who signed her quarterly reviews in clean ink — had left him entirely. What remained was someone smaller, perched on the edge of his chair, tapping the desk’s edge with his index finger in a rapid, irregular rhythm.

“You kept a second set of books.”

“That is not —“He stopped. His finger tapped three more times, then went still. “It is not what you think.”

“Then tell me precisely what it is, Mr. Finch. I have the Beckton readings here. I have the district consumption logs from this office. The difference between them is consistent and it is large. Last night, with Mr. Rook, I saw the bypass pipe installed after the meter, running toward the river. I am not guessing. I am stating what I observed, and I am asking you to account for it.”

Finch looked at the ledger between them. He did not open it.

“Who else knows you went to Beckton?”

“Mr. Rook. You. And whoever told you I was there, if someone did.”

“Varney came to this office on Tuesday.”

Ada’s hand tightened on her notebook. The edges of the paper pressed into her palm, and she felt the corner of the cover bite into the base of her thumb.

“What did you tell him?”

“I told him nothing. He told me.” Finch’s voice dropped, the words coming fast and low, as though the walls had ears. “He said you had been seen at the gasworks with an engineer, after hours, in the meter room. He said if you continued to involve yourself in matters beyond your duties, he would hold me responsible for failing to supervise you properly.”

“And you believed him.”

“I believed he was watching this office, Miss Quill. That is not the same thing.”

Ada set her notebook on the desk, beside the black ledger. Her copied meter readings faced upward, the columns of figures in her own careful hand, each digit formed with the precision she had been trained to maintain.

“These readings show gas leaving Beckton at a volume the district ledgers do not record. The bypass pipe I saw last night runs from the main output line, after the meter, into a secondary conduit heading south. Gas flows through it. Mr. Rook traced the pipe to a junction beneath the Commercial Road branch. That junction does not appear on any distribution map in the engineering offices.”

Finch’s hand moved to the black ledger. His fingers rested on the cover, and the tapping stopped.

“You have been thorough.”

“I have been accurate. There is a difference. Now open that ledger and tell me what it contains, or I will take my notebook and my testimony to the coroner’s office and let them ask you the same questions under oath.”

The room was very quiet. The gas lamp hissed faintly, a sound Ada had stopped noticing months ago but which now seemed to fill the space between them. Finch opened the ledger.

The pages were ruled in the same format as the company’s standard books — date, district, volume, initials — but the entries were in different inks. Some blue, some black, some a reddish-brown that might have been ordinary ink turned with age or might have been something else. Ada leaned forward without picking the book up, reading the first page upside down from where she stood.

The dates began in April. The district column read “Whpl-S” in a hand she recognised after a moment as Finch’s own — the same looping W, the same abbreviated S. The volume column held figures in cubic feet. The initials column held two sets: one she did not know, and one she did.

“H.S.,” she said. “Those are the initials in the meter-reading column.”

“Harold Sefton. He reads the Beckton master meter for the southern district twice daily.”

“The Beckton meter is considered irrefutable. I was told that by Mr. Rook and by every manual of practice this company prints.”

“It is irrefutable. The meter records what leaves the works accurately. What Sefton writes in the official log is what the meter says — most of the time.”

Ada pulled the ledger toward her across the desk. The leather was cold under her fingers, smoother than the standard company bindings, and it smelled of damp and old glue. She turned to the second page. The entries continued, week by week, each with the same two sets of initials — Finch’s and Sefton’s — and each with a volume figure that, when she compared it mentally to her copied readings, was slightly lower than the actual meter output.

“You altered the official log.”

“Sefton alters the log. I record what was actually taken. This book —“He placed his finger on the open page. “This book is the true record. The official log shows what the meter should have read if nothing had been diverted. This shows what was diverted.”

Ada turned another page. May. The volumes increased. A third column appeared, unlabelled, with figures in pencil that were smaller, and a notation she could not immediately parse.

“What is this column?”

“Profit. Calculated at the company’s wholesale rate per thousand cubic feet, less Sefton’s portion.”

She stared at the figures. The monthly totals were large — not the sums of a petty embezzler skimming a few pounds from the till, but amounts that would have supported a business, a household, a small enterprise. The March entry alone showed a diversion of nearly forty thousand cubic feet above what the official log recorded.

“Who receives these payments?”

“That column does not say.”

“You are lying.”

Finch’s jaw tightened. His finger resumed its tapping on the desk edge, the same irregular rhythm as before. He did not meet her eyes.

“I am not lying about that. The payments are collected by Sefton. He distributes them. I was never told to whom.”

“And you did not ask.”

“I was told not to ask, Miss Quill. There is a difference, and if you have spent any time at all considering the position I have been in, you will understand the difference without my explaining it.”

Ada turned to the last written page. The entries ran to October the fourteenth. The volume figure was modest compared to March — perhaps eight thousand cubic feet — and in the initials column, beside Finch’s F and Sefton’s H.S., a third set appeared. T.G.

Thomas Griggs.

Her finger rested on the initials without her deciding to place it there. The ink was the same reddish-brown as several earlier entries, and it had bled slightly into the paper’s grain, spreading at the edges of each letter.

“Griggs is in this book.”

“Griggs found the bypass on the fourteenth. He was on the Commercial Road route that night — not his usual round, but Hargreaves was ill and they needed a man on the southern section. He came back from his round and went straight to Sefton. He had seen the junction pipe. He had followed it from the lamppost base to the main.”

Ada’s throat was dry. She swallowed, and the sound was audible in the quiet room.

“What happened then?”

“Sefton came to me the next morning. He said Griggs was asking questions, wanted to know why the pipe existed, who had authorised it. Sefton told him it was a company maintenance line — a relief valve for pressure testing. Griggs did not believe him.”

“Griggs said the ledger was wrong. He told me the entry for October fourteenth was false. He said he had not lit those three lamps on Commercial Road.”

“He had not. The lamps were already lit when he reached them. The gas was flowing through the bypass, feeding those lamps and others — shops, a public house on Cable Street — connections that do not appear on any authorised distribution map. Whoever lit those lamps was not a company lamplighter.”

Ada closed her eyes. The reddish-brown ink burned behind her eyelids. When she opened them, Finch was watching her with an expression stripped of authority, stripped of office, stripped of everything but a frightened man sitting across a desk from someone who now knew what he knew.

“You recorded his name in this book on the day he found the pipe. And he was dead within the week.”

“I recorded his name because Sefton told me to. Sefton said Griggs had been persuaded to keep quiet, that he had accepted a share — you can see the figure in the profit column, it is the smallest entry in the book. I wrote it down because that is what I do. I keep the record.”

“And then he was found in the canal.”

“I did not —“Finch’s voice broke. He pressed his hand flat against the desk, and the tapping finally stopped. “I did not touch him. I did not order anything. I received a message on the seventeenth, two days after he was found, that the matter was resolved. That is the word it used. Resolved.”

Ada pulled the ledger fully toward her. It sat in front of her now, open to the page with Griggs’s initials, the columns of figures and dates and profits laid bare under the hissing gas lamp. She turned back through the pages slowly, reading each entry. The pattern was exactly as she had inferred from the meter readings — a systematic, regular diversion, hidden by adjusted official logs, tracked in this private record that Finch had kept because Finch kept records, because that was the one thing he could control in a situation that had stripped him of every other authority.

The company’s order — the ledgers, the twice-daily readings, the precise columns, the quarterly reviews she had always trusted — had been turned into the instrument of the theft itself. The system she had believed in had not failed. It had been used.

“There are names in this book besides Sefton and Griggs.”

“Initials. Not names.”

“Whose initials are in the profit distribution column for June? The entry that shows the largest figure — there are three sets of initials there, and only one of them is Sefton’s.”

Finch looked at the page she indicated. His mouth opened, and then closed. He shook his head.

“I was told those were the people Sefton answered to. Their names were not given. I was told that if I stopped keeping this record, the same thing that happened to Griggs would happen to me.”

Ada’s hands were on the ledger. The leather had warmed under her palms, and the weight of it — the physical weight of the book, its pages and their ink and their figures — pressed against the desk. She had come into this room with a notebook of copied readings and the memory of a pipe she had seen in the dark. She was leaving with the names — the initials — the dates, the profits, the mechanism, the proof.

“You want me to take this.”

“I want you to understand that I cannot keep it. If they come to this office and find it —““When did they last come?”

“September. Sefton came to check that the entries were current. He looked at the book, he closed it, and he told me I was doing well. That is the word he used. Well.” Finch’s voice was barely above a whisper. “I have not been able to sleep through a night since Griggs was pulled from the canal. I keep this desk locked. I check the drawer every morning. I cannot —“He stopped. His hand was trembling on the desk.

“Griggs came to me on the twelfth,” Ada said. “He said the ledger entry for the fourteenth was wrong — that he had been sent to cover another run, that he had not lit those lamps. He was agitated. He said someone was stealing gas and that the records were being changed to hide it.”

“He came to you.”

“He came to me because I was his superior and he did not trust you. He told me the police had already spoken to him about the discrepancy, and he told me the official position was that it was a clerical error. He knew it was not. He knew, and he was frightened, and two days later his name appears in your book, and five days after that he was dead.”

Finch said nothing. The gas lamp hissed. The office smelled of ink and leather and the stale warmth of a man who had been sitting in this room for hours, sweating into his clothes.

Ada picked up the ledger. It was heavier than it had looked, the binding dense with pages of close-written figures, and the leather cover was cold again where her hands had not been touching it. She placed it in her satchel. It sat against her notebook, the two books pressing together — one hers, one his — and the buckle of the satchel was stiff under her fingers as she closed it.

“If I take this and they discover it is missing from your office, they will come to you.”

“They will come to me regardless. You have been to Beckton. Varney knows. Sefton may know already. Taking the ledger does not change what is coming to this office — it only changes whether there is a record of it when it arrives.”

She adjusted the satchel’s strap on her shoulder. The weight pulled the bag against her hip, heavier than when she had arrived.

“You should leave London, Mr. Finch.”

“I cannot leave. If I leave, it confirms what they already suspect — that I have been keeping evidence. If I stay, I am a clerk who goes to his desk and does his work and keeps his head down. That is the only protection I have.”

“It did not protect Griggs.”

The words landed between them, and Finch flinched — a small, involuntary movement of his shoulders, as though the name itself had a physical weight. He looked at the empty drawer, then at the desk, then at his own hands resting on the wood.

“Griggs was a lamplighter. He walked a route. He was alone, at night, on streets where anyone could approach him. I sit in an office with a door that locks and a clerk who can see everyone who enters. It is not the same.”

Ada looked at him. The careful, measured chief clerk who had signed her reviews, who had told her a discrepancy was not an accusation but an invitation to re-check one’s sums, who had tapped his fingers on the desk and avoided her eyes and kept a black ledger in a locked drawer — he was still sitting there, but the office around him had changed. The desk was a thing she had respected. The gas lamp was a fixture she had trusted. The walls were the walls of an institution she had believed in, and all of it now looked like the furniture of a room where a frightened man had sat for months, keeping records of a crime he was too afraid to stop and too honest to destroy.

“Be careful, Miss Quill.”

“You have said that.”

“I am saying it again. What happened to Griggs was not an accident. He was a man who knew one thing too many and said it to the wrong person. You now know everything he knew, and you know more — you have the meter readings, you have seen the pipe, and you have that book in your bag. If they learn what you carry, they will not send a message.”

Ada’s hand was on the door handle. The brass was cold. She did not turn it.

“Who are they?”

“I do not know. Sefton is the only name I have. The initials in that book — I cannot tell you whose they are because I was never told. I was told to write. I wrote.”

“You were told to write because you are accurate and because your handwriting is on every official record this office produces. If this ledger were ever examined alongside the company’s books, your hand would be identified in both. You are not merely a keeper of this record, Mr. Finch. You are the proof that it is genuine.”

Finch stared at her. His mouth worked, and no sound came. Then he looked down at his hands, and his shoulders dropped, and the last of his authority settled out of him like sediment falling to the bottom of still water.

“Take it. Take it and do not come back to this office.”

Ada turned the handle. The corridor outside was dim, the gas lamp at the far end burning low, the way it always burned at this hour — a small, steady flame in a fixture she had passed a thousand times without thinking about what fed it. She stepped into the corridor. The door closed behind her with a soft click, and the weight of the black ledger pulled against her hip with every step.

The stairs were narrow. She climbed them slowly, her hand on the wall, the satchel pressing against her side. The ledger’s corners dug into her ribs through the canvas. At the top of the stairs, she paused, and the gas lamp in the corridor threw her shadow long and thin across the stone floor.

She had entered this building this morning with copied meter readings and the memory of a pipe running in the dark beneath Beckton. She was leaving with a book that named the man who read the meter, the man who altered the log, the profits taken, and the dates of every diversion for six months. She was leaving with the initials of a dead man written in reddish-brown ink on the page for October the fourteenth — the same date as the false entry that had started everything, the entry that said Thomas Griggs lit three lamps on Commercial Road when Thomas Griggs had lit nothing at all.

The night air hit her face as she stepped through the front entrance. The street was quiet. A lamplighter was working the far corner, his long pole reaching up to the gas lamp on the iron post, and the flame caught with a small pop that she heard across the distance. He moved to the next post, and the next, and the street brightened behind him in a line of small steady lights.

Ada walked north. The satchel hung from her shoulder, and the ledger inside it sat against her notebook, and the weight of it was the weight of a record that could not be unmade, could not be burned, could not be locked in a drawer again — because she had seen it, and read it, and carried it out into the street where the gas lamps burned and the city moved through the dark, lit by fuel that someone had stolen and someone had sold and someone had killed to keep.
