Chapter 1

The Impossible Entry

The nib of Ada Quill’s pen hesitated over the ledger, caught by three consecutive entries in the same, impossible hand. The gas bracket above her desk threw a cone of yellowish light across the open page, and beyond the frosted window of the Gas Light and Coke Company’s clerking office, the Thames moved in its slow, industrial darkness. Half past seven on a Thursday evening in October. The office had emptied an hour ago, leaving her with the lamplighters’ rounds ledger, the time sheets, and the particular silence of a room built for fifty clerks now housing one.

She had been at this work for six years. Every evening, the same sequence: collect the rounds ledger from the lamplighters’ depot, cross-reference each entry against the time sheets, confirm the lamp numbers, the routes, the times, the initials, and log the verified figures into the master ledger under her own hand. The system was simple, which was why it worked. A lamplighter walked his route, lit the lamps assigned to him, and noted the time beside each lamp number. The clerk checked the note against the schedule. Discrepancies were rare and, when they appeared, straightforward: a lamp missed because the wick was frayed, a time late because the weather turned, a route covered by a substitute because the assigned man was ill. Every discrepancy had a reason that could be found, stated, corrected, and entered in the margin in Ada’s careful hand.

She pulled the time sheet for Tuesday last and laid it beside the open ledger. Tuesday’s page in the rounds ledger showed Griggs’s route in his own blocky script: lamps 14 through 31 on the Mile End Road and Burdett Road, times running from six eighteen to seven oh four, the initials T.G. at the foot of the column. Her own master ledger entry for Tuesday matched, verified and copied on Wednesday morning. That was the order of things. The lamplighter wrote, the clerk verified, the record stood.

But Tuesday’s page also held three entries she had not verified, because she had not seen them until tonight.

They sat below Griggs’s column, separated by a blank line, in a hand that was almost his. Almost. The letters were slightly narrower, the downstrokes pressed harder, the tail of the G in T.G. hooked left where Griggs always hooked right. Lamp 7, Commercial Road, six forty-two. At Commercial Road, lamp 8 read six forty-four. Lamp 9, Commercial Road, six forty-six. The same initials: T.G.

Commercial Road was not Griggs’s route. Commercial Road was not anyone’s route on a Tuesday. The schedule assigned it to Daniel Poole on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays, and to no one on Tuesdays or Thursdays, because the Commercial Road lamps were wired to the meter at the corner of Watney Street and lit by the meter reader on his rounds, not by a lamplighter at all.

Ada laid her finger on the first entry and traced it left to right, as though the motion might reveal what the letters concealed. Lamp 7. Six forty-two. T.G. The ink was the same stable brown as the rest of the page — the company’s standard supply, nothing mixed on the sly. The spacing was regular. Whoever had written these entries had written them calmly, without hurry, with a pen held at the proper angle and a hand resting on the desk at the proper height.

She checked the time sheet again. Griggs, Thomas, assigned Tuesday 14 October, route M-7, Mile End Road and Burdett Road, lamps 14 through 31, start time six fifteen. No mention of Commercial Road. No mention of lamps 7, 8, or 9. No substitute assignment, no route change, no note in the margin authorising a deviation.

The ledger said Griggs had lit three lamps on a road he was not assigned to walk, at times that overlapped with the lamps he was assigned to light on a different road entirely. The time sheet said he had not.

Ada closed the time sheet and placed it on top of the stack. She capped her pen, set it beside the inkwell, and sat with her hands flat on the desk. The gas bracket hissed faintly above her, the flame steady behind its wire guard. The ledger lay open to Tuesday’s page, the three entries visible in the cone of light, the blank line above them marking the boundary between what she had verified and what she had not.

She picked up the ledger, closed it, and reached for her coat.

The lamplighters’ yard sat behind the company depot on Horseferry Road, a rectangle of brick and packed earth enclosed on three sides by the lamp store, the oil shed, and the wall of the adjacent brewery. Ada crossed the yard at a quarter to eight, the ledger under her arm, her lamp casting a bobbing circle of light on the cobbles. The air tasted of coal dust and the sour yeast from the brewery vats.

Griggs sat on a stool inside the lamp store, a lantern disassembled across his knees. He was a compact man in his fifties, with thick grey sideburns and hands that looked built for the work — broad palms, short fingers, the nails blackened with lamp oil that no amount of washing would lift. His coat hung on a hook behind him, still damp from the afternoon’s rain. He was cleaning the burner assembly with a rag, drawing it through the brass tube with the mechanical patience of a man who had cleaned ten thousand burners and would clean ten thousand more.

“Mr. Griggs.”

He looked up. “Miss Quill. Bit late for the books, isn’t it?”

“I need to ask you about Tuesday’s round.”

“Tuesday.” He set the rag aside. “What about it?”

Ada opened the ledger to Tuesday’s page and held it out, the three entries facing him under the store’s single gas bracket. “Lamps seven, eight, and nine on Commercial Road. Six forty-two through six forty-six. Initials T.G.”

Griggs looked at the page. His eyes moved across the entries once, then again, and his hand, which had been reaching for the rag, stopped mid-air and returned to his knee.

“I didn’t write that.”

“The entries are in this ledger, Mr. Griggs, under your initials, on a night you were assigned to Mile End Road and Burdett Road. Can you explain how they came to be there?”

“I wasn’t on Commercial Road Tuesday. I was on Mile End, like I always am. Burdett Road, lamps fourteen through thirty-one.” He pointed at the upper entries, his own column. “That’s mine. That’s right. These down here — I never wrote these.”

“I have your time sheet here as well. It confirms your assignment to route M-7, Mile End and Burdett Road, with no notation of a route change or substitution.”

“Because there wasn’t one. I walked my round same as always, lit my lamps, came back, and logged off at the depot at seven ten. You can ask Wright at the desk. He took my slip.”

Ada produced the time sheet. “I checked. Your slip was stamped seven twelve by the night porter, which is consistent with your usual return from the Burdett Road end of the route. Commercial Road is a quarter mile east of your farthest point on Burdett Road. To light lamp seven at six forty-two, you would have had to leave your route on Burdett Road at approximately six thirty-eight, walk to Commercial Road, light three lamps in four minutes, and return to Burdett Road to light lamp twenty-two at six fifty, which is the next entry in your own column above these.”

“I didn’t do that. I was on Burdett Road the whole time. Lamp twenty-two, twenty-three, twenty-four — I lit them in order, same as every night. You can check the wicks. I trimmed twenty-two’s wick Tuesday because it was charring on the left side. There’s a fresh cut on it. Go and look.”

“I am not disputing your work on Burdett Road, Mr. Griggs. I am asking you to account for three entries on Commercial Road that carry your initials.”

“And I’m telling you I don’t know anything about them.”

His voice had risen, not to a shout but to the pitch of a man repeating himself against a current. He was looking at the ledger the way a man looks at a document that has accused him of something he cannot answer, his jaw set, his shoulders drawn up.

Ada held the ledger steady. The gas bracket above them flickered once, throwing his shadow large against the brick wall behind him, then steadied.

“Someone wrote these entries. The hand is close to yours — the letter forms are consistent with your writing, though the downstrokes are heavier and the G is hooked in the opposite direction from your usual hand. I am not prepared to say it is yours. I am prepared to say it is not mine, and that it appeared in this ledger between my verification on Wednesday morning and my audit this evening.”

“Then someone put it there after you’d checked the page.”

“The ledger was in the office safe from Wednesday morning until I removed it this evening at half past six. The safe combination is known to me, to Mr. Finch, and to the senior audit clerk. No one else.”

Griggs stood. The stool scraped back against the floor, and the burner assembly on his knees clattered against the workbench. His hands hung at his sides, and the fingers of his right hand opened and closed once, slowly, as though gripping and releasing something he could not see.

“You’re saying someone in the office wrote those entries.”

“I am establishing a sequence of verifiable facts, Mr. Griggs. The entries exist. You deny writing them. Your time sheet places you on a different route. The ledger was secured. Those are the facts I have. I am asking whether you can add to them.”

“I can’t add to what I don’t understand. I lit my lamps on Burdett Road. I came home. I logged off. Now you’re telling me someone put my name on lamps I never touched, on a road I wasn’t walking, and the book was locked up the whole time.”

“That is what the record shows.”

He turned away from her, then turned back. His right hand was at his side now, and the tremor was visible — not the coarse shaking of a man in the cold, but a fine vibration in the fingers, the kind that comes from holding the whole arm rigid.

“Miss Quill, I’ve been lighting lamps for this company for nineteen years. I’ve never had a mark against my name. Not one. You can go back to the year they hired me and look at every page I’ve signed, and you won’t find a single entry I didn’t make myself.”

“I am not marking anything against your name. I am trying to understand how three entries appeared in a ledger that was locked in a safe between my verification and my audit. If you did not write them, someone else did, and that someone else had access to the safe and knowledge of your handwriting and your route schedule.”

Griggs looked at her. The tremor had moved into his left hand now, and he pressed it flat against his thigh.

“Then it’s someone in the office.”

“That is the logical conclusion, yes.”

“And you’ll take that to Finch?”

“I will report the discrepancy to Mr. Finch in the morning, yes.”

He nodded slowly, his eyes still on the ledger. “You do what you have to do, Miss Quill. But I’ll tell you this — I didn’t write those entries, and I didn’t light those lamps, and if someone in this company is putting my name on work I didn’t do, then I’d like to know who and I’d like to know why.”

“So would I, Mr. Griggs.”

He sat down again, heavily, and picked up the burner assembly and the rag. His hands resumed their motion, drawing the rag through the brass tube, but the rhythm was off — too fast, the pressure uneven, the rag catching where it should slide.

Ada closed the ledger. “I will need you to sign a statement confirming that you deny authorship of these entries and that you were not on Commercial Road on Tuesday evening. I will prepare it tonight and bring it to the depot in the morning.”

“Fine.”

“Mr. Griggs.”

He looked up.

“The entries record three lamps lit on Commercial Road at six forty-two, six forty-four, and six forty-six. If you did not light them, someone did, or someone wants it to appear that someone did. Either way, gas was drawn through those lamps on Tuesday night, and that gas passed through the meter at Watney Street. The meter reader’s log will show whether the consumption was recorded.”

Griggs’s hand stopped on the rag. He looked at her, and for a moment his face was open in a way she had not seen before — not angry, not defensive, but bare, as though she had named something he had been circling without being able to say.

“The meter,” he said.

“The meter.”

He set the burner assembly down on the bench and folded the rag once, twice, and laid it on top. “I’ll sign your statement, Miss Quill. I’ll sign it in the morning.”

“Thank you.”

She turned and walked back across the yard, the ledger under her arm, her lamp throwing its circle on the cobbles ahead. The brewery wall loomed to her left, and the lamp store fell away behind her into darkness. She did not look back.

The office was as she had left it — empty, lit by the single gas bracket above her desk, the inkwell capped and the time sheets stacked neatly beside it. She sat down, opened the ledger to Tuesday’s page, and placed her finger on the first of the three entries. Lamp 7. Commercial Road. Six forty-two. T.G.

The ink was brown, the letters narrow, the G hooked left. She had verified the entries above the blank line on Wednesday morning, and the page had ended at lamp 31, Burdett Road, seven oh four, T.G. She had signed the verification in the margin: AQ, Wed 15 Oct, 8: 15 AM. Below her signature, the blank line. Below the blank line, three entries that had not been there on Wednesday morning.

She opened the master ledger and found her own entry for Tuesday. It ended at lamp 31, Burdett Road, seven oh four, T.G., verified, AQ. No mention of Commercial Road. No mention of lamps 7, 8, or 9. Her record was clean. The rounds ledger was not.

She pulled the meter reader’s schedule from the drawer. The Watney Street meter — which serviced lamps 7, 8, and 9 on Commercial Road, among others — was read on the first Monday of each month by the meter reader assigned to the Tower Division, a man named Hobbs. The next reading was scheduled for Monday, 3 November. The previous reading, taken on Monday, 7 October — five days before the entries appeared — would be recorded in the meter reader’s log, which was kept in the meter office on Bow Road.

She did not have access to the meter office. The meter reader’s logs were maintained by the inspection department, and the inspection department reported to Mr. Finch.

Ada sat back in her chair. The ledger lay open on the desk, the three entries visible in the gaslight, the blank line above them marking the edge of what she had verified and what she had not. Her pen lay capped beside the inkwell. The ink in the well had skinned over, dull and still.

She read the entries again. Three lamps, Commercial Road, six forty-two through six forty-six, T.G. — a record of work done by a man who said he did not do it, entered in a book that had been locked in a safe, written in a hand that was almost his but was not, on a road where no lamplighter was assigned to walk on a Tuesday night.

The gas bracket hissed above her, steady and indifferent. The ledger lay open. The entries did not change.

Griggs was in the corridor. He had not moved. When she stepped out of the office he was standing where she had left him, but differently — his weight had shifted, his hands were no longer at his sides but pressed flat against the wall, as though the plaster might give way and let him through.

“The book is wrong,” he said. He said it the way a man says something he has rehearsed, not to convince but to survive. His voice had dropped since the interview. Something had left it. “It’s wrong, Miss Quill. You know it’s wrong. I wasn’t there. I told you I wasn’t there.”

“You’ve told me.”

“Then why are you still — “He stopped. His jaw worked. He looked at the office door behind her, at the strip of light beneath it, and his face changed. The fear came up through it like water through cloth. “Someone put me on that road. Someone wrote me down. You understand? I wasn’t there, but someone put me there.”

“Mr. Griggs — ““I’ve said enough.” He pulled his hands from the wall. His palms were chalked white. “More than enough. I’ll not say another word without a union man present.” He turned and walked down the corridor, not quickly but with the rigid deliberateness of a man who knows he is being watched from behind.

Ada watched him until he reached the stairhead. He did not look back. His boots on the treads were slow, then faster, then gone.

She went back into the office and closed the door. The ledger was where she had left it, open, the three entries facing the ceiling. She did not sit. She stood over the book and looked at the hand that was almost Griggs’s — the slight leftward lean of the T, the abbreviated crossbar of the G — and felt the floor beneath her shift, not physically but in the way a thing shifts when you discover it was never what you believed it to be. She had trusted the ledger, the safe, the lock.

She picked up her pen. She did not uncap it. She held it and looked at the blank line above the three entries, and for the first time since she had begun this work, she did not know what to write next.
