Chapter 5

Finch's Fidgeting Pen

The summons slip lay crisp and white on the corner of Ada Quill’s desk, folded once along a precise crease, bearing only three words in Mr. Finch’s careful hand: See me. — F.

She turned the slip between her thumb and forefinger, testing the paper’s weight as though the stock itself might tell her something. Good company paper, the same she stacked in reams beside the gas bracket, but the fold was slightly uneven for Finch, whose margins never wandered. She set the slip flat on the Tuesday sheet and placed her pen precisely parallel to its edge. The quarterly lamp maintenance figures waited in a neat pile beside the inkwell, already tallied and cross-referenced twice. She gathered them, squared the pages against the desk, and stood.

The stairwell to the upper floor was narrow and unlit at this hour, the gas bracket on the landing turned low. Her shoes rang against the wooden treads, each step measured, and by the time she reached Finch’s door she had arranged the quarterly figures in their proper order and composed her face into what she hoped was professional readiness. The frosted glass panel showed the blurred shape of Finch at his desk, his shadow bent over the ledger. She knocked once, firmly.

“Come in, Miss Quill.”

His office occupied the corner of the upper floor, where the afternoon sun slanted through a tall window overlooking the Thames. The light caught dust motes turning slowly above the desk, above the open ledger, above Finch’s hands. He sat behind the desk with his sandy hair neatly combed and his moustache trimmed to its usual precise line. His pince-nez caught the sunbeam and threw a small bright coin of light across the ceiling. The ledger lay open before him, and beside it sat his silver pen, positioned parallel to the spine with the nib facing inward. Everything in its place.

“You sent for me, Mr. Finch.”

“Indeed.” He did not look up immediately. His fingers found the pen and lifted it, turning it slowly between thumb and forefinger, the way a man turns a thing he has forgotten he is holding. “The quarterly figures, Miss Quill. I wanted to review them before they go to the board.”

She stepped forward and placed the pages on the edge of his desk, then stepped back to her original position. “The tallies are complete for all districts. Whitechapel, Limehouse, Mile End. Each entry is cross-referenced against the lamplighters’ daily logs and the gas consumption charts.”

“Very thorough.” He drew the pages toward him without looking down. “You have been keeping well, I trust? No difficulties with the work?”

“None, Mr. Finch.”

“Good. Good.” He set the pen down. It tapped the ledger page once, a small sharp sound in the quiet room. “And the Tuesday sheets? Everything in order there?”

Ada’s fingers, which had been resting at her sides, curled slightly inward. The Tuesday sheet was in her ledger, downstairs on her desk, and the three entries on Commercial Road were still there in her careful hand, each letter and figure exactly as she had transcribed them from the daily log.

“The Tuesday sheets are filed and current, sir.”

“I ask only because these things matter,” Finch said. He picked up the pen again, and this time his fingers did not merely turn it. The nib touched the ledger page. Tap. Tap. Tap-tap. A rhythm that was not the rhythm of a man thinking, but of a man waiting for something to pass. “Accuracy in the small things is what separates a reliable clerk from a careless one. Would you not agree?”

“I would agree entirely, Mr. Finch. That is why I take particular care with every entry.”

“Particular care.” He repeated the phrase as though testing its weight. “Particular care is admirable, Miss Quill. But I wonder — has there been anything troubling you? Anything that has occupied your attention beyond the ordinary routine?”

The dust motes turned in the sunbeam. The room smelled of ink and old paper and, faintly, of the pink blotting powder that dusted the cuffs of Finch’s shirtsleeves. His eyes were on her now, steady behind the pince-nez, but his hand had not stopped. The pen moved against the ledger page in that same irregular pattern, denting the paper in small crescents she could see from where she stood.

“I am not certain I understand the question, Mr. Finch.”

“I think you do.” He removed the pince-nez and polished the lenses with a cloth drawn from his breast pocket, slowly, carefully, the way a man polishes something when he needs his eyes elsewhere. “The Griggs matter. You recall it, of course. The unfortunate business with the Commercial Road entries.”

Ada kept her hands at her sides. Her left thumb pressed hard against the side of her index finger, the nail blanching white. “I recall it. I raised the discrepancy because the entries did not match the lamplighter’s assigned route. I was performing my duty as a clerk.”

“Your duty.” He replaced the pince-nez and looked at her over the rims. “Your duty is to transcribe, Miss Quill. To copy what is given into the ledger without error and without question. That is the scope of your role. Is that not correct?”

“The scope of my role is to maintain accurate records, sir. When the records contradict themselves, accuracy requires investigation.”

“Investigation.” The pen stopped. Finch set it down precisely parallel to the ledger spine, the way it had been when she entered. “That is a strong word for a clerk to use. The police investigated, as I understand it. Detective Inspector Varney spoke to Griggs and concluded the matter. A clerical error, he called it. A misplaced entry, corrected and closed.”

“I am aware of Detective Inspector Varney’s conclusion.”

“Then you are aware that the matter is resolved.”

“The matter was resolved by the police, yes.”

“And yet.” Finch leaned forward slightly. The sunbeam shifted across the desk, catching the edge of the ledger and turning the inked entries luminous. His voice dropped, not to a whisper but to a register quieter and more deliberate than his usual tone. “And yet I understand you have been asking questions. Not only of the records, but of Mr. Griggs himself. Visiting the yard, consulting the roster, checking the time sheets against the daily logs.”

Ada’s throat tightened. She swallowed once, slowly, and kept her eyes on the inkwell beside his right hand. “I was verifying the entries against the source documents. That is standard procedure when a discrepancy is identified.”

“For how long, Miss Quill? How long does standard procedure continue after the police have closed the case?”

The pen was in his hand again. She had not seen him pick it up. Tap. Tap-tap. Tap. The rhythm was faster now, the crescents closer together, and the page beneath showed a constellation of small dents where the nib had struck repeatedly in the same spot. His knuckles had whitened around the barrel, the tendons standing taut across the backs of his hands.

“I completed my verification and filed my findings, Mr. Finch. The entries were noted and the ledger was corrected to reflect the accurate record.”

“Corrected.” He said the word as though it had a sour taste. “The ledger does not require your corrections, Miss Quill. The ledger is the company’s record, not your personal inquiry. When you alter an entry, you alter the company’s account. When you question a lamplighter, you question the company’s appointment of that lamplighter. Do you see the distinction?”

“I see the distinction between an accurate record and an inaccurate one.”

Finch’s hand went still. The pen rested on the page, the nib pressed into the paper, and a small dark bloom of ink spread outward from the point where it touched. He stared at the bloom for a moment, then lifted the pen and set it down.

“You are a diligent clerk, Miss Quill. I have always said so. Your transcription work is exemplary, and your attention to detail is exactly what the company requires in that position. But diligence and curiosity are not the same thing. One is a virtue. The other can become a liability.”

“I do not consider accuracy a liability, Mr. Finch.”

“No.” He drew a breath and released it slowly through his nose. “No, I suppose you would not. But I will tell you what I consider, and you may take it as you wish. There are records in this company that serve purposes beyond what a clerk’s desk is equipped to understand. Consumption charts, supply agreements, district allocations. These are matters for the senior officers and the board, not for the transcribing pool. When a clerk begins to examine those matters without authorization, the clerk ceases to function as a clerk and begins to function as something else entirely.”

The sunbeam had narrowed. The dust motes still turned within it, but the light was thinner now, pulling away from the desk toward the window. The room was cooling. Ada’s hands were clasped behind her back, her right hand closed around the wrist of her left, and the pressure of her grip left a dull ache in the joint.

“I transcribe lamplighters’ entries, Mr. Finch. I do not examine consumption charts or supply agreements. My work is confined to the daily logs and the ledger.”

“Then I am sure it will remain so.” Finch leaned back in his chair. He reached for the quarterly figures she had brought, drew them toward him, and squared their edges against the desk with three precise taps. “These look in order. I will review them before the board meeting on Thursday. Thank you, Miss Quill.”

She stood. The dismissal was plain. She had collected nothing from his desk because she had brought nothing that required collecting, and she turned toward the door with her hands still clasped behind her back and her posture carefully upright.

“One more thing.”

She stopped.

“Some records, Miss Quill, are better left unexamined.”

His voice was quiet, almost gentle, the tone of a man offering advice to a junior colleague. But the pen was in his hand again. She could hear it, even with her back to him. Tap. Tap. Tap-tap-tap. The rhythm was not steady. It was the rhythm of a hand moving without its owner’s permission, a signal his composure could not contain.

“Understood, Mr. Finch. Thank you.”

She opened the door, stepped through, and pulled it shut behind her. The stairwell was dim and cool, and she descended the treads with her eyes fixed on the wall ahead, counting the steps. Fourteen. She had counted them before, on other errands to this office, and the number had never mattered. It mattered now because counting was a thing her mind could do that did not require her to think about the sound of that pen on the ledger page.

The clerking office was empty. The gas bracket on the wall burned steadily, its mantle throwing a flat yellow light across the desks and filing cabinets and the frosted window that overlooked the Thames. The river was grey in the late afternoon, and a barge was being drawn along by a tug whose smoke trailed black against the pale sky. Ada sat at her desk. She placed her hands flat on the surface, one on either side of the ledger, and looked at them. The knuckles were still white. She opened her fingers, stretched them wide, and watched the blood return slowly to the joints.

The ledger lay open to Tuesday’s column. The three Commercial Road entries sat in their row, each letter and figure precisely formed in the hand that had written them, which was her own. She had transcribed them from the daily log without error. The daily log recorded that Griggs lit those lamps. The roster recorded that Griggs was assigned to Whitechapel southern section that night, not Commercial Road. But it was the Cable Street entry that had first caught her—the one line in the daily log lacking a corresponding shift log signature. Griggs had denied the Commercial Road work to her face, and then to the police, and the police had called it a clerical error and closed the case. Varney had warned her. And now Finch had warned her too.

She picked up her pen. The nib was clean, the ink fresh. She uncapped the inkwell and dipped the nib, and in the margin beside the three Commercial Road entries she made a small, careful cross. Beside the Cable Street entry, she made another. Each mark was precise, no larger than a fingernail, visible only to someone who knew where to look.

The ink dried quickly in the warm air from the gas bracket. She closed the ledger and placed her hand flat on the cover, pressing it against the desk as though the weight of her palm could keep the pages from turning themselves to something else.

Outside the frosted window, the tug and its barge had passed from view. The river moved in its grey channel, and the gas bracket hissed softly in the empty office, and the ledger sat beneath her hand with its small new mark drying in the margin, and the sound of Finch’s pen was still in her ears though she was two floors below it.

She gathered the Tuesday sheet and the roster into a single stack, squared their edges against the desk, and stood. Her chair scraped the boards. She crossed to the stairhead and climbed with the documents held before her in both hands, the way one carries a plate of cut glass—level, visible, steady.

Finch sat behind his desk. He did not look at the reports when she placed them. His pen was moving. She stood with her hands folded and waited.

“You’re very thorough, Miss Quill.” He did not glance up. “Almost”—the pen continued its stroke—“compulsively so. Have you always had these habits? This need to examine what doesn’t concern your section?”

She kept her shoulders square. “I was ensuring the ledger’s accuracy, sir. It’s my clerical duty to flag discrepancies.”

“Discrepancies.” He set the pen down. “Concerning Mr. Griggs’s route.”

The name landed like a coin on stone. She had not written Griggs’s name on any sheet she’d submitted, not on the Tuesday sheet, not in any report routed through the office. She had told no one above stairs. Her visit to his lodging had been her own affair, off the paper trail entirely.

She fixed her gaze on the brass tack at the corner of his desk blotter. The tack’s head was tarnished green at one edge. She studied it as though it were the only object in the room.

“Some records,” Finch said, “are better left unexamined. Wouldn’t you agree?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Good. That will be all.”

She descended the stairwell with even steps, pushed through the yard door, and shut it behind her. Then she bent forward and let the air leave her in a single sharp rush that fogged in the cold.

The sound of his pen. She heard it again now, not from below but in memory—that rapid, irregular scratching she’d stood beneath while waiting for him to speak. It had not been the rhythm of a man composing correspondence. It was too fast, too uneven, the strokes of someone marking the same line over and over because his hand could not be still.

Finch was afraid.

She straightened, pressed her hand to her sternum, and walked across the yard toward the lamplighters’ bench where Griggs sat trimming a wick, his thick fingers steady and slow.
