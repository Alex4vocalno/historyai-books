Chapter 2

A Visit from the Police

The three entries stared up from the Company’s bound ledger, which lay open on her clerking desk where she transcribed from it each morning as part of her daily work—lamp numbers 14, 15, and 16, Commercial Road, marked lit at 6: 42, 6: 46, and 6: 51 respectively, initialled T.G.—and every one of them, according to Thomas Griggs, was wrong.

She had spent the night turning the problem over the way one turns a cracked gear, hunting the fault line that could not possibly be there. The ledger did not make errors. She did not make errors.

Yet Griggs had stood in the lamplighters’ yard with his thick fingers wrapped around his trimming scissors and said, flatly, that he had not lit those lamps, that he had not been on Commercial Road that night, that the book was lying. She had reported the discrepancy through the proper channels, as procedure demanded, and now she sat waiting for whatever the proper channels had produced.

A broad shadow fell across the desk, blotting out the column of figures she had been re-reading for the third time that morning.

A man stood in the doorway of the clerking office, his coat still carrying the damp of the morning fog. He was thick-set, with a square jaw and the unhurried posture of someone accustomed to entering rooms and finding them already arranged for his convenience.

“Miss Quill?” he said. He did not wait for an answer. “Detective Inspector Silas Varney, Metropolitan Police. You reported an anomaly in the company records.”

Ada rose from her chair. “I reported a discrepancy, Detective Inspector. The distinction matters. An anomaly suggests the cause is unknown. The discrepancy is precisely defined: three entries recording lamps as lit on Commercial Road on the evening of October the fourteenth, initialled T.G., when the lamplighter assigned to those initials states he was not on that route.”

Varney crossed the narrow office in two strides and pulled the chair from the opposite desk without asking. He sat down, and the chair groaned under his weight.

“You’ve a tidy mind, Miss Quill. I’ll give you that.” Varney placed both hands on his knees. His gloves were on the desk now, beside the ledger—wool-lined, dark with coal dust at the fingertips. “Walk me through it. Slowly. I want to understand what you think you’ve found.”

Ada opened the ledger to the relevant page. Her fingers found the entries without hesitation; she had memorised their positions the way one memorises the location of a stain on a clean tablecloth. She pointed to the first.

“Lamp fourteen, Commercial Road, opposite the Whitechapel workhouse gate. Logged as lit at six forty-two in the evening, October the fourteenth. Initialled T.G. Lamp fifteen, approximately one hundred yards east, by the chandler’s shop. Six forty-six. Same initials. Lamp sixteen, at the junction with New Road. Six fifty-one. Same initials.” She paused. “The Tuesday sheet for October the fourteenth shows Thomas Griggs assigned to the southern section of Whitechapel, which includes Commercial Road. His route sheet confirms it. I transcribed these entries from the lamplighters’ daily return sheets on the morning of October the fifteenth, as I have done every morning for four years. The handwriting is mine. The information came from the return sheet, which Griggs himself signed.”

“And Griggs says he didn’t light them.” Varney said it without inflection, as though confirming the price of bread.

“Mr. Griggs states he was not on Commercial Road that evening. He states the entries are incorrect.”

Varney leaned forward. The movement brought the coal-dust smell of his coat across the desk—damp wool and something acrid, the residue of a city that burned fuel the way a furnace consumes air. He looked at the page, then at Ada, then back at the page.

“Is it possible, Miss Quill, that Griggs lit the lamps, signed the sheet, and has now decided he’d rather not have done so?”

“The return sheet bears his signature. I compared it to eleven previous sheets. The hand is consistent.”

“Consistent hands can still sign false things.”

“Consistent hands can also sign true things. The question is which occurred, and the ledger cannot answer that alone. It can only tell us what was recorded, when, and by whose authority. What it records is that those three lamps were lit and that Thomas Griggs recorded them as lit.”

“And Griggs says otherwise.” Varney tapped the open page with a blunt finger. The nail left no mark, but a faint bloom of coal dust transferred from his skin to the paper, a grey smudge spreading across the column where Ada’s figures stood in their careful rows. “See? Ink. Paper. A man’s word against it.”

Ada looked at the smudge. It sat on the figure 6: 42, partially obscuring the four. Her hand moved toward the pen stand, then stopped, the fingers curling back into her palm. She set the hand flat on the desk instead, pressing the knuckles white.

“When did you speak with Mr. Griggs?” she asked.

“Yesterday afternoon. At the yard.” Varney settled back in the chair. “He was trimming a wick when I arrived. Seemed glad enough to talk, at first. Then I mentioned the entries, and he put down the scissors very carefully and said he hadn’t lit those lamps. Said he hadn’t been on Commercial Road that night at all. Said the ledger was wrong.”

“That is not for me to determine. My responsibility is to the record. The record states what it states.”

“And if the record is wrong?”

The question landed on the desk between them like a stone dropped into still water. Ada’s hand rested on the edge of the Company’s ledger, her fingertips pressing the page flat against the board.

“The record is the record,” she said. “If it is wrong, the question is how it came to be wrong, not whether we should disregard it. Disregarding it would make the entire system of logging meaningless.”

Varney exhaled through his nose. “Miss Quill, I’ve been on the force for nineteen years. In that time, I’ve seen more ledger entries than I care to count. Dockets, charge sheets, property logs, evidence inventories—every one of them written by someone with tidy hands and a clear head, and a good third of them wrong. Not because the clerks were dishonest, mind you. Because someone transposed a number, or read a six for an eight, or wrote down what he thought he saw instead of what was actually there.” He picked up one of his gloves and slapped it lightly against his knee. “Your man Griggs was on a different route that night, you say?”

“The Tuesday sheet assigns him to the southern section.”

“But Griggs says he wasn’t on Commercial Road.”

“He was on the southern section. Commercial Road is within the southern section.”

Varney stopped slapping the glove. “Is it, now.”

“It is. The southern section runs from Aldgate to Whitechapel High Street, south along Commercial Street, and includes the length of Commercial Road as far as the junction with New Road.”

“So the route sheet says he was there, and he says he wasn’t.”

“Yes.”

“And you’ve checked the sheet itself. Not your copy—the original.”

“I have the original. The Tuesday sheet is filed in this office. I transcribe from it directly.”

“Right.” Varney turned back to Ada. “Here’s what I think happened, Miss Quill. Griggs did his round. He lit the lamps. He signed the sheet. You transcribed it faithfully, as you do. Sometime later, Griggs decides—for reasons of his own—that he’d rather not have been on Commercial Road that night. Perhaps he was somewhere else he shouldn’t have been. Perhaps he’s covering for something. Perhaps he simply forgot and, being confronted with the entry, panicked and denied it. Men do that more often than you’d think. The instinct to say ‘not me’ is strong, especially when the alternative is explaining yourself to the police.”

“That is a reasonable interpretation,” Ada said. The words came out steadily enough, though she had to retrieve them from somewhere deeper than her usual speaking voice. “It is not the only one.”

“Give me another.”

“Someone else lit those lamps and signed the sheet using Mr. Griggs’s initials. The handwriting on the return sheet is consistent with his, but I am not a handwriting expert. A qualified examiner might—““A qualified examiner.” Varney repeated the phrase as though tasting it and finding it bland. “You want us to send a return sheet from a gas company’s daily log to the document bureau at the Yard. Over three lamps that were lit or weren’t lit on a Tuesday in October.”

The question of whether three public lamps were lit is a matter of public record and public expense. The gas consumed by those lamps was metered. When the lamps were lit, gas was consumed. If the meter does not reflect that consumption, then either the meter is wrong or the gas was diverted. If the lamps were not lit, then the entries are false, and the question is who falsified them and why.

Varney stared at her for a long moment. Then he laughed—a short, dry sound, like a door closing.

“Miss Quill. You’re a clerk. A good one, by the look of it—tidy desk, clean ledger, everything in its place. But you’re a clerk, not a detective, and this is not a detective’s case. This is a clerical matter. A man signed a sheet. The sheet may or may not be accurate. The man says it isn’t. You say it is. The difference between your position and his is a matter of ink and memory, and neither one of them is a criminal matter.”

“If the entries were falsified, that is a criminal matter.”

“If.” Varney stood. The chair scraped back. “If is a large word, Miss Quill. It contains every possibility and proves none of them. I’ve spoken to Griggs. He denies the work. I’ve looked at your ledger. It records the work. Somewhere between the two, there is a mistake—either Griggs is mistaken about where he was on October the fourteenth, or you are mistaken about what you transcribed. Either way, it is a matter for your company’s internal discipline, not for the Metropolitan Police.”

He picked up his gloves. As he pulled the right one on, he glanced down at the open ledger page. The coal-dust smudge had spread slightly, the grey powder sitting on the paper like a thin film of soot on a windowsill. He did not remark on it.

“I’ll file a report noting the discrepancy and the company’s internal handling of it. If your supervisor wishes to pursue the matter further, he knows where to find us.” He moved toward the door. “And Miss Quill—“Ada looked up.

“Don’t make trouble over a misplaced decimal.”

The doorway filled and then emptied. His footsteps receded down the corridor—heavy and even, unhurried, the gait of a man who had closed a matter and felt no need to glance back. The outer door opened and closed. The fog muffled everything after that.

Ada sat in the chair. The office was quiet. The high window let in a grey, diffuse light that flattened the surfaces of the desk and made the ledger look like a slab of slate. The coal-dust smudge on the page had settled into the paper’s grain, and when she tilted the ledger, the light caught it faintly—a dull mark, like a thumbprint left by someone who had handled the book carelessly.

She closed the ledger. The brass clasp was cold under her fingers. She pressed it shut and held it, both palms flat on the leather cover, as though the book might open itself again if she let go.

The building settled. A pipe knocked somewhere in the wall. The clock above the door read half past ten. She had been in this office for four years, and in that time she had transcribed every entry without error, had filed every sheet without misplacement, had maintained the record as the record was meant to be maintained. The record was the foundation. If the record could be wrong, then the foundation was not stone but sand, and everything built upon it—the billing, the metering, the routes, the assignments, the entire architecture of the company’s daily operation—was resting on something that might shift.

She drew the ledger toward her across the desk. The leather was warm where her hands had been. She pressed her palm flat on the cover again, harder this time, and held it there.

The clock ticked. The pipe knocked once more. The coal-dust smudge was sealed inside, pressed between the closed pages, invisible now but permanent.
