Chapter 14

The Inquest Announcement

The pattern was complete, and its scale was sobering. Ada sat at her desk in the Gas Light and Coke Company’s clerical room, her own ledger open beside the notebook of copied meter readings, and traced the column of dates she had copied from the Beckton meter logs against the lamplighters’ entries for Commercial Road. Every instance of unlogged lamp usage aligned with a deficit in the district meter readings. Not approximately. Exactly. The gas had gone somewhere, and someone inside the Company had arranged for the books to hide it. The question was no longer whether the discrepancy was real. It was who had the access and the authority to alter both sets of records—and what they had done to Thomas Griggs when he refused to go along with it.

The envelope arrived at half past nine, carried by a boy in a constable’s cast-off coat who left it on the corner of her desk without a word. The seal was red wax stamped with the coroner’s crest, and it sat squarely atop the parallel lines of figures she had been ruling, breaking their order. She did not open it immediately. The morning shift bell had rung, the lamplighters had collected their assignments and departed, and the clerical room had settled into its familiar drone of nib on paper and the occasional cough. Around her, three other clerks worked steadily, heads bent over their own columns. The envelope remained, pale and officious, its presence a small disruption in the morning’s routine.

When she finally broke the seal, the notice inside was brief. The coroner for the Liberty of the Tower had scheduled a public inquest into the death of Thomas Griggs, lamplighter, on the ninth of November, at the Town Hall on Whitechapel Road. Witnesses with relevant evidence were invited to present themselves.

The language was formal, impersonal, and utterly ordinary—the kind of administrative notice she had filed a hundred times without reading. But this one carried Griggs’s name, and in the notes she had found among his things, the week before his body was pulled from the canal, Griggs had insisted the ledger entry for October fourteenth was false.

He had not lit those three lamps on Commercial Road. The log said he had. The meter at Beckton said the gas had not passed through the district main that night in the quantities the ledger claimed. Both could not be true, and Griggs was dead.

She read the notice twice, folded it, and placed it inside the notebook. Then she returned to the day’s work, ruling columns, entering the previous evening’s meter readings from the district stations, checking each figure against its counterpart in the supply ledger. The pen moved steadily. The numbers fell into their columns. But her mind kept returning to the ninth of November—eleven days from now, a public room, a coroner with the authority to ask questions that the police had refused to ask.

Varney arrived at half past ten.

The door to the clerical room opened without announcement, and his tall, narrow figure filled the frame for a moment before he stepped inside. His coat was damp at the shoulders, and the smell of wet wool preceded him across the floor. He carried no files. His hands were empty, which meant this was not an interview. It was a visit.

“Miss Quill.” His voice carried its usual murmuring quality, the kind that forced a listener to lean forward. “I hope I’m not disturbing your routine.”

“Not at all, Inspector.” She set her pen down precisely parallel to the ledger’s edge. “The morning figures are nearly entered.”

“Good. Good.” He did not sit. The chair beside her desk remained unused. Instead, he stood close enough that his shadow fell across the open page, his finger extending to tap the wood near the column where Griggs’s name would have appeared, had this been the October roster. The thud was dull, measured, almost conversational. “I’ve come about the inquest. The coroner has seen fit to open a public hearing into Griggs’s death.”

“I received the notice this morning.”

“Then you know it’s a formality. The coroner will hear evidence, consider the circumstances, and return a verdict consistent with what we already understand. A tragic accident. A man who drank too much and wandered into the canal in the dark.” Varney’s finger tapped again, a rhythm that matched nothing in the room. “These things happen, Miss Quill. The river police pull bodies out every week.”

Ada looked at the finger, then at his face. His expression was designed to discourage prolonged conversation—the flat mouth, the heavy brow, the eyes that offered nothing and expected nothing in return. But he had come here himself. No constable had been sent with a message. He had walked from Leman Street to the Company offices on a wet morning to deliver this personally.

“The notice invites witnesses with relevant evidence,” she said.

“It does. Standard language. The coroner’s court is a public forum, and anyone may attend.” Varney withdrew his hand from the desk and placed it in his coat pocket. “But attending and testifying are different matters. The coroner will call witnesses he deems necessary. Others may volunteer, but the court is not obliged to hear them.”

“On what basis does the coroner determine relevance?”

Varney’s mouth tightened. The question was procedural, and he answered it procedurally, but his weight shifted from one foot to the other—a small, telltale movement. “On the basis of direct knowledge of the deceased’s movements, the circumstances of death, or any medical evidence. The court will not entertain speculation about company records or meter readings. That is not its purpose.”

“If a witness had documentary evidence—records that contradict the official account of the evening in question—would the coroner be obliged to consider it?”

The room was quiet except for the scratch of pens from the other clerks, who had not looked up but whose nibs had slowed. Varney studied Ada for a long moment. His jaw worked, the muscle tightening once beneath the skin before he spoke again.

“The coroner’s court examines how a man died, Miss Quill. Not how gas was distributed on a particular evening. If you were to appear and attempt to introduce company ledgers into a proceeding about a drowning, the coroner would likely rule you out of order. You would have travelled across London for nothing.”

“But if the ledgers established that the deceased was not where the official record places him—if they showed he had been given a different route, in a different district, on the night he died—would that not bear directly on how he came to be at the canal?”

Varney’s hand came out of his pocket. He placed it flat on the desk, fingers spread, and leaned forward until his face was level with hers. The wool smell was stronger now, mixed with something stale—tobacco, perhaps, or the damp that clung to the walls of the Company office.

“You are a clerk, Miss Quill. Your expertise is in maintaining records. The court will have no use for your interpretation of those records. If the Company wishes to submit documentation, it will do so through proper channels, through its solicitors, not through a clerk who has taken it upon herself to investigate a matter that has already been settled.”

“The matter was settled before I raised it. I was not consulted.”

“And you will not be. The verdict is accidental death. The evidence supports it. The body was recovered from the canal. There was a bruise on the temple, consistent with a fall. The man had been drinking.” Each sentence landed with the flat certainty of a door closing. “There is nothing more to investigate.”

Ada held his gaze. The other clerks’ pens had stopped entirely now, though no one looked up. The silence in the room had the texture of held breath.

“If the court rules my evidence irrelevant, I will have lost nothing but a morning,” she said. “If the court finds it relevant, the verdict may change. That is the court’s function, not mine.”

Varney straightened. His hand lifted from the desk, and he brushed the surface lightly, as though smoothing an invisible crease. The gesture was careful, deliberate—a man composing himself before he spoke again.

“You should consider,” he said quietly, “that the Company has been very patient. Your position here depends on the goodwill of your superiors. If you were to embarrass the Company at a public inquest—to imply, however indirectly, that its records were unreliable, or that its employees were involved in something irregular—that goodwill would not survive. I am not threatening you, Miss Quill. I am advising you, as one professional to another, that some doors, once opened, cannot be closed again.”

“Is that advice, or a warning?”

“It is the same thing, in my experience.”

He turned and walked to the door. At the threshold, he paused and looked back. The flat mouth had shifted—not into a smile, but into something harder, a tightening that pulled the lines of his face into a configuration she had not seen before. His eyes moved from her face to the desk, to the ledger, to the satchel that hung from the chair back. Then he left, closing the door behind him with a quiet click.

The room exhaled. One of the other clerks—a young man named Peters, who worked on the supply accounts—coughed delicately and resumed his writing. The nib-scratch filled the silence. Ada sat motionless for a count of ten, then picked up her pen and continued her column.

But the figures would not settle. She entered the same reading twice, caught the error, and corrected it with a careful strike of the pen through the duplicate. Her hand was steady. The entries were correct. But beneath the surface of the morning’s routine, something had shifted, and the shift had Varney’s name on it. He had not come to inform her. He had come to warn her. And the warning had revealed something he perhaps had not intended to reveal—that the inquest frightened him. Not the verdict, which he clearly considered fixed, but the forum itself. A public room. A coroner with questions. Witnesses who could not be managed or dismissed as easily as a clerk in a back office.

She finished the morning’s entries, closed the company ledger, and placed it in its designated drawer. The drawer locked with a small brass key she returned to her pocket. Then she opened the satchel and took out her notebook.

The pages were filled with her own careful hand—meter readings copied from Beckton, lamplighter rosters transcribed from the weekly sheets, dates aligned and cross-referenced. Three lamps on Commercial Road, logged as lit on October fourteenth. Thomas Griggs’s signature in the ledger, attesting to the round. Griggs had denied lighting them. He had denied it to her face, in this building, three days before his body was found. And the roster—the original roster, not the amended version that appeared in the ledger the following week—had placed him south of the river that evening, walking a circuit nowhere near Commercial Road.

Someone had changed the roster after the fact. Griggs’s signature—or a convincing imitation of it—had been entered into the ledger for a round he had not walked. And someone had adjusted the Beckton meter readings to mask the gas that had flowed through pipes it was not supposed to flow through. The pattern was not a clerical error. It was a system, operated by someone with access to both sets of records, someone who understood the metering machinery well enough to know exactly how much to alter and when.

She turned to a fresh page and began to organize. The notebook would not be enough. The coroner would want documents—original records, not copies. She would need the October roster, the one that showed Griggs assigned south of the river. The amended version in the company ledger placed him on Commercial Road, but the original, if it still existed in the lamplighters’ assignment sheets, would contradict it. She would need the Beckton meter logs for the dates in question, and the district supply readings that showed the deficits. And she would need to present them in a way that a layman—a coroner, a jury of shopkeepers and tradesmen—could follow without technical knowledge.

The pen moved down the page. She listed the documents in order of relevance: the original October roster, the amended ledger entry, the Beckton meter readings for October fourteenth and the six corresponding dates, the district supply logs, the lamplighters’ round sheets. Beside each, she noted where the document was stored and whether she could obtain a copy legitimately. The roster sheets were kept in the lamplighters’ yard, filed by week. The meter logs were at Beckton, but Rook had already copied the relevant readings. The district supply logs were in this building, in the cabinet behind Finch’s desk.

Finch. The name stopped her pen. Finch had been nervous about the ledgers—fidgeting with his pen, watching her when he thought she was not looking, hovering over the records cabinet as though guarding something. If Finch was involved, or if he suspected what she suspected, approaching the supply logs would alert him. And if Finch was not involved, but someone above him was, then Finch’s nervousness was the nervousness of a man who had been told to keep things quiet—a man who knew enough to be afraid but not enough to act.

She would need to be careful. The inquest was eleven days away. Eleven days to gather the documents, prepare the evidence, and find a way to present it that the coroner could not dismiss. Varney would be there. He would object, deflect, invoke procedure. She would need to be precise, patient, and absolutely certain of every figure she put before the court.

The notebook filled. Page after page of dates, readings, cross-references, and document locations. When she reached the last relevant entry—the Beckton meter reading for the night Griggs died—she stopped. The reading was low. Lower than the corresponding district ledger claimed. The same pattern, repeated one final time, on the night a man who had refused to sign a false ledger entry was found in the canal with a bruise on his temple.

She closed the notebook and placed it in the satchel. The coroner’s notice sat inside, folded once, its red seal broken. The ninth of November. Whitechapel Road. A public room where the truth, if she could lay it out clearly enough, would have nowhere to hide.

The morning’s work was done. She picked up her pen and began the afternoon’s ruling, the pencil lines going down the page straight and even, preparing the columns for the figures that would fill them. Inside the satchel, the notebook waited.

A knock at the glass partition broke the rhythm of her ruling. Varney stood in the doorway, jacket on, hat in hand, files tucked beneath one arm—the posture of a man making one final effort before washing his hands of the matter.

“Miss Quill.” He did not enter. “You’ve had your morning to think. I’ll be plain with you, Miss Quill. Plain enough that even you can’t mistake it. A clerk employed by the Gas Light and Coke Company walks into a police station with accusations against that same company, and what does she imagine happens next? A commendation?” He turned his hat slowly. “There are people who will not thank you for dragging a dead man’s name into a public proceeding. People with the means to make your continued employment—your continued presence in certain rooms—very difficult.”

Ada set her pen down. “Inspector, what is the proper procedure for submitting documentary evidence to a coroner’s court? Does it require a covering schedule, or may one submit primary ledgers directly? And secondly—if a witness has reason to believe a death was materially connected to a fraud she has documented, does the coroner have the authority to compel further police investigation, or must that come through the Director of Public Prosecutions?”

The hat stopped turning. Varney’s jaw worked once, as if chewing something that refused to soften. “You’re not listening to me.”

“I am listening very carefully. I am asking you specific questions.”

He glanced around the Company’s archive—her territory, not his—and leaned forward, his voice dropping to something almost private. “A public inquiry—that’s what you’re courting. You understand what that means? Not a quiet word in the right ear. A room full of strangers, press, solicitors, every discrepancy picked apart by people who don’t know a gas meter from a drainpipe. If there are irregularities, that is where they will be found, Miss Quill, and not everyone who is found will be guilty of anything worse than proximity.” He straightened. “Think about who you’re protecting. And who you’re not.”

He left without waiting for an answer.

Ada picked up her pen. The pencil lines continued down the page, straight and even. But the columns she ruled now were not for the company’s figures. She titled the first page in small, precise letters: Schedule of Discrepancies — Beckton and District Meter Readings, July–October. Beneath it, a second line: Prepared for the Coroner’s Court, 9 November.

Griggs had refused to sign. That refusal had cost him his life. She would not let the silence cost her her purpose.
