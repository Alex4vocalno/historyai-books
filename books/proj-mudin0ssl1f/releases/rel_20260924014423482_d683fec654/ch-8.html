Chapter 8

Rook's Proposition

The fog had thinned to a damp residue on the stone steps of Leman Street station, and Ada Quill walked south with the careful, measured pace of someone carrying an injury no one could see. Her satchel hung heavy against her hip. Inside it, the ledger and the Tuesday sheet and her own notebook pressed together like arguments waiting to be restated. She did not look back at the station’s facade. The building behind her had already said everything it intended to say.

The route to the engineering offices took her along the river road, past the wharves where coal barges sat low in the water, their decks black with wet dust. Men moved between the crates without speaking, their lanterns throwing short columns of light that the fog swallowed before they reached the ground. The smell of the river came up thick and mineral—wet rope, tar, the sour breath of the tide. She walked past all of it. Varney’s voice stayed with her—not the words themselves, which had been forgettable in their official blandness, but the tone, the way he had murmured as though reading from a script he found personally tedious. She had presented a sequence. He had presented a door.

The engineering offices occupied the ground floor of a brick annex behind the main Gas Light and Coke Company yard. She entered through the side door, the one the fitters used, and passed through the short corridor that smelled of coal dust and machine oil. The lamplighters’ locker room was at the far end. She had arranged to meet Rook there at half past six, when the evening shift had gone and the room would be empty.

He was already inside, sitting on the long wooden bench that ran between the rows of iron lockers. His hands rested on his knees, the grease stains on his fingers dark against the fabric of his trousers. A folded sheaf of paper lay on the bench beside him. He looked up when she entered but did not stand.

She closed the door behind her. The latch clicked, and the sound echoed off the white tile walls with a clarity that made the room feel smaller than it was.

“Miss Quill.”

“Mr. Rook.” She crossed to the bench and set her satchel down. Her fingers went to the clasp, opened it, and withdrew the folded ledger page—the copy she had made of the Commercial Road entry for October the fourteenth, the one that recorded three lamps lit by Thomas Griggs on a route he had been assigned to, on a route he swore he never walked. She placed it on the bench between them.

“This is yours,” she said.

Rook looked at the page. He did not pick it up.

“I transcribed it accurately. Every figure corresponds to the master ledger entry. If you need it for your report to the engineering office, it is complete.”

“You’re giving it back.”

“I am returning it to the department that generated it. The investigation is closed. Detective Inspector Varney has classified the matter as a clerical error, and the company has been informed.”

Rook’s eyes moved from the page to her face. The locker room was silent except for the faint drip of water from a tap in the corner, where someone had left the valve imperfectly turned.

“You went to Leman Street.”

I went to Leman Street with the meter readings you helped me copy. I presented the discrepancy between the Beckton district meter and the logged lamp hours, along with Griggs’s denial. I presented the dates.

“And?”

“And I was told that a lamplighter’s denial is not evidence, that a meter reading can be explained by mechanical variance, and that the Metropolitan Police do not investigate clerical errors on behalf of private companies.” She paused. “He also suggested that my continued interest in the matter might reach the attention of my superiors.”

Rook exhaled slowly through his nose. His hand went to the folded sheaf of paper beside him, and he picked it up, turning it in his fingers.

“Griggs is dead,” Ada said. Her voice was level, each word placed with the precision she applied to ledger entries. “He was found in Regent’s Canal nine days ago. The police have ruled it an accidental drowning. I am a clerk, Mr. Rook. I transcribe figures. I do not investigate murders, and I cannot pursue a matter that the police have formally closed and my employer has no interest in reopening.”

“You think that’s what this is? You think Griggs slipped into the canal?”

“I think it does not matter what I think. I think that Detective Inspector Varney was precise on that point as well.”

Rook unfolded the sheaf of paper he was holding. He laid it flat on the bench, smoothing the creases with the side of his hand. The page was covered in his handwriting—dense columns of figures, each row dated, each followed by a pair of numbers separated by a line.

“Beckton gasworks,” he said. “Main distribution meter, weekly readings. I copied these from the engineering office last Tuesday, before you went to Varney. Look at the right-hand column. That’s the gas delivered, in cubic feet, recorded at the Beckton master meter.”

Ada looked. The figures were neatly arranged, each corresponding to a week in the previous three months. She read them the way she read any column of numbers—top to bottom, comparing each entry to the one before it.

“The left column is what the district ledgers say was consumed,” Rook said. “Lamp hours, domestic supply, industrial contracts. All of it added up and recorded as total district usage.”

Ada’s finger stopped on the third row. The right-hand number was larger than the left. She moved to the fourth row. The same. The fifth. The same.

“There’s a gap,” she said.

“There’s a gap every week. The meter at Beckton records more gas leaving the works than the district ledgers account for. Every week, for three months. The deficit runs between four and seven hundred cubic feet per week. That is not mechanical variance. A meter drifts five, maybe ten cubic feet in a season. This is consistent, it is systematic, and it tracks exactly to the weeks where your lamplighters’ ledger shows unlogged lamp hours on Commercial Road.”

Ada stared at the page. Her own notebook was in her satchel. She could see, in the neat rows of Rook’s handwriting, the same pattern she had found in the lamplighters’ ledger—except larger, broader, and far more damning. The three lamps on Commercial Road were not an anomaly. They were a fragment of a system.

“Griggs told me the ledger was wrong,” she said quietly. “He said they put him on another route that night. He said he never lit those lamps.”

“I believe him.”

“Then someone entered his name on the Commercial Road page. Someone who had access to the lamplighters’ ledger and knew the route assignments.”

“Someone who also has access to the Beckton meter room,” Rook said. “Because the meter logs don’t get adjusted by accident. Those pages are kept in the engineering office, in the locked cabinet. I have one of two keys. The other belongs to the senior inspector.”

“You’re saying the meter readings have been altered.”

Rook tapped the right-hand column. His finger rested on the figure for the week of October the fourteenth—the same week as the impossible ledger entry. The number was clean, uncorrected, and it exceeded the district total by six hundred and twelve cubic feet.

“I’m saying the meter read what it read. The Beckton meter is a mechanical instrument. It counts the gas that passes through it. It does not make clerical errors, and it does not lie. What was altered is the district ledger—the consumption side, not the delivery side. Someone reduced the recorded usage to match the deficit, so the weekly totals would balance and no one would ask where the missing gas went.”

Ada sat back on the bench. The tile walls of the locker room seemed closer than they had been a moment ago. The dripping tap counted seconds in the silence.

“That is theft,” she said.

“That is theft on a scale that’s been running for three months, minimum. Maybe longer. I only copied back to July.”

“Who has access to the district consumption ledger?”

“You do. Finch does. The senior clerks on each district rotation.”

“And the meter room?”

“Engineering staff. Two keys, like I said.”

The intersection was narrow. It required someone with access to both the clerical records and the engineering office, or two people working together. Ada’s mind moved through the possibilities the way it moved through a column of figures—methodically, eliminating the impossible, retaining what remained.

“Finch has been nervous,” she said. “He asked me twice last week whether I had found errors in the October pages. He never asks about specific weeks. He reviews the quarterly totals, not the daily entries.”

“Finch doesn’t have a key to the meter room.”

“No. But Finch has access to every ledger in the clerking office. He could alter the consumption figures. He could not alter the Beckton delivery record.”

Rook was quiet for a moment. He folded his hands on his knee, the calluses catching the light from the single gas lamp that hung above the lockers.

“So we have two records,” he said. “One set has been tampered with. One has not. The delivery meter at Beckton is the one thing that can’t be faked, because it’s a physical count. Gas goes through the pipe, the wheel turns, the number goes up. You can write whatever you like in a ledger. You can’t make a meter lie.”

Ada looked at the folded Commercial Road page still lying on the bench between them. She had placed it there meaning to leave it. The paper sat on the dark wood, its creases sharp, its contents a record she had transcribed with her own hand from a ledger she had trusted without question for six years.

“Varney told me that if I continued, he would speak to the company directly,” she said. “He said it would be unfortunate if the company were to discover that one of its clerks was conducting an unsanctioned inquiry into matters that had been formally resolved.”

“And you believe he’d do it.”

“I believe he has no reason not to. I am a clerk. I have no standing to make accusations against the company or the police. If Varney contacts the company, I lose my position. If I lose my position, I lose access to the records.”

“Then we don’t make accusations. We build a case. Quietly. The meter logs, the lamplighters’ ledger, the route assignments—everything that shows the gap between what was delivered and what was recorded. We compile it. We date it. We make it irrefutable. And then we decide who to show it to.”

“And if whoever altered the ledger learns we are looking?”

Rook picked up the Commercial Road page. He held it out to her.

“Griggs knew something was wrong. He told you the ledger was false. A week later he was in the canal. You can walk out of this room and back to your duties, Miss Quill. Copy figures and pretend the numbers balance. But the next time someone notices a discrepancy, it won’t be you. It’ll be someone who doesn’t know what to look for. And the gas will keep disappearing, and the ledgers will keep lying, and nobody will ask why a man who told the truth ended up drowned.”

Ada took the page from his hand. The paper was warm from his grip. She folded it once, precisely along the original crease, and placed it inside her notebook. She closed the notebook and put it in her satchel. Her hand lingered on the leather flap for a moment before she pressed the clasp shut.

“The Beckton delivery records,” she said. “Can you copy more of them? Back to January, if they exist.”

“They exist. I can get them.”

“And the district consumption ledgers for the same period—I can access those from the clerking office. Finch leaves at five on Wednesdays. The office is empty until the night porter comes at seven.”

Rook nodded. He stood, and the bench creaked faintly under the shifting weight. He gathered his own copies of the Beckton readings and folded them into the inside pocket of his jacket.

“Wednesday, then,” he said. “You take the consumption side. I’ll take the delivery records. We compare on Thursday.”

“Thursday,” Ada repeated. She picked up her satchel and stood. The locker room was still empty, still silent except for the tap. The gas lamp above them hissed softly, its flame steady behind its wire cage.

She walked to the door. Her hand closed around the iron latch, and she paused. The tile walls reflected the lamplight in a dull, pale wash. The room smelled of coal dust and old wool and the particular staleness of a space that held nothing but empty lockers and the residue of the day’s labor.

“Mr. Rook.”

He turned.

“Griggs said the ledger was wrong. He was specific about it. He said October the fourteenth, Commercial Road, three lamps. He said his name was on the page and he did not light them. He was frightened when he told me. He was not a man given to exaggeration.”

“No,” Rook said. “He wasn’t.”

Ada opened the door. The corridor beyond was dim and empty. She stepped through, and her footsteps echoed on the stone floor with a steadiness that had not been there when she had walked in from the river road an hour before. The satchel hung at her side, heavier now by a single folded page and the weight of what it carried. She did not look back.
