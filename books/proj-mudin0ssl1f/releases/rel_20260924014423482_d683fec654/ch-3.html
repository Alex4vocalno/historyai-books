Chapter 3

Ledgers and Lamplighters

Varney’s voice sat in her skull like a stone dropped into still water — don’t make trouble over a misplaced decimal — and the ripples had not stopped.

Ada stood at the archive shelf with her hand on the cloth-bound spine of the shift log, her fingers pressing the rough weave as though the book might tell her something the words inside it would not. The gas mantle at the end of the corridor threw a thin, jaundiced light that reached the shelf but did not warm it. The leather was cool and dry under her palm. She pulled the volume free. It was heavier than she expected — eleven years of lamplighter assignments compressed into cloth and board — and she braced it against her hip, closing the archive door with her shoulder. The latch caught with a flat, decisive click that carried down the empty corridor.

Twenty minutes past six. The other clerks had gone. Finch had locked his office and left without speaking to her, which was not unusual but which tonight had the quality of a door closing on something.

She carried the log to her desk and set it beside the open ledger. The main lamplighters’ ledger, which she maintained daily, lay on the left. The shift log, older and thicker, sat on the right, its pages soft and dark at the edges from the oils of hands that had set it on its shelf every evening for over a decade. She opened it to the first week of October. The pages gave off a smell like dry bread and old dust, and the binding cracked softly as it fell open. Her pen lay in the inkwell. She drew it out, wiped the nib on the cloth, and set a fresh sheet of scrap paper beside the ledger. The gas mantle above her desk hummed its thin, constant note. The light turned the ink to amber and shadow.

She had chosen the last six months deliberately. The older archives were kept in a separate store across the yard, locked and inaccessible after the office closed, but that was not the only reason. The Commercial Road entry that had started all of this was dated October. If there was a pattern, it would be in the months surrounding that entry — close enough to trace, recent enough that the shift logs had not yet been moved to the remote store.

October third. She placed her finger on the first entry in the ledger — Whitechapel northern section, lamps fourteen through twenty-two, lit by J. Harkness at five forty-two p. m. — and moved it across to the corresponding page in the shift log. Harkness, J. — northern section — 5: 40. The times differed by two minutes. She noted it on the scrap, the pen scratching the figure in a small, precise hand. Two minutes was nothing. Lamplighters did not carry watches synchronized to the second. She turned the page.

October fourth. Southern section, Griggs, T. — Commercial Road — 5: 45. The ledger entry read five forty-three. She noted the two-minute variance and continued. October fifth. Northern section, Harkness again, but the shift log recorded him on the eastern section. A cross-assignment, or a logging error. She noted it and moved on. October sixth, seventh, eighth. The entries matched within a minute or two. The pen moved down the scrap in a column of small, tidy figures. The gas mantle hummed. The room was very quiet.

Her eyes ached. She rubbed the right one with the heel of her hand, smudging the faint ink stain that lived permanently on her forefinger, and blinked at the page. She had been at this for the better part of two hours. She had worked through the first week of October and continued, page by page, into the second. October ninth, tenth, eleventh — more small discrepancies, the kind any audit would surface. A lamp count off by one, corrected in pencil. A route abbreviation that did not match the official format. A signature omitted on October ninth, then added in a different ink the following day, as though someone had remembered after the fact.

She noted each one, the column on the scrap growing longer, but none of them had the clean, surgical quality of the Commercial Road entry. None of them showed a location recorded in the ledger that did not appear on the daily sheet. None of them showed a blank where a name should be. They were the ordinary sediment of daily bookkeeping — plausible, explicable, unremarkable. She kept going.

October twelfth. Southern section, Griggs again. The ledger recorded three lamps lit on Cable Street at five fifty-one p. m. Her finger tracked across to the shift log. The entry read: Griggs, T., southern section, Commercial Road, 5: 50. Cable Street was not listed. The route column showed Commercial Road, not Cable Street. The signature line at the bottom of the log entry bore Griggs’s mark, a rough, heavy T and G pressed into the paper, but the route and the location did not agree.

Ada’s finger stopped. It rested on the Cable Street line in the ledger. The entry was clean, the ink steady, the figures precise. Three lamps, five fifty-one p. m., Cable Street. Written in her own hand, four days after the fact, transcribed from the daily lamplighter’s sheet that Griggs had submitted. She remembered the sheet. She remembered the careful, slanting letters. She did not remember questioning them. She had copied what was written.

The gas mantle hummed above her. The light pooled on the page, turning the blank lamplighter’s column beside the Cable Street entry a pale, luminous nothing. The column where a signature should confirm the route was empty. Not smudged, not partially erased. Simply blank, as though the hand that wrote the entry had lifted the pen and set it down without completing the record. Her index finger, smudged with ink, pressed into the paper. The fibre gave slightly under the pressure. She could feel the grain of it, the faint tooth of the surface catching against the callus on her fingertip. The entry sat in its row, perfect and damning, the blank space beside it wider than any gap a careless clerk would leave. Someone had written the location and the count and the time and then stopped. The pen had been lifted. The signature had not come.

She checked the shift log again. Griggs, T., southern section, Commercial Road, 5: 50. No mention of Cable Street. No second entry. No corrected or supplementary sheet filed behind it. She checked the daily lamplighter’s sheet for October twelfth, which she kept in the folder at the back of the ledger. There it was. Griggs’s handwriting, the same slanting letters, the same heavy pressure: Commercial Road, three lamps, five fifty. Cable Street did not appear on the daily sheet. It appeared only in the ledger, in her hand, and nowhere else.

The room was very quiet. The gas mantle hummed its thin note. The smell of ink and old paper and the faintly metallic tang of the gas line above her head sat in the air. She pressed her finger harder into the blank space, as though pressure might conjure a name from the paper. It did not.

She pulled the scrap of paper closer. She wrote, in her precise hand: October 12. Cable Street. Three lamps, 5: 51 p. m. No corresponding shift-log entry. No lamplighter signature. Daily sheet records Commercial Road, not Cable Street.

She folded the scrap once, twice, and slipped it into the pocket of her jacket. The paper sat against her hip, a small, sharp rectangle. She closed the shift log and stacked it on the ledger. She would need to come back to both. She would need to check every entry, every route, every signature, going back as far as the records allowed.

But first she needed to understand what she was looking at. The Commercial Road anomaly (three lamps logged as lit, Griggs swearing he was not on that round, Varney reporting that Griggs denied the work, then calling it a clerical error) was not alone. Cable Street sat in the same ledger, in the same hand, with the same absence where a name should be. Two instances in twelve days. She would not call it a pattern yet, not from two data points, but the shape of the second absence was identical to the first.

The gas mantle hummed above her. She stood for a moment with her hand on the stacked books, looking down at the blank column where the gas light pooled, and then she gathered her bag, checked that the inkwell was capped, and left the office.

The corridor was long and dim. The gas lamp at the far end burned low, its flame a small blue tongue behind smoked glass. Her shoes sounded flat and measured on the boards. At the end of the corridor, the door to the yard stood ajar. She pushed it open and stepped out onto the cobbles, which were damp and gave back the cold in a steady wave that climbed from the soles of her boots to her knees. The air smelled of coal smoke and iron and the river.

The lamplighters’ yard was empty. The benches stood in their row, the tools hung on their pegs, the lanterns sat dark in their rack. Griggs’s bench was at the far end, the wood worn smooth and pale where he sat to trim his wicks. The small scissors lay on the seat where he had left them. She did not go near it.

The gate to the street was open. The night porter had propped it with a brick and retired to his box. She walked through and turned east, toward the engineering works. The yard was behind the main offices, separated by a wall of soot-blackened brick. A door in the wall led to the lane where the company’s engineering workshop sat, a low, shed-like building with a single gas lamp burning in the window.

She had been inside it once before, last week, when she noticed the gas-flow readings on her ledger did not match the consumption figures she had glimpsed on a worksheet tucked under the foreman’s arm. She had gone looking for the source of the readings and found Rook, the junior engineer, a young man with grease under his nails and a habit of looking at the floor when he spoke. He had been precise and quiet and had answered her questions without asking any of his own.

When she had pressed him on the discrepancy between the meter readings and the logged entries, he had gone still, then said, almost to himself, that the numbers on the worksheet were not the numbers the meter showed. He had said it in the tone of a man who had been waiting for someone to notice. She had left that conversation with a direction, and tonight she needed to know if the readings he had shown her were part of the same fault line she had found in the ledger.

The workshop door was unlocked. She pushed it open and stood in the doorway. The interior was warm and close, the air thick with the smell of tallow and machine oil and the dry, hot mineral scent of a forge that had been banked but not cooled. A single gas mantle burned at the back, throwing a hard, yellow light across the workbenches. Tools hung on pegboards along the walls: spanners, calipers, pipe wrenches, their handles dark with use. A bench vise sat open, its jaws wide, a length of lead pipe clamped in its grip. The floor was stone, gritty with metal filings that caught the light. Against the far wall, a rack of pipe sections stood like organ pipes, their cut ends showing bright silver against the dark patina of the rest.

A gauge, a brass gas-flow meter of the kind she had seen in the meter houses, sat on the nearest bench, its glass face reflecting the mantle’s flame. A small notebook lay open beside it, its pages covered in tight, mechanical drawings and columns of figures that were not hers.

The workshop was empty. Rook was not there. The bench where he worked held the evidence of recent labor — a half-assembled valve, a pot of tallow with the knife still in it, a rag folded neatly beside the brass gauge. He had been here. He had left. The notebook lay open, as though he had stepped away and intended to return. She stood in the doorway with her bag on her shoulder and the folded scrap of paper in her jacket pocket, pressing against her hip.

She crossed to the bench. The notebook’s pages showed columns of figures, pressure readings, pipe diameters, flow rates, in a hand that was small and square and bore no resemblance to hers. Beside the figures, small annotations in the same hand noted locations: Commercial Road, main feeder, 0.4 bar. Cable Street, branch line, 0.2 bar. She read the words. The gas mantle hummed above her, the same thin note as the one in her office, as though the whole building breathed on the same frequency. The names of the streets sat on the page beside technical figures she did not fully understand. Commercial Road and Cable Street, side by side, connected by a pipe system that fed gas to the lamps she logged every day.

She had never thought about the pipes. The lamps were lit, the gas flowed, the entries were made. What happened between the meter and the flame was not her concern. It was not in the ledger.

She reached into her pocket and drew out the folded scrap. She unfolded it, smoothing the creases with her thumb, and set it on the bench beside the open notebook. Her handwriting, October 12. Cable Street. Three lamps, 5: 51 p. m. No corresponding shift-log entry. No lamplighter signature., sat next to Rook’s tight, square figures. The two sets of words did not explain each other. But they occupied the same surface now, and the names matched. She looked at the bench, the gauge, the notebook, the scrap. The gas mantle hummed. The forge ticked as it cooled, a small, irregular sound in the warm, oily air. She folded the scrap again and put it back in her pocket.

She had no business being here. The workshop was not her domain, and the notebook was not her record. But the scrap was hers, and what it described was hers, and the ledger it had been copied from was hers. She had copied the entry in good faith. The entry was wrong. Something had entered the ledger that did not belong to the world the ledger was meant to describe: a lamp lit on a street the lamplighter did not walk, at a time the shift log did not record, by a hand that left no name.

She stepped back from the bench. The door was still open behind her, and the cold air from the yard moved across the back of her neck. She turned and walked out. The cobbles were damp under her shoes. The gas lamp in the workshop window threw a rectangle of yellow light across the lane, and she walked through it and into the street beyond.

The pavement lamps were lit. Their flames burned steady behind glass, each one a small, controlled fire fed by a pipe she had never seen, connected to a meter she had never read, in a system she had transcribed for four years without understanding. She walked between them. The scrap of paper sat in her pocket, folded and sharp against her hip. The ledger was locked in her office. The shift log was stacked on her desk. Rook’s notebook lay open on his bench, and he did not know she had been there.

She knew what the next step was. She would bring the gas-flow readings to Rook and lay them beside the ledger entries, and together they would trace the pipe system from the meter to the lamp and find where the record diverged from the physical fact. The Commercial Road entry was not alone. The blank space beside the Cable Street line was the same shape as the blank space beside the Commercial Road line. The shape of an absence, once you had seen it twice, was harder to call an accident.

The street stretched ahead of her, lamp after lamp, each one lit and steady and accounted for in the ledger she kept. Or perhaps not accounted for. Perhaps the ledger contained other streets, other lamps, other blank spaces she had not yet found. Six months of entries. One hundred and eighty-three days. She had audited twelve. Two confirmed anomalies, the same kind, the same structure, the same absence. She would not yet call it systemic. Two data points could be coincidence. But two identical absences, twelve days apart, in the same hand, on connected streets, was enough to know it was not a clerical error. If there were two, how many more were there? The question walked with her down the pavement, past the lit lamps and the dark ones, past the closed shopfronts and the locked doors, and she did not turn around.
