Chapter 7

Varney's Stone Wall

If gas was lit but the meter did not turn, then someone had built a way around the meter — and that someone was still doing it, night after night, beneath the streets where the pipes ran. The question Rook had left her with in the meter room was not going to answer itself, and the evidence folded inside her ledger was the only language the authorities would understand.

Ada sat on the bench outside Detective Inspector Varney’s office and spent eleven minutes arranging her materials in the correct order on her lap: the Tuesday sheet first, then the notebook with Rook’s meter readings copied out in her own careful hand, then the lamplighters’ ledger opened to the page that had started all of this. The corridor was tiled in a greenish glaze that caught the overhead gaslight and threw it back in sick, watery patches. A constable passed with a mug of tea, his boots squeaking on the floor, and did not look at her.

The station sergeant had told her to wait. She was good at waiting. Waiting was a large part of keeping records.

When the door opened, Varney did not call her in so much as stand in the gap and look at her until she rose. He was a tall man, narrow through the shoulders, with a face that seemed built for the purpose of discouraging prolonged conversation. His jacket hung on the back of his chair. The room smelled of stale tobacco and ink, and the window behind his desk gave onto a yard where pigeons strutted along a high brick wall.

“Miss Quill,” he said, settling into the chair with a creak of springs. “You’ve been waiting some time.”

“I have materials to present, and I wanted them in order.”

“Materials.” He did not gesture at the chair opposite him, but Ada sat in it anyway, placing the ledger and the notebook and the Tuesday sheet on the edge of his desk, where the lamplight from the bracket above cast a warm circle across the wood. Varney glanced at the papers the way one might glance at a stack of advertising handbills — a brief, automatic inventory followed by complete disinterest.

“The Gas Light and Coke Company sent a letter,” he said. “Two weeks ago. They confirmed the matter of Mr. Griggs was an accident, and that the clerical irregularity in the lamplighters’ ledger has been noted and corrected internally.”

“I am here because it has not been corrected. It has been papered over.”

“And what brings you to Leman Street today, specifically?”

Ada opened the ledger to the marked page. “On the night of October fourteenth, three lamps on Commercial Road were logged as lit. The lamplighter assigned to that route was Thomas Griggs. Mr. Griggs denied lighting those lamps. He stated, on record, that he was assigned to a different section that night — the Whitechapel southern run, Cable Street to Penfold Street. The ledger entry exists, but the man who supposedly made it says he did not.”

“Griggs said a good many things, I understand.”

“He said he did not light those lamps. He said the ledger is wrong. That is not a good many things. That is one thing, stated plainly.”

Varney picked up a pen from his desk and turned it slowly between his fingers, not looking at the ledger. “Griggs is dead, Miss Quill. Drowned. The coroner returned a verdict of accidental death. The case is closed.”

“Griggs is dead, and within a week of telling me the ledger was wrong. I am not asking you to take my word for the connection. I am asking you to look at the meter readings.”

She placed the notebook beside the ledger, open to the page where she had copied Rook’s figures in two parallel columns. “These are the Beckton district meter readings for the Whitechapel southern section, taken twice daily over a six-week period. On the nights when lamps appear in the ledger as lit but the assigned lamplighter denies the work, the district meter shows a drop in consumption. On the nights when the lamps are correctly logged, the meter reads normally. The pattern is consistent across four separate dates.”

Varney looked at the notebook. His eyes moved across the columns. Then he looked at Ada.

“You’ve been spending time in the meter room.”

“With one of the company engineers, yes. These are his readings. I copied them myself.”

“And what do you believe these readings indicate?”

“That gas was consumed on Commercial Road on nights when the meter did not register it. If the lamps were lit, gas flowed through the pipes. If the meter did not turn, then something was diverting the flow around it. That is not a clerical error. That is a physical intervention in the gas main.”

The room was quiet for a moment. A pigeon landed on the window ledge outside, and Varney watched it with more attention than he had watched anything on the desk.

“You’re a clerk,” he said.

“I am a clerk, which is why I noticed the discrepancy in the first place.”

“You’re a clerk who has taken it upon herself to enter the meter room, copy engineering data, and bring it to a police station. Does your supervisor know you’re here?”

“Mr. Griggs is dead. He told me the ledger was wrong, and then he was found in the canal. I am not here because I enjoy entering police stations, Detective Inspector. I am here because a man who insisted on a fact is now dead, and the fact he insisted on has been verified by physical measurement.”

Varney set the pen down. He leaned back in his chair, and the springs protested with a long, low creak. His blunt index finger came to rest on the notebook, tapping the edge of the page without reading the figures.

“Miss Quill,” he said, and his voice dropped to a murmur that sat in the room like something placed carefully on a shelf. “The Gas Light and Coke Company is a large institution. It supplies light and heat to a great many people. Its reputation rests on the orderly conduct of its affairs — its meters, its ledgers, its employees. You are telling me that one of those employees, now deceased, disputed a ledger entry. A meter reading, taken by an engineer whose name you have not given me, shows a pattern, you say. And now you are asking me to reopen an accidental death on the basis of a clerk’s notes and an engineer’s numbers.”

“I am asking you to read the numbers.”

“I have heard them described.”

“You have not looked at them.”

Varney’s finger tapped the notebook once more, then withdrew. He folded his hands on the desk. “The Company itself has stated this is a clerical matter. They corrected the record. The man is dead by misadventure. You work for the Company, Miss Quill. You carry their ledger, their pen, their authority. If you persist in bringing these claims to outside parties — to the police, to anyone who will listen — what do you imagine happens to the Company’s confidence in its own records? What do you imagine happens to the confidence of the people who rely on those records?”

The question was not really a question. Ada’s knuckles whitened on the spine of the ledger, which still lay open on the desk between them.

“I am not bringing claims. I am bringing data.”

“And I am telling you what data sounds like when it arrives without a body that shows a wound.” Varney straightened a stack of papers on the corner of his desk, squaring their edges against the wood. “Griggs drowned. There was no wound. There was no sign of struggle. The water in his lungs was canal water. The pathologist was thorough. I read his report before you arrived, Miss Quill, not after.”

“You read the report. You did not read the meter.”

“I have a great many meters in this district, and a great many clerks. Most of them are content to keep their books.”

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full — full of the particular weight that settles into a room when one person has finished saying what the other person has already decided not to hear. Varney picked up his pen again and pulled a file toward him, opening it at a page he did not appear to read. The meeting was over. It had been over before she sat down.

Ada closed the ledger. She gathered the Tuesday sheet and the notebook and placed them in her satchel in the order she had arranged them on her lap. She stood. Varney did not look up.

“Miss Quill,” he said, as she reached the door. She stopped. “A solved crime is a quiet street. I prefer quiet streets. The Company prefers them too, I should think. Go back to your books.”

She walked down the corridor. The green tiles threw their watery light across the floor, and her boots made a sound on them that was too loud in the stillness. The constable with the tea mug was gone. A different constable sat at the front desk, writing in a log with the slow, deliberate strokes of a man fulfilling a duty he did not enjoy.

The door opened onto Leman Street. The steps were granite, worn to a smooth dip at the center by decades of boots, and the air outside tasted of damp coal and river fog. Ada stopped on the top step. Her right hand found the iron railing, and the cold of it passed through her glove and into the bones of her fingers. She held it. The fog sat low over the street, thickening at the crossings where the gas lamps burned with their uncertain yellow halos. A costermonger was packing barrows across the way, stacking cabbage crates with a dull, rhythmic thud.

She stood there for a long time. Her grip on the railing did not loosen.

The ledger was in her satchel, and the notebook, and the Tuesday sheet. Varney had not read a single figure. He had not turned a page. He had looked at her, and at the notebook, and at the pigeon on the ledge, and then he had told her to go back to her books. The route from the station to the Gas Light and Coke Company offices was twelve minutes on foot. She could walk it in her sleep. She could return to the clerking office, open the ledger to the next night’s entries, and resume the work she had been hired to do. No one would ask her what had happened at Leman Street. No one would know.

The costermonger glanced up as a hansom clattered past. Ada descended the steps, her hand leaving the railing one finger at a time. The fog closed behind her.

She did not turn toward the Company offices. She turned south, toward the river, where the streets narrowed and the gas brackets were older and the pipes ran closer to the surface. Rook had said the Beckton main fed the Whitechapel southern section through a feeder line that ran beneath Commercial Road. If the meter at the district station was not registering consumption that the lamps confirmed, then the diversion had to be between the main and the meter — somewhere in the network of pipes that ran under the cobblestones. Rook would know where the junctions were. Rook would have the distribution maps.

She walked faster. The satchel bumped against her hip, and inside it the ledger’s weight was the weight of a record that nobody in authority wanted read. Varney had made that clear enough. The police would not look at the numbers. The Company had called it a clerical error. Griggs had called it a lie, and Griggs was in the canal.

The fog thinned as she crossed toward Wapping, and the gas lamps along the pavement stood out sharper, each one burning its small, steady flame above the iron standard. Someone had lit them. Someone lit them every evening, walked the route, trimmed the wicks, and carried the tallow. The lamps burned. The gas flowed. The question was where it went before the meter could count it.

She would find Rook in the engineering offices and ask him for the distribution maps. And she would not bring those maps to Leman Street.
