Chapter 10

Ink, Ghosts, and a Choice

The lamplighter’s ledger lay open on her desk, every X in its column a small inked tombstone, and Ada Quill began to copy.

She had told Samuel Rook it was murder. The word had left her mouth at Regent’s Canal, standing over brown water where Thomas Griggs had been pulled with a bruise on his temple and the official verdict already settling around him like silt. Rook had gone quiet. She had walked back through the streets without seeing them. The gas lamps were lit along Commercial Road, and she had looked at each one and thought: you are burning gas that someone is paying for, or not paying for, and the record of which is a lie I helped make legible.

Now the office was empty. The other clerks had gone at six. The gas lamp above her desk hissed faintly, casting its greenish light across the yellowed pages. She had the lamplighters’ ledger open to Griggs’s section, September through October, and her own notebook beside it, and her pen moved down the columns with the steady rhythm of someone who had been transcribing figures since she was seventeen.

September 14. Griggs lit twenty-three lamps on the Whitechapel southern section. Three of those — numbers 41, 42, and 43 on Commercial Road — were marked with the X that meant attended and lit. The meter readings copied from the Beckton master meter showed no corresponding draw for those three lamps. She had established this already. What she had not done was lay out the man’s entire working life on a single page and read it the way one reads a biography.

Her pen moved. September 21, twenty-three lamps, all marked lit. September 28, twenty-three, all marked lit. October 5, the same. The same held for October 12. October 14 — the Tuesday sheet, the anomalous date — three lamps marked lit that drew no gas, and three lamps on Commercial Road that Griggs had denied touching before anyone told him the ledger said otherwise.

She copied the entries in chronological order, marking each anomalous date with a small circle in the margin. There were four in total, stretching back to September. Four instances where Griggs’s ledger showed lamps lit that the master meter said had burned nothing. Four instances where the gas had gone somewhere, and the ledger said Griggs was the man who had attended them.

She laid her pen down and looked at the page. The circled dates stood out against the neat columns. September 14. September 28. October 12. October 14. She stared at them. Two weeks apart, then two weeks apart, then two days. The last one was the outlier — the one that had brought everything crashing down. The first three followed a rhythm.

Her roster sheet listed the company’s pay schedule. Lamplighters were paid fortnightly, on Wednesdays. She checked the dates against the circled entries. September 14 was a payday. September 28 was a payday. October 12 was a payday. Three of the four anomalies fell on Griggs’s paydays. October 14 was not a payday, but it was the entry that had triggered Griggs’s complaint, the one that had brought him to her desk insisting the ledger was wrong.

A man who skims gas on payday, banking on the fact that the meter reader and the wage clerk work from different schedules. A man who needs cash and uses the lag between the lamp ledger and the master meter to cover the gap. It was plausible. It was, she had to admit, the kind of pattern that a detective inspector would look at and close his file.

Varney would have said: your lamplighter was stealing gas, and he drowned because he drank and fell. The bruise was an accident. The ledger shows what it shows.

But Griggs had denied it. He had come to her before anyone accused him, before the police had spoken to him, before his name was attached to any discrepancy. He had said the ledger was wrong. Not wrong the way a man caught stealing says the ledger is wrong — blustering, defensive, offering no alternative. Wrong the way a man says it who has seen his own name used to record something he did not do.

She picked up the pen again and turned to the wage ledger.

The chief clerk’s cabinet stood against the far wall, its lock a simple ward mechanism that any clerk in the office could open with the company’s standard key. Ada had that key. She had been entitled to it since her second year, when her duties expanded to keeping attendance records and checking the rosters against the meter logs. Finch had insisted on handling the wage ledger himself last quarter — she remembered that now, his hand closing over the book when she reached for it, his voice too casual, his explanation too prepared. She had thought nothing of it at the time. Finch fussed over every ledger that crossed his desk. It was his nature.

She unlocked the cabinet and lifted the wage ledger out. The cloth binding was coarse under her fingers, and the faint smell of pipe tobacco rose from the pages — Finch’s, clinging to the book he had held. She carried it back to her desk and opened it to October.

The wage entries were arranged by section, then by name, then by date. Griggs’s row was where it should be, in the Whitechapel southern section, between Gravell and Harkins. The entries were neat. The amounts were correct. She traced her finger along the row, reading the payment dates.

September 15. September 29. October 13.

She stopped.

The anomalous entries in the lamplighter’s ledger were dated September 14, September 28, October 12, and October 14. The wage entries showed Griggs being paid on September 15, September 29, and October 13 — the day after each of the first three anomalies. If Griggs was skimming gas on payday to cover a cash shortfall, he was doing it the day before he received his wages. A man who steals on the day before he is paid is not covering a shortfall. He is creating one.

She looked at the dates again. The first three anomalies fell on the day before each payday. Not on payday itself. The day before. Someone had entered the false lamp readings twenty-four hours before Griggs received his wages, which meant someone had access to the lamplighter’s ledger on the evening before the wage clerk made his disbursements. Someone who knew when Griggs would be paid and used that knowledge to make the false entries appear as though Griggs was skimming to cover debts that would not exist until the following day.

She turned back to October. The fourth anomaly — October 14 — was not a payday. It was the day after payday. Griggs had been paid on October 13. The entry appeared on the Tuesday sheet, the one she had copied the meter readings onto, the one she had carried to Varney. It was the entry Griggs had denied. The one that had made him come to her desk and say: I did not light those lamps.

Her finger rested on the October 13 wage entry. The ink was wrong.

It was subtle. The difference between one batch of company ink and another was measurable only in shade, and only under the gas lamp’s particular light. The entries above and below Griggs’s October 13 payment were written in the standard brown-black that the company supplied in half-pint bottles from the same stationer on Whitechapel High Street. Griggs’s entry was the same brown-black, but the numerals had a faintly bluer cast, visible only where the pen had pressed harder — at the top of the 1, the curve of the 3, the downstroke of the G in his surname. The same hand had written it. The same slant, the same pressure, the same spacing between the numerals. But the ink was from a different bottle.

Someone had written the original entry, and someone had written over it. The date had been changed. October 13 had been written as October 15 — two days after the anomalous October 14 entry appeared in the lamplighter’s ledger. The overwriting was careful. The 5 was built on top of a 3, the lower curve thickened slightly, the top stroke extended. Under the gas lamp, with the page tilted, the original numeral showed through the second layer of ink like a watermark.

Griggs had been paid on October 13. The wage ledger said October 15. The lamplighter’s ledger said the anomalous entry appeared on October 14. The sequence was: Griggs was paid, the false lamp entry appeared the next day, and then the wage ledger was altered to make it look as though Griggs was paid after the false entry — after the theft it appeared to record.

The first three anomalies followed the same structure. She checked. September 14’s anomaly preceded a wage entry dated September 16, but the ink on the 6 was thicker than the surrounding numerals. September 28’s anomaly preceded an entry dated September 30, and the 0 was built over what might have been a 9. October 12’s anomaly preceded an entry dated October 14, and the 4 was slightly broader than the 4 in the entries above and below it.

Three dates, all altered. Three wage entries, all overwritten to push the payment date two days forward — past the anomalous lamp entry, so that the records would show Griggs was paid after the false readings appeared. So that any investigator would see a man who stole gas, then collected his wages, then stole again. A man whose thefts followed his paydays, not preceded them.

Griggs had insisted the ledger was wrong. He had been assigned to a different route that night — she had found that in the roster, his name moved to the northern section, the substitution initialled by the section chief. Three lamps on Commercial Road were logged as lit under his name on a night he was not on Commercial Road. He had denied lighting them because he had not been there.

Someone had used his rounds as a template. They had taken his standard route, the one he walked every night, and entered false readings on the lamps they knew he would not be attending — because they had moved him to the northern section for that shift. The ledger, they had used to frame him. And then they had altered the wage ledger to make the frame airtight.

She had transcribed those false entries into her own notebook and carried them to Varney. She had presented them as evidence of a discrepancy that needed investigation, and Varney had looked at them and seen a dead lamplighter with a drinking problem and a ledger that said thief. The frame she had made legible. She had given it the authority of her own handwriting, her own analysis, her own reputation for accuracy.

Her pen hovered over the wage ledger. The blue-black ink caught the lamplight. The altered numerals sat in their perfect column, indistinguishable from the entries around them unless you tilted the page and watched the original figures show through. Someone had done this with the same care she brought to her own work. The same precision. The same belief that a well-kept ledger was a record of truth.

The office was silent except for the gas lamp’s hiss. The gas lamp’s light made the ink on the page look almost black, and the altered numerals disappeared into their neighbours. She tilted the page again. The 3 beneath the 5 surfaced for a moment, then sank back.

She closed the wage ledger. She closed the lamplighters’ ledger. Her fingers stayed on the covers, pressing them shut. The leather was warm under her hands. She had carried these books for years, trusted them the way a carpenter trusts a level. They were the ground she stood on. And someone had turned them into a weapon.

She picked up the wage ledger and placed it in her satchel beside the lamplighters’ ledger. She buckled the flap. The weight of both books settled against her hip.

The office was dark beyond her desk. She stood, and the chair scraped against the floor, and the sound was loud in the empty room. She put on her coat and lifted the satchel onto her shoulder. The gas lamp above her desk continued to hiss, throwing its green light across the empty chair, the closed inkwell, the notebook she had filled with a dead man’s rounds.

She would not return the ledgers or sleep. She would find the hand that had written Griggs into a thief and a dead man, and she would write that hand into the light.
