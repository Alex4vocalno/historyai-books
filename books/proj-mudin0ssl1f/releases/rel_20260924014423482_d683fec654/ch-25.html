Chapter 25

A New Page

Sunlight, thin and pale, fell across Ada’s desk for the last time. She had arranged the pen tray parallel to the desk’s leading edge, squared the corners of her blotting paper, and placed the inkwell at exactly the distance her wrist required to dip without lifting her forearm from the wood. Twenty-three months of mornings lived in those motions. Her hands performed them now with the precision of a mechanism that had not yet been told it was being decommissioned. The gas lamp above the stairwell hissed its small, constant note, and the smell of coal dust and size drifted through the half-open window from the yard below, where a wagon was being loaded with the last of Finch’s personal effects, sealed in a crate and directed to his wife’s address by order of the Board.

The previous afternoon still sat in her bones. She had stood on the steps of the Gas Light and Coke Company offices with Rook, watching the police wagon depart with Finch inside, the gasworks chimney rising behind it against a grey sky. The numbers were correct again. The ledger matched the meter. But the correction had cost a man his liberty and another man his life, and the neatness of the restored columns could not undo either of those things. She had left the building with a resolved, different kind of purpose, and now she had returned to discover what shape that purpose would take.

The clerking office was almost empty. Two of the remaining desks had been cleared, their incumbents reassigned to other districts pending the Board’s review of the Commercial Road records. Ada’s desk was the only one still fully dressed. She opened the top drawer, removed her satchel, and placed inside it the notebook with copied meter readings, the Tuesday sheet, the roster, and her own ledger — the private one, not the company’s, with its careful parallel columns of dates, names, and figures that no one had asked her to keep. She buckled the strap. The pen went into the satchel’s side pocket, where it had always ridden.

She was reaching for the handle of the door when it opened from the corridor side, and Samuel Rook stood in the frame, blocking the light from the hallway windows. His hands were, as always, marked with the day’s work — grease in the creases of his knuckles, a faint smudge of red lead on his left cuff. But he was carrying something: a ledger, bound in dark leather, held in both hands the way a man carries a thing he has repaired and is not certain will hold.

“Brought you something,” he said. “Found it on the bench in the meter room. Someone had bent the spine back hard enough to crack the stitching. I re-stitched it and reset the boards.”

He held it out. Ada took it. The leather was warm from his hands, and the spine was straight and firm, the new thread pulling the signatures together evenly. She opened it. The pages were the company’s standard lamplighter’s ledger — route assignments, lamp numbers, times of lighting and extinguishing. But this one was blank. Every page, clean.

“There’s nothing in it,” she said.

“A ledger don’t light a lamp. A man does. But a book’s got to be right before a man can write in it.” He wiped his palms on his trousers, a habitual gesture. “The old one’s with the police now, what’s left of it. Figured you could use a new one.”

Ada turned the blank pages under her thumb. The paper was good stock, heavy and smooth. The binding would last years. She closed it and held it against her chest for a moment, then placed it carefully in the satchel beside the other ledger.

“The Board sent word,” she said. “They want to see me at eleven.”

“I know. I’m to be there too. They’ve assigned me as engineering liaison to whatever they’re setting up.” He leaned against the doorframe. “Don’t know what that means yet.”

“It means you’ll be assisting me, presumably.”

“Presumably.”

They looked at each other. The gas lamp hissed above the stairwell. A cart horse clopped past the window, its harness jingling.

“You re-stitched the binding,” Ada said.

“Took me half the morning. The thread kept slipping on the second signature.”

“Thank you, Mr. Rook.”

“It’s just a book.”

She almost smiled. “I had better go and find out what they want.”

The boardroom was on the second floor, at the end of a corridor lined with framed gas-light patents and district maps whose borders she had come to know better than the streets they depicted. The door was oak, heavy, and bore a brass plate that read BOARD OF DIRECTORS. It had read the same yesterday, but yesterday the men behind it had been different men. She knocked once and was told to enter.

The room was long, panelled in dark wood, with a table that could seat twenty and currently seated seven. The new Board Chairman sat at the head, a man of perhaps fifty with grey temples and spectacles that he removed and folded as she entered. The other members — six men she recognised by sight but not by name — turned in their chairs. The air carried the faint citrus scent of furniture polish, freshly applied to the table’s surface. The morning light came through tall windows and lay in long bands across the wood.

“Miss Quill,” the Chairman said. “Please, sit down.”

She took the chair at the far end of the table, opposite him, and placed her satchel on the floor beside her. Her hands rested on the table, one over the other, still.

“We have reviewed the findings of the internal investigation,” the Chairman continued, “and the correspondence from Detective Inspector Varney’s office regarding the arrests. The Board has also examined the meter records you copied and the engineering reports Mr. Rook provided concerning the bypass at Beckton.” He paused, replacing his spectacles. “You uncovered a systematic fraud that this institution failed to detect. That failure cost a man his life. The Board accepts responsibility for that failure.”

Ada’s grip tightened on the edge of the table. She held the position, knuckles white, and waited.

“In light of these events, the Board has authorised the creation of a new position. We are offering you the role of Internal Compliance Auditor, with a mandate to examine, verify, and report on all company records pertaining to gas distribution, metering, and lamplighter assignments. You would report directly to this Board. Mr. Rook has been permanently assigned as your engineering assistant.” He placed a document on the table and slid it toward her. “The terms are outlined here.”

Ada did not reach for it immediately. She looked at the document, at the embossed company seal at the top, at the title beneath it: INTERNAL COMPLIANCE AUDITOR — APPOINTMENT OF MISS A. QUILL. The ink on the title was typeset, clean and regular. Her name appeared in a space left blank for it, waiting.

“What precisely would my authority cover?” she asked.

“All company records. Meter readings, distribution logs, lamplighter rosters, maintenance schedules. You would have access to every district office and every meter room in the company’s network. Any discrepancy you find, you would report directly to this Board, with the power to suspend operations in any district where fraud is suspected.”

“And if the discrepancy originates with a member of this Board?”

The Chairman met her gaze. “Then you report to the Metropolitan Police, as you did in this instance.”

One of the other members shifted in his chair. The Chairman did not look at him.

“The role is not a reward, Miss Quill. It is a necessity. What you found, another could find. We would rather it be found by someone inside this company than by someone outside it.”

Ada reached for the document. She read the terms carefully — the salary, the access privileges, the reporting structure, the clause that granted her independent authority to inspect any company facility without prior notice. She read it twice. Then she took the pen from her satchel, uncapped it, and signed her name on the line beneath the title. The nib scratched cleanly across the paper. The ink was dark and wet.

The Chairman collected the document and placed it in a folder. “Your office is on the third floor. Room 312. It has been prepared this morning.”

Ada stood, picked up her satchel, and left the boardroom. The corridor was quiet. She climbed one flight of stairs, turned right, and found room 312 at the end of the hall. The door was new, or newly painted — the varnish still held a faint tackiness under her fingers when she turned the handle.

The office was small but clean. A desk stood beneath a window that faced south, and the same thin sunlight that had crossed her old desk now fell across this one, unobstructed. There was a chair, a lamp bracket on the wall, a shelf with empty binders, and a small gas heater in the corner that ticked as it cooled. She set the satchel on the desk and took out the repaired ledger.

She opened it to the first page. The paper was blank, the binding tight. She turned to where Griggs’s entry would have been — the entry he had insisted was false, the entry that recorded him lighting three lamps on Commercial Road on a night when he had been assigned to a route on the other side of the district. The page was empty. The false entry existed now only in police evidence and in her own private ledger, where she had copied it the day Griggs had first told her he had not lit those lamps.

She ran her thumb across the clean page. The paper was smooth and unmarked. Griggs was dead. Finch was in custody. The bypass at Beckton had been sealed. The meters turned honestly now, and the columns in the company’s books matched the columns in the master records. The ledger could be written in again, and what was written would be true.

She picked up the pen. It was her own pen, the one she had carried through every corridor and every station and every meter room of the last six months. The nib was sharp. The ink was fresh. She turned to the first page, before any entry, and wrote the date, and beneath it, a single line.

Internal Compliance Audit — Volume One.

The ink dried on the page. The gas-jet bracket above the desk caught a thread of sunlight from the window and held it, a small bright point on the brass, steady and still.

The next morning she arrived early. The office assigned to her was narrow, formerly a storage cupboard for surplus valve handles and bound copies of parliamentary regulations. Someone had cleared it. A desk stood against the far wall, a chair tucked beneath it, and on the desk sat a new lamp — not gas, but electric, the company’s concession to modernity.

She set out her materials. The notebook with copied meter readings. The Tuesday sheet, now archived. Rook had left a folded note under the lamp. She opened it. Three lines in his steady engineer’s hand: Beckton main running clean. Pressure holding. I’ll send weekly reports Tuesdays.

She placed the note inside the front cover of Volume One and began the first substantive entry. She recorded the date, the scope of the audit, the districts to be reviewed, and the method of cross-referencing field readings against central ledgers. She wrote the way she had always written — each letter formed precisely, each figure checked once before the nib moved to the next line.

Outside, the street lamps along Whitechapel Road stood cold in daylight, their mantles spent, their posts upright and ordinary. She had walked beneath them last night on her way home and looked up at each one. They burned at the proper height. They burned at the proper rate. She had not needed to stop, but she had.

The pen moved down the page. The office was quiet. The electric lamp hummed at a frequency just below hearing, a vibration felt in the desk rather than the air. Griggs had given her the first number — the one that did not belong — on a Tuesday evening in February, standing in a corridor that smelled of coal gas and cold brick. She remembered his hand, how it had trembled around the scrap of paper. She had not trembled. She would not.

She finished the entry, set the pen across the page, and looked at the room. Small. Adequate. Hers.
