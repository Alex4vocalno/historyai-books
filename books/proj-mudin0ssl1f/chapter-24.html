Chapter 24

The Ledger's Verdict

The gasworks chimney drew its thin line of smoke into a sky already drained of color, and beside her Rook shifted his weight on the stone step, the leather of his boots creaking faintly. The police wagon had turned the corner some minutes ago. What remained was the ordinary traffic of the afternoon and the silence between two people who had done something that could not be undone. Rook cleared his throat, looked at her once, and then nodded and walked down the steps and away across the yard without speaking further. Ada watched him go. Her shoulders eased by degrees, and her breath came more slowly, and after a moment she turned and went inside.

The clerking office was quiet. The lamp on her desk had been left burning, and its flame burned steadily behind its glass chimney, casting a small warm circle across the blotter and the stacked papers and the inkwell. Morning light came through the high windows in flat panels that caught the dust motes suspended in the still air. Everything was where she had left it—the Tuesday sheet squared neatly with the desk’s edge, her notebook closed beside the inkwell, the roster pinned to its board above the shelf. The room smelled of paper and ink and the faint sulphur bite of gas, the smell of her working life. She set her satchel on the hook behind the door and pulled out her chair and sat down.

The ledger lay in the center of the desk, bound shut with a ribbon of official red wax. The coroner’s seal pressed into the wax was a small, crude thing—the letters partially obscured by a thumbprint where someone had pressed too hard before the wax had fully cooled. A second seal held the binding ribbon to the cover, and this one was cleaner, its impression sharper: a crown above the words Central Criminal Court, the lettering fine and deliberate. Between the two seals, the leather cover was darkened at the corners where handling had worn it smooth. She ran her thumb along the edge of the ribbon where it emerged from the wax. The seal held. The book was closed by authority and reopened by the same authority, and now it sat on her desk as though it had never been anywhere else.

She lifted the wax ribbon free of its catch and opened the cover. The first page was the title page, unchanged: Lamplighters’ Daily Ledger, District Four, Gas Light and Coke Company. The date stamp at the top read the current morning. She turned past the index, past the district map, to the first blank entry line. The pen was in her hand before she had consciously picked it up, and she dipped the nib and entered the date and the lamplighter’s name and the route number in the prescribed columns. The ink flowed evenly. She entered the meter reading from the morning slip, the serial number of the lamp bracket, the time of ignition. Each figure sat in its column as it should. She turned the page and entered the next line, and the next.

The work moved along in its usual rhythm, and the office was quiet around her, and the lamp burned steadily, and then she turned to the page for Commercial Road.

The entry was there, corrected in a hand that was not hers. A clerk from the coroner’s office had written it—she could tell by the slight leftward slant, the overlarge capitals. The entry read: October 14, Commercial Road, Lamps 7, 8, and 9—Entry in error. Lamps unlit. Route reassigned. T. Griggs, lamplighter (deceased). Assigned route: Commercial Road east section, per roster amendment. Actual route on Oct 14: Cannon Street Road north, per dispatcher’s log. The corrected figures sat in their columns with the precision of a surgeon’s stitch. Beneath them, a line in the coroner’s hand: Verified against dispatcher’s log and meter record. Seal applied.

Ada’s pen was still in her hand. A drop of ink gathered at the nib and hung there, swelling, and then fell onto the page and bloomed into the paper grain, a small dark circle spreading outward from the point of contact. She blotted it with her sleeve cuff, pressing hard, and the ink smeared into a faint grey streak across the margin. The blot covered nothing—no entry, no figure—but it sat there on the page like a bruise on clean skin.

She set the pen down. Griggs had stood in this office, in front of this desk, and had said the entry was wrong. He had said it plainly, without ornament, the way a man states a fact about the weather. His voice had not been raised. The lamps were not lit, he said, and the ledger said they were, and he could not account for the difference. She had listened to him and written down what he said and filed it, and a week later he was pulled from the Regent’s Canal with water in his lungs and a bruise on his temple, and the official record said accidental death, and Varney had told her to stick to her books.

The corrected entry now said what Griggs had said. The lamps were unlit. The route was reassigned. The ledger had been wrong, and now the ledger said so, in a clerk’s careful hand, sealed by the coroner’s authority. The truth of it was simple, ordinary, bureaucratic—a line amended, a seal applied, a record corrected. Griggs had carried that truth in his body to the canal, and the canal had taken him, and now the truth sat on a page in a book on a desk in an office where the lamp burned steadily and the dust motes turned in the light.

She picked up the pen again. She turned to the next page and entered the next route’s figures. The numbers came readily, and the columns filled, and the morning passed in the scratching of the nib and the turning of pages. When she reached the end of the day’s required entries, she set the pen in its rest and flexed her fingers, which had cramped slightly around the barrel. The office was still quiet. Somewhere in the building a door closed, and footsteps receded down the corridor.

She turned back to the Commercial Road page. The entry was there, corrected, sealed. Her blot still sat in the margin, the grey smear where the ink had fallen. She looked at it, and then she dipped the pen and, in the margin beside the corrected entry, drew a small star. Five points, each line drawn in a single stroke, the lines meeting cleanly at the junctions. The ink bled slightly into the paper grain, softening the points, but the shape held. It was no larger than a fingernail. It sat in the margin the way a mark sits on a map—a notation that says something is here, attend to this.

She turned to the next page. The entries there were routine—Whitechapel Road, lamps eleven through eighteen, all lit, all metered, all in order. She checked the figures against the morning slip and found no discrepancy. She entered the verification initials and moved on. The work was ordinary. The pen moved, the columns filled, and the lamp burned behind its glass, and the office held its silence like a vessel holds water.

The door opened behind her. She heard the latch turn and the hinge give its small creak, and then the particular sound of boots on the floorboards—heavier than a clerk’s, the tread of someone who spent his days on iron grating and in cellars. She did not turn around.

“Miss Quill.”

Rook’s voice was quiet, stripped of its usual directness. He crossed the room and stopped beside the desk, his hands at his sides. The smell of machine oil came off him faintly, mixed with the cold air from outside.

“Mr. Rook.”

“They’ve remanded him. Finch. Committal for trial at the Old Bailey, the clerk said next sessions.” He paused, and when she said nothing, he went on. “Varney was there. He spoke to me in the corridor after.”

“What did he say?”

“He asked whether the company would be making a statement. I told him I didn’t know. He said—“Rook stopped, and she heard him shift his weight. “He said the cooperation of the company’s officers was noted in the file. Shook my hand.”

“Did he.”

“His hand was dry. He held it for a long time.” Rook looked at the ledger on the desk, at the open page, at the coroner’s seal. “He’s a man who knows which way the wind is blowing.”

Ada’s gaze stayed on the page. The corrected entry sat in its columns. The star in the margin caught the light from the desk lamp, its points sharp, its ink still drying.

She had drawn it without thinking, and now she looked at it as though someone else had put it there. It sat in the margin of the official record, small and exact, a mark that did not belong to the company’s system of notation. It was hers.

It said: this page was corrected, and I am the one who corrected it, and I am watching to see that it stays corrected. The ledger had been wrong, and now it was right, and the star said that rightness required vigilance—that a figure in a column was not a fact of nature but a thing made by a hand, and a hand could make it falsely, and a hand could make it true.

“The meter-reader,” she said. “The one who confessed. What happens to him?”

“Varney said he’d be charged separately. Receiving, rather than fraud. He cooperated.”

“He was threatened.”

“I know.”

“Does Varney know?”

Rook’s silence was answer enough. She heard him breathe, a slow exhale through his nose. The lamp flame wavered slightly in the draft from the door, then steadied.

“He’s building a file that closes cleanly,” Rook said. “Two men, two charges. The meter-reader cooperated, Finch fought. That’s the shape of it.”

“And the men who threatened the meter-reader. The men who threatened Bill Hobbs.”

“Varney didn’t mention them.”

“No.”

Rook put his hands in his pockets. His jaw worked, the muscle tightening once beneath the skin, and he looked at the window and then back at the ledger. “A ledger don’t light a lamp. A man does. And a man can put out a light too.”

“The bypass at Beckton,” she said. “Has it been sealed?”

“I checked yesterday. The valve’s shut. The pipe’s still there—they’ll need to excavate to remove it—but the flow is stopped.”

“Stopped.”

“Stopped.”

She nodded once, slowly, her eyes on the star in the margin. The ink had dried fully now, the points sharp and dark against the paper. Rook looked at the page, at the corrected entry, at the small mark in the margin. He did not ask what it meant. His gaze rested on it for a moment, and then he looked at her, and something passed between them that did not require words—an acknowledgment that the book on the desk was no longer the book it had been, and that neither of them was the person who had opened it a month ago.

“I’ll be in the district engineer’s office,” he said. “If you need anything.”

“Thank you, Mr. Rook.”

He left. The door closed behind him, and the latch caught, and his footsteps receded down the corridor. The office was quiet again. The lamp burned. The dust motes turned in the light from the high windows. The ledger lay open on the desk, its pages holding their figures in their columns, the coroner’s seal glinting dully on the adjacent page, the star in the margin small and dark and precise.

Ada closed the ledger. The covers came together with a soft sound, the pages settling against each other, and the star disappeared from view. She pressed her palm flat against the cover. The leather was cool and slightly tacky under her hand. She gathered her satchel from the hook, tucked her notebook inside, and lifted the ledger from the desk. It was heavier than she remembered, or her arms were more tired than they had been a month ago. She held it against her chest with both hands and crossed the office and pulled the door open and stepped into the corridor.

The building was quiet for the hour. Most of the clerks had gone for their midday meal, and the corridor stretched ahead of her, its floor tiles worn to a dull sheen by decades of boots. She walked steadily, the ledger pressed to her chest, her satchel over one shoulder. The figures inside the book were correct. The meters would register what flowed through the mains, and the lamplighters would light their routes, and the entries would be made in the prescribed columns, and the pages would turn, and the ledger would hold. But the star sat in the margin of the Commercial Road page, and it said what it said, and it was hers.

She pushed through the street door and onto the steps. The afternoon was grey and still, the gasworks chimney marking its thread of smoke against the sky. She stood for a moment on the top step, the ledger held against her coat, and looked out across the yard toward the gate. Then she descended the steps, one hand on the iron railing, and walked out through the gate and into the street, carrying the book and everything it now contained away with her into the ordinary traffic of the afternoon.
