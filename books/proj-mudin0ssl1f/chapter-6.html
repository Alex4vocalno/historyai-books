Chapter 6

The Meter Room

The frosted glass of Mr. Finch’s office door still held the ghost of his handprint, the oil from his fingers catching the corridor lamp at just the angle where Ada Quill’s eye fell as she passed. She did not slow her stride. The print would be wiped clean by morning, or it would not. The words he had spoken an hour ago sat in the lining of her coat like a stone dropped into still water, and the ripples had not yet reached the edges. Some records, Miss Quill, are better left unexamined. She had nodded, had gathered her ledger and her Tuesday sheet, had walked out of that office with her spine held straight and her chin level with the topmost filing cabinet, and she had not looked back.

The corridor beyond Finch’s door was long and narrow and lit by gas brackets set too far apart, so that the pools of yellow light thinned to grey between them and a person walking alone could see her own shadow stretch and compress and stretch again against the plaster. The floor was linoleum, scuffed at the threshold of every doorway by years of boot heels, and the scuff marks accumulated into dark crescents that pointed toward the stairwell at the far end. A notice pinned to the board outside the lamplighters’ break room advertised a shift swap for the following Thursday, the handwriting cramped and slanting. Below it, a second sheet, typed, reminded all clerical staff that meter readings for the second quarter were due by Friday noon. The ink on the typed notice had run slightly where someone had brushed a wet sleeve across the lower corner.

The stairwell door stood ajar, and from below came a thread of warm air carrying the smell of hot oil and iron filings.

Ada took the stairs down.

The meter room occupied the basement of the Gas Light and Coke Company’s Horseferry Road depot, a long vaulted chamber whose brick walls sweated a fine, persistent damp that collected in the mortar joints and darkened them to black. The ceiling was arched and low enough that Samuel Rook, who had arrived before her and was standing with his back to the door, had to keep his head ducked slightly beneath the lowest course. The room ran the full length of the building above, and every surface along its walls was occupied by brass-faced dials mounted in pine cases, each dial roughly the size of a dinner plate, each needle trembling with a minute oscillation that threw a hundred tiny shadows across the white enamel faces.

The dials were stacked three high and ran in unbroken rows from the stairwell door to the far wall, where the largest of them, the district mains, were mounted singly on heavy iron brackets bolted directly into the brick. The floor was flagstone, cracked along one seam where the foundation had settled unevenly decades ago, and the crack had been filled with a hardened compound that was now the color of old bone. A single inspection desk stood in the center of the room, a battered table with a surface scarred by compass points and ink rings, lit by a gas bracket whose flame was so steady in the still basement air that it cast no flicker at all. Two tall stacks of bound meter logs stood on the desk beside a brass-handled inspection lamp and a chipped enamel mug holding two pencils and a ruled straightedge.

The hum of the mechanisms, each one a tiny clockwork of gears and escapements driven by the pressure of gas flowing through the mains above, combined into a single low chord that seemed to rise through the flagstones and settle into the soles of the feet.

Samuel Rook turned when the stairwell door scraped open. He had spread three bound volumes across the inspection desk and was holding a fourth open against his chest.

“You came.”

“I said I would.”

“Finch didn’t stop you?”

“He spoke to me. I listened. Then I came downstairs.”

Samuel set the volume down on the desk and pressed it flat. “What did he say?”

“Nothing I intend to act upon.”

Samuel studied her face for a moment, then nodded toward the desk. “I’ve pulled the Beckton district logs for the past six months. These three volumes cover Commercial Road, Whitechapel south, and the Docklands spur. The problem is that each volume covers all three districts simultaneously. They aren’t separated by route. They’re separated by date.”

“Because the meter records total flow through the main, not individual service pipes.”

“Right. So the meter reads what passes through the Beckton main on a given night, and that total includes every lamp and every premises on every branch fed by that main. To find what happened on Commercial Road specifically, you need the ledger entries for every other branch on the same night and subtract them from the total.”

Ada set her ledger on the desk beside the open volumes. “How many branches?”

“Fourteen. Twelve street-lamp circuits and two domestic-supply feeds.”

“Then we need the branch readings for each of those fourteen on the nights in question.”

“That’s the second problem. The branch meters are logged separately, by the meter inspectors, on their rounds. Those logs are in the filing cabinets upstairs.”

Ada opened her ledger to the Tuesday sheet. “I have the lamplighters’ entries for Commercial Road for the full week. Three lamps logged as lit on Tuesday night. Griggs’s route sheet shows he was assigned to Whitechapel southern section that night, not Commercial Road. He denied lighting them. The Company sent someone to question him — a gentleman from the office, he called it — and I expect they will call the entry a clerical error.”

“And you don’t believe it is.”

“I am not paid to believe. I am paid to record accurately. The entry is in my handwriting, in my ledger, and I did not write it.”

Samuel’s hands went still on the volume. “A forged entry in the company ledger. If someone reproduced your hand well enough to pass inspection, they had access to your prior records.”

“The entry is in my hand. I did not make it. Someone reproduced my handwriting and entered it in the ledger. That is why I need to know whether those three lamps were actually lit. If they were, gas flowed through the Beckton main that should appear on the meter log. If it does not appear, then the meter was bypassed or the log was altered.”

“Or the meter is broken.”

“That is the third possibility. We will need to rule it out.”

Samuel exhaled and looked down the long row of dials. “The Beckton main is the fourth bracket from the end. The big one with the brass plate. I checked it on my way in. The needle moves.”

“Then we begin with the logs.”

They pulled the desk lamp closer and opened the first volume. The pages were ruled into columns: date, meter number, opening reading, closing reading, consumption in cubic feet, inspector’s initials. Each entry was made in pencil, the figures small and precise, with occasional corrections where a reading had been struck through and rewritten. Samuel ran his finger along the column headers.

“Tuesday. Here. Beckton main, opening reading four hundred and twelve thousand, closing four hundred and twelve thousand, nine hundred and six. That’s nine hundred and six cubic feet for the full twenty-four-hour period.”

Ada opened her ledger. “Tuesday’s lamplighters’ entries. Commercial Road: three lamps lit, standard burn, dusk to dawn. That is approximately ninety-six cubic feet if the jets are the standard four-cubic-feet-per-hour type.”

“Which they are. I replaced three of them myself last winter.”

“Then Commercial Road alone accounts for ninety-six of the nine hundred and six cubic feet. The remaining thirteen branches must account for the balance: eight hundred and ten.”

Samuel pulled the second volume open to the branch meter section. “Let’s take them in order. Branch one, the Docklands spur. Opening three thousand two hundred, closing three thousand four hundred and twelve. That’s two hundred and twelve cubic feet. Branch two, Wapping High Street, one hundred and forty-seven. Branch three, Wapping Low Street, one hundred and three.”

They worked down the list. Samuel read the figures aloud. Ada entered each one in the margin of her Tuesday sheet, subtracting from the total as she went. The gas bracket above them hissed softly, its flame a perfect blue cone. The hum of the dials filled the spaces between their voices like a third speaker.

“Branch twelve, Penfold Street, sixty-eight. Branch thirteen, domestic feed A, one hundred and ninety. Branch fourteen, domestic feed B, two hundred and seven.”

Ada set down her pen. “Total of all branches: nine hundred and six.”

“Exactly the meter total.”

“Which means there is no discrepancy. The meter and the branch logs agree. Gas flowed through the Beckton main on Tuesday night, and the total consumption recorded by the fourteen branches matches the main meter reading.”

Samuel leaned back. “Then where is the theft?”

Ada looked at the Tuesday sheet. The figures ran down the margin in her small, precise hand, each one aligned to the ruled line. “The branch logs include Commercial Road.”

“Yes. Branch four. I read it: ninety-six cubic feet.”

“And my ledger says three lamps were lit on Commercial Road on Tuesday night, consuming ninety-six cubic feet.”

“Right.”

“But Thomas Griggs says he did not light them. He was assigned to Whitechapel southern section. His route sheet confirms it. If Griggs did not light those three lamps, then no one did, and no gas should have flowed through the Commercial Road branch meter.”

Samuel tapped the desk. “But the branch log shows ninety-six cubic feet. If the meter registered that volume, gas physically moved through the Commercial Road line. A meter mechanism doesn’t record what wasn’t drawn.”

“Which means the branch log was falsified to match the ledger entry. The consumption figure aligns too neatly with Griggs’s reported route.”

“Or someone lit the lamps who wasn’t Griggs, and the gas was genuinely consumed.”

“That is also possible. But in that case, the meter shows ninety-six cubic feet of gas that was used but not officially paid for, because the lamplighter’s log does not reflect actual labor. Someone lit those lamps, and someone entered my ledger to make it appear that Griggs did the work.”

Samuel stood and walked to the far wall, where the district meters sat on their iron brackets. He stopped at the fourth from the end, a large dial with a brass plate engraved BECKTON DISTRICT MAIN. The needle trembled with the same minute oscillation as every other dial in the room, its shadow shifting back and forth across the white enamel. He crouched and peered at the gauge face, then pulled a small lamp from his coat and held it close to the glass.

“This one’s fine. Needle’s responsive. No sticking, no drift. The mechanism is sound.”

“Then the reading is accurate.”

“The reading is accurate.” He straightened. “The meter is not broken. The gas flowed. If the branch log was falsified to match the ledger entry, that’s meter-tampering — someone is using the meter record to cover consumption that doesn’t belong to the lamplighting route. The gas went through the main, but the paperwork is being shaped to hide where it actually went.”

Ada rose and carried her ledger to the wall. She held it open beside the brass plate, the Tuesday entry facing the dial. The needle trembled. The ink on the page did not.

“Someone lit three lamps on Commercial Road on a night when Griggs was on a different route. Someone entered my ledger in my hand to record the work. And ninety-six cubic feet of gas passed through this meter and was logged as consumed.”

“Which means someone used company gas and made it look like routine lamplighting.”

“And Griggs is the man whose name is on the entry. If anyone had checked, the blame would have fallen on him.”

Samuel stared at the dial. “If he were gone, no one would contradict the ledger.”

Ada closed the ledger. “Griggs has denied it. The Company will call it a clerical error. I intend to prove otherwise.”

“And you intend to prove it isn’t.”

Ada’s finger rested on the ledger’s spine. The needle on the Beckton meter trembled once and was still, then trembled again, recording the flow of gas through pipes that ran beneath streets she could not see, feeding lamps she could not count, measuring consumption that someone, somewhere, was accounting for in a book she had not yet found.

“The gas went somewhere, Mr. Rook. Ninety-six cubic feet on Tuesday. If it was lit but not paid for, it was used for something. The meter tells us it flowed. The ledger tells us it was recorded. Griggs tells us he did not light it. All three cannot be reconciled by a clerical error.”

“Then what reconciles them?”

Ada looked at the far wall, where a second row of smaller dials was mounted below the first, their faces clouded with age and their brass plates so tarnished that the engraved numbers were barely legible. One of them, set apart from the others on a separate bracket, bore no plate at all. Its needle was motionless.

“What is that one?”

Samuel followed her gaze. He crossed to the wall and crouched again, holding his lamp close to the unmarked dial. The glass was grimed over, and he wiped it with his sleeve. The needle beneath was brass, not steel, and it did not move.

“I don’t know. There’s no plate. No number. It’s on a separate feed from the main line.”

“Is it connected to the Beckton main?”

Samuel traced the pipe from the back of the dial casing. It ran into the wall behind a cluster of larger pipes, disappearing into brick. “I can’t tell from here. It goes into the wall.”

Ada wrote the observation in her notebook: Unmarked dial, separate bracket, pipe enters wall behind main line. Needle stationary. No plate.

“If there is a second meter,” she said, “there is a second record.”

Samuel straightened. “A second ledger?”

“Or a second log. Something that accounts for gas that does not appear in the official record. The branch logs show ninety-six cubic feet for Commercial Road. The main meter shows nine hundred and six total. They agree. But if gas was diverted before it reached the main meter, it would not appear in either log.”

“Diverted where?”

“That is what I intend to find out.”

Ada gathered her ledger, her Tuesday sheet, and her notebook. She squared the edges of the volumes on the desk and set the inspection lamp back in its place. Samuel Rook stood by the unmarked dial, his hand resting on the bracket, his face lit from below by the small lamp he still held.

“Miss Quill.”

She paused at the stairwell door.

“Finch told you not to examine the records.”

“He told me some records are better left unexamined. He did not say which records. He did not say why.”

“And you’re going to keep going.”

Ada looked back at the meter room. The dials hummed. The needles trembled. The unmarked dial on its separate bracket sat motionless, its face clouded, its pipe disappearing into brick. Somewhere above them, the clerking office waited with its frosted window and its filing cabinets and the desk where her ledger lived. Somewhere above that, the Thames moved past the window, carrying barges and refuse and the reflected light of a city that burned gas the company could not fully account for.

“The meter does not lie, Mr. Rook. It has no reason to. If someone has made it appear to lie, then someone has altered what it records. I intend to find the true record.”

She took the stairs up. The corridor was empty. Finch’s door was closed, the handprint gone from the glass, wiped clean or simply dried. Ada walked past it without pausing, her ledger under her arm, the Tuesday sheet pressed flat against its cover, the notebook in her pocket open to the page where she had written: *Ninety-six cubic feet. Commercial Road. Tuesday. Beckton main. Unmarked dial. Second record.
