Chapter 15

Beckton at Night

The pen stopped. Ada Quill held it motionless above the last line of her notebook, where she had transcribed the Beckton meter readings in two neat columns. The ink gathered at the nib’s tip, swelling into a small dark bead that would blot the page if she waited any longer. She lifted the pen and set it down.

The notes for the coroner lay arranged across her desk in the order she intended to present them: the Tuesday sheet with its three Commercial Road entries, the roster showing Griggs detailed elsewhere that night, the copied meter readings from the Beckton district main, and the summary she had drafted in her most careful hand. Each document was a piece of a sequence she could verify. Each was also a piece of a sequence that would ruin her if she presented it to a coroner who had already been told, by the police, that the matter was closed.

She gathered the pages into the satchel and fastened the clasp. The sound was small and definite in the empty office. The frosted window cast its usual grey Thames light across the floor, and the gas bracket on the wall burned with a steady, even flame that she had once found comforting. She looked at it now and wondered where the gas had come from, and whether the meter that measured it had been turned back.

Varney had warned her. More than warned — he had stated, in his murmuring, blunt way, that her position depended on her silence. He had not needed to say what would happen if she continued. The question he had used to dismiss her still sat in her ears: And you propose to tell a coroner that the Gas Light and Coke Company is defrauding its own ledgers, on the evidence of a clerk’s notebook? The company’s official statement, accepted by the police, was that the discrepancy was a clerical error. Griggs was dead. The ledger entry for October fourteenth, which Griggs had insisted was false, had been entered by a hand Ada could not yet identify. Three lamps on Commercial Road had been logged as lit, and the lamplighter assigned to that route had not lit them.

She had organized every fact she possessed. The facts were not enough. What she needed was the physical mechanism — the pipe, the valve, the connection between the district main and wherever the stolen gas was going. The meter room had shown her the deficit. Only the gasworks itself could show her the cause.

She pulled on her coat, picked up the satchel, and left the office.

Rook was waiting by the lamplighters’ yard, leaning against the depot wall with his hands in his pockets. The gas bracket above him threw his shadow across the granite setts. He straightened when he saw her.

“You’ve got the readings?”

Ada opened the satchel and showed him the notebook, the two columns of figures she had copied from the Beckton district meter logs. Rook studied them by the bracket’s light, his grease-smudged thumb holding the page flat.

“These match what I saw in the meter room,” he said. “The consumption’s low for the district. Consistently low, on the nights the ledger shows those three lamps lit.”

“And the deficit is physical. It is not a clerical error.”

“A ledger don’t light a lamp. A man does. But a pipe can move gas without a man ever touching a lamp.” He closed the notebook and handed it back to her. “You want to go to Beckton.”

“I need to go to Beckton. Tonight.”

Rook looked at her for a moment. His hands, which had been resting at his sides, curled slightly, then opened again. “You know what happens if we’re caught inside the works after hours.”

“I know what happens if we are not caught. I know what happens if no one goes.”

“The company sees gas. I see light. Ain’t the same thing.” He pushed himself off the wall. “But I’ve been inside Beckton. I know the layout. If there’s a bypass on the district main, it’ll be at the valve house, where the branch lines meet the feeder.”

“Then we go to the valve house.”

They walked south together, past the company offices, toward the river road that curved east to the gasworks. The night was cold and damp, and the streets were quiet. Ada’s satchel bumped against her hip with each step. Inside it, the notebook and the copied meter readings sat beside the Tuesday sheet and the roster — the documents she had gathered, the sequence she had built, now pointing toward a place she had only seen on maps.

The Beckton gasworks rose from the flat riverside ground like a dark industrial landscape of retort houses, condensers, and gasholders. The silhouette of the largest holder blotted out the stars above the northern section, its curved iron frame rising against the sky. The smell hit them first — coal tar, sulphur, the thick chemical reek of manufactured gas that clung to everything within a quarter mile of the works.

Rook led her along the perimeter wall, keeping to the shadow of a coal yard fence. The wall was brick, twelve feet high, topped with broken glass set in mortar. A gate faced the river road, where a night watchman sat in a wooden hut, his lantern casting a yellow circle on the cobbles.

“There’s an engineer’s entrance,” Rook murmured, close to her ear. “Round the north side, where the maintenance crews come in for the holders. It’s a gate in the wall, steel, and the bolt’s been dodgy for months. I reported it twice.”

They moved along the wall, crouching below the line of the glass. The ground was soft with cinders and coal dust. Ada’s boots crunched, and she slowed, placing each step where Rook had placed his. The watchman’s lantern bobbed in the distance, then steadied. He had not moved.

Rook found the gate. His fingers traced the edge of the steel panel until they found the bolt. He worked it sideways, slowly, and the gate swung inward on hinges that groaned once, softly, and stopped. Rook held it open. Ada stepped through.

Inside the works, the darkness was different. It was alive with sound — the low, constant throb of the exhauster house, the distant clank of machinery, the hiss of steam from the condenser pipes that ran overhead in black iron racks. The ground beneath their feet was packed earth and brick, crossed with drainage channels and cable runs. The air was warm and thick, tasting of oil and iron filings and the acrid bite of ammonia.

Rook moved ahead of her, his hand touching the pipes that ran along the walls of the retort houses, using them as guides. They passed the base of the number three gasholder, its massive iron frame rising above them into darkness, the guide cables humming faintly in the wind. Beyond it, the purifying sheds stood silent, their doors shut.

“The district mains branch off the feeder line here,” Rook said, his voice barely above a breath. “The Beckton-Whitechapel main runs east from the valve house. That’s where we need to be.”

“How far?”

“Past the exhauster house. Two hundred yards, maybe less.”

They crossed an open yard, keeping to the shadow of a coal bunker. The exhauster house throbbed beside them, its windows glowing with the light of the furnaces inside. A worker moved past a window, his silhouette framed for a moment against the fire, then gone.

Rook stopped. His hand came back and pressed against Ada’s arm, holding her still.

Ahead, across a narrow lane between two condenser racks, a building stood that was smaller than the others. It was brick, single-storeyed, with a slated roof and a steel door. The valve house. A faint light leaked from under the door and through a crack where the frame had warped.

“That’s the district valve house,” Rook said. “There shouldn’t be anyone in there at night. The valves are set during the day shift and left. The meters record automatically.”

Ada looked at the line of light under the door. Someone was inside.

Rook crouched below the condenser rack and moved along the lane until he reached the side of the valve house, where a ventilation grille was set into the brick wall at ground level. He dropped to his knees and pressed his face to the grille. Ada followed, kneeling beside him on the packed earth.

Through the grille, the interior of the valve house was visible in the unsteady glow of an oil lamp set on a wooden crate. Brass valve wheels were ranked along the far wall, each one labelled with a district code. The main feeder pipe ran across the ceiling, branching into smaller lines that descended to the valve array. The meter for the Beckton-Whitechapel district main was mounted on the near wall — a brass drum with a counter dial behind a glass face, its pointer steady.

A man stood at the valve array with his back to them. He wore a dark coat, too heavy for the heated air of the valve house, and his hands moved along the valve wheels with a precision that came from long practice. He selected the wheel marked BW-3 — the Commercial Road branch — and turned it counterclockwise. The valve groaned. Then he reached behind the valve rack, where a pipe ran that was not painted with the standard company markings. It was bare iron, newer than the surrounding pipework, and it connected the Commercial Road branch to a line that ran through the back wall of the valve house, exiting into darkness.

The man opened a second valve on the bare pipe. Gas hissed — a low, steady sound that mixed with the ambient noise of the works and was lost in it, unless you were listening for it. The meter on the wall did not move. The gas flowed past it, through the bypass pipe, to wherever the line outside led.

Ada watched the man’s hands. They were pale, well-shaped hands, the nails trimmed short, the left one bearing a smudge of ink on the index finger. She had seen those hands before. They signed her pay chit every fortnight. They initialled the margin of the master ledger when a page was approved. They had signed the route assignment for October fourteenth — the sheet that placed Griggs on Commercial Road, the sheet that placed three lamps as lit when Griggs had not lit them.

Griggs had insisted the ledger was wrong. Griggs had been assigned to a different route that night, but the ledger showed him on Commercial Road. The official position was a clerical error. The police had already spoken to Griggs, and Griggs was dead in the canal. And the hands that had written the false entry, that had signed the route sheet, that had initialled the ledger page, were now turning a valve that bypassed the district meter and sent gas through an unregistered pipe to a destination that did not appear on any company map.

The man turned from the valve array. The oil lamp caught the line of his jaw, the sharp angle of his cheekbone, the steel-rimmed spectacles. Finch. Chief Clerk Finch, who signed off every ledger page, who had been unusually nervous about the ledgers, who had been aware of Ada’s interest in Griggs.

Ada’s hand closed on the strap of her satchel. Her knuckles pressed white against the leather. Rook’s hand was on her wrist, pulling her down, away from the grille. She eased back, her eyes still on the lit rectangle of the ventilation grille, where Finch’s shadow moved across the wall as he checked the meter dial — the meter that was not recording the gas he was diverting.

They retreated along the condenser rack, back through the narrow lane, past the exhauster house and the coal bunker and the base of the gasholder. Rook moved quickly now, his earlier caution replaced by a tight urgency that showed in the set of his shoulders and the length of his stride. Ada kept up, the satchel pressed against her side, the notebook inside it carrying the figures that now had a physical mechanism attached to them.

The engineer’s gate opened and closed behind them. They walked along the perimeter wall until the coal yard fence sheltered them, and then Rook stopped. He was breathing hard. His hands, braced on his knees, were shaking.

“That pipe,” he said. “It runs out the back of the valve house, under the yard. I’ve seen the trench marks. I thought they were drainage.”

“Where does it go?”

“I don’t know. But that’s a four-inch main. At the flow rate I heard, that’s not three lamps’ worth of gas. That’s a whole district’s supply going somewhere it shouldn’t.”

Ada’s fingers were numb. She pressed them against her coat, feeling the cold of the iron grille still on her skin, and the warmth of the satchel strap against her palm. The notebook inside held the meter readings, the Tuesday sheet, the roster, the copied entries. It now also held what she had just seen — though she had not written it down yet, and would not, until her hands were steady enough to hold a pen.

“We need to find where that pipe goes,” she said.

“Not tonight.”

“No. Not tonight.”

They walked back toward the yard, the gasworks shrinking behind them into its dark silhouette against the sky. The smell of coal tar faded as they moved north, replaced by the ordinary stink of the streets. Ada’s satchel bumped against her hip. The clasp held firm.

She had gone to Beckton looking for the physical proof behind a ledger anomaly. What she found was a bypass pipe, an unmarked valve, a meter that did not turn — and the man who operated it. The hands that signed her pay chit, turning a valve in the dark.

The question was no longer whether the gas was being stolen. The question was where it went, and who else knew, and how long before Finch discovered that someone had watched him from the other side of a ventilation grille.

Rook unlocked the yard gate with the key he kept on a chain inside his collar. The yard was empty at this hour, the lamplighters’ stores locked, the bench cold. He lit the single gas mantle above the workbench and turned it low.

Ada opened her satchel and laid the notebook flat. The meter readings she had copied from the Tuesday sheet formed two columns — the official figures and the ones she had transcribed from the secondary readings Thomas Griggs had kept in his own hand before he died. The discrepancy between them was consistent. Fourteen hundred cubic feet per night, give or take.

“That’s not pocket money,” Rook said.

“No.”

She traced the distribution map she had memorized from the company’s mains register. The bypass at Beckton tapped the low-pressure trunk that ran south along the river wall. The trunk served the public lamps on the Thames embankment. But the volume stolen exceeded what any lamp circuit would consume — even if every lamp on the embankment burned double.

Rook pulled the district map from the shelf and spread it over the workbench. His finger followed the trunk line south from Beckton, past the embankment junction, to where the main continued into the private supply network — the lines that fed metered customers who paid the company directly.

“Here.” He tapped a point where the trunk crossed beneath a block of warehouses on Wapping High Street. “There’s a private take-off on this section. Unregistered. I passed it on inspection three years ago and they told me it was a dead leg from the old Imperial system. Capped.”

“Is it capped?”

He looked at her.

They went to Wapping. Rook carried a probe rod and a lantern. Ada carried her notebook. The streets were quiet. The river smell was strong.

The access cover was set into the cobbles behind a warehouse with no sign on its door. Rook pried it up. The shaft dropped into a brick chamber where the trunk main ran. He lowered the lantern.

The dead leg was not capped. It connected to a pipe that ran through the warehouse wall — a private line, unmetered, feeding gas to a customer who paid nothing and appeared on no register. Ada could see the pipe disappearing into the brickwork, could hear the faint hiss of flow through an open valve.

She looked at Rook. The lantern threw his shadow long against the chamber wall.

“This isn’t skimming,” she said.

“No,” he said. “This is a second company.”
