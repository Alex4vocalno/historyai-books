Chapter 22

Varney's Reckoning

What lingered after a verdict was not silence but the building’s own breath — a muffled hum, the sound of stone settling into its weight. The doors of the coroner’s court swung shut behind Ada, and she stood in the marble corridor with both ledgers pressed flat against her chest: the official one and the one Finch had unlocked for her, the one where his handwriting tightened across the columns as the weeks wore on. The corridor smelled of floor polish and old paper.

Down the passage, Finch was being led away by a constable, and just before he turned the corner he looked back at her. His face was grey, the skin loose around his jaw, and his eyes held hers for a full second — not gratitude, not warning, something closer to the look of a man handing off a weight he could no longer carry. Then he was gone, and Varney was left standing ten paces ahead, his back to her, already walking.

“Inspector Varney.”

His stride did not break. The heels of his shoes struck the marble in measured beats, each one carrying him closer to the staircase at the end of the passage.

“A moment, Inspector. I am not finished.”

He stopped. His narrow shoulders rose once, held, and dropped. When he turned, his face was arranged into the expression she had seen at Leman Street — the one that discouraged conversation, that made constables look at their boots and clerks remember urgent errands elsewhere. Behind him, two constables who had been walking toward the stairs slowed. A man in a solicitor’s coat paused at the wall, pretending to read a notice.

“The inquest is concluded, Miss Quill. The coroner has entered his finding. Accidental death.” Varney’s voice was barely above a murmur, the words pushed out as though they cost him something to spend on her. “I don’t believe there’s anything further.”

“The coroner entered a finding based on the evidence presented to him. The evidence was incomplete.”

“Evidence.” He repeated the word as though it were a coin he was weighing on his palm. “You’ve become rather attached to that word.”

Ada stepped forward. The ledgers were warm against her ribs where she had been holding them. She extended the official one first, then Finch’s, side by side, both open to the fourteenth of October.

“The lamplighter’s roster assigns Thomas Griggs to Cable Street through Penfold Street on the night of October fourteenth. The official ledger records three lamps lit on Commercial Road under his initials. Commercial Road is not on his route. He was never assigned there.”

“Clerical error. We’ve been through this.”

“Griggs denied lighting those lamps. He denied it in person, to me, three days before his body was pulled from the canal. The entry in the official ledger is not his. It is in a different hand — a hand that matches the entries in this second ledger.” She held Finch’s book open. “The same clerk wrote both. The official record was altered to match the fabricated one.”

Varney glanced at the page. His gaze moved across it quickly, then returned to her face. “A second ledger. Produced by a clerk who, from your own account, was in some distress at the time. A man under pressure will write many things. It proves nothing except that Finch was frightened, and frightened men make poor copyists.”

“Finch was frightened because he was coerced. The entries in this ledger record gas volumes that do not appear in the Beckton meter logs. The meter registered lower consumption than the delivery ledger claims. Gas was diverted after it passed the meter. The three lamps on Commercial Road were the cover — three lamps logged as lit, their gas charged to the company, while the actual volume went somewhere else.”

“Somewhere else.” Varney let the phrase sit. “You don’t know where.”

“I know the direction. The bypass at Beckton runs toward the river.”

“You visited the works. With the company engineer.” Varney’s lip moved in what might have been a smile. “Unannounced, unaccompanied, and — if I understand the company’s regulations correctly — unauthorized. I wonder what the Gas Light and Coke Company would say about that.”

“They would say what the meter says. The meter does not lie.”

“Meters break. Pipes leak. Numbers get transcribed incorrectly by clerks who spend too long staring at columns.” He stepped toward her, closing the distance. “You are obsessed, Miss Quill. You have taken a discrepancy in a book, a regrettable drowning, and the ramblings of a frightened clerk, and you have built them into a conspiracy because you cannot accept that the world does not run as neatly as your ledgers do.”

The constables behind him had stopped entirely. The solicitor at the wall was no longer pretending to read. Ada could hear the building’s ventilation, a low mechanical breath somewhere above the ceiling.

“You are upset,” Varney continued, his voice dropping further. “You are a woman who has worked very hard in a position that does not afford much recognition, and you have found something that makes you feel important. I understand that. But this is a court of law, not a counting house, and your arithmetic —““Inspector.”

The voice came from behind Varney. The junior constable — a young man with a narrow face and a uniform that fit him as though it had been cut for someone broader — shifted his weight from one foot to the other. His hand went to his collar.

“Sir, about the canal wall.”

Varney turned slowly. “What about it?”

The constable swallowed. “I attended the scene, sir. At Battle Bridge. The body was recovered from the south side of the towpath. The bruise on the deceased’s temple — I noted it in my report. The stone on the south side is rounded. Brick face, rendered over. You’d scrape against it, maybe, but you wouldn’t take a bruise like that from it.”

“And the north side?”

“North side is the retaining wall. Quarry stone, sharp edges. The towpath runs between them, but the body was on the south side. If he’d struck the north wall and fallen in, the current would have had to carry him through the bridge arch and deposit him on the opposite bank. The water doesn’t run that direction there. It’s a still section. That’s why the bargeman found him caught in the pilings.”

The corridor was silent. Varney’s jaw worked once, the muscle tightening visibly above his collar. The solicitor turned a page in the notice on the wall, slowly, as though the text had suddenly become absorbing.

Ada let the silence hold. Then she spoke.

“On the night of October fourteenth, a valve was opened on the Beckton-Whitechapel district main, after the meter. I observed the mechanism. Samuel Rook, the company engineer, confirmed the installation and traced the bypass pipe. Gas was diverted from the company’s supply through an unregistered channel. The three lamps on Commercial Road were recorded as lit to account for the volume that was taken. Thomas Griggs was assigned to a route that did not include Commercial Road. When he saw the entry under his initials, he contested it. Three days later he was dead, with a bruise on his right temple that does not correspond to the stone at the location where his body was found.”

She lifted Finch’s ledger and held it open directly before Varney’s face. The pages were close enough that he could not look past them. His eyes tracked the columns — the tight, cramped figures, the dates, the volumes that did not match the meter logs. Behind him, the junior constable stood with his hands clasped at his belt, and in the polished brass of the constable’s tunic button, Varney’s face appeared in miniature — the narrow features, the mouth compressed, the eyes moving across Finch’s handwriting. The reflection showed what the man himself would not: the stillness that settles over a face when the arithmetic stops working.

“The diversion was the reason,” Ada said. “Griggs saw the false entry. He knew the ledger was wrong. He said so. Someone silenced him.”

Varney’s hand came up and closed the ledger. His fingers pressed the cover flat. He did not push her hand away. He held the book shut between them.

“Do you imagine,” he murmured, “what you are doing?”

“I am establishing a sequence of verifiable facts.”

“You are pulling at threads that are attached to people who will not sit still to be documented.” His voice was barely audible. The constables behind him had gone very still. “This is not your counting house.”

“It is a court corridor. And the court has just heard evidence that the coroner did not have.”

Varney released the ledger. His hand dropped. He stood straight, and his gaze moved past her to the wall, the notice board, the ventilation grille — anywhere that was not her face or the books she held.

“Sir,” the junior constable said. “If the bruise doesn’t match the scene —““I heard you, Perkins.”

The corridor held its quiet. The solicitor turned another page. Somewhere below, a door closed and footsteps receded.

Varney walked to the side of the passage, where an alcove held a small writing desk built into the wall — a clerk’s station, inkwell and blotter, a stack of official forms in a wooden tray. He pulled a form from the tray and spread it flat. His pen went into the ink. He wrote the date, then the case number, then Griggs’s name. The pen moved slowly, each letter formed with deliberate pressure, his knuckles whitening around the barrel.

Ada watched from three paces back. The ledgers were closed now, held at her side. The ink on the form gleamed wetly under the gas lamp above the desk. Varney’s hand paused. He read what he had written. Then he continued, and the pen scratched through the remaining fields — cause of death, circumstances, investigating officer — and at the line marked RECOMMENDATION he wrote, in letters that leaned slightly forward, as though the words were being pushed ahead of something: Reopen. Suspicious circumstances. Murder investigation.

He blotted the form. He pulled a second copy from the tray and pressed it against the first. Then he turned, holding the carbon copy out to her.

Ada took it. The paper was still warm from the pressure of his hand. Their fingers did not touch — he released the edge before she had hold of it, and the sheet dropped a quarter-inch before she caught it.

“The case is reopened,” Varney said. “Murder investigation. You will, of course, make yourself available to the investigating officers.”

“I will.”

“And your engineer. Mr. Rook.”

“He is available.”

Varney’s gaze met hers. The murmur of the court had faded entirely. The corridor was empty now except for the junior constable, who stood at a respectful distance, and the solicitor, who had given up on the notice board and was watching openly.

Varney’s face was composed. The jaw was set, the shoulders squared, the posture of a man who had conceded nothing except the form. But his eyes held hers, and in them was something flat and patient — not defeat, not anger, something closer to a ledger being kept, a column of debits marked carefully against a name.

“You’ve had your moment, Miss Quill.” He adjusted his cuffs with the deliberation of a man who considered the matter beneath further discussion. “Don’t mistake it for anything it isn’t.”

He walked past her, his shoes striking the marble in the same measured beats, and descended the staircase without looking back. The junior constable watched him go, then turned to Ada. He opened his mouth, closed it, and gave a small nod — not deferential, not pitying, something more careful than either. Then he followed his inspector down.

Ada stood in the alcove with the form in her hand. The ink had dried to a dull sheen. She folded it once and placed it in her satchel beside the two ledgers. The gas lamp above the desk hissed faintly, and the flame behind its glass shade burned steadily, throwing a small, steady circle of light onto the marble where Varney had stood.

She descended the staircase three minutes after them. Varney had reached the corridor below, his junior constable trailing, and was angling toward the courthouse’s front entrance. Ada did not call out. She walked until she was level with him and then stepped into his path, blocking the corridor.

“You haven’t answered my question, Inspector.”

Varney stopped. The constable stopped behind him.

“I have answered every question you’ve brought me, Miss Quill. Repeatedly. With patience I am not generally credited for.”

Ada opened her satchel and produced both ledgers — the company’s official copy and her own transcription of Griggs’s figures — holding them open side by side. “Three hundred and forty-seven meter readings, Inspector. These entries don’t differ by accident. They differ by the same margin, in the same direction, every week for eleven months. That is not clerical error. That is arithmetic designed by someone.”

Varney glanced at the pages. His expression did not change.

“You’ve brought me two books of numbers and a theory, Miss Quill. I have a station full of actual crimes.”

“Then I’ll bring you the rest.” Ada turned to a marked page in her notebook. “Griggs told me the meter at Beckton gasworks read six per cent higher than any branch reading on the same supply line. I confirmed it Tuesday. If the branch readings were systematically lowered in the ledger, the difference between actual and recorded output is unaccounted gas. Gas that was drawn off, metered, and sold outside company records.”

She paused.

“Griggs discovered the same thing. A week later he was found dead in the river.”

The corridor was quiet. Somewhere behind a door, a clerk’s pen scratched.

Varney looked at the notebook, then at her. His jaw worked once, the muscles pulling tight beneath the skin.

“Wait here,” he said.

He turned and walked back up the corridor to a door marked with his name. He entered, closed it. The junior constable remained, standing very still.

Twelve minutes later, Varney opened the door. He held a fresh form.

“The Griggs case is reclassified. Murder investigation, active.” He held the form out. His face was composed, professional. “Take it to the clerk at the end of the corridor. He’ll process the filing.”

Ada took the form.

Varney’s eyes held hers for a moment longer than necessary. Not anger. Something colder, more patient — the look of a man marking a name in a ledger of his own.

“Good evening, Miss Quill.”
