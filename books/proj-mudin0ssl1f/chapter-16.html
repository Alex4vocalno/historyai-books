Chapter 16

The Stoker's Silence

The gasworks at Beckton sat against the grey sky like a beached ironclad, its retort houses throwing up plumes of smoke that flattened and spread against the low cloud. Samuel Rook walked the causeway from the ferry landing with Ada Quill half a step behind him, her satchel pressed flat against her hip. The smell hit first — sulfur and coal tar, thick enough to coat the back of the throat — and beneath it the constant, low hiss of gas moving through the main headers, a sound that lived in the walls and the ground and the air itself.

He had last walked this path two nights ago, in the dark, crouched behind the retort house with Ada beside him. They had watched the overflow pipe. They had seen the gas diverted through a valve that should have been sealed, feeding an unregistered line that ran south under the river wall. The meter had been bypassed. The fraud was not skimming. It was a second system, parallel to the official one, drawing gas the company’s own records said did not exist.

Now he was walking back in daylight to get the man who had shown them the valve.

Bill Hobbs worked the afternoon shift on Number Four retort bench. Twenty-three years at Beckton had put thickness through his shoulders and smoothed the skin across his knuckles to candle wax where the burns had healed over burns. He had found the bypass valve during a routine inspection. He had told Rook about it on the canal path, quietly, after hours, because Rook was company and Hobbs trusted company more than he trusted the police.

That had been before last night.

Rook pushed through the mess-room door. The room was a brick box with a tin roof, furnished with benches and a long trestle table where the shift crews ate. Steam from the boiling house next door leaked through the shared wall and left a film of moisture on everything — on the table, on the benches, on the men’s shoulders. Four men sat at the far end, eating bread and drinking tea from enamel mugs. Hobbs sat alone at the near end, his back to the door, hunched over a mug he was not drinking from.

“Hobbs.”

The man’s shoulders pulled together. He did not turn around.

Rook crossed the room and sat across from him. Ada took the bench beside Hobbs, her satchel on her lap. Hobbs stared into the mug. His hands rested on the table, and Rook saw they were raw — not burned raw, but scrubbed raw, the kind of scouring a man does when he is trying to remove something that is not on his skin.

“Bill. We need to talk about what you showed us.”

“Not here.” The words came out low and flat, barely audible under the hiss of the steam pipe. Hobbs did not look up. His eyes moved — left, right, checking the men at the far end of the table — and then fixed on the mug again.

Ada opened her ledger. “I have the meter readings from Tuesday the fourteenth copied here. The district main shows a deficit of three hundred and forty cubic feet against the lamplighters’ ledger. You confirmed the bypass valve on the south header was open that night.”

“I confirmed nothing.”

“You did, Mr. Hobbs. On the canal path, Thursday last. You described the valve, the position of the handle, and the unregistered pipe leading south from—““I said not here.” Hobbs’s voice cracked on the last word. One hand came off the mug and gripped the edge of the table. The fingers were shaking — a fine, rapid tremor, the kind a man cannot stop because it comes from somewhere deeper than his muscles.

Rook glanced at the men at the far end. None of them were looking over. He leaned forward. “Bill, nobody’s asking you to sign anything. We need you to say what you saw, same as you said it Thursday. The valve, the pipe, where it runs. That’s all.”

Hobbs finally looked up. His face had changed. Two nights ago he had been wary, careful, but there had been anger in him — the anger of a man who knew something wrong was happening on his bench and nobody with authority cared to fix it. That anger was gone. What remained was pale, washed out, the skin under his eyes bruised with sleeplessness.

“Men came,” he said, almost inaudibly. “After you left. Last night, after dark. Two men. They knew I’d talked to company people.”

Ada’s pen had stopped. “Who were they? Did they identify themselves?”

“They didn’t need to.”

“Bill.” Rook kept his voice level. “Did they say what they wanted?”

“They said I should forget the valve. The valve was company business, they said, and company would handle it. They said if I remembered it wrong, that was fine, but if I remembered it right, I’d remember to keep my mouth shut about it.” Hobbs’s hand was still gripping the table. “They knew your names. Both of you.”

Ada’s pen remained motionless on the page. Rook saw her fingers tighten around it until the knuckles whitened, but she did not speak.

“When you say they knew our names—““I’m saying they knew your names, Engineer. I’m saying they described you. Her too. The clerk with the ledger. They had it exact.”

Rook sat back. The hiss of the steam pipe filled the silence between them. The men at the far end of the table were gathering their things, pulling on coats, leaving one by one through the far door. The mess room emptied. The silence deepened.

“Bill, I need you to understand something. What you showed us — the bypass, the unregistered line — that’s evidence of theft. Systematic theft. People are profiting from gas the company records say doesn’t exist. Griggs died over this.”

“Don’t.” Hobbs’s hand came off the table and he pressed both palms flat against his thighs, as if holding himself together. “Don’t say that name to me.”

Thomas Griggs told Miss Quill the ledger entry for October fourteenth was false. He said he didn’t light those lamps on Commercial Road. He had been sent to cover another route entirely that night. Three lamps were logged as lit by his hand, and he denied it. He denied it to her face. Two weeks later he was pulled out of the canal with a bruise on his temple and the police called it an accident.

Hobbs was shaking his head, slowly, rhythmically, like a man trying to dislodge water from his ears.

“The meter proves Griggs was right,” Rook continued. “The gas didn’t flow to those lamps. It was diverted. You saw where. You showed me the valve.”

“I showed you a pipe.”

“You showed me a bypass on a sealed main, Bill. You told me it shouldn’t be there. You told me—““I told you nothing.” Hobbs stood up. The bench scraped against the floor. His hands were shaking badly now and he pressed them against his thighs again, hard. “I showed you a pipe. That’s all I did. There are pipes everywhere in this place. I show pipes to engineers. That’s my job.”

Ada looked up from her notebook. Her voice was steady, precise, controlled. “Mr. Hobbs, I am establishing a sequence of verifiable facts. You showed Mr. Rook the bypass valve on the south header at approximately half past nine on Thursday evening. The valve was open. The unregistered pipe was carrying gas. These are facts. They do not require your permission to be true, but they do require your confirmation to be entered into evidence.”

“You’ll get no confirmation from me.”

“Then you are allowing the people who threatened you to control what the company knows about its own distribution system.”

Hobbs turned to face her. His eyes were red-rimmed, and there was something in them — not anger, not defiance, but a kind of desperate, cornered clarity.

“They saw you,” he said. “Both of you. Here. Last night. You think I’m the only one they talked to? You think there’s a man on this bench they don’t own?”

His soot-blackened hand came up, trembling, and pointed past Rook’s shoulder into the cavernous space beyond the mess-room door. The gasworks stretched away into gloom — retort benches, valve galleries, the massive vertical holders rising like sentinels in the steam. Somewhere in that maze, a valve handle clanked and settled.

“They saw you,” Hobbs whispered. “You’re marked.”

His hand dropped. He stepped forward and seized Rook’s arm with a grip that was hard and urgent, and shoved him toward the door. Ada stepped back, clutching her ledger. Hobbs pushed them both into the corridor, and the mess-room door slammed behind them. The bolt shot home with a sound like a hammer falling.

Rook stood in the corridor. The gasworks hissed around him, vast and indifferent. Ada was beside him, her face white, her notebook still open in her hands, the page blank where the confirmation should have been.

“Walk,” he said. “Don’t run. Walk.”

They walked through the valve gallery, past the holders, down the causeway to the ferry landing. The fog had thickened while they were inside. It lay on the river in pale, dense banks that swallowed the far shore and reduced the ferry to a dark shape with a lantern. Rook paid for both of them. They stood at the rail, not speaking, as the boat crossed.

The ferry landed at the southern stair. Rook took the eastward path along the river wall, the one that led to the Commercial Road steps. Ada walked beside him. Her ledger was pressed against her side and her hand rested on it, guarding it. The fog muffled their footsteps and turned the gas lamps along the embankment into soft yellow blurs.

Three streets. Two turnings. A man passed them going west — collar up, hat low, walking quickly. Rook watched him until he disappeared.

“I should not have brought you back there.” He said it quietly, facing forward.

“You needed someone who could verify the ledger entries.”

“I needed a witness. I got you instead.”

“I am perfectly capable of—““That’s not what I mean.” He stopped. Ada stopped beside him. The fog closed around them, wet and cold, smelling of river and coal smoke. “They know your name, Miss Quill. They know your face. They described you to Hobbs. Whatever I thought we were doing — quietly, carefully, by the book — that’s finished. We’re not investigating anymore. We’re being investigated.”

Ada’s hand tightened on the satchel strap. She looked at him directly, and he saw the calculation behind her eyes — the same precise, methodical attention she gave to columns of figures, now applied to the question of her own safety.

“Griggs was right,” she said. “The ledger entry for October fourteenth is false. He denied lighting those lamps. He had been working another route. The meter confirms the gas was diverted. These facts do not change because I am known to the people diverting the gas. They become more urgent.”

“Urgent doesn’t matter if you’re dead.”

The words landed between them. Ada did not flinch. She held his gaze for a moment, then turned and began walking again, north toward the lamplighters’ yard and the streets beyond.

The tram rattled past them on the Whitechapel Road, its windows yellow, its passengers dark shapes behind glass.

They turned into a narrow street. A lamplighter was on his ladder ahead of them, pulling the chain, and the lamp came alive with a soft pop. The light fell on the wet cobbles and on the lamplighter’s back as he climbed down and moved on.

Rook’s hands were deep in his coat pockets. His right hand closed around the lighter he carried on shift — a short iron rod with a hook — and held it.

They passed the yard gates. The lamplighters’ depot was dark, the office shuttered. A light burned in the upper window where the chief clerk had his desk. Rook stopped. Ada stopped beside him.

The light moved behind the glass. A shadow crossed the window — a figure, sitting down, standing up, sitting down again. The shadow’s hand went to its face, then away, then back. A repeated, fidgeting motion, the gesture of a man who cannot keep still at his desk.

Rook watched the window. The hand went to the face again.

“Finch is still at his desk,” he said.

Ada looked up. The shadow moved behind the glass, restless, jerky, the hand rising and falling.

“It’s past nine,” she said.

The light stayed on. The hand kept moving.

They walked on, past the yard, into the streets where the lamps stood lit and the fog turned each one into a halo. Rook matched his stride to Ada’s. He kept his eyes on the pavement ahead, on the doorways, on the gaps between buildings where alleys ran dark. Every figure ahead of them was a shape to be watched until it passed.

The stairs to Ada’s lodging were narrow, stone, slick with damp. Rook climbed behind her to the landing and waited while she found her key. The corridor was empty. A gas lamp burned at the far end, its flame jumping behind a cracked glass.

Ada unlocked the door. She stood on the threshold, her satchel still pressed against her side, and turned to face him.

“Thank you for walking me back.”

“I’ll come by in the morning. Seven o’clock. Don’t leave this building before then.”

She nodded once, precisely, the way she nodded when a figure balanced. She stepped inside and began to close the door. Rook put his hand against the frame.

“Miss Quill.”

She paused.

The fog had followed them up the stairs. It hung in the corridor, pale and thick, softening the edges of the gas lamp, turning the far wall into a grey blankness. Somewhere below, a door opened and closed. Footsteps on the street outside — measured, unhurried — passed the front of the building and continued into the fog.

Rook looked at the fog. He looked at the corridor. He looked at Ada Quill standing in her doorway with her satchel and her notebook and the pen behind her ear, and he asked the only question that mattered.

“Do you feel watched?”

The gas lamp at the end of the corridor jumped. The fog pressed against the window behind him. Ada’s hand rested on the edge of the door, and she did not answer.
