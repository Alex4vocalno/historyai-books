Chapter 19

Preparing the Testimony

The kitchen table had become a field of evidence, every surface covered, every edge claimed. Finch’s secret ledger sat at the center, its cover warped where his sweating hands had gripped it. The leather was still damp. Ada had carried it home against her ribs, pressed beneath her coat like a wound that needed holding, and the smell of it — ink, sweat, the staleness of a drawer kept locked too long — still rose from the pages when she turned them. Around it she had arranged the rest: her own ledger laid flat, its columns a rebuke to the chaos surrounding it; the notebook of copied meter readings from Beckton, open to October; the Tuesday sheet that had started everything, pinned under a cup of tea gone cold and dark.

Rook’s sketches of the pipe network leaned against the wall. Griggs’s duty roster lay faceup beside the secret ledger, its entries in her own careful hand, transcribed weeks ago when the discrepancy had been a puzzle rather than a grave.

She had begun sorting at half past seven, pulling the pieces apart and reassembling them by date, then by district, then by type. The work should have steadied her. It had always steadied her, the orderly motion of classification, the comfort of a column that balanced. Tonight the papers multiplied under her hands. Every sheet she lifted exposed another beneath it, and every entry she checked sent her back to a cross-reference that contradicted or complicated the one before it. The meter readings from Beckton showed a deficit on October fourteenth. The lamplighters’ ledger showed three lamps lit on Commercial Road that same night. Griggs’s roster showed him assigned to Cable Street, two districts north. The secret ledger showed something else entirely, written in Finch’s cramped, hurried hand, and she had not yet read past the first page because the first page had stopped her cold.

The gas lamp on the wall hissed, its mantle a trembling white point. Coal dust from the grate had settled on the table’s edge. Her pen rested where she had set it down, its nib leaving a small dark pool on the wood — ink that had been gathering while she sat motionless, staring at the entry she had uncovered. She wiped the pool away with her sleeve, already moving before the thought had formed, and turned back to the ledger’s first page.

Finch had given it to her with shaking hands. His voice had cracked on the word careful. The ledger’s cover still held the impression of his grip, four pale crescents where his thumbnails had dug into the leather.

She had opened it on the kitchen table and the pages had fallen apart in her hands, loose and disordered, as though Finch had leafed through them so often the binding had given way. Some entries were written in his usual clerk’s hand, precise and small. Others were rushed, the letters jagged, the ink smeared where his hand had dragged across wet lines.

The dates ran from August through October. The districts covered the eastern mains: Commercial Road, Cable Street, the Highway, Three Colt Street. Each entry followed the same pattern — a location, a number of lamps, a volume of gas diverted in cubic feet.

Some carried a notation she did not recognise: a single letter, followed by a sum in shillings. The entries with that notation corresponded exactly to the dates when the Beckton meter showed its largest deficits.

She had copied the first six entries into her notebook before the pattern forced her to stop. The numbers were too regular, too deliberate, to be anything but systematic. This was not a clerk’s error or a lamplighter’s dishonesty. It was a record of theft, maintained in a hidden ledger by the man responsible for keeping the official books clean.

A knock at the door. Two sharp raps, a pause, then one more — Rook’s signal. She crossed the room and lifted the latch. He stood in the hallway with his satchel over one shoulder, grease still black in the creases of his knuckles, and a roll of paper under his arm.

“You look like you haven’t moved since yesterday.”

“I haven’t.”

He stepped past her into the kitchen and set the satchel down. His gaze moved across the table, reading the arrangement of papers the way he read a pipe junction — tracing flow, looking for the blockage. Without speaking, he unrolled the paper he had brought and weighted its corners with the teacup and the salt cellar. It was a diagram of the Beckton distribution network, drawn in his precise engineer’s hand, showing the main feeder pipes, the district branches, and — marked in red ink — the bypass they had found behind the meter house.

“This is what we found at Beckton.” He traced the bypass with his thumb. “Pipe comes off the main, after the meter, before the district branch. Gas flows through it, goes unrecorded, and feeds whatever’s at the other end.”

“Which is what?”

“Can’t say from the pipe alone. But the volume matches.” He pulled a folded sheet from his satchel — a column of figures, the Beckton meter readings for October, which he had copied on their second visit to the works. “Look. October ninth, deficit of twelve cubic feet. October eleventh, deficit of nine. October fourteenth —“His finger stopped on the figure. “October fourteenth, deficit of fifteen. That’s the largest single diversion in the month.”

Ada opened the secret ledger to the entry she had been staring at when he arrived. Her finger rested on the line. The ink was smudged, the letters pressed deep, as though the pen had been driven into the page. Commercial Road, three lamps, divert 15 cu. ft. G. knows. Deal with it. The date was October fourteenth.

Rook leaned forward. His hand, still grease-darkened, hovered over the page without touching it.

“G. knows,” he said.

“Thomas Griggs.”

“The night he drowned.”

Ada pulled Griggs’s duty roster closer. The entry for October fourteenth was in her own hand, transcribed from the lamplighters’ assignment board. Griggs had been assigned to Cable Street — two districts north of Commercial Road, a forty-minute walk at lamplighting pace. He should not have been anywhere near Commercial Road that night. But the official lamplighters’ ledger, the one she had kept and trusted, recorded three lamps lit on Commercial Road under his name.

“He told me the ledger was wrong,” she said. “He came to me on the twelfth, two days before. He said he had not lit those lamps, that the entry was false, that someone was using his name to cover something. I wrote it down as a complaint. I filed it with Finch.”

“Finch.”

“Finch received the complaint. Finch had the secret ledger. Finch recorded the diversion for October fourteenth in this book, with the notation ‘G. knows. Deal with it.’ Two days later, Griggs was dead in the canal.”

Rook straightened. He looked at the diagram, then at the ledger, then at the roster. His jaw worked once, the muscle tightening, and he sat down in the chair opposite hers.

“So Finch ordered the diversion. Griggs found out, or was getting close. Finch writes ‘deal with it,’ and Griggs turns up drowned.”

“That is what the dates show. Whether Finch gave the order himself or passed it to someone else — that I cannot prove from this ledger alone.”

“But you can prove the diversion happened. The meter confirms it.”

“I can prove gas was stolen. Finch recorded it—I can prove that too. I can prove Griggs was assigned to a different route that night, which means the official ledger entry under his name is false. He complained about that false entry; I can prove it. And I can prove he died two days after the complaint, on the same night the largest diversion occurred.” She closed the secret ledger, then opened it again to the same page. The entry stared up at her, Finch’s hand unmistakable. “What I cannot prove is who killed him, or on whose authority.”

“Will that matter? At the inquest?”

“It will matter to Varney. He will say the diversion is a company matter, the death is an accident, and the two are unconnected. He will say Finch is a frightened clerk who kept poor records. The meter deficit, he will say, is a mechanical fault, and he will point to your own report, which states the bypass pipe could not be dated.”

Rook’s mouth tightened. “I wrote what I found. The pipe was old, the joints corroded. I could not date the installation.”

“And Varney will use that. He will say the bypass could have been there for years, that the deficits are consistent with leakage, that the whole thing is a maintenance failure, not a crime.”

The gas lamp hissed. A draft from the window stirred the edges of the papers, and Ada pressed her hand flat on the table to hold them still.

“Then we present what we have,” Rook said. “We present the meter readings, the secret ledger, the roster, the false entry. We let the coroner draw the conclusion.”

“The coroner who was appointed by the same magistrate who accepts the company’s statements?”

“You have a better idea?”

She did not. That was the problem. The evidence was complete — it lay before her in ordered piles, each document cross-referenced, each date aligned, each figure checked twice. But evidence was not the same as proof, and proof was not the same as justice, and the distance between the three was exactly the width of a corridor in Leman Street police station where Varney sat behind his desk and decided which truths were inconvenient.

“We go to the inquest,” she said. “We present the full record. The secret ledger, the meter readings, the roster, the false entry, the bypass diagram. We do not editorialise. We do not accuse. We lay the documents before the coroner and let the dates speak.”

“And if Varney challenges the authenticity of the secret ledger?”

“Finch gave it to me himself. His hand is on every page. The company’s own handwriting analyst can confirm it.”

“And if the company refuses to produce one?”

“Then we have copies.” She opened her notebook to the pages where she had transcribed the ledger’s entries. “I made these last night. Every line, every date, every figure. If the original disappears, the copies remain.”

Rook looked at her steadily. His hands rested on the table, grease-blackened, scarred from pipe fittings and meter housings. He had come to her lodgings at nine in the evening with a roll of diagrams and a satchel of copied readings, knowing that what they were assembling would end his career if it failed and endanger them both if it succeeded.

“If we do this, there’s no going back. The company will dismiss us both. Varney will have grounds for obstruction, or worse.”

“I know.”

“You hold a clerk’s position, Miss Quill. A wage, a place in the company structure. I’m a field engineer with no name that carries weight. If this fails, twelve years of work in gas distribution ends.”

“I am aware of the cost.”

“Are you?” He leaned forward. “Because the way you’re sitting there, with those papers laid out like a sermon, you look like a woman who’s already decided. And I need to know — are you asking me to stand with you, or telling me I already have?”

She met his gaze. The lamp hissed between them, its light steady and white, casting their shadows long against the far wall. The evidence lay on the table between them, complete and ordered, each piece in its place, each date aligned with the next, the whole of it forming a pattern that pointed from a false ledger entry through a dead man’s complaint to a smudged line in a hidden book that read G. knows. Deal with it.

“I am asking you.”

Rook exhaled slowly. He picked up his diagram of the Beckton network and studied it for a long moment, tracing the bypass pipe with his thumb, following its path from the main feeder to the point where it disappeared into the dark beneath the river.

“Then I’m standing.”

They worked for another hour, arranging the documents in the order they would present them. Ada wrote the sequence on a fresh sheet: the official ledger entry for October fourteenth, the duty roster showing Griggs on his regular Whitechapel southern section, the Beckton meter readings for the same date, the secret ledger entry with its smudged ink and its notation, the bypass diagram showing where the unrecorded gas had gone. Each piece led to the next. Each date locked into the one before it. The false entry under Griggs’s name had been the first thread, pulled weeks ago in a clerking office that now seemed to belong to another life.

When the sequence was complete, Ada read it aloud, slowly, the way she would read it to the coroner. Rook listened, occasionally nodding, once raising a hand to pause her so he could check a figure against his own copy of the meter readings. The numbers matched. They had always matched. The system was not broken; it had been bent, deliberately, by hands that knew exactly where to apply pressure.

She finished reading. The room was quiet. The embers in the grate had gone to ash, and the lamp’s mantle had dimmed to a pale yellow. The evidence lay before them, ordered and complete, each document in its place, waiting for morning and a room where it would be spoken aloud. Ada placed her hand flat on the secret ledger’s cover. The leather was dry now, the impression of Finch’s grip still visible in the four pale crescents at its edge. She closed her eyes, then opened them. The work was done. What remained was the speaking of it.
