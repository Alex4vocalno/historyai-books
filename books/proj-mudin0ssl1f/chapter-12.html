Chapter 12

Varney's Ultimatum

Rook’s question stayed with her long after the canal towpath gave way to paving stones. If the gas wasn’t being stolen, where was it going? The question had no answer yet, but it had edges, and those edges cut into her as she walked south through the fog, her satchel pressed against her hip with the weight of everything she had copied and carried and cross-referenced to reach this point. She had turned the question over during the omnibus ride, held it against the rhythm of the engine and the clatter of windows in their frames, and by the time she stepped down onto the pavement near Leman Street, the question had become something harder: not where the gas was going, but who was directing it there, and whether the same hand had pushed Thomas Griggs into the canal.

The station smelled of pipe tobacco and old paper, a scent that clung to the plaster walls and the dark wood of the bench in the corridor. Ada had been here before. The duty sergeant nodded her through without a word, and she climbed the stairs with her satchel held close against her ribs, the notebook inside shifted to the top so she could reach it quickly. The gas bracket on the landing threw a weak yellow light across the numbered doors. She stopped outside the one she remembered, straightened her collar, and knocked.

“Come in.”

Detective Inspector Varney sat behind a desk burdened with stacked files and a tin ashtray still smoking from his last pipe. His jacket hung on the chair behind him, and his shirtsleeves were rolled to the forearm, though the brass buttons of his waistcoat were done up to the collar. The room was narrow, warm from the gas heater in the corner, and lit by a single bracket that put a hard edge on every surface. He looked up from a file he had been reading, and his face, built to discourage prolonged conversation, settled into an expression of professional patience that was worse than hostility.

“Miss Quill.”

“Detective Inspector. I have new information regarding the Griggs case.”

“The Griggs case is closed.”

“With respect, it should not be.”

She set her satchel on the corner of his desk and drew out the notebook, the Tuesday sheet she had copied, and the chart she had drawn late the previous night by the light of her own gas bracket. The chart was clean work. She had ruled it herself, three columns: date, Beckton meter reading, and lamplighter ledger entry for the Commercial Road lamps. The discrepancies ran down the page in a column of red ink.

“The October fourteenth ledger entry records three lamps lit on Commercial Road under Thomas Griggs’s name. Griggs told me directly that he was assigned to the Commercial Road route that night, and that he never lit those lamps. Someone else entered his name.”

“You told me this before.”

“I am telling you again because I now have the meter readings to prove the entry is false.”

She opened the notebook to the page of copied Beckton logs and laid it beside the chart, her finger tracing the column of figures. The numbers were steady, regular, unremarkable — except on the dates corresponding to the disputed ledger entries, where the Beckton meter recorded a drop in district consumption that coincided exactly with gas the ledger claimed had been burned.

“If three lamps on Commercial Road were lit as the ledger states, the district meter should show an increase in consumption for those hours. Instead, the meter shows a decrease. The gas was not consumed by those lamps. It went somewhere else.”

Varney did not look at the chart. He looked at her hand on the page, then at the page itself, then at her face. His expression did not change. He picked up his pipe, tamped the half-burnt tobacco with his thumb, and relit it with a match he struck on the edge of the tin ashtray. The smoke rose between them in a blue-grey curtain.

“And where, exactly, do you believe it went?”

“I do not yet know where. I know it did not go where the ledger says it went. The ledger is wrong, and the meter proves it. Griggs insisted the ledger was wrong before he died. He insisted he was on a different route. He insisted he did not light those lamps. The meter confirms every one of those claims.”

“The Company has stated, in writing, that the ledger entry was a clerical error, subsequently corrected.”

“The Company’s statement is itself incorrect. A clerical error would not produce a matching deficit in the Beckton meter. A clerical error is ink on paper. This is a physical discrepancy in gas flow. Someone diverted that gas, and someone falsified the ledger to conceal the diversion. Griggs discovered it, or he was blamed for it, and now he is dead.”

Varney set the pipe down in the ashtray. The bowl clinked against the tin. He leaned back in his chair, and the chair creaked under the shift of his weight. His hands folded across his waistcoat, and the brass buttons caught the gaslight.

“You have been busy, Miss Quill.”

“I have been thorough.”

“You visited the Beckton gasworks. On the night of October the twenty-third. In the company of a Mr. Samuel Rook.”

The chair edge pressed hard into the backs of her thighs. She had not told anyone outside Rook. The visit had been after hours, through the maintenance entrance, with Rook’s key. The meter room had been warm and thick with the taste of hot oil and iron filings, and the brass meters had ticked in the dark like clocks counting down to something she could not yet name. No one had seen them. She was certain no one had seen them.

“You were seen.” Varney’s voice was low, almost conversational, which made it worse. “The Company’s night watchman keeps a log, as part of his duties. The log was forwarded to this station, and it happens to note two visitors entering through the maintenance gate at eleven-forty p. m. and leaving at twelve-twenty a. m. He did not identify you by name, but he described a woman in a dark coat and a man with grease on his hands. That would be Mr. Rook, I take it.”

Ada’s hand was still on the notebook. The page beneath her fingers showed the column of red ink figures, the neat rows of dates and meter readings she had copied so carefully, and the chart she had ruled by lamplight in her own room. The evidence was all there, laid out in the correct order, the sequence of verifiable facts she had spent weeks assembling. It looked exactly as she had intended it to look: clear, methodical, irrefutable. On the desk between them, under the gaslight, it looked very small.

“What I want to understand,” Varney continued, picking up his pen and turning it slowly between his fingers, “is what you imagine the purpose of this visit was. You are a clerk. Your duties involve recording meter readings supplied to you by the Company. They do not involve entering Company property after hours to inspect mechanical instruments. That is not clerical work, Miss Quill. That is trespass, at minimum. At maximum, it is an attempt to interfere with evidence in an investigation that this station has already concluded.”

“The investigation concluded that Griggs drowned accidentally.”

“The investigation concluded that Thomas Griggs, a lamplighter with a known history of drink, fell into Regent’s Canal while intoxicated. The coroner returned a verdict of accidental death. The Company accepted the verdict. The case is closed.”

“There is a bruise on his temple.”

“There was a minor contusion consistent with striking his head against the pilings. The pathologist noted it in the report. It is not evidence of foul play.”

“It is evidence of a blow.”

Varney looked at her. His eyes were pale, and in the gaslight they seemed almost colourless, like water over stone. He let the silence hold for several seconds, and then he leaned forward, slowly, until his face was close enough that she could smell the tobacco on his breath and see the grain of the stubble along his jaw.

“Miss Quill. I am going to tell you something, and I suggest you listen with particular care.” He tapped a thick finger on her chart, on the column of red ink discrepancies she had drawn so precisely. “This is a ticket to the workhouse. Not a clue.”

He straightened. His hand stayed on the chart, covering her careful columns.

“You will cease your inquiries into the Griggs matter. Your visits to Company property after hours must also stop, as must your collaboration with Mr. Rook, which I suspect your superiors have not authorized. If you continue, I will be obliged to inform Chief Clerk Finch that one of his clerks has been conducting an unauthorized investigation, trespassing on Company property, and attempting to interfere with a concluded police investigation. I do not think I need to describe what that would mean for your position.”

“You are threatening my employment because I brought you evidence of a crime.”

“I am advising you of the consequences of your actions. There is a difference, though I appreciate it may seem a fine one from where you sit.”

Ada’s fingers were still on the notebook page. The paper was slightly damp from her palm, and the red ink had blurred at the edge of one figure. She looked at the blur, at the small imperfection in her careful work, and then at Varney’s hand covering the rest of the chart. His knuckles were broad, the nails trimmed short. A policeman’s hand.

“Griggs told me the ledger was false. He told me he was on the Commercial Road route. He told me he did not light those lamps. Two weeks later he was pulled from the canal with a bruise on his head. You are asking me to believe that is coincidence.”

“I am asking you to accept the findings of a coroner’s inquest and the official statement of your employer. That is not a great deal to ask.”

“It is, when the findings are wrong.”

Varney picked up the file he had been reading when she entered and opened it, angling the page toward the gaslight. The conversation, by every signal he could send, was over. He did not look at her.

“You have your advice, Miss Quill. I would take it.”

Ada closed the notebook. She gathered the Tuesday sheet and the chart, folding the chart along the crease she had made for her satchel, and placed everything back inside. The satchel buckled with a small snap of metal. She stood, and the chair scraped against the floorboards with a sound that seemed very loud in the quiet room.

“Thank you for your time, Detective Inspector.”

Varney turned a page in his file. “Good afternoon, Miss Quill.”

The corridor outside was dimmer than the office, the gas brackets spaced further apart, and by the time she reached the stairwell the light had gone mostly grey. She descended the stairs with her hand on the iron railing, her satchel against her hip, her breath coming in short, measured pulls that she counted to keep even. The duty sergeant did not look up as she passed through the lobby. The door was heavy, and the street hit her with a wall of cold, damp air that tasted of coal-smoke and the river.

Fog had thickened since she had entered the station. It lay across Leman Street in pale bands that turned the gaslamps into dim yellow smears. The pavement was slick, and her boots found the kerb by feel as much as by sight. She stood on the edge of the street with her satchel held against her chest, and the fog closed around her until the station door behind her was only a darker shape in the grey.

She had prepared for dismissal, for condescension, for the murmur and the rhetorical questions and the bureaucratic shrug. But she had not prepared for this. Varney knew about Beckton. The night watchman’s log, the descriptions, the times — someone had brought that information, and whoever had brought it had done so for a reason. Varney was not merely indifferent. He was actively, deliberately, and specifically obstructing her, and he had the means to end her employment with a single letter to Finch.

Finch, who was already aware of her interest in Griggs. Finch, who had been nervous about the ledgers, whose pen had hovered over the page when she asked about the October fourteenth entry, whose hand had trembled almost imperceptibly before he found his answer. If Varney wrote to Finch, the letter would not merely cost her a position. It would place her inside a system where the person responsible for verifying the accuracy of the ledger was the same person who had reason to fear what the ledger might reveal.

She began to walk. The fog muffled her footsteps and the footsteps of the few other figures on the pavement, all of them grey shapes moving through grey air. A cart passed, its wheels hissing on the wet stones, the driver a dark smudge on the box. She walked south, toward the river, though she had no destination. The cold ate through her coat in minutes, and her hands, gripping the satchel strap, went numb.

Griggs had stood in front of her desk, cap in hand, and said the ledger was wrong. He had been plain about it, not angry, not agitated, just certain — a man who knew his own route and knew that someone else’s name had been written over his. She had written down what he told her, in her own hand, in her own notebook, and the notebook was in the satchel against her chest. The ink was still there. The fact was still there. Griggs was in the ground, and the fact was still there.

If she stopped now, the fact would die with him. The meter readings would sit in her notebook until the notebook was lost or thrown away, and the ledger entry would remain as it was — corrected, official, closed. Varney would have his quiet street. The Company would have its orderly records. And Thomas Griggs, lamplighter, would have walked along the canal in the dark and fallen in, and no one would ever ask why.

She crossed a narrow street where the fog thinned enough to show the cobblestones, and on the far side a gaslamp burned with a steady yellow flame that threw a small circle of light on the wet ground. She stopped inside the circle. The light was warm on her boots, and the flame sat behind its glass panel without flickering, fed by a pipe that ran beneath the street she was standing on, connected to a main that ran from Beckton through the district, metered and recorded and logged by clerks who sat at desks and wrote down numbers that were supposed to be true.

The numbers were not true. She knew it. She had the proof in her satchel, and the proof was a sequence of verifiable facts, and the facts led from a false ledger entry to a dead man in a canal to a police inspector who wanted her to stop asking questions. She was not going to stop.

She turned north, toward the yard, where the maps were kept in the engineering offices, where Rook would be at his bench with his grease-smudged hands and his knowledge of every pipe and valve in the district. Varney could write to Finch. He could take her position, her income, the small room she rented and the orderly life she had built around the keeping of accurate records. He could not make her unsee what she had seen, and he could not make her unknow what she knew.

The fog pressed close. She walked into it, and the gaslamp behind her faded to a smear, and then to nothing.
