Chapter 20

The Night Before

What the secret ledger revealed was a man writing under duress. The cold tea had gone bitter an hour ago, but Ada drank it anyway, swallowing the last of it as she turned the page to October the fourteenth. Finch’s handwriting filled the columns in a cramped, obedient script — nothing like the confident flourishes of his official entries. These numbers had been written by a man composing them under someone’s eye. She traced the row for Commercial Road, southern section: three lamps, logged as lit, gas consumption recorded and reconciled to the master meter at Beckton. Griggs’s route. Griggs’s initials.

But Griggs had told her, standing in the yard with his tallow tin in one hand and his small scissors thrust through his belt, that he had never walked Commercial Road that night. He had been sent east, to the canal bridges, covering for another man who called in sick. The roster sheet in her satchel confirmed it — a substitute named in the margin, a route altered by a single stroke of Finch’s pen.

The official ledger showed Griggs on Commercial Road. The roster sheet showed him at Battle Bridge. Two records, two truths, and a dead man in the canal who had insisted the ledger was wrong until the day someone made certain he could not insist anymore.

She closed the secret ledger and opened her own notebook, where the copied meter readings lay in her careful transcription. The numbers were dry, exact, irrefutable. Beckton’s output for the southern district on October the fourteenth exceeded what the official consumption ledger recorded by enough cubic feet to supply three lamps for six hours. The discrepancy was not a clerical error. It was the mechanical signature of gas moving through pipes that no one had authorized, past a meter that someone had prevented from turning. Finch’s second ledger recorded the true figures — the actual output, the actual consumption, the actual deficit — and then recorded them again, falsified, to match the books the company would show if anyone asked. Two sets of numbers. One for the men who took the gas. One for the men who paid for it.

The lamplight from the window of her lodging caught the edge of the page and threw a faint bar of gold across the ink.

She looked at the figures and saw, for the first time, not arithmetic but a man — Griggs, standing in the yard, his lantern hanging from his fist, the flame jumping in the draft while he told her the entry was wrong. He had not been angry. He had been frightened. She had not understood that then. She had thought his voice rough from the cold, his hands unsteady from the work. But the roughness was fear.

He knew what the false entry meant, and he knew who had written it, and he told a clerk with a notebook anyway, because eleven years of lighting lamps on the same street had given him a stubbornness about what was his. His route. His initials. His name in a ledger that lied.

Finch’s face came next — Finch at his desk, Finch with the second ledger unlocked and open, Finch’s fingers trembling so badly that the pages rattled. He had not spoken smoothly. The words came out in broken pieces, each one dragged loose. He said he had been told to keep the record, that he had no choice, and that the men who told him would know if he refused. His eyes had gone to the door twice while he talked, and his hand stayed on the ledger as though letting go of it would let something worse through. She had watched his hand on the leather cover and thought: this man is not the architect. This man is the mortar between the bricks.

She pressed her palms flat against the desk and closed her eyes. The room smelled of cold tea and ink and the tallow candle she had been burning for three hours. Tomorrow she would sit in a room with a coroner and speak these numbers aloud, and the numbers would do what numbers always did — they would stand where she put them and say exactly what they said. But the room would also contain Finch, and whatever came for Finch would come because she had spoken. The ledger was true. The ledger would also be a weapon. She had spent her life keeping records so that the records would keep others, and tomorrow she would carry a record into a room and use it to break a man who had already been broken by the same machinery that had broken Griggs.

The candle guttered. She blew it out, gathered her satchel, and left the lodging.

The walk to Regent’s Canal took twenty minutes through streets she knew by heart — past the lamplighters’ yard, where the gate was chained for the night, along the towpath where the bricks were slick with damp. The canal was black at this hour. The surface barely moved, and what movement there was caught the distant gaslight from the bridge and broke it into copper fragments. She walked slowly along the bank, past the mooring rings set into the stone, past the bollards where the rope lines had worn grooves over decades. The police report said Griggs had been pulled from the water near the third bollard. She stopped there and looked down.

The water lapped against the stone in a slow, rhythmic slap. The smell rose from the canal in a wave — river mud, rotting weed, the mineral tang of iron from the old mooring chains. She crouched and let her hand rest on the damp stone. The canal was a working waterway. It carried coal barges and refuse and the runoff of a hundred streets. It had taken Griggs from its surface and held him under, and the official record said he had fallen. The bruise on his temple said something else, but bruises were not testimony, and the coroner had already written his verdict.

She turned to leave. Her hand, bracing against the stone, touched something that was not stone — something soft, heavy, waterlogged, caught on the rusted mooring ring just below the lip of the bank. She pulled it free. The weight of it surprised her.

A lamplighter’s cap, the wool kind the company issued, dark with canal water, the brim misshapen and the lining rotted through. The company badge was still stitched to the front, the brass tarnished green. She turned it in her hands. The wool was cold, and the water ran from it in a steady thread across her fingers and down her wrist. The smell of decay came off it — not the clean mineral smell of the canal but something organic, something that had been under the water for a long time, softening, breaking down.

The cap was Griggs’s. She knew it the way she knew his handwriting — by the shape of it, the particular way the brim had been bent from years of being tucked under his arm while he trimmed wicks. He had worn it the day he spoke to her in the yard. The cap had been in the canal since the night he died, and no one had searched for it, because no one had been looking.

She held it in both hands. The water dripped from it steadily, tapping the stone between her shoes. The canal moved against the bank with its slow, indifferent rhythm, and the gaslight from the bridge caught the surface and scattered. She pressed the sodden wool against her chest. The cold of it went through her coat, through her blouse, against her skin. This was not a number, nor an entry in a column. It was the cap of a man who had told the truth and been killed for it, and the canal had kept it, and the canal had given it to her, and she would carry it into the room tomorrow and set it on the table beside the ledger and let the coroner look at both.

She folded the cap inside her satchel, where the notebook and the copied meter readings already lay. The weight of the bag had changed. She walked north.

The Beckton gasworks gate was a quarter-mile from her lodging, set back from the road behind a low wall. Rook was already there, standing with his hands in his coat pockets, the grease stains on his sleeves invisible in the dark but present — she could smell the coal tar and machine oil on him from three paces. He straightened when he saw her.

“You shouldn’t be walking alone.”

“I am not alone. You are here.”

“That ain’t what I mean.”

“I know what you mean.” She stopped in front of him. “I found something at the canal. Griggs’s cap. It was caught on a mooring ring near where they pulled him out.”

Rook pulled his hands from his pockets. “His cap.”

“The wool kind. Company badge still stitched to it. It has been in the water since he died.”

Rook looked at her satchel, then at her face. The gasworks behind him was quiet, the retorts banked for the night, only the distant clank of a cooling pipe breaking the silence. He was quiet for a moment.

“You bringing it tomorrow?”

“I am bringing everything tomorrow. The ledgers, the meter copies, the roster sheet, the cap.”

“Finch will be there.”

“Yes.”

“And Varney.”

“Varney can attend or not. The coroner’s inquest is a public proceeding. I have been called as a witness. I will witness.”

Rook nodded slowly. His hands went back into his pockets. He looked at the ground, then at the gate, then at her again. “I’ll stand with you. I can speak to the bypass pipe. I measured it. I can give the dimensions, the flow rate, the direction of the lateral. That’s physical evidence. That’s not a clerk’s interpretation — that’s a pipe a man can put his hand on.”

“You do not have to do that.”

“I know I don’t have to. A ledger don’t light a lamp. A man does. And a pipe don’t carry gas by itself neither. Someone laid it. Someone opened a valve. I can tell the coroner what I saw, and what I saw ain’t in any book Ada Quill ever copied.”

She regarded him steadily. The coal smell from the works was strong tonight, carried on a draft that moved across the yard and out toward the river. His face was difficult to read in the dark, but his voice was the same as always — direct, level, the words chosen for their mechanical accuracy.

“The company will dismiss you.”

“The company can do what it likes. I’ve been inspecting pipe for nine years. I know what a clean joint looks like and what a tampered one looks like, and the joint on that bypass was tampered. I’ll not sit in an office and pretend I didn’t see it.”

“Then we go together.”

“We go together.”

He extended his hand. She took it. His grip was rough, the palm calloused from wrenches and pipe fittings, the fingers strong. He held her hand for a moment longer than courtesy required, then released it. He stepped back.

“Get some sleep.”

“I will try.”

She walked home through the empty streets. The lamps along the road burned steadily, their flames upright in the still air, each one a point of light that someone had lit and someone had recorded. She passed under them and counted them without meaning to — a habit so deep it was nearly involuntary. Fourteen lamps on this stretch. Fourteen entries in the ledger. Fourteen small flames burning because a man had walked a route and done his work and written his initials. The system was sound. The records were sound. The fault was not in the gas or the pipes or the lamps. The fault was in the men who had bent the system to their own purpose and used the ledger — her ledger, the instrument she had been trained to trust absolutely — to cover the bending.

Her lodging was dark when she entered. She lit the candle again and set it on the desk. She removed the cap from her satchel and laid it on a cloth to dry. The wool would not recover its shape. The lining was gone. But the badge was legible, and the stitching was intact, and the cap was unmistakably a company-issued article, unmistakably old, unmistakably Griggs’s. She placed it at the corner of the desk.

Then she opened her notebook and laid out her testimony in order. The roster sheet first — Griggs’s assignment to the canal bridges, not Commercial Road. The official ledger entry next — Griggs’s initials on a route he never walked. The secret ledger beside it — the true figures, the matching deficit, Finch’s cramped hand recording what the official record concealed. The copied meter readings from Beckton — the output that exceeded consumption by exactly the volume three lamps would burn in six hours. And the cap, last, the physical relic of the man whose truth the ledgers had been built to bury.

She arranged the pages in a row, left to right, the way she would arrange them on the coroner’s table. Each document was numbered in her hand. Each figure was circled where the discrepancy occurred. The sequence was clean, each piece leading to the next, each fact resting on the one before it. She had built it the way she built any record — carefully, sequentially, without gaps.

She sat back and looked at the row of papers. The candle flame was steady. The room was quiet. The cap sat at the end of the line, dark and still, slowly dripping onto the cloth. She placed her hand flat on the desk beside it. The wood was cool. The candle threw her shadow against the wall, and the shadow was still.

She closed her eyes. The ledger was true. The ledger had always been true — not the false entries, not the numbers Finch had been forced to write, but the real record, the one that existed beneath the falsification, the one that gas and pipe and meter had kept regardless of what any clerk put on any page. The gas had moved. The meter had measured it. The pipe had carried it. And a man had died because he refused to let his name stand in a book where it did not belong. Tomorrow she would carry that truth into a room full of people who had looked at the false record and seen nothing wrong, and she would set the real record in front of them, and the real record would speak for itself, because that was what records did. That was what records were for.

She opened her eyes. She looked at the papers. She looked at the cap. She blew out the candle.
