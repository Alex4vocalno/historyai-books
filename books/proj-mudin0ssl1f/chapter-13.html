Chapter 13

A Pattern Emerges

The gaslight at the corner of Leman Street hissed against the damp, casting a cone of yellow that stopped a yard short of the pavement where Ada Quill stood. Her satchel pressed against her ribs. Her knuckles had gone white on the strap. Three minutes since she had left the station, and Varney’s murmur still sat in her ears — the careful, unhurried tone of a man who did not need to raise his voice because the institution spoke for him. The threat had not been loud. It had been a pen lifted from a desk, a glance toward the door, a question about where her loyalties lay. She turned south. The river was two streets away, and beyond it, the lamplighters’ yard and the Company archives. She walked without looking back, her pace steady, her grip tightening each time a constable passed on the beat.

The Company archive occupied a narrow room on the second floor of the engineering offices, above the locker room where the lamplighters stored their coats. A single desk lamp burned there now, its flame turned low, casting a warm circle across a surface already crowded with paper. The room smelled of old binding glue and coal dust — the particular mustiness of records handled by many hands and aired by none.

A large map of the gas distribution district lay flat on the desk, its corners weighted with a brass paperweight and a chipped ceramic mug. Beside it, a tin of pins sat open, heads painted red. Meter reports were stacked in two uneven piles, their edges squared to the desk’s front. Ada’s notebook lay open to a page of dates and figures, the ink still fresh. A cup of cold tea sat at the desk’s far corner, untouched, a skin forming on its surface.

Through the single window, the yard below was empty, the lamplighters’ poles resting in their rack against the far wall, brass tips catching the faint moonlight that cleared the rooftops. A broom had been left propped against the doorway of the locker room, its bristles splayed, as though someone had set it down mid-sweep and forgotten to return.

Ada closed the archive door behind her and stood a moment in the quiet. Rook was already there, seated at the desk, his grease-smudged fingers spread across a meter report he had been reading by the lamp. He looked up. His face carried the particular alertness of a man who had been listening for footsteps.

“You came,” he said.

“You expected otherwise?”

“Varney’s office was lit an hour after you left it. I watched from the canal bridge.”

“He wanted me to see the light. It’s cheaper than a letter.”

Rook pulled a second chair from against the wall and set it across from him. Ada sat, placed her satchel on the floor, and opened her notebook to the page she had marked. The Tuesday sheet lay flat beside it, its columns of figures neatly ruled.

“I copied the meter readings for the Beckton feeder line from the first of September through the fifteenth of October. Twice-daily readings, morning and evening, for six weeks.”

Rook nodded slowly. “I’ve got the district meter reports for the same period. The master readings from the works.”

“Then we start with the fourteenth of October.” Ada turned her notebook to face him, her finger resting beside a column of dates. “Three lamps on Commercial Road logged as lit. Griggs denied it. The official position is that the entry is a clerical error. But the Beckton meter for that evening shows a deficit of four hundred cubic feet against the delivery log.”

Rook pulled a meter report from the left-hand pile and turned it so the printed figures faced them both. His thumbnail, still dark with grease, rested under a number. “Here. Evening reading, October fourteenth. Fourteen thousand two hundred against a delivery log of fourteen thousand six hundred.”

“That is not a clerical error.”

“No. A clerk misreads a number. He doesn’t invent four hundred cubic feet of gas that wasn’t delivered.”

Ada opened her ledger to the annotated pages. The margins carried her own handwriting, small and precise, cross-referencing each anomalous entry to its corresponding meter report. She laid the pages flat on the desk, beside Rook’s report, so the two documents sat side by side.

“The third of September. Two lamps on Narrow Street, logged as lit. The morning meter shows a deficit of three hundred and ten cubic feet.” She turned to the next page. “The seventeenth of September. One lamp on the Highway, logged as lit. Deficit of two hundred and eighty.” She looked at Rook. “These are not isolated entries. I stopped counting at nine.”

Rook leaned forward. He studied the figures, his eyes moving between her annotations and his own meter reports, tracing the same numbers from different sources. His hand moved to the tin of pins and withdrew one, holding it between his thumb and forefinger.

“Give me the dates again,” he said.

Ada read them aloud. Rook stood, leaned over the map, and pressed the first pin into the location for Narrow Street. The paper dimpled under the point. He pressed the second into the Highway, slightly east. The third went into Commercial Road. He worked through the list, each pin marking a lamp logged as lit on a night when the Beckton meter registered a deficit. When he reached the ninth, he stepped back and looked at the map.

The pins were not scattered. They formed a cluster, tight and unmistakable, along the eastern stretch of the district, following the line of the Commercial Road and the streets branching south toward the river. The cluster sat directly over the thick black line on the map that marked the main gas conduit running from the Beckton gasworks into the city.

“There,” Rook said quietly. He traced the line of the conduit with his finger, from the cluster of pins toward the eastern edge of the map where the gasworks was marked. “Every one of them sits on the Beckton feeder line.”

Ada stared at the map. The pins caught the lamplight, their red heads bright against the pale linen of the paper. Weeks she had carried these numbers in her notebook, reading them in sequence, trusting that the sequence would resolve into sense. Now the sense was visible, and it was not a clerical error or a lamplighter’s dishonesty. It was a shape drawn on a map of London.

“Griggs was assigned to the Commercial Road route on the fourteenth,” she said. “But he was also assigned to Narrow Street on the third and the Highway on the seventeenth. His name is on the roster for every one of those nights.”

“You think he was part of it?”

“I think he was placed there. Someone put him on those routes on those specific nights, and then someone wrote his name in the ledger as though he had lit the lamps. He denied it. He denied it to me, and then he was found in the canal.”

Rook’s hand hovered over the map. He pulled another pin from the tin and pressed it into the point where Regent’s Canal crossed the edge of the district, marking where Griggs had been pulled from the water. It stood alone, west of the cluster, but the distance was not great.

Ada turned to the shift rota she had brought from the lamplighters’ office. It was a broad sheet, ruled into a grid of names and dates, the assignments for each man on each night printed in a clerk’s careful hand. She laid it flat beside the map and began matching dates to names.

“The third of September. Griggs, Narrow Street. The seventeenth. Griggs, the Highway. The fourteenth of October. Griggs, Commercial Road.” She ran her pencil down the column. “But the nights between, the nights with no anomalies, different men. Hargrove on the sixth. Pell on the twentieth. No deficits on those nights.”

“So it’s not every night.”

“It is specific nights. The same man assigned, the same deficit pattern, the same feeder line.” She set the pencil down. “This is a schedule, Mr. Rook. Someone planned this weeks in advance.”

Rook sat back in his chair. The wood creaked under him. He rubbed his thumb across the grease stain on his knuckle, a habitual motion, and looked at the map again.

“A schedule means coordination. You don’t run a schedule alone.”

“No.”

“And Griggs was the man on the route. If he didn’t light the lamps, someone else did. Someone who needed his name in the ledger to make the entry look ordinary.”

Ada’s pencil rested on the rota. The grid of names and dates, the orderly system she had spent her working life maintaining, now showed her something else entirely. The order was not broken. It was being used.

“The ledger entry for October fourteenth was written in a hand that is not Griggs’s,” she said. “I compared it to his signing-in sheet from the locker room. The capital G is formed differently. The downstroke on the R curls left where Griggs curls right. Someone else wrote that entry and signed his name.”

Rook looked at her steadily. “Someone with access to the ledger.”

“Someone with access to the ledger, the rota, and the meter room at Beckton.”

A floorboard creaked in the corridor outside the archive door. Ada’s hand closed on her pencil. Rook went still, his fingers resting on the edge of the desk. The sound came again, a slow, deliberate pressure on the third board from the door, the one that always groaned under weight.

Ada reached across the desk and turned down the lamp. The flame shrank to a blue point and then went out. The room dropped into darkness, the only light a thin line of moonlight from the window falling across the floor in a pale, uneven strip. The map disappeared. The pins disappeared. The papers on the desk became a single dark surface.

They sat in silence. The corridor was quiet now. Whatever had caused the sound had moved past, or had stopped. Ada could hear her own breathing and the faint hiss of the extinguished lamp wick. The cold from the window pane reached the back of her neck. Rook’s arm was close to hers on the desk, the warmth of it just perceptible in the dark.

Thirty seconds passed. A minute. The corridor remained silent. No footsteps. No further creak. The building settled around them, the old timbers of the engineering offices making their familiar nighttime sounds, small contractions and shifts that meant nothing.

Rook leaned close. “Gone,” he murmured, barely audible.

Ada did not move. She waited another ten seconds, then reached for the lamp and turned the wheel. The flame caught, small at first, then steady, and the room reassembled itself around them. The map lay where they had left it, the cluster of red pins bright again in the warm light.

Ada looked at the map. Her finger traced the line from the cluster of pins to the thick black conduit, following it eastward to the edge of the paper where the Beckton gasworks was marked with a small printed square. The lamplight glinted off the brass compass Rook had used to draw the radius around the cluster, its point still fixed in the paper.

She counted the pins. Nine nights. Nine entries in the ledger, each signed with a name that was not written by the man it belonged to. Nine deficits in the Beckton meter, each matching the volume of gas that would have flowed through three or four lamps for a single evening. The arithmetic was exact. The geography was exact. The schedule was exact.

Rook stood beside the desk, his hands dark against the pale linen of the map, his face half in shadow and half in the thin moonlight from the window. He looked at the cluster of pins, then at the gasworks, then back at the pins.

“Nine nights,” he said. “Nine entries. All on the same feeder line, all with the same man on the route, all with the same deficit at the meter. That’s not theft by chance. That’s a system.”

Ada closed her notebook. The pages fell together, and the dates and figures disappeared between their covers. She placed the notebook in her satchel and buckled the strap.

“Who inside the Company could do this?” she said. The question was not rhetorical. She looked at Rook, and the lamp between them threw their shadows long across the floor and up the far wall, where the company’s framed distribution charter hung behind glass, its lettering gilt and official.

Rook did not answer immediately. He began collecting the meter reports, tapping the edges square against the desk, stacking them with the careful habit of a man who handled paper the way he handled tools. He placed them in the leather folder he had brought and tied the cord.

“Someone who can write in the ledger,” he said. “Someone who can set the rota. They’d also need to walk into the meter room at Beckton and adjust a reading.”

“That is a short list.”

“It is.”

Ada stood. The chair scraped softly against the floorboards. She picked up her satchel and held it against her side. The map remained on the desk, its pins still in place, the cluster of red heads marking the geography of a crime that had been running for weeks under the cover of ordinary clerical entries.

Rook looked at the map one last time. Then he reached for the brass paperweight and set it on top of the pins, pressing them flat against the paper.

“Leave it,” he said. “Come back for it tomorrow. If anyone finds it, it’s just a map with pins in it. Means nothing without your notebook.”

Ada nodded. She turned toward the door. The corridor beyond was empty and dark, the floorboards catching the faint moonlight in pale, uneven strips. She stepped out. Rook followed, pulling the archive door shut behind him with a soft click that carried down the stairwell and faded into the building’s silence.

They descended the stairs together, their footsteps careful on the worn treads. At the bottom, the yard was still empty, the lamplighters’ poles still resting in their rack. The moon had moved, and the light through the gate had shifted, but the yard was the same. Ada paused at the gate and looked back at the building. The archive window was dark. The map was inside it, and the pins, and the shape they made.

Rook stepped past her into the street. He turned east. He did not say goodnight. He walked with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders set, the leather folder tucked under one arm.

Ada watched him until he reached the corner. Then she turned north. The gaslight at the end of the street hissed above her, and she walked beneath it, her satchel heavy against her hip, the notebook inside it carrying the dates and figures that had drawn a shape on a map of London. The shape pointed east, toward Beckton, toward the gasworks where the gas left the works and entered the pipes and turned the meters that were supposed to measure it. Someone inside the Company had built a system out of that gas, had planned it weeks ahead, had written false names in a true ledger, and had drowned the man whose name they used.

The map was complete. The question was not whether the fraud existed. It was who, inside the Company that metered every cubic foot and recorded every lamp and signed every roster, had built the machine that ran beneath all of it.
