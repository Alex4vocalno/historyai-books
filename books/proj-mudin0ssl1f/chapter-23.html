Chapter 23

Arrests at the Company

The warrant paper crackled as Varney laid it on Finch’s polished oak desk, and the sound carried in the stillness of the clerking office like a pistol shot.

Three days ago, Varney had stood in a courthouse corridor and told Ada Quill she was obsessed. That was before the coroner’s declaration, before the secret ledger’s exposure at the inquest had stripped the Company’s position to kindling. The magistrate’s warrant bore the weight of that official reckoning, and Varney carried it now the way a man carries a thing he can no longer put down.

Finch stared at the document. His fingers rested on the desk’s edge, and they tightened until the knuckles went white. The beeswax polish on the oak caught the morning light from the tall windows, and the coal dust that hung permanently in the air of this building drifted in the shafts of sun. Behind Finch, the rack of ledgers filled the wall floor to ceiling, every spine aligned, every volume in its place. The order of the room had always been an extension of the man who governed it.

“This is a warrant for your arrest,” Varney said. He spoke the way he always spoke, murmuring, but there was nothing soft in it now. “Fraud, falsification of company records, and conspiracy in the death of Thomas Griggs. You’ll come with me to Leman Street.”

Finch did not touch the warrant. He looked at it the way a man looks at a dead animal on his doorstep, something that should not be where it is. His breathing went shallow, audible in the quiet.

“I shall require a Company solicitor before I respond to any charge,” Finch said. “The Gas Light and Coke Company maintains a legal department for exactly this purpose. You cannot simply walk in here and remove a chief clerk without due process.”

“I just did walk in here,” Varney said. “The warrant’s signed by the magistrate. Your solicitor can meet you at the station.”

“The Company’s protocols are not optional, Inspector. There are channels. There are procedures for the investigation of company personnel, and you are obliged to follow them before any arrest can be effected on company property.”

Varney did not move. He stood with his hands behind his back, his jacket hung on the back of a chair, his shirtsleeves rolled to the forearms. He had the look of a man who had argued himself into a corner and then decided to stop arguing.

“I followed your channels for three months,” Varney said. “I followed your procedures. Your solicitor can take it up with the court.”

Finch’s gaze moved from Varney to Rook, who stood two paces behind the inspector, near the door. The clerks beyond the glass partition had stopped working. Pens had stopped scratching. Through the partition, a row of faces turned toward Finch’s office, and among them, at the far desk, sat Ada Quill. Her pen was down. Her ledger was closed. She watched with the still, fixed attention of someone who had been waiting a long time for this particular moment.

Finch’s mouth thinned. “Rook. You’re a party to this?”

Rook said nothing. He held the rolled schematics in his left hand, the paper warm and faintly damp from his grip during the walk from the engineering offices. He had copied them two nights ago at the drafting table, tracing the pipe routes from the Beckton distribution maps by lamplight, his fingers black with ink. The diversion line ran from after the main meter, through a buried conduit beneath the retort house, and down toward the river. He had drawn it in red ink, the same red that marked emergency repairs and unauthorized connections on company maps. The forgery on the maintenance log was signed with a name that did not belong to the hand that wrote it.

“I’m here as a witness,” Rook said.

“You’re here as a traitor to the company that employs you,” Finch said. He straightened in his chair and adjusted his cuffs, a gesture so habitual it carried him through the first beat of the crisis. “This warrant is obtained on the basis of perjured testimony from a disgruntled clerk and a junior engineer who has overstepped his remit. The Company will—““The Company will do what it always does,” Varney said. “Cooperate with the police. Isn’t that what your superintendent told my superintendent when I came here in October asking about Griggs? Full cooperation. Official statements. Access to records.”

Finch’s jaw worked. He had no answer for that, and the absence of an answer left a silence that filled the room like water.

Varney turned to the door and spoke to the constable who waited in the corridor. “Bring him in.”

The meter-reader was escorted through the outer office. He was a thin man who had walked the Beckton-Whitechapel route for eleven years, recording the district meters twice daily, morning and evening, in a logbook that was supposed to be inviolable. He came in with his cap in his hands, turning it by the brim, and his eyes went to Finch and then away, the way a dog looks from its master to the stranger at the gate.

“Sit down,” Varney said. “Over there.” He pointed to the chair beside Finch’s desk, the one reserved for visitors, the one where solicitors and auditors sat when they came to review the books.

The meter-reader sat. His knees pressed together. He did not look at Finch.

Varney turned to Rook. “Show him.”

Rook stepped forward and drew the copied Beckton schematics from his satchel, unfolding them on the desk and smoothing the paper flat with his palm. Beside it, he laid a separate sheet — the maintenance log entry he had transcribed from the Beckton records, dated and signed. The red line of the diversion pipe ran across the schematic, a thread of ink that started at the Beckton meter and terminated at a junction near the river.

“This junction,” Rook said, tapping the red line on the schematic where it branched from the main. “This pipe was installed after the meter. Gas flowing through it doesn’t register on the Beckton master.”

The meter-reader stared at the paper. His cap stopped turning.

Rook moved his finger to the maintenance log entry beside the schematic. From his coat, he drew the meter logbook he had carried from the engineering offices. “This signature here,” Rook said. “This is your name. You signed off on the installation as a routine repair.”

The meter-reader’s throat moved. “I didn’t—“he started, and then stopped.

Rook waited. The clock on the mantel ticked, and somewhere in the outer office a chair scraped the floor.

“You signed it,” Rook said. He opened the meter logbook and placed it beside the maintenance log entry. “The handwriting matches your entries in the meter logbooks. I compared them line by line. The letter forms are identical, same pressure, same slant. You signed a maintenance authorization for a pipe that bypassed the meter, and you logged the meter readings that hid the deficit.”

“I didn’t know what it was when I signed it,” the meter-reader said. His voice had dropped to something barely above the sound of the clock. “He told me it was a drainage line. A relief valve for pressure buildup. That’s what he told me.”

Finch’s hand slammed the desk. “Shut your mouth.”

The meter-reader flinched but did not look at Finch. He was looking at the schematics, at the red line, at his own name on the separate sheet.

“Then what did you think it was,” Rook said, “when the readings stopped matching the delivery logs? You’ve been recording the Beckton meter twice a day for eleven years. You know what the numbers should look like. You knew the gas leaving the works didn’t match the gas billed to the district.”

The meter-reader’s hands tightened on his cap, crushing the brim. “He said the difference was condensation. Line loss. It happens in cold weather.”

“And you believed that for three years?”

The silence was its own answer. The cap was a ruin now, the brim bent double.

“He paid you,” Varney said. It was not a question.

The meter-reader’s mouth opened and closed. “Ten shillings a week. He said it was a supplementary stipend for additional duties. I didn’t—I told myself—““You told yourself what you needed to tell yourself,” Varney said. “And when Thomas Griggs noticed the lamps on Commercial Road were lit on a route that wasn’t his, when he came in here and said the ledger was wrong, what did you tell yourself then?”

The meter-reader looked at Finch. Finch looked at the meter-reader. Something passed between them that Rook could not name, a reckoning that had been building behind closed doors for months.

“He came to me first,” the meter-reader said. “Griggs. He came to me because I was the meter-reader and he knew the numbers didn’t work. He showed me his own log. His route that night was Cable Street to Penfold, not Commercial Road. He’d been assigned elsewhere. Someone had put his name on the Commercial Road lamps, and he wanted to know who.”

“What did you do?”

“I told him to take it up with the chief clerk.” The voice was flat now, emptied of the struggle. “I told him it was probably a routing error. A clerical mistake.”

“And what did the chief clerk do?”

The meter-reader looked at Finch again. “I don’t know what happened after that. I only know Griggs stopped coming to me. And then two weeks later he was in the canal.”

Varney turned to Finch. “You have the right to remain silent. Anything you—““I want my solicitor,” Finch said. His voice was steady, but his hand had moved to the desk drawer, the one with the lock, the one that held the second ledger. “I will not say another word without the Company’s legal representation present.”

Varney stepped forward. “Open the drawer, Mr. Finch.”

“That property is company property. It is secured under company protocols, and you have no authority to—““Open it, or I’ll have the constable open it.”

Finch’s hand rested on the drawer. His thumb rubbed the brass fitting. The clock ticked. The clerks beyond the partition were standing now, a row of dark sleeves and pale faces pressed against the glass.

Finch turned the key. The drawer opened. Inside, the second ledger sat where it had always sat, its cover scuffed, its pages dense with entries in Finch’s careful hand. Varney reached for it.

Finch pulled it free. In one motion he was out of the chair, around the desk, and at the grate, where the fire that warmed the office had been burning since seven o’clock. The coals were high and white-edged. He lifted the ledger above the flames.

Rook moved. He had been standing three paces from the desk, and the distance to the grate was four strides, but his body covered it in two lunges, his left hand reaching for the ledger’s spine while his right caught the edge of the grate. The ledger was already dropping. Finch’s fingers released it, and the pages began to curl in the heat, the ink running and darkening.

Rook’s palms hit the coals.

The pain was immediate and complete, a white sound that filled his hands and traveled up his forearms. He closed his fingers on the ledger’s edge and pulled. The paper came free, trailing ash and heat, and he clutched it to his chest, pressing the burning pages against his waistcoat. The wool scorched. The ink smelled of hot iron and something chemical, a sharp note beneath the burning.

“Rook—“Varney started.

Rook stepped back from the grate. The ledger was in his hands, singed along the bottom third, the corners gone, but the entries were intact. Column after column of dates, quantities, signatures. The record of every falsified meter reading, every adjusted delivery figure, every payment made to keep the diversion hidden. He could see the entries for October, the week Griggs died, and the figures for Commercial Road’s three lamps were there in Finch’s handwriting, recorded as routine consumption.

His palms were red and blistering. The pain was a living thing, climbing his wrists.

Varney was at the grate, pulling Finch back from the coals. The constable came through the door and took Finch’s arm. Finch’s face was a mask, composed and rigid, the composure of a man who had spent twenty years controlling every room he entered and now controlled none of it.

“You’ll never work in this city again,” Finch said. He was looking at Rook. “You understand that. The Company will see to it.”

Rook held the ledger. The pages were warm against his blistered palms, and the ash from the burned edges drifted down to the floorboards.

Varney produced the irons. The handcuffs were heavy, dark with use, and they clicked shut on Finch’s wrists with a sound that was small and final. The constable held Finch’s elbow. The chief clerk stood straight, his cuffed hands in front of his waist, his jacket hanging open.

“Take him out through the office,” Varney said. He was speaking to the constable, but the words carried through the glass partition to the clerks beyond. “Straight through. No back door.”

The constable walked Finch to the office door and opened it. The glass partition separated Finch’s office from the clerking floor, and as the door swung wide, every clerk on the floor was visible. They stood at their desks in a line, pens down, ledgers open, and they watched. Some looked away. Some could not. Ada Quill stood at her station, her hands flat on her desk, her face still and composed, and she watched Finch pass down the aisle between the desks, the irons on his wrists catching the light from the tall windows.

Finch walked straight. His step did not falter. He passed his clerks the way a general passes his troops after a surrender, with the dignity that is left when everything else has been stripped.

The meter-reader was brought after him, escorted by a second constable who had come up from the corridor. He walked with his head down, his crushed cap in his hands, and none of the clerks looked at him at all.

The outer door closed. The sound of boots on the stone stairwell faded. The clerks stood in the silence, and then, slowly, one by one, they sat. Pens did not resume. The scratching did not start again.

Rook stood in Finch’s office. The warrant lay on the desk. The schematics lay beside it, the red line of the diversion pipe still visible. The grate was open, the coals disturbed, and on the floor beside it, a few pages of the ledger had come loose and lay in a scatter of ash and ink.

Varney came back in. He looked at Rook’s hands and said nothing for a moment. Then he pulled a handkerchief from his pocket and held it out.

“Wrap those. I’ll send the police surgeon to the station.”

Rook took the handkerchief and wound it around his right palm. The fabric was rough against the blisters. He picked up the ledger with his left hand and held it out to Varney.

“Evidence,” Rook said.

Varney took it. He weighed it in his hand, the singed pages, the missing corners, the dense record of fraud that had been kept in a locked drawer for three years.

“The inquest testimony and this ledger, that’s the case,” Varney said. “The meter-reader’s confession seals it. Finch will have a solicitor by noon, but there’s nothing to argue. The figures are the figures.”

Rook looked at the desk where Finch had sat for twenty years, where the beeswax polish shone and the coal dust drifted in the light. The drawer was still open, the lock still turned. Inside it, a pen and a bottle of ink, nothing else. The second ledger had been the only thing it held.

He walked out through the clerking office. The clerks had returned to their work, but the scratching of pens was sparse, intermittent, the sound of people doing something with their hands because they did not know what else to do with them. Ada fell in beside him as he passed her desk. She picked up her satchel and her notebook and walked with him, not speaking, matching his pace down the corridor and down the stone stairs to the street.

The police wagon stood at the kerb. The horse was grey, its breath steaming in the cold air. Finch was already inside, the doors closed. The meter-reader was being helped in by the constable, and the second door clanged shut with a sound like a furnace damper closing.

The wagon pulled away. The wheels ground on the damp cobblestones, and the sound of it receded down the street, past the gas lamps that stood unlit in the morning air, past the offices and the warehouses and the corner where Commercial Road began.

Rook stood on the steps. The gasworks rose behind the building, its holders massive against the sky, the retort house chimneys trailing smoke that flattened in the wind. The network of pipes beneath the street was still there, still carrying gas to the lamps, still metered and recorded and billed. The works would go on. The gas would flow.

Ada stood beside him. She had not spoken since the office. Her satchel hung from her shoulder, and her notebook was in her hand, and her pen was behind her ear, where she always kept it when she was working. She watched the wagon turn the corner and disappear.

“His hands,” she said.

Rook looked down at his palms. The handkerchief was darkening. The blisters had risen and the skin was tight, and when he flexed his fingers, the pain moved through his hands like something with its own pulse.

“The tissue will granulate,” he said. “They’ll mend.”

Ada did not look away from the corner where the wagon had gone. “He denied it from the first day. Griggs. He stood in this building and said the ledger was wrong, and nobody believed him. Not me. Not Varney. Not anyone.”

“You believed him,” Rook said. “Eventually.”

“I believed the numbers. The numbers came after. Griggs was dead before I understood what the numbers meant.” She turned to face him. Her jaw was set, and her eyes were dry and direct. “The inquest recorded it. The coroner recorded it. Thomas Griggs was sent to another route that night. The lamps on Commercial Road were lit under his name by someone else. The gas that fed those lamps came through a pipe that doesn’t appear on any company map. The official position was clerical error.”

“Varney’s position.”

“Varney’s position, and the Company’s position, and the position of every man in that building who didn’t want to look at the numbers twice.” She paused. “He’s in the canal because of three lamps that someone logged under his name. That’s the whole of it.”

Rook flexed his hands. The blisters pulled. The handkerchief was wet through, and the air on the exposed skin was cold and sharp.

“He went to the meter-reader first,” Rook said. “Griggs. Not Finch. He went to the man who read the meters, because the meters were where the lie was. He knew the numbers didn’t match. He’d been walking those routes for years, lighting lamps, and he knew how much gas a lamp burns and how much the district logs showed, and those two figures didn’t agree.”

“And the meter-reader sent him to Finch.”

“Sent him to the man who was paying him ten shillings a week to look the other way.”

Ada was quiet. The gas lamps along the street stood in their iron posts, the glass clean, the mantles intact. They would be lit tonight by a different lamplighter, one who would walk the route and turn the cock and light the mantle, and the gas would flow through the pipes and the meter would turn, and the reading would be honest.

“Will they keep you on?” she asked. “The Company.”

Rook looked at the building behind them. The windows of the clerking office were bright with morning light. The engineering offices were around the corner, where his drafting table sat with the ink still on it, the Beckton maps folded beside the lamp.

“Probably not,” he said.

Ada nodded. She shifted her satchel on her shoulder. “Finch said that to hurt you.”

“I know what it was.”

“Will it?”

He watched the last of the smoke from the wagon’s passage thin and dissolve in the damp air. The river was close, beyond the warehouses, and the smell of it came through the coal smoke and the gasworks fumes, the smell of water and algae and the traffic of barges.

“Someone has to maintain the pipes,” he said. “Someone has to read the meters. The gas doesn’t stop because the clerk is gone.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

He turned his hands over. The blisters were rising on both palms, the skin stretched and shining. The handkerchief was useless now, soaked through, and he pulled it off and let it drop. The cold air found the raw skin and stayed there.

“No,” he said. “It won’t. But that’s not why I did it.”

Ada looked at his hands and then at his face. She did not ask why he did it. She had been in the clerking office when the warrant was read, and she had watched him lay the schematics on Finch’s desk, and she had watched him put his hands into the fire. She knew what those things cost.

The street was quiet. The morning traffic had not yet started in earnest, and the steps where they stood were still damp from the night. The gasworks breathed behind them, the low constant sound of gas moving through pipes, the hiss and rumble of the retort house, the measured pulse of an industry that did not stop.

The wagon was gone. The corner where it had turned was empty. Somewhere beyond it, Finch sat in irons in the back of a police van, and the meter-reader sat beside him, and the second ledger was in Varney’s hands, and the warrant was signed, and the inquest record was filed, and Thomas Griggs was in the ground in a cemetery off the Commercial Road, where three lamps stood that he had never lit.

Rook put his hands in his pockets. The wool of his coat was rough against the blisters, and the pain settled into a steady heat that he could carry. He turned from the steps and walked, and Ada walked beside him, and behind them the gasworks stood in the morning light, the holders full, the pipes running beneath the street, the meters turning.

They did not speak for a full block. Then Ada stopped and opened her satchel and took out the notebook where she had copied the meter readings, the Tuesday sheet, the roster, the whole architecture of evidence she had built night after night in the clerking office. She held it in both hands and looked at it the way a person looks at a thing that has cost more than it should.

“I need to write this down,” she said. “All of it. What happened in that room.”

Rook waited. He recalled the sequence plainly: Finch leaning back when the warrant hit the desk, composure holding like maintained pressure — calm, bureaucratic. The demand for a Company solicitor. The insistence on written authorization from the director’s office. Varney’s answer, flat and final: he was not leaving the building without Finch. Then Finch’s hand drifting toward the grate.

Rook had been closest. The ledger was already in the fire, edges curling. He had reached in without thinking. The heat had closed around his fingers. He pulled it free by the spine — singed but legible, every forged entry, every displaced reading, every signature that was not Griggs’s.

The meter-reader had denied everything first. Said he followed instructions, recorded what he was told to record. But when Rook spread the Beckton schematics across the desk and put his finger on the forged inspector’s mark — the stamp that could not exist on that date because the Beckton valve had not yet been commissioned — the man’s face had gone grey. He had looked at Finch, and Finch had not looked back. Then the words came, quiet and fast, pulled out of him by the weight of mechanical fact.

They had walked Finch out in handcuffs. The clerks had watched from their desks. No one spoke. The sound of the office had been the sound of pens stopping.

Ada closed the notebook. “I’ll have it on your desk by morning,” she said.

Rook nodded. His hands throbbed beneath the bandages. “I’ll have the pipe maps,” he said. “All of them. Every district, every valve, every meter. If there are more discrepancies, we will find them.”

They parted at the corner. Ada turned toward the office. Rook walked on, toward the Commercial Road, where three unlit lamps stood at the gate of a cemetery, and a man who should have lit them was buried beneath the soil they illuminated.
