Chapter 21

Ledgers in the Light

The ledger had spent the night on her bedside table, and by dawn its cloth cover had taken on the damp of the room, the pages softening at the edges where they pressed against the wood. Ada had not slept beside it so much as kept watch, her pen and notebook arranged on the counterpane in the order she would need them, the Tuesday sheet folded into a square and tucked inside the front cover where it would be ready. When the grey light came through the window she was already dressed, her satchel packed with the official lamplighter’s ledger and the roster and the notebook of copied meter readings, and the second ledger — Finch’s ledger — wrapped in a square of cotton to keep it dry.

The inquest was held in the coroner’s court on Cable Street, a long, narrow room with a vaulted ceiling and windows set high in the brick so that the light fell in steep angles across the public gallery. Oak benches ran along the walls, and at the far end a raised table held the coroner’s papers, a glass of water, and a brass lamp that had been lit against the morning fog. Twelve jurors sat in two rows of wooden chairs to the left, and behind a low railing the public gallery was already three-quarters full when Ada arrived. She took a seat on the second bench from the front, her satchel on her knee, and watched the room fill.

Detective Inspector Varney stood near the coroner’s table, his jacket hung on the back of a chair. He was speaking in his low murmur to a uniformed constable, and when he straightened and turned his head he saw Ada and held her gaze for a moment before returning to his conversation. His face carried the expression of a man conducting routine business on a routine morning, and his hands were still.

Alistair Finch sat in the front row of the public gallery, to the right of the center aisle. His collar was fresh, his cuffs clean, but his jaw worked as though he were chewing something that would not break down. His hands rested on his knees, and the fingers of his right hand opened and closed in a slow rhythm against the wool of his trousers. He did not look toward Ada when she entered, but his head turned slightly, and the movement stopped.

Samuel Rook came in behind her and took a place on the bench at the back, near the door. He was in his working clothes, grease still visible in the creases of his sleeves, and he carried a rolled set of distribution maps under one arm. He met Ada’s eyes briefly and gave a single nod.

The coroner — a Mr Aldous Crewe, a small, precise man in his sixties with spectacles and white hair cut close to his skull — entered from a side door and took his seat behind the table. The court fell quiet. He arranged his papers with both hands, moving each sheet a fraction to align its edge with the table’s edge, and then looked up over his spectacles at the room.

“This inquest resumes into the death of Thomas Griggs, lamplighter, found in Regent’s Canal at Battle Bridge on the morning of the sixteenth of October.” His voice was dry and unhurried. “We have heard evidence from the bargeman who recovered the body, from the police surgeon who examined it, and from Detective Inspector Varney, who has given his professional opinion that the death was accidental, the deceased having lost his footing on the towpath in darkness.” He paused and turned a page. “Miss Ada Quill, of the Gas Light and Coke Company clerking office, has come forward to give evidence. Miss Quill.”

Ada stood. Her satchel was on the bench where she had left it, and she lifted the strap over her shoulder and walked to the witness chair, a plain wooden seat beside the coroner’s table. The chair scraped on the stone floor as she drew it back, and the sound was sharp in the quiet room.

“You are employed as a clerk by the Gas Light and Coke Company,” the coroner said.

“I am.”

“In what capacity?”

“I maintain the lamplighters’ ledgers, the district rosters, and the meter-reading sheets for the Whitechapel southern section. I transcribe the daily entries made by the lamplighters into the master records.”

“And you knew the deceased, Thomas Griggs?”

“He was a lamplighter on the Whitechapel southern route. I processed his ledger entries daily. I spoke with him on several occasions regarding discrepancies in his October returns.”

The coroner looked down at his papers. “You contacted this court on the twentieth of October and asked to give evidence. You indicated that you had documentation relevant to the death of Mr Griggs. Is that correct?”

“It is.”

“What is the nature of this documentation?”

Ada opened her satchel and placed the official lamplighter’s ledger on the table beside the coroner’s water glass. The cloth cover was dark blue, the corners worn white from handling. She opened it to the page marked with a slip of paper. “This is the official lamplighter’s ledger for the Whitechapel southern section, October fourteenth. The entry for that date shows three lamps on Commercial Road — numbers forty-one, forty-three, and forty-five — logged as lit by Thomas Griggs.”

“And?”

“Thomas Griggs told me himself that he did not light those lamps. He was assigned to the Commercial Road east route that night, which covers lamps numbered fifty and above. Lamps forty-one, forty-three, and forty-five fall under the Commercial Road west route, which was covered by a different lamplighter, a Mr Pell.”

Varney shifted in his chair. “The witness is offering hearsay about a conversation with a dead man.”

“Miss Quill,” the coroner said, “can you corroborate this conversation?”

“Griggs made a written note on his October fourteenth sheet. He wrote, in his own hand, ‘Did not light 41, 43, 45. Wrong route assigned.’ I have his original sheet here.” She produced the Tuesday sheet from inside the ledger’s front cover and placed it on the table. The paper was creased from folding, the ink brown, the handwriting cramped and slanting hard to the right.

The coroner picked it up and held it beneath the lamp. His spectacles caught the light as he tilted the sheet. “This is in the deceased’s handwriting?”

“It is. I am familiar with every lamplighter’s hand in the southern section. I transcribe their entries six days a week.”

Varney leaned forward. “With respect, sir, this proves nothing. A lamplighter made a note. He may have been mistaken about his route. He may have lit the lamps and failed to mark them properly. Clerical errors of this sort are common in the company’s records, and they have nothing to do with the man’s death.”

Ada turned to face him. “The Beckton master meter for the Whitechapel southern main recorded a deficit of approximately eight hundred cubic feet on the nights of October seventh, tenth, fourteenth, and twenty-first. I have copied the relevant meter readings.” She opened her notebook and set it beside the ledger. “On each of those nights, the lamplighter’s ledger shows three lamps logged as lit on Commercial Road. On each of those nights, the meter recorded less gas passing through the district main than the ledger says was consumed.”

“How do you account for that?” the coroner asked.

“I don’t account for it. I record it. The numbers are there. The deficit appears on nights when three specific lamps are logged as lit by a lamplighter who did not light them.”

Varney’s chair scraped against the floor. “Meter readings fluctuate. Temperature, pressure, the condition of the mains — any of these can produce a variance. The company’s own engineers will tell you that.”

“Mr Rook can tell you,” Ada said. “He is present.”

The coroner looked toward the back of the room. Samuel Rook stood, the distribution maps still under his arm. “Mr Rook, you are an engineer with the Gas Light and Coke Company?”

“I am.”

“You have examined the meter readings Miss Quill has provided?”

“I have. The Beckton-Whitechapel district main passes through a meter that records gas leaving the works. That meter is a mechanical instrument. Its readings are considered irrefutable by the company. I inspected the meter on the seventeenth of October and found it in proper working order.” He paused. “I also found a bypass pipe installed after the meter, siphoning gas toward the river.”

The room went still. A man in the gallery leaned forward, and the bench beneath him creaked.

Varney’s hand was on the table, and his fingers pressed flat against the oak. “This is highly irregular. No such inspection was authorised by the company, and Mr Rook’s presence here is—““Mr Rook,” the coroner said, cutting through, “this bypass pipe. Can you describe it?”

“It is a three-inch iron pipe connected to the district main downstream of the meter. It runs beneath the valve gallery floor and exits the gasworks compound toward the south, in the direction of the river. It was installed without authorisation and does not appear on any company distribution map.”

“Meaning gas flowing through that pipe would not be recorded by the meter.”

“It would not.”

The coroner turned back to Ada. His hand rested on the official ledger, fingers spread across the open page. “Miss Quill, you said you had documentation. You have shown me a lamplighter’s note and your own copied meter readings. Is there further evidence?”

Ada reached into her satchel. The second ledger was wrapped in cotton, and she unwrapped it slowly, the cloth falling away to reveal a book smaller than the official one, bound in dark brown leather gone soft with use, the corners rounded and the spine cracked in two places. The pages inside were ruled in pencil, not printed, and every entry was written by hand — the same hand, a cramped, precise script with tall capital letters and downward strokes that pressed hard enough to groove the paper. She set it on the table beside the official ledger, and the two books sat there side by side, one dark blue and institutional, the other brown and private.

“This ledger was kept by Mr Alistair Finch, chief clerk of the Gas Light and Coke Company Whitechapel office. I obtained it from his desk on the seventeenth of October, in his presence, after he unlocked the drawer where it was stored.”

Finch’s jaw stopped moving. His hands went flat on his knees, the fingers rigid.

The coroner opened the brown ledger. The pages were soft, almost cloth-like, and they rustled as he turned them. His hand, speckled with age, rested on the open page. He turned another, the rustle loud in the silent room. His finger traced a column of figures, then stopped on an entry in Finch’s handwriting. He looked up, not at Varney, but at Ada.

“This ledger,” he said, his voice low and grave. “Where did you obtain it?”

“From Mr Finch’s desk drawer, in his office. He unlocked it himself. He said he had been keeping it because he was instructed to record the discrepancies between the metered gas and the gas recorded in the official ledgers.”

“Instructed by whom?”

“He would not say.”

The coroner looked down at the open page. “This entry, dated October seventh. It reads: ‘Meter deficit, 812 cubic feet. Commercial Road, three lamps. Value: £1 four shillings.’ And again on October tenth: ‘Meter deficit, 798 cubic feet. Commercial Road, three lamps. Value: £1 two shillings.’ October fourteenth: ‘Meter deficit, 803 cubic feet. Commercial Road, three lamps. Value: £1 three shillings.’ October twenty-first: ‘Meter deficit, 791 cubic feet. Commercial Road, three lamps. Value: £1 one shilling.’”

He turned more pages. “These entries run back to June of this year. Each one records a meter deficit, a number of lamps, and a monetary value. The handwriting is consistent throughout.”

“It is Mr Finch’s hand,” Ada said. “I have transcribed his writing daily for three years.”

The coroner looked toward the front row of the gallery. “Mr Finch.”

Finch did not stand. His hands were still flat on his knees, the fingers white against the wool. His throat moved.

“Mr Finch, do you acknowledge this ledger as your own?”

Finch’s jaw opened, and closed. He said nothing.

“You are not obliged to speak,” the coroner continued. “But this court will note your silence.”

Varney had risen from his chair. “Sir, this is a company document obtained without authorisation. It cannot be admitted as evidence without—““It is a private record, not a company document,” Ada said. “It was kept in a locked drawer in Mr Finch’s personal desk. It is not stamped, not filed, not entered in any company index. It is a record of theft, kept by the man who profited from it.”

Varney turned to face her. His mouth opened, and for a moment the murmur that came out was not a word but a sound, as though the mechanism that produced his dismissals had jammed. He put both hands on the coroner’s table and leaned forward, his weight on his palms. “The word of a clerk against—““Mr Varney,” the coroner said. “You have given your evidence. This witness is giving hers. Sit down.”

Varney straightened. His hands came off the table, and he stepped back, and then sat. His right hand went to his knee and gripped it.

The coroner turned to Ada. “Miss Quill. You stated that Thomas Griggs discovered a discrepancy in the meter logs. What happened after he discovered it?”

“Griggs came to me on the eleventh of October. He had noticed that the meter readings for October seventh did not match the gas logged as consumed. He told me the ledger was wrong. He said he had not lit the three lamps on Commercial Road that the ledger said he lit.”

Ada opened the official ledger to the October fourteenth page. “On the fourteenth, three lamps on Commercial Road were logged as lit by Thomas Griggs. Griggs denied lighting them. He was assigned to the Commercial Road east route that night. Lamps forty-one, forty-three, and forty-five — the three in question — are on the west route, which Mr Pell covered. Griggs never walked that section.”

She turned to the brown ledger and opened it to the October fourteenth entry. “On the same night, Mr Finch’s private ledger records a meter deficit of 803 cubic feet, three Commercial Road lamps, and a value of £1 three shillings.”

She closed both books and placed her hand on the brown one. “On the morning of the sixteenth, Thomas Griggs was found dead in Regent’s Canal. He had told me he intended to confront Mr Finch about the discrepancy. He told me this on the twelfth of October.”

The coroner looked at the brown ledger under Ada’s hand, then at Finch, then at Varney. “Mr Varney, your report states the death was accidental. A fall from the towpath.”

“It was.”

“Does the evidence you have heard this morning alter that conclusion?”

Varney’s grip on his knee tightened. His knuckles went pale, and he released them, and put his hand flat on the bench beside him. “The evidence is circumstantial.”

“Mr Varney. A man with knowledge of a fraud is found dead in a canal two days after he announced his intention to confront the man who kept the fraud’s records. The fraud is documented in the suspect’s own hand. You call that circumstantial.”

The room was silent. A woman in the gallery put her hand to her mouth. The constable by the door shifted his weight, and his boots scraped on the stone.

Varney looked at the coroner. He looked at the brown ledger. He looked at Finch, who sat with his hands flat and his jaw still and his eyes fixed on the table. Varney stood, slowly, and turned to the constable.

“Take him into custody.”

Finch’s hands came off his knees. His fingers curled inward, the knuckles tight, and he pressed his fists together in his lap. The constable crossed the room and stood beside him, and Finch rose from the bench without being touched. He walked toward the door with the constable at his elbow, and as he passed the place where Ada sat, he turned his head.

His eyes met hers. They were dry, and wide, and they did not blink. The line of his mouth was straight, and his chin was level, and he held her gaze for three steps before the constable guided him through the door and into the corridor beyond.

The coroner waited until the door closed. “This inquest is adjourned. The death of Thomas Griggs is to be reclassified as suspicious. The evidence before this court — the official lamplighter’s ledger, the meter readings, and the private ledger of Alistair Finch — will be retained and entered into the record.”

He gathered his papers and stood, and the court rose. The jurors filed out through a side door, and the gallery began to empty, the murmur of voices building until it filled the vaulted ceiling.

Ada remained in the witness chair for a moment after the room had cleared. The two ledgers sat on the coroner’s table where she had placed them, side by side. The official one was closed, its dark blue cover marked with the company stamp. The brown one was open to the October fourteenth page, Finch’s handwriting visible in the lamplight, the tall capitals and the grooved downward strokes and the figure at the bottom of the column: £1 3s.

She closed it and put it in her satchel, and the cotton wrapping went around it, and she buckled the strap.

Rook was standing by the door when she came out into the corridor. The stone was cold underfoot, and from the street beyond the open front door came the sound of hooves and wheels and a voice calling a fare. The corridor smelled of damp wool and ink.

“It’s done,” Rook said.

“It’s done.”

He shifted the distribution maps under his arm. “What will you do now?”

Ada looked at the closed door of the court behind them. “I don’t know.”

Rook nodded. He did not say anything else, and after a moment he turned and walked toward the street, and the sound of his boots faded on the stone.

Ada stood in the corridor with her satchel on her shoulder. The brass lamp at the end of the passage guttered in a draught from the door. The stone was cold through the soles of her boots, and the air carried the smell of the river, coming in from the street, and beneath it the smell of ink and old paper, and she stood there for a long time, looking at nothing, the weight of the ledger in the bag against her hip.
