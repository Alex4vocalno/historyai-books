Chapter 11

Whispers at the Gasworks

Rook pressed his hand flat on the scarred table. Griggs’s body had come out of the canal still and black under a sky the color of wet slate, and the weight of it sat in Rook’s fingers now. Ada’s voice came back to him in fragments: not a clerical error, not an accident, not a mistake. Murder. She had said it quietly, the way she said everything, as though the word were simply another column in her notebook, another figure to be entered and verified. The bruise on Griggs’s temple. The water. The ledger entry that put him on Commercial Road when his own route sheet put him elsewhere. All of it added up to a frame, and the frame had killed him.

In the corner booth of the King’s Arms he waited for a man who smelled of coal. The pub sat wedged between a chandler’s shop and a boarded-up warehouse on Thomas Street, far enough from the yard that no one from the day shift would stumble in for a lunchtime pint. He had chosen it for exactly that reason. Stale beer and pipe smoke had soaked into the plaster over years. The barman had given him the corner booth without comment. Two glasses stood on the table, one filled, one empty, and his thumb worried the callus on his forefinger in a rhythm he could not stop.

The door opened. A short man in a stained greatcoat entered, blinking, and stood in the doorway long enough to scan the room before he moved. Bill Hobbs walked with the careful gait of someone who expected the floor to shift. He found the booth and sat down opposite Rook without speaking. His hands went straight to the empty glass, turning it in slow circles. The glass left a wet ring on the wood where condensation had already gathered on its rim.

“You’re Rook,” Hobbs said. Not a question.

“Sit easy, Bill. I asked you here because I think you can help me with something at the Beckton gasworks.”

“Lot of men work the works.” Hobbs glanced toward the bar. The barman was polishing a glass with his back to them. “I don’t know what you think I know.”

Rook leaned forward, elbows on the scarred wood. “I think you know what happens on the thin shift. The night rounds. The meter house.”

Hobbs’s hand stilled on the glass. His fingers were broad and calloused, the nails rimmed with black that no amount of soap would lift. He looked at Rook and then looked away, toward the window where the street showed grey and empty through the grime.

“I shouldn’t be here.”

“You came because something’s sitting wrong with you. I can see that. I’m not company brass, Bill. I’m an engineer. I fix pipes. Right now I’ve got a pipe that doesn’t add up.”

“Pipes don’t add up.” Hobbs almost smiled. “That’s books.”

“Books and pipes. They’re meant to match.”

“They don’t, do they.” Hobbs pulled at his collar. The greatcoat was too heavy for the pub, and sweat had gathered along his hairline despite the chill. “That’s why you’re asking.”

Rook pushed the full glass across the table. Hobbs looked at it, then picked it up and drank half in one pull. His free hand, the one that had been turning the empty glass, now rested flat on the table, pressing down as though to keep the wood from moving.

“What have you seen, Bill?”

“Seen a lot of things. Night shift, you see things. Men sleeping in the retort house. Men drinking when they shouldn’t.”

“I’m not asking about men drinking.”

“No.” Hobbs stared at the table. “No, you’re not.”

Rook waited. The pub was nearly empty, just an old man nursing a half-pint at the bar and a woman reading a newspaper by the fire. The clock above the mantel ticked steadily. Sawdust on the floor absorbed the damp from boots tracked in from the street.

“You’ve been in the meter house,” Rook said. “After hours.”

“I do the night stoking. The meter house is on my round. You walk past it, you check the seals, you log the readings. Twice a night, that’s the routine.”

“And the readings don’t always match what you see.”

Hobbs drank the rest of his pint. He set the glass down carefully, precisely, and the wet ring it left overlapped the first one. His hand trembled as he withdrew it.

“There’s nights I walk past and the index marks are moving. The dials. You can see the brass fingers turning, slow, like clocks. Gas is flowing. You can hear it in the pipes, that low hum.”

“But the logs show different.”

“The logs show what the logs show. I write down what the dials read. But then Croker comes in after, and the figures are different.”

Rook’s hand tightened on his own glass. He kept his voice level. “Croker comes in after.”

“Foreman Croker, yes. He’s on the thin shift some nights. Not every night. But certain nights, regular, you could set your watch. He goes into the meter house after my round. I’ve seen the lamp in there when I’ve come back round. Light under the door.”

“What’s he doing in there?”

“I don’t know. Adjusting. That’s the word, isn’t it? He’s adjusting flows. But you don’t adjust flows at two in the morning. Adjustments happen in the day, with the engineer present, with the logbook open.”

“I know what the procedure is, Bill,” Rook said. “Day hours, engineer’s signature, both witnesses on the index. Croker’s not adjusting. He’s bypassing the index mechanism entirely.”

Hobbs looked at him sharply. “You’ve seen that done?”

“I’ve seen the results. Dials that read low against retort output. Meters that don’t track.” Rook met his eyes. “What he’s doing in that meter house at two in the morning isn’t in any manual.”

“This is something else,” Hobbs said quietly.

Rook turned the name over. Elias Croker. He had seen the man at the Beckton gasworks, a big fellow with a careful manner and clean cuffs who managed the night stokers and kept the retort schedules. Croker had been at Beckton longer than Rook, long enough to know every pipe and valve in the distribution network. Long enough to know which meters mattered and which ones could be touched without notice.

“How long has this been happening?”

“Months. Maybe longer. I only noticed because I started checking the index marks against the log entries. You do that enough times, you see the gap. The dials move, but the entries don’t. Someone is writing down numbers that don’t match what the brass is doing.”

“Have you seen him write in the log?”

“I’ve seen him leave the meter house with the book under his arm. He takes it out, and he brings it back. What he does with it in between, I can’t say. But there’s another book I’ve seen him carry. Smaller. Green cover. Not the company log. Something of his own.”

Rook filed that. A second book. A private record, separate from the official meter log. If Croker was keeping his own figures, the company books and the actual gas flow had been split into two streams, one for the record and one for whatever Croker was running.

“You said certain nights. Regular. Can you give me dates?”

Hobbs’s eyes darted to the window again. His shoulders had drawn up toward his ears, and his whole body had the look of a man preparing to bolt. “Why do you need dates?”

“Because I’ve got ledger entries that don’t match the meter readings on specific nights. If your nights line up with my nights, we’ve got something.”

“What’s in it for me?”

“The truth. And maybe keeping your name out of it.”

Hobbs rubbed his face with both hands. Coal dust left dark smears across his cheeks. “Tuesdays. Mostly Tuesdays. Sometimes the Wednesday after. He comes in around one, two in the morning, and he’s there for an hour, maybe less.”

Rook pressed his hand flat on the table. Tuesday. The sheet Ada carried in her satchel. The same night three lamps on Commercial Road were logged as lit when the route sheet said Griggs was elsewhere. The Beckton meter showed a deficit that matched the missing lamp hours exactly. Tuesday was not a coincidence. Tuesday was a schedule.

“The gas that moves when Croker adjusts the flows. Where does it go?”

“I don’t know. I’m a stoker. I feed the retorts. I don’t follow pipes.”

“But the district meters show less gas leaving the works than the retorts are producing.”

“Then someone’s tapping the line. Before the district meters. Between the works and the district mains. There’s a junction house, underground, where the main feeder splits to the districts. If you opened a valve there, you could draw gas off before it ever reached the district meters. The district meters would read low. The company books would show a deficit. But the gas wouldn’t be gone. It would just be going somewhere else.”

Hobbs spoke faster now, the words coming like a confession he had rehearsed in his head a hundred times without ever saying them aloud. His hands moved as he talked, tracing pipes and valves in the air above the table. Then he stopped. His eyes went to the window.

Rook followed his gaze. Through the grime, the street was grey and wet. A figure stood across the road, still, half in the shadow of the chandler’s awning. Not moving. Not walking on. Just standing.

“Who is that?” Hobbs’s voice had dropped to something barely above breath.

“I don’t know.”

“He’s been there. I saw him when I came in. I thought he was just a man waiting.”

“Maybe he is.”

“Maybe he isn’t.” Hobbs was already sliding out of the booth. His greatcoat caught the empty glass and knocked it sideways. It rolled across the table and Rook caught it before it hit the floor. “I have to go.”

“Bill. If you need to talk again, send word through the yard. Ask for me by name.”

But Hobbs was already at the door, pulling his collar up, his head down. He stepped out onto the street and turned left, moving quickly, not running but close to it. The figure across the road did not follow. It stood for another moment, then turned the other way and disappeared into the grey.

Rook sat back in the booth. His hand was still tight around the glass. The wet rings on the table overlapped, two circles intersecting, and he stared at them without seeing them. Croker. A second book. Tuesday nights. The junction house. He needed Ada.

He found her where they had agreed, on the Embankment near the Temple stairs. The river was at half-tide and the water lapped against the stone steps with a sound like slow tearing. Fog sat on the water, thick enough to blur the far bank to a smudge. She stood with her satchel pressed against her side and her notebook already open, pen in hand.

“You look like something happened,” she said.

“Something did.” Rook stopped beside her. The cold came off the river in a wall. “I met the stoker. Bill Hobbs. He works the night shift at Beckton.”

“And?”

“And he’s seen Croker. Foreman Elias Croker. Goes into the meter house after hours on certain nights. Adjusts the flows. Takes the company log out and brings it back. And he keeps a second book. Green cover. Private.”

Ada’s pen stopped. She looked at Rook, then back at her notebook. “A second set of figures.”

“That’s what I thought.”

“Which nights?”

“Tuesdays. Mostly Tuesdays, sometimes Wednesday.”

Ada turned pages in her notebook. Rook could see the columns she had copied, the meter readings from Beckton matched against the lamplighters’ ledger entries. Her pen traced down the Tuesday column, tapping each entry as she went.

“October fourteenth. Tuesday. Three lamps on Commercial Road logged as lit. Griggs assigned to that route in the ledger, but his own route sheet puts him on the back lanes. The Beckton district meter for that night shows a deficit of roughly four hundred cubic feet against the retort output. Griggs denied lighting those lamps. He denied it to me, and he denied it to the police. And now he is dead.”

“You’re saying Croker killed him.”

“I am saying the ledger was altered to put Griggs on a route he was not on, and the man who could prove he was not on it is drowned in the canal with a bruise on his temple. That, I am saying, is not an accident. I am saying that is a sequence of verifiable facts that constitutes a frame-up.”

Rook leaned on the parapet. The stone was cold and damp under his forearms. Below, the river moved grey and slow. “Hobbs told me something else. He said the gas could be drawn off before it reaches the district meters. There’s a junction house where the main feeder splits. If someone opened a valve there, the district meters would read low.”

“And the company books would show a deficit.”

“Right.”

“But the gas wouldn’t be stolen. It would be going somewhere.”

Ada’s pen moved across the notebook. She wrote quickly, the scratch of the nib loud against the fog-muffled quiet of the Embankment. Rook watched her write, and the shape of what she was assembling became clearer with each line she laid down.

“The old reading was theft,” she said. “Someone tapping the mains, stealing gas, and altering the ledger to cover the shortfall. But if Croker is adjusting flows at the Beckton gasworks itself, before the district meters, then the gas isn’t being stolen from the system. It is being diverted within the system. The district meters read low because the gas never reaches them. It branches off earlier.”

“So the deficit in the books isn’t a theft. It’s a reroute.”

“Precisely. And a reroute has to go somewhere. Pipes don’t just stop. Gas flows until it finds an outlet. If someone is diverting gas before the district meters, they are sending it to a specific destination. A destination that does not appear on the company distribution maps.”

Rook straightened. The name sat in his mouth. “Croker.”

“Croker is your department, Mr. Rook. The pipes are yours. The numbers are mine.” She closed the notebook and slid it into the satchel. “I need to compare Hobbs’s Tuesday nights against the full meter log. If the deficits match his schedule, we have the pattern. And I need to find out what that green book is. A second ledger means a second set of records. Someone is keeping their own accounts.”

“Ada. If Croker killed Griggs to keep this quiet, he’ll kill again. Hobbs is scared out of his wits. He ran because he saw someone watching the pub.”

“I know.” She turned to face him. The fog had put a fine dampness on her hair and coat, and her face was set and still. “That is why we do this carefully. I will work the numbers. You find the junction house. If the gas is being diverted before the district meters, there will be physical evidence at the junction. A valve that’s been opened. A pipe that shouldn’t be there.”

“And if there is?”

“Then we have the mechanism. Not just the paper trail, but the physical apparatus. The company can dismiss a ledger discrepancy as a clerical error. They cannot dismiss a valve.”

Rook nodded. Below them, the river shifted, and a gust carried the smell of brine and industrial smoke across the Embankment. Somewhere east, the Beckton gasworks sat in its own haze, the retort chimneys barely visible against the sky. Croker would be there tonight, on the thin shift, adjusting the flows with his own hands.

“If the gas isn’t being stolen,” Rook said, “where is Croker sending it?”

Ada did not answer. She turned and walked north toward the yard, her satchel pressed close, her notebook inside it. Rook watched her go, then looked back at the river. The fog was turning to proper rain now, fine and cold, and it stippled the surface of the Thames with small circles that appeared and vanished and appeared again.

Rook turned up his collar and walked east.
