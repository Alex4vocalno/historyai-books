Chapter 9

A Body in the Canal

The ledger page sat in her satchel like a stone — the copied meter readings, the Tuesday sheet, the roster with Griggs’s name circled in faint pencil — all of it pressed flat against her hip as she walked the corridor back toward the clerking office. The frosted window threw its grey Thames light across the floor in a long, watery rectangle. She had barely settled into her chair, had barely opened her own ledger to the page where the three Commercial Road entries sat in their neat, impossible row, when the door opened behind her with a sound like a hand slapped flat against wet wood.

“Miss Quill.” Samuel Rook’s voice had lost its usual technical steadiness. He stood in the doorway holding a cap in both hands — Griggs’s cap, recovered from the canal bank — turning it slowly. His face had gone the color of tallow. “It’s Griggs.”

Ada’s pen stopped where it rested against the page. The nib left a small, round blot of ink that spread outward into the fibre of the paper. She watched it for perhaps two seconds, then set the pen down and stood.

“What’s happened to him?”

Rook looked at the cap, then at her. “They pulled him from Regent’s Canal not an hour ago. A bargeman found him caught under the pilings at Battle Bridge.”

“Who told you?”

“A constable came to the yard asking whether we could identify him. I went with the constable to the canal bank.” He stopped turning the cap. “I’ve just come from there.”

Ada reached for her satchel. The strap caught on the corner of the desk, and she pulled it free with a sharp tug that rattled the inkwell. Rook watched her do this without comment, but he shifted his weight from one foot to the other as though the floor had become untrustworthy.

“Take me there,” she said.

“Miss Quill, the police are already—““Take me there, Mr. Rook.”

He finally held Griggs’s cap crushed against his chest as he turned into the corridor. Ada followed him through the yard, past the gas bracket that hissed its small blue flame into the damp air, and out through the gate onto the street. The cold hit her cleanly across the face and hands. She had not worn her gloves. The satchel bumped against her hip with each step, and inside it the ledger page and the notebook with the copied meter readings shifted against each other with a sound like paper breathing.

They walked south and east, past the brick flank of the works and the narrow houses that leaned against each other as though propping themselves upright against the morning. Rook set a pace that was nearly a run for a man of his build, and Ada matched it without speaking, her breath coming in short, visible plumes. The streets smelled of coal dust and horse and the particular sourness of the river at low tide. Twice she had to step around a costermonger’s barrow, and once a dray horse swung its head and clipped her shoulder with its muzzle, and she kept walking without breaking stride.

Regent’s Canal announced itself before they reached it — a smell of algae and coal-tar and stagnant water that thickened the air until it sat in the back of the throat. They came down the towpath at a half-jog, the ground soft underfoot, and Ada saw the cluster of men and the flash of a lantern ahead where the path curved past Battle Bridge. A police constable stood with his arms out, holding back a small knot of onlookers who had gathered with the idle patience of people drawn to trouble. Beyond them, on the canal bank, two men were crouching over something on the ground.

Rook stopped walking. “There,” he said, and pointed with Griggs’s cap.

Ada passed the constable without slowing. He put out an arm, and she ducked under it, and he said something she did not hear because the canal was suddenly very loud in her ears — the slow, thick sound of water moving against wood — and then she was standing on the bank above the body.

Thomas Griggs lay on his back on the canal bank, his clothes black with water and pressed flat against his chest and legs as though they had been painted on. The tallow tin he always carried must have been in his pocket; the grease had worked through the soaked fabric in dark patches along his thigh. His face was turned slightly toward the towpath, and his mouth was open. One of the men crouching beside him — a police surgeon, by the black bag open on the ground — was reaching for a length of grey wool cloth that lay folded beside the body. As the surgeon lifted the cloth and drew it upward across Griggs’s chest, Ada saw the bruise.

It sat on the left temple, just above the cheekbone — a dark crescent, the color of overripe plum skin, its edges fading into the surrounding flesh in a way that no fall into water could produce. The shape was clean at the lower curve and ragged at the upper, as though something had struck him and then dragged across the skin. Ada stared at it. The surgeon’s hand moved, pulling the wool cloth higher, and the bruise disappeared under the fabric. The cloth was drawn over Griggs’s face, and the open mouth and the staring eyes and the dark crescent were gone.

The smell of canal water — algae and coal-tar and something older, something mineral — filled her throat. She swallowed against it. The orderly world of her ledgers, the neat rows of figures that she had copied and checked and rechecked, had produced this: a man on his back with water in his lungs and a bruise on his temple that no canal could have made.

A hand closed on her arm. Rook, standing behind her, his fingers cold through her sleeve. “Miss Quill. There’s someone you should—““Sergeant.” The police surgeon straightened and looked past Ada toward the towpath. “We’re ready to move him.”

Ada turned. A man in plain clothes was walking down the canal bank toward them — a sergeant from the Leman Street station, his coat collar turned up against the cold. He stopped beside the body, and the surgeon lifted the edge of the wool cloth. The sergeant looked at what lay underneath for approximately three seconds, then nodded once and let the cloth fall back.

“Right,” he said. “Inspector Varney’s given his view from the station. Drunken accident — fell in on his way home, likely. You’ve seen the state of these lamplighters at the end of a shift.” He spoke to the surgeon, but the words were aimed outward, at the onlookers, at Ada, at the morning itself. “Get him to the mortuary. The inspector’ll file the report this afternoon.”

Ada stepped forward. “Sergeant.”

He turned his head. His eyes found her face, and something in them — not recognition, exactly, but the tired alertness of a man who has identified a source of future inconvenience — settled into place. “Miss Quill. The Gas Light and Coke Company.” He said the company’s name the way one might say the name of a distant relative at a funeral, with a correctness that carried no warmth. “What brings you to the canal bank at this hour?”

“Thomas Griggs was a lamplighter on the Commercial Road route. I had business with him — company business. I need to know what happened to him.”

“What happened to him is that he drank more than was good for him and walked into the canal.” The sergeant shifted a file of papers from one hand to the other, then tapped it once against his thigh. “These men work nights, Miss Quill. They work with fire and gas and they drink to take the edge off, and sometimes the edge they take off is their own footing. It’s an accident, far as the inspector’s concerned. The file closes today.”

“Did you examine the bruise on his temple?”

The sergeant’s hand stopped moving. He looked at her, and his face rearranged itself very slightly — the eyebrows settling a fraction lower, the mouth tightening at one corner. “The police surgeon has examined the body, Miss Quill. Inspector Varney’s office has reviewed the findings and is satisfied.”

“I saw the bruise before the cloth was drawn over his face. A crescent-shaped mark above the left cheekbone. A fall into water doesn’t produce that.”

“A fall against a piling does. The canal is full of them.” The sergeant tucked the file under his arm and spoke around the gesture. “Miss Quill, I understand you’ve been troubled by some irregularity in the company’s books. A clerical error, as I recall. You came to the inspector’s office about it, and he explained his position, and you went away. I’d have thought that would be the end of it.”

Ada’s hands were cold. The satchel strap cut into her shoulder where she had pulled it tight. “Thomas Griggs told me he did not light three lamps on Commercial Road on October fourteenth. He said they had sent him elsewhere that night. The ledger entry was wrong, he told me, and he had not been near those lamps. He told me this, and now he is lying on the canal bank with a bruise on his head.”

The sergeant pulled the file back out from under his arm. He held it in front of him, squinting at the top page as though checking the heading. “Miss Quill. You are a clerk. Your work is with figures and pages, not with bodies and canal banks. I appreciate that you’ve taken an interest in the welfare of the company’s employees — it speaks well of you, in a general way — but I must tell you plainly that there is nothing here that requires your attention. The man drank, the man fell, the man drowned. These things happen, and they will continue to happen whether or not a clerk from the Gas Light and Coke Company comes to the towpath to inspect the body.”

He tucked the file back under his arm and turned to the police surgeon. “Get him moved. And clear these people off the bank.”

The constable on the path began herding the onlookers backward. Ada stood where she was. The canal water moved against the pilings with a slow, thick sound, and the smell of algae and coal-tar sat in her throat like something she could not swallow. The sergeant was already walking back up the bank, his narrow shoulders cutting the cold air ahead of him. He did not look back.

Rook’s hand found her elbow. He guided her gently away from the body, back up the bank, past the constable and the dispersing onlookers, and onto the towpath. They walked in silence for several minutes, the canal on their left and the soot-blackened backs of warehouses on their right. The sound of the water faded behind them, replaced by the rattle of carts on Commercial Street and the distant clang of a railway shunting yard.

Ada stopped. She turned to Rook and stood looking at him. His face was still the color of tallow, and the canal damp had settled darkly into the fabric of his coat. He held Griggs’s cap in one hand, and the brim was crushed almost flat from the pressure of his grip.

“He was not a drunkard,” she said. “I have spoken with him. I have sat with him in the lamplighters’ yard and listened to him describe his rounds. He was precise about his work. He knew which lamps he lit and which he did not, and he was frightened.”

“Frightened of what?”

“Of the ledger. Of the fact that his name was on it for lamps he never touched. Of what it meant that someone had put it there.”

Rook turned Griggs’s cap over in his hands, running his thumb along the seam where the brim met the crown. “The inspector will close the file.”

“Yes. He will write ‘accidental death’ and the date and his name, and he will put it in a drawer, and no one will look at it again.”

“And the bruise?”

Ada looked at the canal behind them. The bank where Griggs had lain was empty now. The police surgeon and his assistant had gone. The wool cloth and the black bag had vanished. The ground where the body had been was just canal mud, no different from any other patch of canal mud along the towpath.

“A fall against a piling,” she said. “That is what he told me. A fall against a piling produces a linear mark, or a scattered one. It does not produce a crescent. A crescent is made by something curved — a tool, a pipe, the heel of a boot — striking at an angle and dragging across the bone.”

Rook looked at her steadily. His hands stopped turning the cap.

“Three lamps on Commercial Road were logged as lit on October fourteenth,” Ada said. “Thomas Griggs swore he did not light them. He swore he was on a different route. He swore the ledger was wrong, and he swore this to me, and he swore it to the police, and now he is dead with a bruise on his temple that no canal piling could have made.”

She paused. The satchel strap had cut a red line into her shoulder, and she shifted it without thinking.

“The meter readings for the Whitechapel southern section showed a deficit for that night. Gas flowed through those mains. It left the Beckton works and entered the distribution network, and the district meter recorded it, and the mains showed a deficit that the consumption ledger does not account for. Three lamps burned on Commercial Road that should not have burned, and the gas that fed them passed through pipes that Thomas Griggs never opened.”

Rook put Griggs’s cap in his coat, tucking it carefully into an inner pocket. He pulled his jaw tight, the muscles bunching beneath the skin. “You’re saying someone used his route to cover the diversion.”

“I am saying someone wrote his name in the ledger for lamps he never lit, so that the gas consumed by those lamps would appear accounted for. What I am saying is that when Thomas Griggs denied lighting them — to me, to the police — he became a liability to whoever profited from that gas. And I am saying that a man who falls into the canal drunk does not arrive with a crescent bruise above his cheekbone.”

Rook stood very still. A barge passed on the canal behind him, its horse plodding along the opposite towpath, and the water in the canal shifted and lapped against the pilings with a sound that was almost conversational. He looked at Ada for a long moment, and then he reached into his coat and pulled out a handkerchief and wiped his hands with it slowly, methodically, as though cleaning a piece of machinery before disassembly.

“What do you need?” he said.

“I need the distribution maps for the Whitechapel southern section. The ones that show every junction, every branch, every service pipe that connects to the district main.”

“Those are in the engineering office. Finch keeps them in the locked cabinet.”

“Then I need you to get them.”

Rook folded the handkerchief and put it back in his coat. His hand stayed in the pocket afterward, and the line of his shoulders had changed — settled, squared, as though he had taken something onto them that had not been there a moment ago. He looked once toward the canal bank where the body had been, and then he looked back at Ada.

“Tomorrow morning,” he said. “Finch takes the early tally and doesn’t return until half nine. The cabinet will be unlocked for the maintenance check.”

“Tomorrow, then.”

Ada turned and began walking back toward the works. The satchel bumped against her hip. Inside it, the ledger page and the notebook and the roster and the Tuesday sheet lay pressed together, and the pen was in her pocket, and the cold air moved across her knuckles where her gloves should have been. She could still smell the canal — algae and coal-tar and that mineral undertone that sat in the throat and would not clear. Behind her, Rook’s footsteps fell into rhythm with hers on the towpath, and neither of them spoke again until they reached the yard gate.

She stopped with her hand on the latch. The gas bracket hissed its small blue flame into the damp air above the granite setts, and the light it cast was steady and even, the light of a well-regulated system doing exactly what it was designed to do.

“It was murder, Mr. Rook.”

She said it quietly, the way one states a figure that has been checked and rechecked and found to balance. Rook stood beside her in the yard, and after a moment he nodded once — not in agreement, exactly, but in the manner of a man who has heard a sum stated and is preparing to work it through himself.

Ada lifted the latch and went inside.
