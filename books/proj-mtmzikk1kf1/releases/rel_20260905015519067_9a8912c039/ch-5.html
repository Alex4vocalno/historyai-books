Chapter 5

The German Precedent (1984-1994)

The decision notice from the Federal Review Board for Media Harmful to Minors was a single sheet of paper. It bore the date May 3, 1984, and a file number. Its subject was a floppy disk containing a computer program called The Great Giana Sisters. The board’s finding was succinct: the game glorified criminality and was therefore to be placed on the Index of Media Harmful to Minors. With that administrative stroke, a state apparatus built to regulate pulp novels and exploitation films extended its authority decisively into the digital realm.

The American Senate’s televised panic over Mortal Kombat was still nine years in the future. While the United States would later scramble to invent a voluntary ratings system to avert political wrath—the Entertainment Software Rating Board (ESRB) was officially launched on September 16, 1994, after being announced to Congress on July 29 of that year—Germany already possessed a mature, mandatory censorship machine. Its gears had begun turning decades earlier, forged not in response to a media circus, but in the sober constitutional aftermath of a collapsed dictatorship.

The Bundesprüfstelle für jugendgefährdende Medien—the Federal Review Board for Media Harmful to Minors, or BPjM—was a creature of the 1953 Youth Protection Act. Its legal DNA carried a specific, post-war anxiety. The Weimar Republic’s chaotic media landscape and the Nazi regime’s sophisticated propaganda apparatus had demonstrated the potent corruptibility of mass communication. The new West German constitution, the Basic Law, thus embedded a mandate to protect youth from morally corrupting influences, a clause intended as a democratic bulwark against authoritarian seduction. The BPjM was its operational arm.

It did not rate or advise. It indexed. Placement on its list triggered severe legal restrictions: a banned title could not be advertised, displayed in shop windows where minors might see it, or sold to anyone under eighteen. For all practical purposes in the retail environment of the 1980s, it was commercial death. The Great Giana Sisters, a clone of Nintendo’s Super Mario Bros. developed for home computers like the Commodore 64, seemed an unlikely candidate for such grave sanction.

Its gameplay involved guiding two sisters through a whimsical landscape, bouncing on mushrooms and collecting diamonds. The “glorification of criminality” charge stemmed from a thin narrative veneer: the sisters were ostensibly stealing the gems. The board’s reasoning treated the game’s abstract action as a literal narrative. Jumping to collect a sparkling pixel was not a mechanic of challenge and reward; it was an instruction in theft. This interpretive leap was foundational.

It established that the BPjM would judge software not as a complex system of rules and player agency, but as a linear, representational story, its moral content extracted from the thinnest of pretexts. The developer, a small company called Time Warp Productions, had no lobbyists, no legal department, and no precedent to challenge the ruling. The game vanished from store shelves. The precedent, however, remained. Through the late 1980s, as the American industry grew preoccupied with console wars and mascots, the BPjM’s quiet bureaucracy accumulated power. Its purview expanded with technological change.

The 1985 amendment to the Youth Protection Act explicitly brought “image carriers and data storage media” under its authority, a clause that captured the nascent software market. The board’s composition reflected its quasi-judicial function. It was staffed by volunteer members drawn from relevant social groups—educators, youth welfare organizations, churches, art and literature societies, and the publishing trade. Their deliberations were not public. A case could be initiated by a simple complaint from any of these recognized bodies, or from a state-level authority.

Once a proceeding began, the publisher or distributor bore the burden of proof to demonstrate the work was not harmful. The system was preventative and state-powered, a stark contrast to the reactive, industry-funded model taking shape across the Atlantic. This operational logic met its next major test with the arrival of a new kind of software: the first-person shooter. In 1992, id Software’s Wolfenstein 3D presented German authorities with a dilemma that was both technical and profoundly historical.

The game cast the player as an Allied spy fighting through a Nazi castle, shooting guards, and ultimately confronting Adolf Hitler in a mechanized suit. Its pixelated violence was extreme for the time.

But the BPjM’s primary objection was not the bloodshed. It was the presence of Nazi symbols—swastikas, SS runes, Hitler’s likeness. Their use was prohibited by German criminal law, which bans the public display of symbols of unconstitutional organizations, a law designed to prevent neo-Nazi revivalism.

The board indexed Wolfenstein 3D not merely for potential harm to youth, but for violating these strict statutes. The ruling had immediate, clarifying force. It drew a line in the digital sand that was uniquely German. In the United States, the game was controversial but legally protected speech; its violence was a selling point. In Germany, the violence was almost secondary. The crime was the unauthorized resurrection of banned iconography, regardless of narrative context. A game where the player killed Nazis was deemed, paradoxically, to be propagating Nazi imagery. This created a compliance puzzle for publishers.

To access the German market, publishers had to remove the symbols. Developers at id Software and their licensees scrubbed the game, replacing swastikas with abstract, non-offensive shapes. The act of censorship was not about reducing gore to achieve a Teen or Mature rating; it was a mandatory act of historical sanitization, a rewriting of the game’s own visual language to conform to legal doctrine. The edited version was not an alternative; it was the only permissible product. The Wolfenstein decision cemented a critical principle.

The board could and would enforce existing criminal law through its indexing power, blending youth protection with broader cultural policing. This expanded its effective remit far beyond the vague concept of “moral harm.” It gave its rulings the weight of settled legal precedent, making them harder for publishers to contest on purely artistic grounds. A game could now be banned for two distinct, often overlapping, reasons: for being jugendgefährdend (harmful to minors) under the Youth Protection Act, or for containing criminally punishable content like Nazi propaganda or extreme violence glorification under the penal code.

The latter charge carried greater stigma and more severe distribution limits. For international software companies, Germany was no longer just another market with peculiar tastes. It was a jurisdiction where a game could be declared illegal. The BPjM’s authority solidified just as the global video game industry converged on a single, violent flashpoint. In 1993, the same year American senators brandished footage of Mortal Kombat and Night Trap, the German board received complaints about the game’s domestic releases. The BPjM’s evaluation moved with deliberate speed.

It found Mortal Kombat’s detailed, visceral depictions of dismemberment and murder—its “fatalities”—constituted a glorification of violence that was “brutalizing” and “likely to harm the development of children and adolescents into self-responsible and socially competent personalities.” In early 1994, as the Interactive Digital Software Association (IDSA) in the United States was hastily drafting the ESRB’s code of conduct in response to the 1993 hearings, the BPjM placed Mortal Kombat on the Index. The consequences were categorical. The indexed version could not be sold openly. But the German market was large and lucrative, and the game had immense notoriety.

This presented the first major test of the system’s economic pressure valve. Publishers and distributors, now facing a mature enforcement regime, began to engage in preemptive self-censorship. They submitted edited versions of games to the BPjM for review before release, hoping to secure a ruling that the modified product was not harmful to minors, and thus avoid indexing. For Mortal Kombat, this meant creating a special regional version.

The blood, a vivid red in the American release, was changed to a sickly gray or white “sweat” in some versions, and to a bright, unnatural green in others. The most graphic fatalities were removed or simplified. The board assessed this neutered build. It decided the edited game, stripped of its most sensational violence, no longer crossed the threshold for indexing.

This created a two-tier market. The uncensored, indexed original existed in a legal shadow—unadvertisable, unshelvable, but not contraband for adults. The censored, approved version could be sold freely. Most mainstream retailers, risk-averse and unwilling to navigate the restrictions on indexed titles, stocked only the green-blood edition. The precedent was profound.

The BPjM had not simply banned a product. It had actively shaped its content, creating a sanctioned alternative that met its doctrinal standards. The board was not just a gatekeeper saying yes or no. It was becoming an editorial force, defining what constituted acceptable violence for the German public. The parallel with the American process was stark but superficial. Both systems produced altered versions of Mortal Kombat. In the U.S., the edits were a voluntary choice by publishers seeking a broader rating, a commercial calculation to maximize shelf space.

The ESRB rated what it was given; it did not demand changes. In Germany, the edits were a mandatory condition for legal retail access. The BPjM’s approval was not a rating guiding consumer choice, but a state-issued license to sell. Failure to obtain it meant effective banishment from the mainstream market. The “M” for Mature in America was a passport to broader distribution. The BPjM’s non-index ruling was a visa permitting entry at all. This period from 1984 to 1994 defined the German precedent.

It was a decade of quiet accretion, not loud crisis. The BPjM processed a growing stream of computer and video game cases, each decision layering a new restriction onto its doctrinal framework. A 1990 ruling against a martial arts game called International Karate+ cited its “instructional character” in violence. A 1991 indexing of the horror adventure Maniac Mansion pointed to its “trivialization of serious crimes.”

The board’s criteria grew more elaborate, covering glorification of violence, crime, racism, and the corruption of youth through amoral narratives. Its logic was consistently prophylactic. It sought to prevent harm before it could occur by removing the offending media from public view. The system’s strength was also its point of greatest tension. It was designed to protect youth, a goal that enjoyed broad societal consensus in post-war Germany.

Yet its mechanisms of control—prior restraint, opaque deliberations, and the power to erase content—bore an uncomfortable resemblance to the authoritarian instruments it was meant to supersede. The board navigated this tension by grounding its work in legal procedure and social consensus.

The board’s interpretation of The Great Giana Sisters was not an isolated bureaucratic overreach but a logical application of its foundational worldview. This worldview treated media not as a form of play or a technical system, but as a narrative text with a fixed moral message. In the board’s deliberations, the interactive and abstract nature of software was consistently subordinate to a search for representational content that could be judged by the standards applied to books and films.

This interpretive stance created a peculiar dissonance. A game’s mechanics—the very elements that defined it as a game—were often ignored, while superficial narrative elements, like the premise of stealing gems, were elevated to central importance. This approach ensured that the expanding universe of digital software would always be legible to the board’s existing legal and cultural categories, allowing a system designed in the age of pulp paperbacks to govern the age of the floppy disk without fundamental revision.

The operational quietude of the BPjM through the late 1980s belied its growing institutional confidence. While the American industry’s controversies played out on television and in congressional hearings, the German system worked through written complaints, closed-door deliberations, and official notices. This procedural insulation was a source of strength. It allowed the board to build a body of precedent away from the glare of public debate, framing each decision not as a response to moral panic but as the measured application of settled law.

The 1985 legislative amendment that explicitly brought software under its purview was less a radical expansion than a formal acknowledgment of a regulatory trajectory already in motion. The board’s volunteer members, drawn from society’s established custodial institutions, brought a consistent sensibility to this task. Their evaluations reflected a deep-seated belief that media exerted a direct, corrupting influence on the malleable adolescent mind, a premise that made preventative removal a logical, even necessary, public duty.

The Wolfenstein 3D case, therefore, did not introduce a new power but revealed the full scope of an existing one. By indexing the game for violating criminal statutes on Nazi symbols, the BPjM demonstrated that its function was not limited to protecting youth from abstract moral harm. It could also serve as an enforcement mechanism for the state’s broader project of Vergangenheitsbewältigung—coming to terms with the past. This fusion of youth protection and historical policing created a uniquely potent form of control.

A publisher could not argue that the swastikas in Wolfenstein were historically contextualized or that the player’s goal was to destroy the Nazi regime. The law was absolute, and the board’s application of it was procedural and unforgiving. The resulting compliance ritual—the scrubbing of banned iconography—became a standard operating procedure for foreign companies. It was a tangible, repeated lesson in the cost of market access, teaching the global industry that to sell in Germany was to engage in a specific, state-directed act of editorial revision.

The Mortal Kombat proceedings thoroughly tested and rigidified this compliance framework. The board’s 1994 ruling did more than index a violent game; it operationalized the pathway for its own circumvention. By approving a censored version, the BPjM actively participated in the creation of a new commercial product. This transformed its role from a passive arbiter issuing bans to an active collaborator in content modification. Publishers learned that engagement with the board was not a single, terminal judgment but a negotiation.

Its members represented established pillars of society—churches, teachers, publishers. Their judgments reflected a conservative, culturally specific vision of what endangered the young. This vision had little room for the transgressive, interactive violence that defined the emerging global gaming aesthetic. By the end of 1994, the landscape was set.

America had its new, voluntary ESRB, a system born from a public relations emergency and designed to forestall government action after senators like Joe Lieberman threatened federal regulation. Germany had its mature, state-mandated BPjM, a system born from constitutional trauma and designed to actively purge the public sphere of harmful content. One was a shield for the industry, the other a sword for the state.

Both would claim to protect children. Both, in practice, functioned as regulatory regimes governing market access. The American system asked publishers to label their product for the retailer’s convenience. The German system demanded they change their product for the state’s approval. The BPjM’s indexed list grew into a ledger of prohibition, a public record of what could not be freely sold.

For the international video game industry, that ledger was no longer just a German document. It was a design document. It handed off a relentless pressure, a simple ultimatum that would force every publisher wanting German marks to answer a single question before a single line of code was written: What will we change?
