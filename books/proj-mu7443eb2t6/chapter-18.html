Chapter 18

Harmony Before the Return

Vi Hollis stood on the step, clutching a small parcel wrapped in yesterday’s newsprint. The morning light caught the edges of the paper where it had been folded with deliberate, childlike care, and Ada Penrose felt a tightening beneath her ribs that had nothing to do with the cold air seeping through the gap beneath the door. She had heard the tram rumble past an hour ago—the first in nearly two weeks, its familiar racket a declaration that the world had righted itself. The strike was over. The borrowed time had run out.

“Miss Penrose.” Vi’s voice was steady, but her knuckles were white against the parcel. Her wool cap was pulled low, her cheeks flushed from the walk.

“Vi.” Ada stepped aside, the word feeling heavier than usual. “Come in.”

The girl crossed the threshold with her characteristic seriousness, that borrowed adult demeanor she wore like a coat that didn’t quite fit. Ada closed the door against the February chill and turned to find Vi standing in the center of the front room, not moving toward the piano bench as she always did. Instead, she extended the parcel.

“I made something. For you.”

Ada took it. The newsprint was soft from handling, the ink smudged where small fingers had gripped it. She unfolded it carefully, the rustle of paper loud in the quiet room. Inside lay a single sheet of drawing paper, creased once from being folded in half. On it, Vi had drawn a staff of musical notes in pencil, the lines wavering slightly where her hand had hesitated. The notes were simple, childlike in their spacing, but earnest. At the top, in careful block letters: For Miss Penrose.

Ada looked at the drawing for a long moment. She could see the effort in every mark—the way Vi had tried to make the note heads round and even, the stems straight. It was not music that could be played. It was something else entirely.

“You made this.”

Vi nodded. “I wanted you to have it. Before.”

Before. The word hung between them, unspoken but understood. Before the trams started running again. Before her father’s schedule reclaimed the hours. Before the lesson time dissolved back into the ordinary rhythm of a neighborhood that had forgotten, for nearly two weeks, what ordinary meant.

“Thank you.” Ada’s voice came out quieter than she intended. She carried the drawing to the small table beside the window, where her own sheet music was stacked in neat piles. She placed it on top, where the afternoon light would reach it. “It is the loveliest gift I have received in a very long time.”

Vi’s cheeks flushed deeper, and she looked down at her boots. “It’s not real music.”

“That is not what makes it valuable.”

The girl looked up. Something shifted behind her eyes—a flicker of recognition, quickly banked. Vi Hollis, with her serious face and her starving child’s perception, understood exactly what Ada meant. The knowledge passed between them like a shared breath.

“Shall we have a lesson, then?” Ada moved toward the piano, grateful for the familiar shape of the question. “A final one.”

Vi nodded and took her place on the bench. She removed her cap, folding it in her lap, and placed her fingers on the keys in the position Ada had taught her. The posture was improving. The curved fingers, the relaxed wrists. She had been a good student. She had been more than that.

Ada reached for the sheet music she had set aside that morning, before she had heard the tram, before she had known with certainty that this would be the last time. It was an arrangement her father had used—a simple duet for teacher and student. She had not played it since his death. The paper was yellowed at the edges, the penciled fingerings still visible where he had marked them for her, decades ago, when she had been younger than Vi.

“I thought we might try something different today.” Ada settled onto the bench beside Vi, the wood creaking under their combined weight. The proximity was unusual. She had always kept a careful distance—standing behind or beside, never sharing the bench. But this was not an ordinary lesson. “My father taught me this when I was beginning. It is meant for two players.”

Vi looked at the sheet music, her eyes widening slightly at the handwritten notations. “Your father?”

“Yes.” The word came easier than Ada expected. “He was my teacher, as I am yours. He believed that music was not meant to be played alone. Even when we practice alone, we are always playing for someone. Or with someone.”

She placed her hands on the lower keys, feeling the familiar cool smoothness of the ivory beneath her fingers. Vi watched her, then turned back to her own part—the higher octave, the melody line that her smaller hands could reach.

“Ready?”

Vi nodded.

They began.

The first bars were tentative, the way new duets always are. Ada played the bass line, simple chord progressions that grounded the piece, while Vi’s right hand picked out the melody. There were mistakes—a missed note here, a hesitation there. But Ada did not correct her. She adjusted her own tempo to match Vi’s, slowing when the girl’s fingers stumbled, waiting when she needed to find her place.

And then, somewhere in the third measure, something shifted.

Vi found the rhythm. Her shoulders dropped, her breathing steadied, and her fingers began to move with a confidence Ada had not seen before. The melody rose, simple and sweet—a child’s song transformed by the conviction of the playing. Ada matched her, adding depth and warmth beneath the tune, their hands moving in parallel streams that met and parted like voices in conversation.

The room filled with sound.

Ada felt it in her chest before she heard it fully—the vibration of the strings, the resonance in the piano’s body, the way the notes seemed to hang in the air long after her fingers had left the keys. She had not played this piece since her father’s hands had occupied the space where Vi’s now rested. The memory should have been sharp, a blade to the throat. Instead, it was soft. The pain had worn down to something else, something that felt almost like gratitude.

Vi was not her father. The girl’s hands were smaller, her touch lighter, her mistakes more frequent. But she was present. She was here, on this bench, in this room that had been Ada’s alone for so long. And she was making music.

The duet built toward its final bars. Ada watched Vi’s face—the concentration smoothing into something more open, the furrow between her brows relaxing. The girl was no longer thinking about her fingers. She was no longer thinking about the notes. She was simply playing, caught in the current of the sound they were making together.

The last chord was a whole note, sustained. Ada pressed the keys and held them, feeling the vibration travel up through her arms and into her shoulders. Vi’s hand remained on the upper octave, her small fingers pressing the keys with surprising steadiness. They sat together in the sound as it faded, neither lifting their hands too soon, neither rushing to break the moment.

The silence that followed was not empty.

Ada exhaled. Her hands rested on her thighs, and she became aware of the ache in her fingers, the tension she had been carrying in her wrists without realizing it. The room felt different. Larger, somehow, and yet more contained. The afternoon light had shifted while they played—the rectangle of sun on the floorboards moving several inches toward the wall.

“That was—“ Vi stopped. She looked down at her hands, still poised above the keys. “I didn’t know it could feel like that.”

“Neither did I.” The admission surprised Ada. She had not intended to say it aloud. But it was true. She had played this duet with her father countless times, but it had never felt like this. It had always been a lesson, an exercise, a step in the long progression toward mastery. This had been something else. This had been a conversation between two people who understood, in ways they could not articulate, what it meant to be alone.

She looked at Vi, really looked at her. The girl had grown thinner over the past weeks. Ada could see it in the way her coat hung on her shoulders, the sharp line of her wrist where her sleeve pulled back. The Hollis household had been running out of coal, she knew. Frank Hollis had been walking the neighborhood with that defeated posture of a man who could not provide, and the weight of that defeat had settled somewhere behind his daughter’s eyes.

But Vi had shown up. Every lesson. Every time. She had walked through the cold with her cheeks flushed and her cap pulled low, and she had sat at this piano and played her scales with a determination that had nothing to do with music and everything to do with holding herself together.

“Miss Penrose?”

“Yes, Vi.”

“Will you—“ The girl stopped again. She was looking at the piano, at the keys in front of her, at the sheet music still propped on the stand. “Will you keep the drawing? Even after?”

“After what?”

“After everything goes back to normal.”

The question landed somewhere beneath Ada’s ribs. She thought of the drawing on the table by the window—the penciled notes that could not be played, the careful block letters spelling out her name. She thought of the trams rumbling past, the resumption of schedules, the return of a world that had no room for borrowed time.

“I will keep it,” she said. “I will look at it every day.”

Vi turned to her, and for a moment, her serious face cracked. Something raw and young flickered in her expression—a grief that had no name because she did not yet have the words for it. Then she looked away, and the moment passed.

“I should go.” Vi slid off the bench, her feet finding the floor with a soft thud. She reached for her cap and pulled it over her hair, tucking the loose strands beneath the wool with practiced efficiency. “Father will be home soon. He’ll be looking for me.”

“Of course.” Ada rose as well. She followed Vi to the door, her hands clasped behind her back to keep them from reaching out. She had never touched Vi. She had maintained that distance with the same rigor she maintained everything else. But now, in this final moment, she felt the absence of that gesture as a physical ache.

Vi opened the door. The cold air rushed in, sharp and bright. The street beyond was quiet, the afternoon already fading toward dusk. Somewhere in the distance, a tram bell clanged, the sound carrying on the wind.

“Goodbye, Miss Penrose.”

“Goodbye, Vi.”

The girl stepped over the threshold and turned back, her hand still on the door. For a moment, she seemed about to say something more. Her lips parted, her brow furrowed, and her eyes met Ada’s with a directness that was almost painful. Then she released the door, turned, and walked down the path to the gate.

Ada watched her go. She watched until Vi reached the corner, until she turned and disappeared from view. Only then did she close the door, the latch clicking into place with a sound that seemed to echo through the empty house.

She stood in the hallway for a long moment. The front room was silent behind her, the piano still holding the shape of their absence. She could see the bench, the space where Vi had sat, the sheet music still open to the final page of the duet.

She walked back into the room and sat down at the piano. The keys were cool beneath her fingers, the ivory warming slowly to her touch. She played a single chord—the last chord of the duet—and let it fade into silence.

Her father had taught her that silence was part of the music. The rests mattered as much as the notes. The space between sounds was where the meaning lived. She had understood this intellectually for decades. But she had never felt it until now, sitting alone in a room that had been filled, however briefly, with something that approached harmony.

She looked over at the table by the window. The drawing lay where she had placed it, the afternoon light catching the penciled notes, the careful letters of her name. She rose and crossed to it, picking it up with hands that trembled slightly.

Vi Hollis had walked through the cold to give her this. A child with coal dust on her cuffs and a serious face too old for her years had folded a piece of drawing paper into yesterday’s newsprint and carried it through the streets because she wanted Ada to have something. Because she wanted to leave a mark. Because she understood, in the way that children sometimes understand things before adults do, that what they had shared in this room was rare and fragile and worth naming.

Ada carried the drawing to the small cabinet in the corner—the one where her father’s things were kept. She opened the drawer and placed the drawing inside, beside the metronome he had used, the one she had not wound in years. Then she closed the drawer and stood in the quiet room, listening to the silence that was no longer empty.

The trams were running again. The strike was over. The borrowed time had ended, and the world was resuming its ordinary shape. But something had changed. Some small part of the wall she had built around herself had cracked, and through that crack, a child had slipped a piece of paper with penciled notes that could not be played.

Ada walked to the window. The street was quiet, the afternoon light fading. She reached for the shutters, her fingers resting on the wooden slats. Tomorrow, she would open them. Tomorrow, she would let the light in.

But today, she stood in the gathering dusk and let the silence hold her.
