Chapter 4

Neighbourhood Calculus

The silence that followed was not the silence of peace but the silence of a held breath, a suspension that pressed against the walls of the terrace and waited for release. Frank Hollis sat in his cold parlour at number 12, the previous night’s arguments still echoing in the hollow spaces of his chest. The house was quiet around him—Vi upstairs in her room, the street beyond the window still and grey as washed slate. Nine days now. Nine days of the strike, and the quiet had become a presence itself, something that sat in the room with him and watched.

He rose from the chair, his knees stiff from sitting too long in one position. The fire had been out for two days—a choice, not an accident. Coal was precious, and he had been saving what remained for the worst of it, whenever that might come. The grate stood swept and empty, a black mouth that swallowed the room’s warmth and gave nothing back. Cold dust rose from the hearth rug when he crossed to the window, a small cloud of it catching the grey light. He had not swept properly in days. There had been meetings, rallies, the endless standing together that the union demanded. We hold the line. That’s all. The words had become a rhythm, a pulse beneath the strike’s long silence.

Through the glass, the street lay still. No trams. No carts. Just the terrace stretching away toward the river, its windows dark with the same cold that had settled into his own bones. The quiet was different now—it had weight, a pressing stillness that made him aware of his own breathing, the creak of the floorboards beneath his feet, the distant sound of a child crying somewhere down the road. He straightened. Pulled his waistcoat straight. There was duty in the waiting, he told himself. Duty and dignity.

The knock came before he could sink further into the chair—three measured raps, deliberate as a metronome. Frank crossed to the door and opened it to find Arthur Cullis on the step, his breath a small cloud in the morning air. Arthur wore his usual carefully brushed waistcoat, the watch chain catching what little light there was, and his moustache quivered slightly in the cold. He held his hat in both hands, turning it slowly, a gesture Frank recognised as the prelude to something Arthur considered delicate.

“Frank,” Arthur said, with a small nod. “I wonder if I might have a word.”

Frank stepped aside and let him in. The parlour seemed smaller with another body in it, the cold more present, the empty grate more accusing. Arthur’s gaze moved around the room with the practised assessment of a man who had spent decades noting details in railway offices and passing them on as intelligence. His eyes paused on the unlit fire, the swept hearth, the thin layer of dust on the mantelpiece. Frank saw him see it. There was no hiding anything in this street, not really. The houses were too close, the walls too thin, the lives within them too entangled.

“Sit down,” Frank said, though he remained standing himself.

Arthur perched on the edge of the offered chair, his posture suggesting he might need to leave at any moment. “One hears the negotiations are at a most delicate juncture.” His voice was low, as though the walls themselves might report back. “It is being said, mind you I don’t affirm it, that the dockers may waver.”

Frank’s jaw tightened. “The dockers stand. We all stand. That’s what solidarity means.”

“Solidarity.” Arthur repeated the word as though tasting it, finding it wanting. “A fine word. But words don’t warm houses, Frank. They don’t fill grates.” He gestured toward the empty hearth with a slight movement of his head. “The children in this street—yours among them—are feeling the cold this morning. That’s not hearsay. That’s not negotiation. That’s a fact any of us can touch.”

The words landed in the room like stones dropped into still water. Frank felt the ripples spreading, touching the edges of his resolve. He kept his voice level. “We hold the line. That’s all. There’s a principle at stake here, Arthur. The government wants to break us. If we waver now, if we let the cold and the hunger turn us—“

“The government,” Arthur interrupted, his tone sharp now, “is not in this parlour. The government does not sleep in the room above this one with her breath clouding in the air. The government is not eleven years old and asking why there’s no fire.”

Frank’s hands, which had been open at his sides, curled slowly into loose fists. “Vi doesn’t complain.”

“No. She doesn’t.” Arthur’s moustache quivered again, this time with something that might have been anger or might have been grief. “That’s rather the point, isn’t it? She’s learned not to. She’s learned that her father’s principles matter more than her comfort. A fine lesson for a child.” He rose from the chair, his movements stiff and precise. “I’m not here to argue politics with you, Frank. I’m here because this street is watching, and what we’re seeing is not politics. It’s something else entirely.”

“You’re telling me I should break the strike? Abandon the men I’ve stood with for years?” Frank’s voice rose despite himself, the words pushed out by a pressure in his chest he had been holding back for days. “For what? For a fire? For coal?”

Arthur Cullis turned back from the door. His face was pale, his eyes hard. He pointed a blunt finger at the empty, swept hearth. “You’re telling me the greater good, Frank. She’s telling me she’s cold.”

The words hung in the air between them. Frank followed Arthur’s gesture to the barren grate, the truth of it settling like a cold stone in his throat. The room seemed to contract around him, the walls pressing inward, the ceiling lowering. He thought of Vi upstairs, her small hands wrapped in the ends of her shawl, her breath visible in the mornings now. He thought of the meetings where he had stood and spoken of sacrifice, of the greater good, of holding the line until victory came. The words had felt solid then. They had felt like something he could build a life on.

Now, in the cold light from the window, with Arthur Cullis watching him with something that was not quite pity and not quite contempt, the words felt hollow. They felt like something that might shatter if he touched them.

“I know what I’m doing,” Frank said, but his voice had lost its edge. It came out thin, a reed of sound in the cold air.

Arthur held his gaze for a long moment. Then he placed his hat carefully on his head, adjusting the brim with precise fingers. “A regrettable business, all this. Most irregular.” He opened the door and stepped out into the street, leaving Frank alone in the cold parlour with the empty grate and the weight of what had been said.

The door stayed open. Frank could not seem to move to close it. The street beyond was still there, the terrace with its rows of windows, each one a small eye watching him. He felt exposed, as though Arthur had torn away a curtain he had not known was there. The cold from outside poured in through the gap, mixing with the cold already in the room, until he could not tell where one ended and the other began.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Vi, moving about her room, unaware of what had just passed below. He should go to her. He should explain. But the words he might use had abandoned him, leaving only the hollow echo of his own arguments, the empty shell of principles that felt suddenly insufficient.

Through the wall that separated number 12 from number 3, he became aware of a different kind of silence. Ada Penrose’s house, the front room where she taught piano. The lessons had stopped when the strike began—no pupils coming, no music drifting through the thin plaster. He had noticed its absence without consciously marking it, a gap in the terrace’s ordinary sounds. Now, in the stillness after Arthur’s departure, he found himself listening to that silence, wondering if she was listening too.

The thought was uncomfortable. He did not want witnesses. He did not want the street’s eyes on him, weighing his choices, judging his failures. The strike was supposed to be a public stand, a collective act of resistance. But the cost of it—the real cost—was private. It was upstairs in his daughter’s cold fingers, in the empty grate, in the coal he was saving for a crisis that might already have arrived.

He crossed to the window. The glass was cold against his fingertips, a thin barrier between his own cold parlour and the street beyond. He looked out, meaning to watch Arthur’s retreating figure, to mark his progress down the terrace. But his gaze caught instead on the window across the way—number 3, Ada Penrose’s front room.

She was there. Standing at her own window, her hands still, her face turned toward the street. She had been listening. He knew it with the certainty that came from living nine doors down from someone for years, from sharing a wall and a street and a silence that had grown between them. She had heard Arthur’s voice raised, his own voice raised in response. She had heard the argument that was not really about politics at all.

Their eyes met.

It was only a moment—a heartbeat, a breath. But in that instant, Frank felt the weight of being seen. Not judged, not condemned, simply witnessed. Ada’s face was pale in the grey light, her expression unreadable. She did not smile. She did not look away with the studied indifference of a neighbour pretending not to notice. She simply held his gaze, and in the holding, something passed between them. An acknowledgment. A recognition. The silent communication of two people who had each been carrying their own private cold, their own private silence.

Then she looked away. A quick, darting movement, as though the contact had been a breach. The curtain shifted, and she was gone from the window, leaving only the glass and the street and the grey morning light.

Frank let out a breath he had not known he was holding. His hands hung loose at his sides, the fists undone. The parlour was colder than before, or perhaps he was simply more aware of it now—aware of the empty grate, the thin walls, the daughter upstairs who did not complain because she had learned that her father’s principles mattered more than her comfort.

Arthur’s words echoed in the space where his certainty had been. She’s telling me she’s cold.

He thought of Ada’s face at the window. The way she had looked at him—not with pity, not with judgement, but with something that might have been recognition. As though she too knew what it was to sit in a cold room and pretend that the cold was a choice. As though she too had been holding a silence that was slowly becoming unbearable.

He should close the door. He should go upstairs and check on Vi. He should begin the day, whatever the day might bring. But he stayed at the window a moment longer, looking at the space where Ada had stood, at the wall that separated their houses, at the thin barrier that kept their silences distinct and whole.

The strike would end eventually. The negotiations Arthur had spoken of would resolve, one way or another. The trams would run again, the pupils would return to Ada’s front room, the street would resume its ordinary rhythms. But something had shifted in the quiet of this morning. Something had cracked open.

Frank turned from the window and closed the door against the cold. The latch clicked into place, a small sound in the silence. He stood in the center of his cold parlour and listened to the house around him—the creak of the stairs, the muffled sounds of Vi moving in her room, the distant bark of a dog somewhere down the terrace. The ordinary sounds of a morning in the strike’s long pause.

But beneath them, beneath the ordinary, there was something else now. A thread of connection, thin as the wall between the houses, fragile as a glance through glass. He did not know what to do with it. He did not know if it was a comfort or a wound.

He only knew that he was no longer entirely alone in the watching.
