Chapter 9

Hollis Raises His Hand

The single note still hung in the air of his memory, that fragile vibration in the stillness of Ada Penrose’s front room, when Frank Hollis stepped out his own door into the cold. He had left Vi at the table with a slice of bread and the last scrape of margarine, her small hands wrapped around a cup of watered tea. The house behind him held no warmth. The hearth in the front room was cold ash, and the coal scuttle sat empty by the grate, its iron mouth gaping at the ceiling. He had checked it that morning, tipping it forward with his foot as if the motion might conjure a black tumble from somewhere, but the interior was bare, swept clean by his own hand days ago.

The street was quiet. Not the quiet of a Sunday, when the stillness carried the promise of bells and roast meat, but the arrested hush of a city holding its breath. The trams should have been running by now, their brass fittings catching the morning light, the familiar grind of wheels on rails threading through the terraces. Instead, the tracks stood empty, twin silver lines stretching toward the depot and the city center beyond. Frank’s boots found the familiar path without thought, his legs carrying him past the identical doorways, the wiped windows, the small front gardens with their borders of struggling spring bulbs. Number 7 had its curtains drawn, though a thin line of light at the edge suggested Arthur Cullis was already up, already listening to the wireless, already cataloguing the morning’s news for later distribution.

The walk to the depot took him fifteen minutes at his usual pace, but today his feet dragged, as if the paving stones had grown heavier overnight. He passed the corner shop with its papered windows, the newsagent’s stand stripped of its daily stack. A woman he did not recognize hurried past with her head down, clutching a string bag empty but for a knot of newspaper. She did not look at him. No one looked at anyone. The strike had drawn a curtain across the usual pleasantries, the nods and touches of the hat-brim that made a neighborhood out of these identical brick boxes. Now each house was an island, each hearth a private worry, and Frank walked among them carrying his own cold scuttle in his mind’s eye.

The depot loomed ahead, its brick archway spilling smoke-hazed light into the grey morning. The building had once been a stable for the tram company’s horses, and the smell of hay and manure still haunted the corners, layered now with engine oil, tobacco smoke, and the damp wool of men’s coats. Frank pushed through the side door into the main hall, where the union meeting was already assembling. The benches were arranged in rough rows facing a wooden platform, and the air was thick with the murmur of voices and the blue haze of cigarettes. He found a seat near the back, close enough to hear, far enough to observe. Arthur Cullis was already there, perched on the edge of a bench near the front, his watch chain catching the gaslight, his moustache a precise line above his lip.

The hall filled slowly, men filtering in from the cold streets with their caps in their hands and their breath still pluming. Frank recognized faces from the routes, men he had nodded to across the depot yard, men whose names he knew without ever having spoken to them. The tram drivers and conductors sat together in loose clusters, their uniforms marking them out from the engineers and maintenance workers in their street clothes. The union badge glinted on dozens of lapels, the little enamel shield that said they belonged to something larger than their own cold hearths.

The chairman called the meeting to order with a rap of his gavel, and the murmur subsided into silence. The agenda was simple: a vote on whether to hold the line. The strike had been called four days ago, and the government’s response had been swift and brutal. The TUC’s initial optimism had already begun to fray at the edges, and the men in this room knew they were in for a longer fight than anyone had predicted. The chairman spoke of solidarity, of the need to stand together against the employers’ intransigence, of the historic moment they were living through. His words were familiar, the same phrases Frank had heard a hundred times, but today they seemed to drift past him, settling somewhere above his head like the smoke that curled from the cigarettes.

A man near the front stood to speak, a conductor Frank had seen on the Queen’s Road route. His voice was hoarse, and he gestured toward the door as if the streets outside could hear him. “We’ve got to hold,” he said. “We’ve got to show them we mean it. If we fold now, we’ll never get another chance.”

Another voice rose from the middle, a mechanic with grease still blackening his knuckles. “My missus says the butcher’s already stopped giving tick. How long before the baker follows?”

The chairman raised his hand. “We all know the cost. The question is whether we’re willing to pay it.”

The words settled over the room like ash. Frank felt the cold of the hall seeping through his coat, the damp wool heavy on his shoulders. He thought of the empty scuttle by the hearth, the way Vi had looked at him that morning over her slice of bread. She had not complained. She never complained. But her eyes had tracked his hand as he spread the last of the margarine, and he had seen the question there, the one she was too young to know how to ask.

The meeting droned on, voices rising and falling, arguments made and countered. Frank sat in silence, his hands folded in his lap, his thumbs pressing against each other. He could feel the weight of the vote ahead, the simple gesture that would commit him to one path or another. The principle was clear. The union had called the strike, and the union expected its members to stand together. He had worn the badge for fifteen years, had paid his dues, had walked the picket line in the rain. The collective will was supposed to be his will, the collective struggle his struggle.

Then Arthur Cullis rose from his bench near the front.

The old man’s movements were slow, deliberate. He adjusted his waistcoat and stepped into the aisle, turning to face the room rather than the platform. The gaslight caught the grey in his moustache, the pallor of his skin. He looked like a man who had not slept, his eyes rimmed with red, his hands trembling slightly at his sides.

“I’ve been with the railway thirty-two years,” Arthur began, his voice thin but steady. “I remember the strike of ‘eleven. I remember the lockouts and the blacklists.” He paused, his gaze traveling across the rows of faces. “I’ve always stood with the union. Always.”

A murmur of acknowledgment rippled through the hall. Arthur Cullis was known, his history a matter of record. He had been a delegate in the old days, had spoken at rallies, had organized the collection boxes when the men needed support.

“But last night,” Arthur continued, and his voice dropped, “my youngest came downstairs. Not crying. Just came down and stood in the doorway of the kitchen. Said he couldn’t sleep because his belly was hurting.” The old man’s hands found each other, fingers twisting together. “His mother gave him the last of the bread, the heel with the mould cut off. She didn’t eat. I didn’t eat. We watched him chew it slow, like it was something precious.”

The hall had gone silent. Even the chairman had lowered his gavel, the wooden head resting against the podium.

“I’m not saying we should fold,” Arthur said, and his voice cracked on the word. “I’m not saying the cause isn’t right. I’m just telling you what it costs. So when you raise your hand, you know what you’re raising it for.”

He sat back down, slowly, as if the words had taken something out of him. The man beside him reached over and gripped his shoulder, a brief pressure that lasted no more than a second. Arthur stared at the floor, his moustache quivering.

Frank felt the story settle into him like a stone dropped into water. The image rose unbidden: Vi at the table, her small fingers wrapped around the cup, the bread already gone. The hearth cold. The scuttle empty. He had been telling himself that the sacrifice was temporary, that the victory would make it worthwhile. But Arthur’s voice had cracked something open, and now the cold air rushed in.

The chairman called for the vote. “All those in favor of continuing the strike, raise your hands.”

The hall filled with movement, sleeves rustling, arms lifting. Frank watched the hands rise around him, the calloused palms and ink-stained fingers, the wedding rings and bare knuckles. Each hand was a vote, a commitment, a promise to stand together against the employers, the government, the forces that would grind them down if given half a chance.

He thought of his father, a carter who had worked until his back gave out, then worked some more. He thought of Meg, the way she had looked at him the night she left, the quiet in her voice when she said she couldn’t live with a man who put the union before his own family. He thought of Vi, asleep in the cold room upstairs, her breath a small white cloud in the dark.

His hand rose.

It rose slowly, heavily, as if the air itself resisted. His palm turned upward, empty, a gesture that was both offering and surrender. The calloused skin caught the gaslight, the oil stains permanent beneath the surface. He kept his eyes open, fixed on the platform, but he could feel Arthur beside him, the old man’s ragged breath, the weight of his story.

“All those opposed.”

A scattering of hands, fewer than Frank had expected. The chairman counted, his eyes moving across the rows, tallying. The result was never in doubt. The vote carried, and the strike would continue.

The meeting began to disperse, men rising from the benches, voices resuming their murmur. Frank sat for a moment, his hand still resting on his knee, the sensation of raising it still present in his shoulder. He felt as if he had lifted something far heavier than his own arm, something that would not be put down again.

Arthur Cullis passed by on his way to the door. He paused, his hand resting briefly on the back of the bench. His eyes met Frank’s, and for a moment, neither man spoke.

“It’s the right thing,” Arthur said, but his voice carried no conviction, only weariness.

Frank nodded. He could not find words.

Arthur moved on, his footsteps slow, his shoulders bowed. The hall emptied around them, the smoke beginning to clear, the gaslights hissing softly in their brackets.

Outside, the cold hit Frank like a hand pressed flat against his chest. The sky had cleared, and the afternoon light was thin and pale, casting long shadows from the eaves. He began the walk home, his boots finding the familiar path without thought. The streets were still quiet, the strike’s paralysis complete. A dog barked somewhere in the distance, and the sound echoed off the brick facades, sharp and lonely.

He passed the corner shop again, the newsagent’s stand still empty. A group of men stood at the end of the next street, their caps pulled low, their voices low. They glanced at him as he passed, and one of them raised a hand in acknowledgment. Frank raised his own in return, the gesture automatic, hollow.

The terrace came into view, the identical doorways stretching in a long line. Number 3, Ada Penrose’s house, stood dark, its windows reflecting the grey sky. The shutters were closed, the front step swept clean. He slowed as he passed, his gaze drawn to the dark glass. For a moment, he thought he saw movement behind the window, a shift in the shadows, but it was only his own reflection, a grey shape moving through a grey world.

Her house was like his, he realized. Cold. Quiet. Holding its breath.

He thought of the piano note, the way it had hung in the air, the fragile vibration that connected one moment to the next. Ada Penrose had her own cold hearth, her own empty rooms. Her mother’s chair still sat by the fire, or so he had heard, preserved like a monument to a woman who was no longer there. She had kept her distance from the neighborhood’s rhythms, her pupils a steady stream of children from other streets, other lives. But now even they had stopped coming, and she was alone in that front room with the silent piano.

He walked on, past number 4, number 5, the familiar doorways marking his progress. Arthur’s story stayed with him, the boy in the doorway, the mould on the bread. The image tangled with Vi’s face at the table, her small hands wrapped around the cup. The two pictures blurred together, father and son, daughter and father, the chain of hunger and sacrifice that bound them all.

Number 12 came into view, his own door, the paint flaking at the edges where he had promised to touch it up and never had. The window was dark, the curtain drawn. Vi would be upstairs now, or sitting in the kitchen, waiting for him to come home with news.

He stopped at the door, his hand on the latch. The cold had seeped through his coat, his jacket, the shirt beneath. His fingers were numb, the skin tight across his knuckles. He could feel the weight of the vote still pressing against his ribs, the hand he had raised now a permanent part of him, a gesture that could not be taken back.

The union badge on his lapel caught the fading light, the enamel shield bright against the dark wool. He had worn it for fifteen years, had polished it each evening, had straightened it each morning. It was a small thing, a piece of metal and glass, but it carried the weight of every hand he had shaken, every meeting he had attended, every picket line he had walked.

Now it felt like a stone.

He turned the latch and stepped inside. The hall was dark, the air cold and still. From upstairs came the sound of Vi’s footsteps, light and quick, and then her voice, calling down.

“Da? Is that you?”

“Aye,” he said. “It’s me.”

He hung his coat on the hook, his fingers slow and clumsy. The union badge caught on the fabric, and he paused to free it, his thumb brushing the enamel surface. The shield was smooth, cool to the touch.

“Did they vote?” Vi’s voice came from the top of the stairs, her small shape a shadow against the landing’s dim light.

“They did.”

“And?”

He looked up at her, his daughter, eleven years old and already too good at reading silence. Her face was a pale oval in the gloom, her eyes dark and watchful.

“We’re holding the line,” he said. “The strike goes on.”

She nodded, slowly, as if she had expected nothing else. Then she turned and went back up the stairs, her footsteps light on the boards.

Frank stood in the hall, his hand still on the coat, the cold seeping through the fabric. The weight in his chest had not lifted. If anything, it had settled deeper, pressing against his ribs, filling the space where breath should be.

He thought of Arthur’s boy, standing in the doorway with his hollow belly. He thought of Ada Penrose’s dark window, her silent piano. He thought of the empty scuttle by the hearth, the cold ash, the long days ahead.

The vote had been cast. The hand had been raised. And now he would carry it home, this weight that was heavier than any bag of coal, this choice that could not be unmade.
