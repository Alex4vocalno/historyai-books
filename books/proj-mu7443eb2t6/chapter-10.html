Chapter 10

Scales and Silence

The metronome’s brass pendulum swings, dividing the silent front room into perfect, empty intervals. Ada watches it count through its mechanical task, tick, tock, tick, the sound smaller in the room than she remembers from her father’s time, as if the house itself has grown larger around the noise, absorbing it into the dusty corners and the faded wallpaper and the air that smells always of lemon polish and something older, something like sealed envelopes never opened. She has fifteen minutes yet before Vi is due, fifteen minutes to arrange the music on the stand and ensure the pencil is sharpened and the bench is positioned at the correct height, though the girl had seemed comfortable enough last week, perched on the edge with her knees too close together and her wrists angled in a way that would need correcting.

Through the window, the street carries the particular stillness that has settled over the neighborhood these past days. No trams. The strike has entered its second week now, and the silence of the rails has become a kind of weather, a pressure that sits on the rooftops and presses down into the rooms where people wait. Ada turns from the window and straightens the sheet music on the stand. C-major scales. She believes in beginning at the beginning, in the orderly progression from simplicity to complexity, each step built upon the one before it.

The knock comes at precisely three o’clock. Ada counts the beats between the first rap and the second, two sharp knocks, and finds herself satisfied that the girl has at least learned to keep time with her knuckles.

She opens the door to find Vi standing on the step, her coat buttoned to the chin and a wool cap pulled low over her ears. The girl’s cheeks are flushed from the cold, and her eyes move past Ada’s shoulder into the warmth of the hallway with an expression that Ada recognizes from her own childhood, the hunger to step from winter into a room that holds heat.

“Miss Penrose,” Vi says. She does not smile. She has the serious face of a child who has learned that adults prefer seriousness in children, and she wears it like a garment that almost fits.

“Vi.” Ada steps aside. “You’re on time. That’s good. Punctuality is the first lesson.”

Vi crosses the threshold and stands in the hallway, her hands clasped in front of her. She looks at the closed door of the front room as if it might hold something unexpected behind it, some new configuration of furniture or light.

“You may hang your coat on the hook,” Ada says. “The one by the mirror. Your cap as well. We do not wear hats at the piano.”

The girl removes her coat with careful movements, as though the garment might tear if she pulls too hard. She hangs it on the hook and then turns, her hands now free and uncertain, hovering at her sides before she clasps them again.

“Come,” Ada says, and she leads the way into the front room.

The piano occupies the far wall, its black surface catching the grey light from the window. Ada has opened the curtains today, a small concession to the afternoon, and the pale sun falls across the keys in a way that makes the ivory look warm, almost alive. Vi approaches the instrument with the same caution she showed in the hallway, and when she sits on the bench, she does so slowly, her weight settling as if she expects the seat to give way beneath her.

“Posture,” Ada says. She moves to stand beside the piano, her hands folded behind her back. “Feet flat on the floor. Knees together. Back straight but not rigid. You are not a soldier, Vi. You are a musician.”

Vi adjusts herself. Her feet, in worn leather shoes, find purchase on the floorboards. Her spine lengthens. She looks up at Ada with an expression that suggests she is trying very hard to do everything at once.

“Wrists,” Ada continues. “Your wrists are the bridge between your arms and your hands. They must be flexible. Like this.” She demonstrates, holding her own wrist between thumb and forefinger and rotating it gently. “Not stiff. Never stiff. A stiff wrist makes a stiff tone.”

Vi mimics the movement. Her small wrist turns in Ada’s grip, and Ada feels the delicate architecture of bone beneath the skin, the pulse beating steadily just below the surface. She releases the girl’s hand and steps back.

“Today we begin with scales,” Ada says. “C-major. The simplest key, because it uses only the white notes. No sharps, no flats. Pure and natural. You will begin with your right hand alone. Thumb on middle C, then the second finger on D, third finger on E. When you reach F, you pass your thumb under and continue. Do you understand?”

Vi nods. She places her thumb on middle C, and Ada watches her find the keys one by one, her brow furrowing with concentration. The notes sound one after another, C, D, E, F, G, and then the thumb-under, which Vi executes with a small hitch in the rhythm, a pause where the music ought to flow.

“Again,” Ada says. She winds the metronome and sets it on the piano’s lid, the brass pendulum beginning its swing. “One must keep to the schedule, you see. It’s what provides the structure. Without structure, music is merely noise.”

The metronome ticks its patient count, and Vi tries again. This time she manages the thumb passage without stopping, though the rhythm wavers slightly as her hand finds its new position. The scale climbs to the next C and then descends, the fingers passing over the thumb on the way down, and Ada watches the girl’s hand move with a mixture of awkwardness and determination that she has seen in every beginner, the same struggle between the will to make music and the fingers’ refusal to cooperate.

“Better,” Ada says. “Again.”

They work through the scale a dozen times, the metronome marking the time, and gradually Vi’s hand begins to find the shape of the movement. The notes come more evenly, the rhythm steadier, and Ada finds herself almost relaxing into the repetition, the familiar ritual of instruction that she has performed for more years than she cares to count.

Then Vi stops. Her hand rests on the keys, her fingers splayed across the ivory, and she rubs her wrist with her other hand, a small circular motion that suggests soreness or fatigue.

“Does it hurt?” Ada asks.

“A little,” Vi says. “My wrist gets tired.”

“That will pass. The muscles are not yet accustomed to the work. In time, they will strengthen.”

Vi rubs her wrist a moment longer, and then she looks up at Ada. The afternoon light has shifted, and now the sunbeam falls across the piano’s edge, illuminating the dust that hangs in the air between them, particles suspended in the stillness.

“Miss Penrose,” Vi says.

“Yes?”

“Did your mother play?”

The question lands in the room like a stone dropped into still water. Ada feels her hand, which had been reaching toward the metronome to adjust the tempo, freeze in midair. The dust motes continue their slow drift, indifferent to the sudden tension that has entered the space.

“I beg your pardon?”

“Your mother,” Vi says, and her voice is curious rather than prying, the simple curiosity of a child who has noticed an absence. “Did she play the piano? You play so well. I thought maybe she taught you too.”

Ada’s hand falls to her side. She looks at the girl, at the open face turned up toward hers, and for a moment she sees not a pupil but a child standing in a room full of questions, all of them about the people who are missing.

“We are here to study scales,” Ada says. Her voice comes out harder than she intends, and she sees Vi’s shoulders draw in, the girl’s body folding slightly as if bracing against a blow. “Your wrist is tired because you are holding tension in your forearm. That is what we must correct. That is what matters.”

Vi is silent. Her hands drop to her lap, and she looks down at them, her fingers laced together.

“The metronome isn’t about hurry,” Ada says, more gently now. “It’s about agreement. Between the notes and the time. That is all.”

She reaches out and taps the music stand sharply with her fingernail, a crisp sound that makes Vi flinch. “Now. Again. And this time, keep your wrist loose.”

Vi looks up. Her eyes are bright, and Ada wonders if she has been too sharp, if the correction has carried more force than the error warranted. But the girl places her hands on the keys once more, and the scale begins again, climbing and descending in the metronome’s measured time.

They work for another twenty minutes. Ada corrects the angle of Vi’s thumb, the position of her elbow, the curve of her spine. She says nothing more about mothers or fathers or the absence of either, and Vi asks nothing more, though the question seems to hang in the air between them, an unspoken presence that neither acknowledges.

When the lesson ends, Ada accepts the small envelope that Vi produces from inside her coat. One shilling, the agreed price, though Ada knows that the sum represents something more than Vi or her father can easily afford. She does not count the money in front of the girl. She simply takes the envelope and places it in the drawer with the others, a collection of small payments that will become her living when the strike ends and the pupils return, if they return.

“Same time next week,” Ada says.

Vi nods. She stands at the door with her coat buttoned and her cap in her hands, and she looks at Ada with an expression that suggests she wants to say something more, something that the lesson’s formal structure has not allowed.

“Miss Penrose,” she says.

“Yes?”

“Did your father teach you?”

The question is different from the first, and Ada understands that Vi has been holding it, waiting for the right moment to ask. She thinks of her father, of the room upstairs that she has not entered since his death, of the piano that she plays only when she teaches and never for herself.

“He did,” Ada says. The words come out brittle and factual, stripped of feeling. “He was my only teacher.”

Vi considers this. Her face shows the particular concentration of a child trying to understand something that adults have left unsaid.

“Was he…” She pauses, choosing her words. “Was he a good teacher?”

Ada thinks of her father’s hands on hers, guiding her fingers to the keys. She thinks of the metronome ticking in the corner, the brass pendulum swinging through the long afternoons of her childhood. She thinks of the way he would stop the lesson sometimes and simply listen, his head tilted toward the window, as if the music continued somewhere beyond the notes she played.

“He was,” Ada says. “He was very patient.”

Vi nods again, satisfied perhaps, or simply accepting that this is all Ada will give. She pulls on her cap, tucking her hair beneath the wool, and steps out into the grey afternoon.

“Same time next week,” she says, and she walks down the path toward the gate, her small figure receding into the stillness of the street.

Ada watches her go. The girl passes the darkened windows of Number Twelve, the house where Frank Hollis lives with his daughter and his grief, and Ada wonders what Vi will say when she gets home, whether she will mention the question she asked or the answer she received.

She closes the door and returns to the front room. The metronome still ticks on the piano’s lid, though she has not wound it in some time, and the pendulum swings with decreasing force, each arc shorter than the last, marking time that no one is using.

Ada sits on the bench where Vi sat. The wood holds a faint impression of warmth, the ghost of the girl’s presence. She looks at the keys, at the ivory and ebony that her father taught her to read like letters, like words, like sentences in a language that he had learned from his own father before her.

The metronome runs down. The pendulum slows, stops, and the room falls into a silence that seems to press against the walls, the ceiling, the closed curtains. Ada sits with her hands in her lap, and she does not play.

The question about her mother had come without warning, slipped between the scales like a wrong note that could not be unplayed. Vi’s wrists had been sore, her fingers trembling slightly from the repetition, and she had asked it so simply—as though asking about a piece Ada had memorised, as though the answer were just another lesson to absorb.

Ada had corrected her fingering instead of answering. The correction had been sharp, unnecessary, and Vi had flinched but not pulled away. She had looked at Ada with that steady gaze that seemed to see through the sterile order of the room, through the polished surfaces and the precise angles, to something rawer underneath.

“My father taught me,” Ada had said finally, when the silence became unbearable. “He was my only teacher.”

She had not mentioned her mother. She had not needed to. The absence sat between them, a shared recognition that required no elaboration. Vi had accepted the half-answer with a small nod, and for a moment, the transactional distance of the lesson had dissolved into something closer to understanding.

Now Ada stared at the closed door. Her rule—the one she had kept for years, the careful boundary that made these lessons possible—felt less like a wall and more like a fence with gaps wide enough to see through. Vi had slipped through one of them without trying.

Ada lifted her hand to the cold piano lid. The instrument had belonged to her father, and before him, to his father. She had inherited it the way she had inherited the silence that followed the metronome’s final tick—a silence that pressed against the walls, waiting for someone to fill it.

She thought of Vi’s question again. She thought of the answer she had not given.

The lesson had not been about scales.
