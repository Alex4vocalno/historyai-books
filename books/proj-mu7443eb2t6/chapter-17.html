Chapter 17

Strike Called Off

The rain soaked a man through to his bones before he noticed he was wet, and Frank Hollis had been standing in it for the better part of an hour. The depot gates loomed ahead, iron bars slick with moisture. Beyond them the silent trams sat like beached leviathans, windows dark, wheels cold on the rails. Nine days now. Nine days of this unnatural stillness settled over the neighbourhood. The men around him shuffled their feet and pulled their collars up against the damp, but no one spoke. There was nothing left to say that had not been said a hundred times already.

Frank adjusted his cap. Water ran down the back of his neck and into his collar. The picket line had thinned since yesterday—he could see it in the gaps between shoulders, in the way the men clustered closer to the shelter of the depot wall as though seeking warmth from brick and mortar. A week ago there had been songs, jokes, the rough camaraderie of shared purpose. Now there was only this grim endurance, this waiting for news that none of them dared name.

Arthur Cullis came through the gate at half past ten. Frank saw him before the others did—recognised the particular set of his shoulders, the way he held the folded paper in his hand as though it were something precious or dangerous. Arthur had been the one to call them together on the first day, the one who had stood on an overturned crate and told them that solidarity was the only weapon they had. Now he walked toward them through the rain, and there was no crate, no uplifted voice, no fire in his bearing.

“Brothers.” Arthur’s voice carried in the wet air, but it had lost its resonance. “The General Council has sent word.”

The men shifted. Someone near the front spat into the gutter. Frank stayed where he was, his hands deep in his pockets, his eyes fixed on the paper in Arthur’s hand.

“I won’t dress this up,” Arthur continued. He unfolded the bulletin with fingers that trembled slightly—whether from cold or something else Frank could not tell. “The Trades Union Congress has called off the General Strike. As of noon today, all workers are to return to their posts.”

The words fell into the silence like stones into deep water. No one shouted. No one swore. The men simply stood there, rain running down their faces, and Frank felt something inside him go slack. He had expected anger, perhaps. He had expected the roar of betrayed men, the surge of collective fury that had carried them through the first days. What he had not expected was this emptiness, this hollow space where his certainty used to be.

Arthur read the rest of the bulletin in a flat voice. The negotiations had reached an impasse. The government had refused to budge on the core demands. The miners would continue their struggle alone, but the rest of them—the railwaymen, the transport workers, the dockers, the tram conductors—were to go back. The words blurred together, official language that meant nothing and everything: “regrettable necessity,” “best interests of the movement,” “fight another day.”

When Arthur finished, he let the paper drop to his side. Rain had begun to soak through it, the ink bleeding into grey smudges that made the typewritten letters run like tears. The men looked at him, and he looked back, and in that look Frank saw the same question that must have been in his own eyes: What was it for?

Arthur Cullis had been the one who spoke of the children. Frank remembered it clearly—the night they had voted to continue, Arthur had pulled him aside outside the meeting hall and spoken of his own family, of the empty plates on the table, of the youngest one crying herself to sleep. “We hold the line,” he had said, “because if we don’t, what do we tell them? That we gave up?” Now Arthur stood before them with the bulletin in his hand, and his face was the face of a man who had nothing left to tell anyone.

The men began to drift away. There was no formal dismissal, no handshake or parting word. They simply turned and walked, some alone, some in pairs, their shoulders hunched against the rain and against something worse than rain. Frank watched them go. He recognised some of the faces—men he had worked beside for years, men whose children played in the same streets as Vi, men who had stood with him on that first day when everything had seemed possible. Now they were just wet figures receding into the grey morning.

Arthur caught his eye as he passed. There was a moment when Frank thought he might speak, might offer some explanation or apology. But Arthur only nodded, a small jerk of his head that could have meant anything or nothing, and then he was gone too, walking toward the tram sheds where the workers would gather tomorrow to resume their old lives as though nothing had happened.

Frank stayed. He did not know why, exactly. There was nothing left to stay for. The picket line had dissolved, the strike was over, and he was standing alone at the gates of a depot that would soon hum with the machinery of normal life. But his feet seemed rooted to the wet asphalt, and he found himself looking through the bars at the silent trams, trying to remember what it had felt like to believe.

Nine days. Nine days of cold mornings and colder evenings, of counting pennies and measuring out coal, of watching Vi’s face grow thinner and more watchful. He had told himself it was necessary. He had told himself that the hardship was an investment, that the sacrifice would yield something worth having. He had stood in this very spot and felt the solidarity of the men around him like a wall against the world.

Now there was only the rain, and the wet paper lying in the gutter where someone had dropped it, and the knowledge that tomorrow he would put on his uniform and climb onto his tram and say the same words he had always said—“Fares, please,” “Move along now,”—and nothing would have changed except that he would know, deep in the hollow place where his certainty used to be, that it had all been for nothing.

He turned and began the long walk home.

The streets were quiet. The strike had silenced more than the trams; it had stilled the whole rhythm of the neighbourhood, and even now, with the news spreading house by house, there was no surge of activity, no return to the bustle of normal life. People moved behind their windows, shadows in lit rooms, but no one came out. Frank walked alone through the grey drizzle, his boots splashing in the puddles that had formed in the uneven pavement.

He passed the corner shop where Mrs. Davies had been extending credit to the strikers’ families. The window was dark, the CLOSED sign hanging crooked behind the glass. He passed the pub where the union men had gathered each evening, where arguments had flared and songs had been sung and plans had been made. The doors were shut, the windows barred. It looked like any other building now, brick and mortar with nothing inside.

His route took him past Number Three, where Ada Penrose lived. He did not mean to look, but his eyes were drawn to her window as though by habit. The house sat in the row like all the others, its façade unchanged, its curtains drawn. He thought of Vi walking to that door with the empty coal scuttle, of the debt that had passed between the houses without a word spoken. He thought of the woman behind those curtains, the piano teacher who had somehow become tangled in their lives, and he felt a pang of something that might have been gratitude or might have been shame.

The window was dark. No light, no movement. Ada Penrose was in there, he knew, going about her business, teaching her scales, maintaining the order she had inherited from a father Frank had never met. The thought of her filled him with a sudden, irrational resentment. She had nothing to do with any of this. She was not part of the struggle, had never stood on a picket line or counted out coins or worried about the price of coal. And yet she was there, in the back of his mind, a presence he could not shake.

He walked on. The rain had eased slightly, but the damp had settled into his clothes, and he could feel the chill of it in his joints. His feet knew the way without thought—left at the corner, past the small park where the children played, right onto the street where his own door waited. He had walked this route a thousand times, coming home from shifts, from meetings, from the depot where he had spent his working life. But today the familiar landmarks seemed strange, as though he were seeing them for the first time.

The house at Number Twelve—his Number Twelve—came into view. It sat in the middle of the terrace, its windows dark, its door shut. Vi would be inside, waiting. She had been waiting all morning, just as she had waited every day of the strike, her face pale and watchful, her questions unasked. He had told her that they were doing the right thing, that the sacrifice was necessary, that the outcome would justify the hardship. He had said it with conviction, because at the time he had believed it.

Now he would have to walk through that door and face her with nothing.

He stopped at the gate. The small front garden was overgrown, the grass too long, the rosebush by the wall ragged and untended. He had meant to cut it back, had meant to tend to all the small tasks that accumulated around a house, but there had been no time, no energy, no heart for it. The strike had consumed everything. And now the strike was over, and the tasks remained, and he was standing at his own gate like a stranger.

The door opened before he reached it.

Vi stood in the doorway, her slight figure silhouetted against the dim interior. She was wearing the cardigan he had last seen on her three days ago, the wool pilling at the elbows, the color faded from too many washings. Her face was the face he had seen every morning of his life since her mother had died—watchful, patient, waiting for something she never quite named.

“Da,” she said. Not a question, not a greeting. Just his name, falling into the space between them.

He climbed the steps and stopped in front of her. He could see the question in her eyes, the one she was too kind or too wise to ask. Did we win? Was it worth it? What do we do now? He had no answers for her. He had nothing but the hollow space where his certainty used to be.

“The strike is over,” he said. His voice sounded strange in his own ears, flat and tired. “We’re to go back tomorrow.”

Vi studied his face. She was young—ten, though the hollows beneath her eyes made her look older—and thin from the weeks of rationing, but her gaze was steady. She had learned to read faces the way other children learned to read books, parsing expressions for meaning, for danger, for the small shifts that signalled trouble. She read his face now, and he watched her understand.

She stepped aside to let him in.

The hallway was cold. The house held the chill of a spring that had not yet warmed, and the lack of a fire in the grate made it worse. He could smell the faint staleness of a room that had been closed too long, the absence of baking or cooking or any of the small domestic warmth’s that made a house feel lived in. Vi had been alone here all morning, he realized. Alone with her worry and her waiting, just as she had been alone every day of the strike.

He walked past her into the kitchen. The table was bare except for the oilcloth covering, its pattern of faded roses barely visible in the grey light from the window. The stove was cold, the kettle sitting on its side, the coal scuttle empty in the corner. He pulled out a chair and sat down, and the weight of everything—the rain, the bulletin, the long walk home—seemed to settle on him all at once.

Vi followed him into the kitchen. She moved quietly, her footsteps soft on the linoleum, and he heard her go to the stove, heard the rattle of the kettle as she righted it, heard the sound of water being pumped from the tap. She was making tea. It was such a small thing, such an ordinary gesture, and yet it felt enormous. She was offering him comfort when she was the one who had suffered, the one who had gone hungry and cold, the one who had waited and worried while he stood on picket lines and voted to continue.

“Shall I put the kettle on?” she asked. The question hung in the air between them, and he knew she was not really asking about tea.

“Aye,” he said. “Go on, then.”

She lit the stove. The match flared and caught, and he watched her coax the flame, her fingers deft and careful. The gas hissed into life, a small blue circle that seemed impossibly bright in the dim kitchen. She set the kettle on the ring and turned back to face him, and for a moment they simply looked at each other.

He wanted to explain. He wanted to tell her that he had believed in the cause, that he had thought the sacrifice would yield something, that he had not meant for it to end like this. But the words would not come. They stuck in his throat, lodged there like stones, and he found that he could not meet her eyes.

The kettle began to whistle, a thin sound that rose into a full-throated shriek. Vi turned to take it off the heat, and the silence that followed was heavier than any sound. She made the tea in silence, two cups, no sugar because there was no sugar, and she brought his cup to the table and set it in front of him.

“Drink,” she said. “It’ll warm you.”

He picked up the cup. The heat of it seeped through the ceramic into his palms, and he realized how cold he was, how the damp had settled into his bones during the long morning. He drank, and the tea was bitter and too weak, but it was warm, and he felt something in him loosen slightly.

Vi sat down across from him. She did not drink her own tea; she simply sat, her hands folded on the table, her eyes on his face. She was waiting. She had been waiting for nine days, and now she was waiting still, and he did not know what she was waiting for him to say.

“We held the line,” he said finally. The words felt foolish as soon as they left his mouth. What did it matter, holding the line, when the line had crumbled at the first touch of pressure from above?

Vi did not respond. She simply looked at him, her expression unreadable, and he felt the weight of her silence more keenly than any accusation. If she had shouted, if she had cried, if she had demanded answers, he could have borne it. He knew how to defend himself against anger, how to justify and explain. But this quiet acceptance, this patient waiting—it undid him completely.

“I thought—“ He stopped. What had he thought? That the government would yield? That the solidarity of the workers would be enough? That the sacrifice would mean something in the end? He had thought all of these things, and he had been wrong.

“It’s all right, Da.” Vi’s voice was soft, almost gentle. “You don’t have to explain.”

But he did have to explain. He owed her that much, at least. She had given up her warmth, her meals, her certainty, and she had done it without complaint because she trusted him. She had believed in him, and he had led her into a struggle that had yielded nothing.

“It wasn’t supposed to end like this,” he said. “We were supposed to win something. Better wages, better conditions. Something to show for it.” He looked down at the tea in his cup, at the pale reflection of his own face in the dark liquid. “I don’t know what to tell you, Vi. I don’t know what to say.”

She reached across the table and touched his hand. Her fingers were thin and cold, but her touch was steady. “You came home,” she said simply.

He looked at her then, really looked, and he saw the girl she had been before the strike—the one who had walked to Ada Penrose’s door with an empty coal scuttle, who had sat at a piano and learned scales from a woman who had shown her unexpected kindness. The strike had changed her too. It had worn her down, thinned her out, but it had also made her older, wiser, more capable of bearing the weight of disappointment.

“I’m sorry,” he said. The words were inadequate, a small offering against the enormity of what he owed her. “I’m sorry it wasn’t enough.”

Vi withdrew her hand and picked up her cup. She drank, her grip careful around the warmth, and he watched her throat move as she swallowed, watched her set the cup back down with careful precision. When she spoke again, her voice was calm.

“We’ll manage,” she said. “We always have.”

He wanted to believe her. He wanted to take the comfort she was offering and let it fill the hollow space inside him. But he could not forget the faces of the men as they had walked away from the depot, the way Arthur Cullis had looked at him, the bulletin lying in the gutter with its words running into nothing. He could not forget the nine days of cold and hunger and hope, and the bitter knowledge that it had all been for nothing.

The kitchen was silent except for the drip of the tap, a steady rhythm that seemed to mark the passing of time. Frank sat at the table, his tea growing cold in his hands, and Vi sat across from him, and neither of them spoke. The silence between them was not the silence of peace, but it was not the silence of estrangement either. It was something else, something new—a shared understanding that did not need words, a quiet acknowledgment of all that had been lost and all that remained.

He thought of Meg, then. She had died years ago, had closed her eyes in this very house and never opened them again, and he had never understood how to go on without her. “It’s the quiet, Frank,” she had said toward the end, her voice thin and tired. “It’s the never knowing.” He had dismissed it at the time, had told himself that she was speaking of the pain, the uncertainty of illness. But now he wondered if he had been wrong. Now he wondered if the quiet she had meant was not the absence of noise, but the absence of hope—the slow, steady erosion of belief that came from living in a world that offered nothing but struggle.

He looked at Vi, and he saw the same quiet in her, the same patient endurance born of loss too early carried. But there was something else there too, something he had not expected. She was not defeated. She was not broken. She was simply waiting, as she had always waited, for whatever would come next.

“We’ll need coal,” he said. The practical words felt strange in his mouth, a small, ordinary concern against the weight of the morning. “And food. I’ll need to go to the shops before they close.”

Vi nodded. “I can go,” she said. “If you’re tired.”

“No.” He shook his head. “I’ll go. It’s my—“ He stopped, unsure what he had been about to say. My responsibility? My fault? My burden? All of those things, and none of them. “I’ll go,” he finished.

He stood up from the table, and his joints ached with the cold, and his clothes were still damp, and he felt every one of his years. But there was nothing else for it. The strike was over, and life went on, and tomorrow he would put on his uniform and climb onto his tram and resume the life he had left behind nine days ago.

Vi stood too. She collected the cups and took them to the sink, and he watched her rinse them with the same careful precision she brought to everything. She was so like her mother in that way—quiet, methodical, capable of bearing burdens that should have been too heavy for her small frame. But she was like him too. She had his stubbornness, his refusal to give up, his capacity for enduring what could not be changed.

“Da?” She turned from the sink, and her voice was hesitant, as though she were about to ask something she had been holding back.

“Aye?”

She looked at him for a long moment, and he saw the question forming on her lips. But then she shook her head slightly, and the question faded, and she offered him a small, tired smile.

“Nothing,” she said. “It doesn’t matter.”

He knew what she had wanted to ask. The same question he had been asking himself all morning: What was it all for? But she had chosen not to ask it, and he was grateful for that small mercy. Some questions had no answers, and some silences were better left unbroken.

He went to the door and put on his coat, still damp from the morning, and his cap, and he stepped out into the grey afternoon. The rain had stopped, but the streets were still wet, and the air smelled of wet pavement and something else—something that might have been hope, or might have been despair. He could not tell the difference anymore.

Behind him, through the window, he could see Vi moving about the kitchen, putting things to rights, maintaining the order that was her way of coping with chaos. He thought of Ada Penrose, in her silent front room, with her piano and her scales and her own private grief. He thought of Arthur Cullis, walking home to his children with the bulletin in his hand. He thought of all the men who had stood beside him on the picket line, and all the families who had waited and worried and gone hungry, and he felt the weight of their collective defeat pressing down on him.

But beneath that weight, he felt something else too. Not hope, exactly. Not certainty. Just the stubborn, persistent knowledge that he was still here, and Vi was still here, and the house was still standing, and tomorrow would come whether he was ready for it or not.

He walked down the path and through the gate, and he turned toward the shops, and he did not look back.

The shops were closing by the time he reached the high street. He stood outside the grocer’s window, counting the coins in his palm, and realized he had not brought enough for a proper shop. He bought bread, a quarter of margarine, and a small bag of coal—the kind of purchase that announced defeat more clearly than any words could. Mrs. Patterson behind the counter did not meet his eye as she counted out his change. She knew. Everyone would know by morning.

The walk back took longer than it should have. He went by the canal path, past the silent mills, where the wheels had not turned in months and would not turn tomorrow either. The water lay flat and grey, reflecting nothing. He thought of the meetings, the votes, the speeches about solidarity and standing firm, and he felt the hollowness of every word. All those weeks of hunger, the children going without, the women making do—and for what? A few pennies saved, and the owners walking back through the gates with their heads high.

He turned onto the street and saw his own house ahead, the Number Twelve brass dull against the fading light. The curtain twitched as he approached. She was watching.

Vi met him at the door. She had been waiting—her coat still on, her hands clasped in front of her. Her eyes went to his face first, then to the small bag in his hand, and he watched her piece it together. The question rose to her lips. He saw it form, saw her draw breath to ask it, and then he watched her swallow it back down.

She stepped aside to let him in.

He walked past her into the kitchen and set the bag on the table. The bread, the margarine, the coal. The sum total of his victory. He sat heavily in his chair and looked at his hands, which were empty.

Vi did not ask what had happened. She did not ask if they had won, or what the terms were, or how long until things went back to normal. She already knew. She moved to the stove and lit the fire, and when she spoke at last, all she said was, “I’ll put the kettle on.”

The house settled around them into a silence more profound than any they had shared before.
