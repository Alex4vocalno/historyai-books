Chapter 2

The Empty Coal Scuttle

The brass button caught the grey light from the kitchen window as Frank Hollis drew the brush across it in small, deliberate circles. Metal polish gleamed on the cloth, a thin crescent of white that smelled of something acrid and mineral, the scent of maintenance, of order kept. Twenty minutes he had been at this. The uniform jacket hung on its hook above the scullery door, and he worked each button in turn, holding it between thumb and forefinger, the bristles moving in careful arcs until the tarnish lifted and the brass shone clean. His father had driven carts on the docks and kept his brasses bright enough to see your face in. A man’s uniform was his word, his father had said. You kept it right, and it kept you right.

The union lapel badge was pinned to the cardigan he wore beneath, the tiny enamel square pressing its metal edge into his sternum. Frank had not removed it since the vote. None of them had. It said: I am party to this. I have chosen. And the choosing mattered, even now when the trams stood cold in the depot and the streets were empty of their familiar rumble and clatter, even now when the quiet that had fallen over Bristol was not peace but the held absence of a thing in motion.

He set the brush down and inspected the jacket. The buttons gleamed. The wool was brushed clean, the collar straight, the seams pressed. A conductor’s uniform, dark and correct, and in it a man who could stand at the platform of the number nine and call the stops and ring the bell and mean something by the regularity of it. That was the pride. It had a plainness to it, the kind of pride that did not announce itself but simply was, in the polish and the pressing, in the showing up.

Only the pride could not keep the kitchen warm.

Frank turned from the jacket and crossed to the scullery where the coal scuttle sat beneath the shelf that held the kettle and the teapot with its chipped spout. The scuttle was galvanized iron, dented at the rim where he had knocked it against the grate one morning in January, and it had a rust spot near the base that he kept meaning to sand down and never did. He lifted it with one hand. The weight was wrong. It should have had heft, the dense pull of black fuel shifting inside.

Instead the scuttle tilted easily, the few remaining pieces of coal shifting and clicking against the tin with a thin, hollow sound. He could feel the lightness through the handle, through the palm that knew the weight of a full scuttle the way it knew the weight of a full day’s work. This was not that weight. This was the weight of almost nothing.

His thumb found the rust spot. The metal was rough there, textured, and he rubbed it the way a man rubs a worry stone, back and forth, the grit of oxidized iron catching the callused pad of his thumb. Inside the scuttle, he could count the pieces. Seven. Seven lumps of coal, two of them barely larger than gravel. The grate in the kitchen range held perhaps enough to burn for another hour if he banked it carefully, and after that the scuttle would contribute an evening’s warmth, perhaps, and then the house would cool through the night and by morning the linoleum would be cold underfoot and the windows would carry their fern patterns of frost and the kettle would have to be heated on the gas ring, which cost more in shillings than Frank wanted to calculate.

He stood with the scuttle in his hand and did not put it down. The kitchen was dim. Afternoon light came through the window at a low angle, catching the dust motes that hung in the air, and the range ticked as the last of its fire shifted. A thin heat rose from it, barely enough to take the edge off the chill that had settled into the room over the past two days. The strike was three days old. The trams were stopped. The depot gates were locked and the men had voted and the vote had been unanimous and Frank had stood in that hall with his fist raised and his voice joined to the others and the sound of it had been enormous, a sound that filled a man and made him feel that what he was doing was right and necessary and strong.

That had been three days ago. The sound was gone now. In its place was the click of seven pieces of coal in a tin scuttle and the ticking of a dying fire.

The sound of slippers on linoleum came from the hall. Soft, hesitant steps. Frank knew them. He set the scuttle back beneath the shelf and did not turn around. His shoulders drew up a fraction, the muscles across his back tightening, and he placed both hands flat on the shelf and stood very still.

Vi stood in the doorway. He could see her in the faint reflection in the scullery window, her shape pale and slight against the darker frame of the hall. She was twelve. She wore the grey cardigan that was too large for her, the sleeves rolled twice at the wrists, and her hair was plaited but coming loose, wisps framing a face that had gone thin with the season. She did not speak. She stood with one hand on the doorframe and watched him, and in the reflection her eyes were fixed on his back.

“We’ll manage,” Frank said. His voice came out steady, or near enough. He had rehearsed the tone in his head, the one that said there was a plan, that said this was a difficulty but not a crisis, that said a father who had voted for a strike was still a father who could keep his daughter warm. “I’ll see to the grate in a bit. Bank it proper. There’s enough.”

Vi said nothing for a moment. The range ticked. Outside, a bird called once and stopped, as if the sound had surprised even it in the stillness.

“Will we be warm enough tonight?”

The question was quiet. Not a complaint, not a challenge. She asked it the way she asked about homework or whether the milk had come, with a practicality that was worse than complaint because it meant she had been calculating, measuring the coal against the hours of darkness the way he had been doing, the way he was trying not to do. She was twelve and she was counting coal.

“Tonight we will.” Frank turned from the shelf. He looked at her directly, and his face arranged itself into something he did not feel but could project, a steadiness in the jaw, a calm in the eyes. “I’ll see to it. You go on and do your reading.”

Vi held his gaze. Her eyes were his mother’s eyes, grey and direct, and they saw more than a child’s eyes should have needed to see. The too-large cardigan hung from her shoulders and her hands at her sides were red at the knuckles, chapped, the skin rough and dry.

“Vi.”

“I’m going.” She turned and the slippers scuffed the linoleum and she was gone, and Frank stood with his hands on the shelf and the scuttle beneath it and the seven pieces of coal clicking softly as the house settled.

He did not follow her. He listened to her footsteps cross the hall and climb the stairs, the creak of the fourth step, the closing of her door. Then the house was quiet again, the particular quiet of a house with two people in it who were both listening to the silence and not speaking.

The range ticked. The fire was low. Frank crouched and opened the grate door. The embers inside were a deep, bruised orange, pulsing with the last of their heat. He could see the shape of the coal through the grate, the way it had collapsed into ash and fragment, and he took the scuttle and shook a single piece onto the grate. It landed with a small, dull sound. One piece. He could spare one piece for now and save the rest for the evening, and in the saving was the admission that there was not enough, that the careful rationing was itself the proof of scarcity.

The coal caught slowly. The ember glow crept around its edges, and a thin smoke rose, carrying the mineral smell of carbon burning. Frank watched it until the first edge glowed red, then closed the grate.

He stood. His knees ached from crouching, and he pressed his palm against the small of his back, a habit he had picked up from his father, who had pressed the same spot the same way after a long shift. The kitchen was cold enough that his breath did not quite fog but almost, a suggestion of vapour that dissolved as soon as it formed.

The window above the sink looked out onto the street. Frank crossed to it and stood with his hands in his pockets, looking out through the glass that was cold against his forehead when he leaned in.

The street was empty. The tram tracks ran down the center of the cobbles, steel rails catching the thin afternoon light, and they were bare. No tram. No rattle of the number nine on its hourly circuit, no hiss of air brakes at the corner stop, no call of destinations through the open window of the driver’s cab. The stop itself stood with its timetable board still posted, the times printed there now fictions, a schedule for a service that did not exist. A woman Frank did not know by name walked past on the far pavement, her coat collar turned up, her pace quick and purposeful, and then she was gone and the street was empty again.

Across the road and one house to the left, the front room of Ada Penrose’s house showed a light in the window. Frank could see the shape of the room through the net curtain, the edge of a piano, and there, standing at the window, a figure. Ada. He knew the shape of her, the particular stillness of her posture, the way she held herself at a window as if she were not looking out but waiting for something to look in. Her hand rested on the curtain edge. She did not move.

Frank did not move either. They stood at their respective windows, separated by the cobbles and the silent rails and the particular gulf that existed between two people who had never spoken beyond a nod on the street or a passing word about the weather. But in the stillness, in the shared act of looking out at a street that should have been full and was not, there was something. Not a connection, exactly. A recognition. The acknowledgment of two people who were both alone in their houses and both aware that the other was there, and that the being there mattered, that the alternative, which was a street of dark windows and closed curtains and no one watching, would have been worse.

Ada’s hand tightened on the curtain edge. Or seemed to. The distance was enough that Frank could not be certain of the gesture, only that some small movement occurred, and then the curtain shifted and she was obscured, and the window showed only the light and the edge of the piano and the empty room beyond.

Frank pressed his forehead harder against the glass. The cold of it traveled through his skull, a thin ache behind his eyes. Below, on the street, the tram tracks gleamed in their useless perfection, steel polished by the passage of wheels that would not pass again until the strike ended, and Frank could not say when that would be. No one could say. That was the nature of it. You voted and you stood and you held the line, and the line held you too, and in the holding was the not knowing, the never knowing whether the coal would last or the shillings would hold out or the resolve in the hall would survive the quiet of a kitchen where the grate was burning its last pieces and a daughter’s hands were red with cold.

He thought of Vi’s hands. The chapped knuckles, the rough skin. She had been counting coal. She had stood in the doorway and asked the question that he could not answer honestly without admitting that he did not know, that his plan was the plan of a man who had no plan, that the strike was right and necessary and strong and it was also the reason his daughter’s hands were cold.

The union badge pressed against his chest. He pressed his thumb against it through the cardigan wool, feeling its edge, its small, sharp point. The badge said he had chosen. The scuttle said what the choosing cost.

Footsteps on the stairs. The creak of the fourth step. Frank turned from the window. Vi stood in the hall, her hands deep in the pockets of the grey cardigan, her face pale in the dim light. She had come back down. She looked past him to the kitchen, to the range where the single piece of coal was burning its slow, insufficient burn, and then she looked at him.

“Vi, I said I’d see to it.”

“I know.” She did not move from the stair. “I wasn’t asking again. I just came down.”

Frank rubbed his jaw. The shadow of stubble was there, the grey-speckled growth that came by noon no matter how close the morning shave. “There’s enough for tonight. I’ll sort tomorrow.”

Vi nodded. She descended the last two steps and crossed to the front window, the one that faced the street, and stood where he had been standing. The light from outside caught her face, and Frank saw how drawn she looked, how the cold had settled into the hollows beneath her eyes and the set of her mouth.

She gazed out at the empty street, at the silent rails, at the tram stop with its useless timetable, and her hands in the cardigan pockets were curled into fists, and Frank knew, with the certainty of a man who has run out of alternatives to knowing, that the chill he saw in her face was not the chill of the room but the chill of understanding, the particular cold that comes when a child realizes that the structures which hold her world in place are contingent, that they can stop, that a street can go silent and a grate can go dark and a father can stand in a kitchen and say there is enough when there is not enough, and that this is not a failure of provision but a cost of principle, and the principle is the father’s and the cost is the daughter’s, and neither of them can separate the one from the other.

The range ticked. The single coal burned its small, orange circle of heat into the grate. Frank stood in the kitchen with the weight of the empty scuttle still in his palm, the grit of rust still under his thumb, and watched his daughter watch the empty street, and the quiet that had fallen over Bristol was not a pause but a presence, and it was in the room with them, and it would not lift.
