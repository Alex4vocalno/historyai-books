Chapter 7

Wound, Waiting

A single sunbeam cut across the dust on the closed piano lid, and Ada Penrose stood quite still at the window, watching the empty street.

The knock had come yesterday. She had opened the door, flustered by the girl’s request, and told her to return tomorrow for an answer. Now tomorrow had arrived, and Ada stood frozen at the window, unable to bring herself to the door. The girl would come again soon—Ada had watched her cross from Number 3 that morning, her small figure straight with purpose—and the waiting had settled over Number 12 like silt in still water. Now the trams had stopped running, and the street held its breath, and Ada found herself unable to move beyond the threshold of the front room.

She had dressed that morning as she always dressed. Clean blouse. Pinned hair. Shoes wiped free of scuff marks. The routine had carried her as far as the landing at the top of the stairs, and then she had stopped. Below her, the front room waited with its mouth shut tight, and she had gripped the newel post and counted the reasons to descend. One. The room required airing. Two. The dust would settle if left untouched. Three. Her father’s metronome stood on its shelf in the corner cabinet, and two days had passed since she had last lifted it from its place.

The count had not helped. She had descended anyway.

Now she stood at the window with her hands folded at her waist, and the street outside offered nothing. No carts. No children chasing hoops. The tram lines caught the light in parallel streaks, running down toward the depot where Frank Hollis walked the picket. She knew this without being told. The strike had entered its second day, and the neighbourhood had grown accustomed to the absence of the electric hum, the clatter of wheels on rails. What remained was a quieter kind of waiting.

Ada turned from the window. The room stretched before her, immaculate and still. The piano stood against the inner wall, its black lacquer holding the shape of the light. Two porcelain dogs flanked the mantelpiece, their painted eyes fixed on nothing. The armchair by the cold fireplace held the impression of a body that had not sat there in years. Her father’s chair. Her father’s room.

She crossed to the cabinet in the corner and opened the glass door.

The metronome sat on the second shelf, brass and dark wood, its pendulum frozen mid-arc. A pyramid of precision. Her father had kept it wound but rarely used it in his own playing; he preferred the discipline of internal time, the counting of rests and the subdivision of beats in the blood. But for students who had not yet learned to feel the pulse beneath the skin, the metronome was essential—his students, and later hers.

Ada reached for it. Her fingers touched the cool brass housing, and she lifted the instrument from its shelf with the care of a woman handling a reliquary. The weight settled into her palm. She had held it a thousand times. A thousand lessons, a thousand corrections, a thousand times her father’s voice had cut across a stumbling phrase: “The metronome isn’t about hurry. It’s about agreement.”

She had agreed. She had always agreed. That was what the room demanded.

But the girl’s voice had carried through the door, and the request had lodged somewhere beneath Ada’s ribs like a splinter she could not reach.

She carried the metronome to the piano and set it on the closed lid. The brass caught the light from the window, and she saw that the surface was not as clean as she had thought. A thin film of dust had gathered along the edge of the base, and when she brushed at it with her thumb, the powder smeared rather than lifted. She would need the cloth. She would need the polish.

The thought brought her up short. The room did not require her intervention. She maintained it. That was the agreement. She opened the windows each morning to air the stale air. She wiped the surfaces with a dry cloth on Thursdays. She checked the piano for damp and polished the keys with the chamois leather her father had kept in the bench. The routine was fixed. The schedule was fixed. One must keep to the schedule, you see. It was what provided the structure.

And yet the dust had gathered, and the metronome sat on the piano lid, and Ada found herself walking back to the cabinet to retrieve the beeswax polish and the soft cotton cloth she kept on the lowest shelf.

She returned to the piano. She unscrewed the lid of the polish. The smell rose to meet her: wax and turpentine and something sweeter underneath, a scent that belonged to the room before her father had died, before his students had stopped coming, before the front door had closed on the last of them and Ada had turned the lock and mounted the stairs to her cold bedroom and let the silence take the house.

Her hands moved without her permission. The cloth touched the polish. The polish touched the brass. She worked in small circles, the way her father had taught her, and the metal began to gleam under her ministrations. The smell filled the space between her and the window. Outside, a bird called from the bare branches of the lime tree across the street, and the sound was thin and lonely in the quiet.

The metronome grew warm in her hands. She turned it over to clean the base, and her thumb found a roughness in the brass.

She stopped.

The pad of her thumb traced the irregularity. A groove in the metal, shallow and uneven. She tilted the metronome toward the light and squinted at the underside of the base.

A letter had been scratched into the brass. A single capital letter, gouged with something sharp and unsteady. An A.

Ada’s breath caught.

She had not done this. She was certain she had not—until the certainty began to waver. The metronome had been her father’s, purchased before her birth, maintained with a precision that bordered on reverence. He would never have marked it. He would never have allowed it.

But the letter was there. An A. A childish, crooked A, scratched with the unpracticed hand of someone who had not yet learned to hold a tool. Someone like herself, decades ago. The recognition crept over her slowly.

Ada sat down on the piano bench. The metronome rested in her lap, and she traced the letter again with her thumb. The metal had warmed from her handling, and the groove felt less like damage and more like a message, though she could not say what it said.

She had been a child in this room. She knew this, though she rarely let herself remember it. She had sat on this bench with her feet dangling above the pedals. She had watched her father’s hands move across the keys. She had listened to the metronome tick and felt the pulse of it in the floorboards, in the air, in the hollow spaces behind her ears.

And at some point, she had taken something sharp—a pin? a nail? the point of her scissors?—and she had pressed it into the brass and carved her initial into the underside of the base, where no one would see.

Where her father had not seen. Or where he had seen, and said nothing.

The thought opened a space in her chest. A small, terrifying space.

She had been a child who marked things. A child who left traces. A child who existed in this room as more than a custodian.

The girl had asked to learn. Teach me. Two words. A simple request. But nothing was simple in this room. Nothing had been simple since the last lesson had ended and the door had closed and Ada had mounted the stairs and let the silence take the house.

She looked at the metronome in her lap. The brass gleamed from her polishing. The A remained, rough and stubborn, a witness to a version of herself she had nearly forgotten.

Her hand found the winding key on the side of the housing.

The mechanism was stiff. It had been months since she had last wound it, longer since she had let the pendulum swing. The spring resisted at first, and then it began to give. Click. Click. Click. The ratchet moved under her fingers, and the tension built in the coil inside, winding tighter and tighter, storing the energy for a rhythm she had not yet agreed to release.

She wound until the mechanism would take no more. The spring held the tension in its core, and the metronome sat in her lap with its pendulum frozen and its brass body warm from her hands.

She stood. Her legs felt unsteady, and she braced one hand against the piano lid for balance. The room watched her. The porcelain dogs. The empty armchair. The cold fireplace. All of it waiting, as it had waited for years, for her to decide what kind of space this would be.

She placed the metronome on the piano lid, near the edge, where a student could see it. Where a student could reach it.

The pendulum did not move. The room did not fill with the tick of counted time. But the mechanism was wound, and the spring held its potential, and the choice sat before her like a door she had not yet opened.

Ada stepped back. Her hands hung at her sides. The sunbeam had shifted across the piano lid while she worked, and now the light caught the brass and threw a thin reflection against the wall. The room was still a shrine. The dust had been cleared from the metronome’s surface, and the porcelain dogs still watched from the mantelpiece, and the armchair still held the impression of a body that would never return.

But something had shifted. The A beneath the base pressed against the wood of the cabinet shelf in her memory, and the wound spring held its breath, and Ada stood in the middle of the room and felt the weight of a decision she had not yet made.

Tomorrow, perhaps, the girl would knock again.

Tomorrow, perhaps, Ada would open the door.

She turned from the piano and walked to the window. The street stretched empty below, the tram lines glinting in the afternoon light. No movement. No sound but the distant call of a bird and the faint murmur of voices from somewhere beyond the terrace, where the depot stood and the men walked the line.

Ada pressed her palm flat against the glass. The cool surface held her reflection, pale and indistinct, and beyond it the world waited in its suspended quiet. The strike would end. The trams would run again. The street would fill with its ordinary noises, its ordinary rhythms, its ordinary agreements.

But the metronome sat wound on the piano lid, and the A was carved into the brass, and Ada Penrose stood at the window and counted the reasons to stay and the reasons to move.

The reasons to stay were fixed. The room. The schedule. The memory of a voice that had taught her to feel the pulse beneath the skin.

The reasons to move were simpler. A knock at the door. A voice through the wood. A girl who wanted to learn.

Ada let her hand fall from the glass. She turned back to the room. The metronome gleamed in the shifting light, brass catching the last of the afternoon sun, wound and waiting. The pendulum hung still. The spring held its tension. The room held its breath.

Tomorrow, perhaps.

Ada crossed to the door and paused with her hand on the knob. She did not look back. She did not need to. The room had changed, though nothing had moved but her own hands, her own breath, her own memory of a childish hand pressing a pin into brass.

She opened the door and stepped into the hall. The stairs rose before her, and above them the bedroom waited with its cold sheets and its empty hearth. But below her, the front room held its new shape, and the metronome sat on the piano lid, and the wound spring marked time she had not yet agreed to keep.

She climbed the stairs. Her footsteps echoed in the quiet house, and the sound was smaller than she remembered, and the silence that followed was not as heavy as it had been.

In the front room, the metronome sat wound and waiting. The light shifted across the piano lid. The porcelain dogs watched the empty air. And beneath the base of the brass pyramid, a crooked A pressed against the wood, a message from a child who had once believed she was allowed to leave a mark.
