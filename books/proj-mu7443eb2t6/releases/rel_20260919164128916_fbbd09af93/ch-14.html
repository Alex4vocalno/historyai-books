Chapter 14

The Mother's Absence

Vi’s first note of the scale was flat, a dull thud in the silent room that seemed to carry more weight than any wrong note had a right to hold. Ada Penrose sat straight-backed on the edge of her chair, pencil poised above the sheet music, and watched the child’s fingers stumble through the C-major exercise that should have been routine by now. The metronome ticked its measured pulse on the piano lid, but Vi’s hands refused to keep time, lagging behind the beat as though weighted by something invisible.

The front room held its familiar stillness, the lemon polish and old paper smell settling around them like a second skin. Grey light filtered through the curtains, illuminating dust motes that drifted in lazy spirals. Ada had opened the shutters this morning out of habit, but the light brought no warmth. The tram strike stretched into its second week, and the silence of the absent rails pressed against the windows like weather, a low pressure system that had settled over the entire neighborhood and refused to lift.

Ada thought of Frank Hollis standing in his back garden two days ago, looking at the fence he had promised to mend. She had watched from her kitchen window as he stood there, shoulders drawn in, the empty coal scuttle at his feet like an accusation. She knew that scuttle had been emptied daily since—their fire gone cold, the house next door growing colder still. The image had stayed with her through sleepless hours, troubling her in ways she refused to examine directly. There had been something defeated in his posture, something that spoke of costs she could not calculate. She had given him coal because the alternative, letting a child freeze, was unthinkable, but the transaction had left a residue she could not wipe clean.

“Again,” Ada said, keeping her voice level. “From the third measure.”

Vi’s small hands hesitated above the keys. She wore her coat buttoned to the chin despite the warmth of the room, and her wool cap sat firmly on her head, a violation of the no-hats rule that Ada had not found the energy to enforce today. The child’s cheeks still held a flush from the cold outside, or perhaps from something else. Her face, usually so serious and attentive, seemed drawn inward, her attention fixed on some interior distance that Ada could not reach.

The scale began again, and again the notes came out wrong. Not simply flat now, but faltering, as though Vi’s fingers had forgotten the shape of the music entirely. Ada watched the child’s jaw tighten, saw the flush deepen across the bridge of her nose.

“The fourth finger, Vi,” Ada said, her correction softer than usual. “You’re collapsing the knuckle.”

Vi corrected her hand, but the next note still came out wrong. The metronome ticked on, indifferent to the struggle unfolding beneath its rhythm. Ada found herself listening to that tick as though it carried some message she had been avoiding, some truth about time and loss that she had sealed away in the back rooms of her mind.

She thought of her father then, unavoidably. He had sat in this same spot, straight-backed and precise, teaching her the same scales she now taught Vi. The piano had been his before it was hers, and his father’s before that. Three years ago come spring, he had died in the upstairs bedroom, and Ada had not played for months afterward. When she finally opened the piano again, the tuning had shifted, the middle register slightly flat, as though the instrument itself had been grieving. She had never quite corrected it.

“Miss Penrose?” Vi’s voice cut through the memory, small and uncertain.

Ada blinked, refocused. “Continue. The measure isn’t finished.”

But Vi’s hands had stopped moving. They rested on the keys now, still and heavy, pressing down on a discordant cluster of notes that bled together in the quiet room. The metronome ticked on, counting time that no one was using.

“I can’t,” Vi said.

“Can’t is not a word we use in this room.” The phrase came automatically, one of her father’s maxims, but it rang hollow today. The child’s voice carried a heaviness that had nothing to do with scales or finger position.

Vi looked up then, and Ada saw that the child’s eyes were bright with something more than concentration. The whites had a glassy quality, and her lower lip trembled almost imperceptibly.

“Vi.” Ada set down her pencil. “What is it?”

The child’s throat moved as she swallowed. Her hands remained frozen on the keys, pressing those wrong notes into a sustained, aching chord. “My mother,” she said, and then stopped.

Ada felt something shift in her chest, a tightening that she recognized as warning. This was not part of the lesson. This was not part of the careful distance she maintained between herself and her pupils. She should redirect, she knew. She should say something about focus, about the importance of scales, about how music requires our full attention. She had said those things before, to other children, on other days.

But today the words would not come. Today she looked at Vi’s face, at the flush on her cheeks and the wool cap pulled low over her brow, and she saw something she had been refusing to see. The child was not simply distracted. The child was carrying something.

“What about your mother?” Ada heard herself ask, and the question felt like stepping off a edge into unknown depth.

Vi’s chin lifted slightly, a gesture that seemed to require enormous courage. “She left,” the child said. “She was tired of the quiet, she said. She said she couldn’t bear it anymore, the way the house held its breath.”

The words fell into the silent room like stones into still water. Ada felt the ripples spread through her, disturbing sediment she had thought long settled. The quiet. She knew that quiet. She had grown up inside it, had learned to navigate its currents, had eventually learned to make her home within its boundaries. Her father had never spoken of her mother, not once in all the years Ada could remember. The absence had simply been there, a room in their shared life that they never entered.

“I see,” Ada said, but she did not see, not fully. Or perhaps she saw too much, recognized too much in the child’s flattened vowels and careful composure.

“She didn’t take anything,” Vi continued, her voice taking on a rehearsed quality, as though she had practiced these words in private many times. “Not her books, not her photographs. Just her coat and her purse. She walked out the door on a Tuesday morning, and she didn’t come back.”

Ada’s hand moved without her permission, reaching toward the child before stopping itself in midair. She pulled it back, folded it with its partner in her lap. This was not her domain. She taught scales and finger position. She did not minister to grief.

But the child’s face held an expectancy now, a silent question that seemed to demand something more than professional distance. Vi’s eyes, bright with unshed tears, searched Ada’s face for something, some recognition or response that would make the confession bearable.

“You look warm,” Ada said, the words coming out clumsy and strange. “Let me get you some water.”

She rose from her chair too quickly, knocking the pencil to the floor. It rolled under the piano, and she left it there. In the kitchen, she filled a glass from the tap, her hands unsteady. The water ran cold over her fingers, and she held them there for a moment, letting the chill anchor her. The kitchen window looked out onto the back garden, and beyond it, the fence that marked the boundary between her property and the Hollis household. Frank’s fence. The one he had promised to mend.

She thought of him standing there with the empty scuttle, the defeat in his posture. Had he known, then, what his daughter would confess today? Had he sent her here carrying this weight, or had she come on her own, drawn by some instinct toward the only other person in the neighborhood who understood the architecture of absence?

Ada returned to the front room with the glass. Vi had not moved. Her hands still pressed those same wrong notes into the piano’s soundboard, though the chord had faded now, leaving only the metronome’s tick to mark the passing seconds.

“Here.” Ada held out the glass. “Drink.”

Vi took it with both hands, the way a younger child might. She raised it to her lips and drank, her eyes never leaving Ada’s face. When she lowered the glass, a drop of water clung to her lower lip. She wiped it away with the back of her hand, a gesture so young, so vulnerable, that Ada felt something crack inside her chest.

“Mothers,” Ada heard herself say, the word coming out before she could stop it. “Mothers are complicated.”

It was a platitude, a meaningless comfort, and she despised herself for it the moment the words left her mouth. Vi’s expression flickered, something close to disappointment passing across her features before she masked it again with that serious face she wore like armor.

But Ada could not take it back. She could not undo the inadequacy of her response. She stood there, empty-handed now, searching for something better to say, something that would meet the child’s expectancy rather than deflect it.

The metronome ticked on. Ada’s gaze drifted to it—her father’s metronome, still counting out its steady measure on the piano. The dust motes drifted. The grey light through the curtains had shifted slightly, moving across the piano’s surface in a slow, inexorable progression. Time, Ada thought, did not stop for grief. It simply continued, measuring out its beats, indifferent to the music we failed to make.

“My father,” Ada said, and then stopped. The words felt strange in her mouth, unaccustomed to the air. She had not spoken of him to anyone outside the necessary arrangements after his death. The neighbors knew, of course. They had sent cards and casseroles, had murmured their sympathies in the receiving line at the funeral. But Ada had not spoken of him since, not really, not in any way that mattered.

Vi waited. The child had an infinite capacity for waiting, it seemed. She had learned, perhaps, that adults would eventually fill silence if given enough time, that patience could pry loose the things people preferred to keep hidden.

“He taught me piano,” Ada continued, her voice coming slower now, each word a deliberate choice. “In this room. On this instrument. He was the only teacher I ever had.”

She stopped again. The confession seemed inadequate, a fragment of something larger that she could not quite bring herself to voice. But Vi’s face had changed. The expectancy there had shifted into something else, something that looked almost like recognition.

“He died,” Ada said, and the word hung in the air between them, heavier than any wrong note. “Three years ago come spring.”

Vi’s hands finally lifted from the keys. They settled in her lap, folded together, and she looked down at them for a long moment. When she raised her head again, her eyes were dry, but something in her expression had softened, a barrier dropping away.

“The quiet,” she said. “That’s what she couldn’t bear. My mother. She said the house was too quiet, that she could hear herself thinking, and she didn’t like what she heard.”

Ada felt the words land somewhere deep, in a place she had kept carefully sealed. She thought of her own house, her own quiet, the way the rooms held their breath in the absence of her father’s tread. She thought of the piano’s slightly flat tuning, the way she had never quite corrected it, as though preserving some small monument to his absence.

“Sometimes,” Ada said, her voice barely above a whisper, “the quiet holds things we are not ready to hear.”

Vi considered this. Her serious face shifted slightly, something moving behind her eyes that Ada could not quite name. The child reached up and, for the first time, removed her wool cap. Her hair lay flat against her skull, pressed down by hours of wear. She set the cap on the piano bench beside her, a small gesture of compliance that seemed to carry more weight than any correction Ada could have offered.

“Did your mother leave too?” Vi asked.

The question struck Ada like a physical blow. She felt herself recoil, felt the old walls rise automatically, the instinctive withdrawal into protected space. Her mother. The absence that had never been discussed, the gap in her history that her father had simply refused to acknowledge.

“No,” Ada said, and then, more quietly, “I don’t know. She was never here.”

Vi absorbed this, her expression unchanged. She seemed to understand that some absences were not departures, that some voids had existed from the beginning, unnamed and unexamined.

The metronome had wound down, its tick slowing, becoming irregular, and finally stopping altogether. The silence it left behind was different from the silence that had filled the room before. It was not empty. It was charged with something new, some fragile understanding that had been forged in the space between them.

Ada looked at the child sitting at her piano, coat still buttoned to the chin, hands folded in her lap, and she saw something she had not seen before. Not just a pupil. Not just a neighbor’s daughter. A person carrying a weight that should have been too heavy for such small shoulders, bearing it with a dignity that shamed Ada’s own careful distance.

“The lesson,” Ada began, but she could not finish the sentence. The formal instruction felt impossible now, a pretense that had been stripped away. She could not ask this child to play scales while her mother’s absence echoed in every wrong note.

Vi seemed to understand. She did not reach for the keys again. She simply sat there, present and waiting, her small body held in that attentive stillness that Ada had always appreciated but now recognized as something else. Not just obedience. Not just the seriousness of a child trying to please. A kind of company, offered without demand.

“Next week,” Ada said, her voice finding its way back to the formal register through sheer effort of will. “We will continue with the C-major scale. Your fourth finger needs attention.”

Vi nodded. “Yes, Miss Penrose.”

But something had shifted in the way she said it. The formality remained, but the distance had altered. They were no longer simply teacher and pupil. They were two people who had touched, however briefly, the edges of each other’s grief.

Ada walked Vi to the door. The child collected her cap from the bench, but she did not put it back on. She held it in her hand, her hair still pressed flat against her head, and stepped out into the grey afternoon. The street was quiet, no trams running, no traffic to break the stillness. The silence of the rails pressed down on the rooftops, a weight that everyone in the neighborhood had learned to carry.

Vi turned at the gate, her serious face lifted toward Ada. “Thank you,” she said, though she did not specify what she was thanking her for.

Ada stood in the doorway, watching the child walk down the street toward Number Twelve. She thought of Frank, standing in his back garden with the empty scuttle. She thought of the fence he had promised to mend, the boundary between their properties that seemed to mark something more than property lines now.

She closed the door and returned to the front room. The piano sat silent, its keys slightly yellowed, its middle register slightly flat. The sheet music still lay open on the rack, the pencil still under the bench where it had rolled. Ada retrieved it, held it for a moment, and then set it on the piano lid beside the stopped metronome.

The room held its breath around her, the lemon polish and old paper smell settling back into their familiar places. But something had changed. The quiet was still there, but it no longer pressed quite so heavily against her chest. It held something now, some new weight that she could not yet name.

She sat down at the piano, her fingers finding the keys without looking. The C-major scale came easily, muscle memory carrying her through the familiar progression. But when she reached the fourth finger, she paused, feeling the slight collapse that Vi had struggled with, the tendency to let the knuckle give way.

She corrected it, played the scale again, and then again. Each time, she listened for the slight flatness in the middle register, the tuning that she had never quite fixed. It was still there, a small monument to loss, a note that would never quite ring true.

But she played anyway. The notes filled the quiet room, rising and falling, measuring out time in a way that the stopped metronome no longer could. And outside, the grey light shifted, moving slowly toward evening, carrying the weight of the day toward whatever would come next.
