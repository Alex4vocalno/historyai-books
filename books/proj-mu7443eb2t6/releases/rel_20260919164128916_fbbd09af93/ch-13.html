Chapter 13

Unspoken Accounts

The borrowed scuttle sat by Frank Hollis’s hearth, a black accusation. He had placed it there himself, the coal already transferred to the iron grate, but the empty vessel remained, obstinate in its testimony. The house held the kind of cold that settled into the bone, the sort that no single scuttle could remedy. Vi slept upstairs, her breathing shallow and even, while Frank stood in his own front room and regarded the evidence of his failure.

The scuttle had come from Ada Penrose’s house. Number Three. The piano teacher. He had sent Vi to ask because he could not bring himself to knock on that glossy black door and speak the words aloud. His daughter had returned with the coal and something else besides—a weight that pressed against his chest each time he looked at the empty container. He owed Miss Penrose now. A man who paid his debts, that was what he had always been. A man who held the line. Now he was a man with an empty scuttle and a daughter who had witnessed him send a child to beg.

He crossed to the kitchen and pulled open the drawer where they kept the oddments. A few bent nails, a spool of twine, a button that belonged to no garment he could recall. He turned his pockets out onto the scarred wooden table. Three coins clinked against the surface, pathetic and bright. Not enough for a week’s coal. Not enough for a loaf, if the baker would even extend credit to a man whose union had stopped the trams and stopped the wages along with them.

The drawer beside the sink held the tin where his wife used to keep housekeeping money. He had not opened it in months. The lid came away with a faint metallic whine, and inside: nothing. A dusting of flour at the bottom, a single hairpin. He stood there, looking at the empty tin, and felt the silence of the house press against him from all sides. The quiet. It was the never knowing, she had said once, the day she left. She had been speaking of something else, but the words had stayed with him, had become the weather of this house since she was gone.

Upstairs, Vi shifted in her sleep. He heard the creak of the bed frame, the small murmur she made when she turned. He closed the tin and placed it back in the drawer, then moved through the lower rooms with methodical desperation. The cupboard under the stairs yielded a broken umbrella, a pair of boots with the soles separating, a stack of newspaper yellowed with age. Nothing worth a neighbour’s charity. Nothing worth the price of his daughter’s warmth.

In the back room, where they had once taken their meals together—before the strike, before Meg—he knelt beside the small cabinet where they kept the better things. A china figurine of a shepherdess, chipped at the base. A photograph in a silver frame, the silver gone black with tarnish. A pocket watch that had not worked since his father’s time. He turned the watch over in his palm, feeling the weight of it, the cold metal against his skin. It was worth something, perhaps. But the thought of walking to the pawnshop with his father’s watch, of explaining to Vi why it was gone—it rose in his throat like bile.

He put the watch back. He stood. He walked to the window that faced the back garden and saw, through the grey light, the fence that separated his plot from Ada Penrose’s. Two panels had come loose in the last wind, and beyond them, her garden was neat, ordered, the bare stems of rose bushes tied back against the frost. A woman who kept her house the way his father had kept his watch: wound, polished, put away.

The gate at the end of her garden hung crooked on its hinges. He had noticed it when Vi came back with the coal. A small thing, the sort of thing a man with tools and time could set right in an afternoon. He had tools. He had time, now that the trams sat silent in the depots and the union men met in halls that produced nothing but words.

He had nothing to give her. But he had hands.

The thought settled in his chest, uncomfortable and solid. Labor. It was what he had to trade. Not the coin he had promised himself he would always provide, not the steady wage that bought respectability, but the strength of his back and the calluses on his palms. It was not nothing. But it was not enough.

He heard movement through the wall. Ada Penrose’s house was attached to his, separated by a single thickness of brick and the mutual silence of people who did not intrude. Her footsteps were light, precise, moving across her front room. The piano room. He had heard her teaching through the wall sometimes, the scales ascending and descending, the metronome ticking its measured agreement. She had been teaching Vi for weeks now, and each time he had stood in his own front room, listening to the distant sound of his daughter’s uncertain fingers finding the keys, and felt something he could not name.

Now her footsteps paused. He imagined her standing at her window, looking out at the same silent street, carrying the same weight of obligation. The coal had been charity. He knew it, and she knew it, and the knowledge sat between their houses like a third presence, uninvited and unwelcome.

He crossed to his own window and saw that her curtains were drawn, as they always were. A woman who kept her privacy the way she kept her piano: covered, protected, revealed only in the proper measure. He thought of Vi coming home with the scuttle, her cheeks flushed from the cold, her face serious in that way she had adopted since the strike began—the way children learned to wear seriousness when adults forgot to hide their worry.

He would go to her. He would knock on that glossy black door and offer what he had. It was not enough. But it was what remained.

The walk from Number Twelve to Number Three was twelve steps along the pavement, but it stretched in his mind like a passage across a wider gulf. The street held its particular stillness, the absence of trams a pressure that sat on the rooftops and pressed the neighbours into their separate rooms. No one moved behind the windows he passed. The strike had taught them all to stay inside, to count what they had and find it wanting.

He mounted the three steps to Ada Penrose’s door and paused. The brass knocker was polished to a gleam, and when he lifted it, the metal was cold against his knuckles. He let it fall twice. The sound was swallowed by the silent street.

The door opened. Ada Penrose stood in the gap, her face composed in that way he had come to recognize—the face of a woman who had learned to hold her expression still against interruption. She wore a dress of dark wool, neat at the collar, and her hair was drawn back in a twist that seemed on the verge of loosening. Her eyes met his for a moment, then slid away.

“Mr. Hollis.” Her voice was level, controlled. “Is something the matter?”

He did not meet her eyes. Instead, he stared at the doorframe, where a small chip in the paint revealed the dark wood beneath. “I’ve nothing to give you for the coal.” The words came out gritty, scraped from somewhere deep. “But I can fix that.”

He pointed. His finger was steady despite the heat in his face, and he gestured past her, toward the back garden, toward the gate that hung crooked on its hinges. “The back gate. I saw it needs mending.”

She stood motionless in the gap. The draft from the open door carried the faint smell of lemon polish and something older, something like sealed envelopes left too long in a drawer. He could hear, from the front room, the piano sitting in silence, the metronome still, the music waiting to resume.

“You don’t owe me anything for the coal, Mr. Hollis.” Her voice was careful, each word placed like a note in a scale. “It was a neighbour’s kindness. Nothing more.”

“I don’t take charity.” He said it to the doorframe, to the chipped paint, to the space between them. “I fix gates. I mend fences. That’s what I can give.”

The silence stretched. He could hear her breathing, the slight shift of her weight on the threshold. Then she turned, her gaze following his gesture toward the back garden, toward the crooked gate, and something in her expression changed—not softening, exactly, but adjusting, like a pianist finding a new key.

“The back gate has been troublesome for some time,” she said. “The hinge is rusted through. And one of the slats has come away from the crossbar.”

“I can see to it.” He was still speaking to the doorframe, still unable to meet her eyes. “I’ve tools. I’ve time.”

“Time is something we all have, at present.” There was a note in her voice he could not quite read—irony, perhaps, or something closer to acknowledgment. “The strike has given us that, if nothing else.”

He said nothing. The words he wanted to speak—about wages stopped, about union dues, about the picket lines that had become his only occupation—stuck in his throat. He was a man who held the line. That was what he did. And now he stood on a neighbour’s doorstep, offering to mend a gate in exchange for a scuttle of coal.

“Very well.” Ada Penrose’s voice broke through his reverie, crisp and decisive. “If you wish to make yourself useful, Mr. Hollis, the back gate would be a start. The hinge will need replacing. I believe I have some hardware in the shed, but if the tools are insufficient—“

“I have tools.” He said it too quickly, then corrected himself. “I’ll bring what I need. Tomorrow, if that suits.”

“Tomorrow is acceptable.” She stepped back, beginning to close the door, then paused. “Mr. Hollis. Your daughter—Vi—she has a natural hand for the piano. When she practices, she shows promise.”

He looked at her then, finally, and saw something in her face that was not quite warmth but was not entirely distance either. It was the expression of a woman who had learned to give praise in measured doses, the way her father had taught her.

“She enjoys it,” he said. “The lessons.”

“Good.” The word was simple, final. She hesitated, and he sensed her correcting herself—that earlier refusal of indebtedness had been genuine, yet here she was, allowing him this small fiction of repayment. If it preserved his dignity, she would not deny him that. “Then we have an agreement. The gate for the coal. And perhaps—“ She paused again, and in that hesitation he saw the cost of her next words. “Perhaps Vi might continue her lessons, when the strike ends. If she wishes to.”

He nodded, not trusting his voice. The door closed between them, and he stood on the step for a moment, looking at the chipped paint, the polished brass, the glossy black surface that reflected nothing but his own shadow.

When he returned to Number Twelve, Vi was awake. She sat on the stairs, her knees drawn up to her chest, her face shadowed in the dim light from the hallway. She had been watching. He saw it in her eyes—the knowledge of what she had witnessed, the understanding of what it meant.

“Dad?” Her voice was small, careful.

“It’s done.” He climbed the stairs past her, his hand brushing her shoulder as he passed. “Go back to bed, Vi. It’s late.”

She did not move. “Did she take it? The offer?”

“She took it.” He paused on the landing, looking down at his daughter’s serious face. “The back gate needs mending. I’ll start tomorrow.”

Vi nodded, but she did not go back to bed. She sat on the stairs, her small form folded into the shadows, and watched him walk down the hallway to his own room. He felt her gaze on his back, the weight of her witnessing, and knew that she understood more than he had ever intended her to learn.

The house was cold. The scuttle was empty. But tomorrow, he would mend a gate, and the debt would begin to be paid.

He stood in his back garden as the light faded, looking at the fence he had promised to mend, the crooked gate beyond it, the neat garden of the woman who had accepted his labor in place of coin. The fence was old, the wood grey and splintered, and beyond it, Ada Penrose’s roses stood in their ordered rows, waiting for spring.

He had nothing left to give but his hands. And now, even those were promised.

The strike would end eventually. The trams would run again. The wages would return, and he would stand in his own front room and hear the piano through the wall, his daughter’s fingers finding the keys. The debt would remain, woven into the mended gate, the straightened fence, the small repairs that bound their houses together.

He wondered what this new bond would cost him. He wondered if Ada Penrose, in her silent front room with its lemon polish and its ghosts, was asking herself the same question.

The night settled over the terrace, cold and still. Somewhere inside Number Three, a light went on in an upper window, then off again. He watched until the curtains stirred and then went still, and then he turned and went back into his own cold house, where his daughter waited on the stairs, and the empty scuttle sat by the hearth, and the silence pressed against him from all sides.

The quiet. It was the never knowing. But now, at least, there was something he could do with his hands.
