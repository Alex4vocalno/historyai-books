Chapter 16

The Distant Bell

────────────────────────────────────────

The final chord still seemed to hang in the air, though Vi had gone ten minutes since. Ada Penrose stood at the window of her front room, one hand resting against the cool glass, watching the empty street. The girl had played the simple tune perfectly—a phrase from one of her father’s old exercises, nothing ambitious—and the sound had filled the silent house with something Ada could not name. A tangible, joyful result of the broken rule. The memory of it sat in the room like a guest who refused to leave, and Ada found herself unwilling to ask it to go.

She had not moved the metronome back to the mantelpiece. It sat on the piano’s edge, still, its brass gleaming dully in the grey morning light. Nine days now without the trams. Nine days of this suspended quiet, the street holding its breath, the neighbours emerging and retreating like figures in a silent film. The strike had pressed the world flat, and in that flattening, something unexpected had grown. Vi Hollis, eleven years old and serious as a judge, had walked through Ada’s door and asked to learn. And Ada, contrary to every instinct she had cultivated in thirty-four years of living alone, had said yes.

Now she stood at the window and watched Number Twelve, wondering if Frank Hollis knew what his daughter had accomplished this morning. The curtains there were drawn as usual. No smoke from the chimney. The coal must be running low by now—she had seen the evidence in the child’s reddened fingertips last week, the way Vi had kept her gloves on until the moment she sat at the piano. Ada had not asked. One did not pry into the private affairs of one’s pupils. This was the rule her father had taught her, and she had followed it without deviation.

Until now.

She turned from the window and walked the length of the front room, her slippered feet making no sound on the worn carpet. The room felt larger in the silence, the furniture casting longer shadows than usual. Her father’s armchair still sat in its corner, the antimacassar neatly arranged on its back, untouched since his death three years past. She had not moved it. She had not moved anything. The house was a museum to his memory, each object preserved in its appointed place.

But the piano bench was still warm from Vi’s slight weight. That, at least, had changed.

Ada stopped before the small side table where her father used to keep his lesson book. The leather binding was cracked along the spine, the pages yellowed and soft with age. She opened it without thinking, her fingers finding the familiar passages: scales, arpeggios, the progressive exercises he had drilled into her until her hands ached. Her own childish scrawl marked the margins—missed notes, counting errors, the sharp red pencil of correction. He had never praised. He had only corrected. And she had learned, lesson by lesson, that approval was something to be earned through precision, never given freely.

She closed the book. What would he have made of Vi Hollis? A child who arrived with no payment, no pedigree, no assurance of progress. A child whose father could barely keep the coal fire burning, whose mother was absent in the most final sense of the word. He would have turned her away at the door. The lesson, he would have said, begins with proper arrangement. Punctuality is the first lesson.

But punctuality had become complicated when the clocks themselves seemed to run differently.

A man’s shout, sharp as a stone on glass, cracked the terrace’s morning silence.

Ada’s hand tightened on the windowsill. The sound had come from somewhere down the street, past the bend where the houses gave way to the main road. She waited, listening, her body angled toward the source of the noise. For nine days, the only sounds had been footsteps, voices, the occasional bark of a dog, the clatter of a cart. The mechanical heartbeat of the trams had stopped, and in its absence, the street had learned a new rhythm. Slower. Quieter. Almost human.

The shout came again, closer now, words taking shape: “It’s over! The strike is over!”

Arthur Cullis. Ada recognised the voice even before she saw him round the corner, his cap in his hand, waving it like a flag of surrender or victory—impossible to tell which. His face was flushed, his shirt collar open at the throat despite the cold. Behind him, others were emerging from their houses. Doors opening along the terrace, faces appearing at windows, the whole street stirring like a sleeper roused too suddenly from a deep dream.

“They’ve called it off! The General Strike is done! Back to work, boys—back to work!”

Mrs. Patterson from Number Seven was the first to reach Arthur, her apron still tied around her waist, her face a mixture of disbelief and hope. “You’re certain, Arthur? They’ve truly called it off?”

“Official bulletin came through this morning.” Arthur Cullis waved a folded paper in the air, though Ada could not read the print from her vantage point. “Negotiations are at a most delicate juncture, they said, and then—just like that—it’s done. Nine days, Mrs. Patterson. Nine days and it’s finished.”

Ada did not move. Her reflection in the windowpane stared back at her, a woman in grey wool, her face unreadable even to herself. Outside, the news was spreading house by house, door to door. She could hear Mrs. Patterson exclaiming to her neighbour, could see the baker’s boy running up the street with a grin splitting his face. Relief and celebration were spilling through the terrace like water through a breach in a dam.

“Mrs. Cooper! Mrs. Cooper, wake your husband! It’s over!”

A door slammed somewhere down the street. Then another. The baker’s boy collided with a grey-haired man emerging from Number Nine, and for a moment they were both laughing, the man clapping the boy on the shoulder as though they shared some great joke. Children were appearing in doorways now, rubbing sleep from their eyes, drawn by the commotion. A dog barked from a front garden, adding its voice to the chorus.

And Ada felt nothing but a cold stone settling in her chest.

She turned from the window and walked to the piano. The instrument had belonged to her father, and before him, to his father. Three generations of music teachers, each one passing down the same principles: posture, precision, patience. The keys were ivory, yellowed slightly at the edges, and they held the memory of every finger that had touched them. Her father’s hands. Her own hands. And now, impossibly, the small, serious hands of Vi Hollis.

Ada sat on the bench and placed her fingers on middle C. The note was there, waiting. She had only to press.

But she did not. She sat instead in the silence and listened to the noise outside growing louder. Voices overlapping, laughing, calling back and forth. Someone was hammering on a door. A child was crying—or perhaps laughing, the sound distorted by distance and glass. The world was waking up. The long, strange pause was ending. Everything would go back to the way it had been before.

The trams would run again.

The thought struck Ada with such clarity that her hands went still on the keys. Of course. The trams. Frank Hollis’s trams. For nine days, they had been silent, and in that silence, the street had turned inward. Neighbours had become visible to one another. A child had crossed the threshold of a stranger’s house and learned to play a chord. And now the machinery of the world would grind back into motion, and all of it—the fragile, wordless connections that had formed in the quiet—would be swept away.

She thought of her father then, as she often did when the metronome sat idle. He had taught her to keep time, to measure her life in beats and bars, to trust the order of things. Punctuality is the first lesson, he used to say. The world runs on schedule. But the strike had broken the schedule. It had cracked the metronome’s glass face and let the outside noise flood in, and Ada had discovered, to her horror and wonder, that she did not entirely mind.

She had spoken to her neighbour last Tuesday. A brief exchange about the weather, the coal shortage, nothing of consequence. But she had spoken. She had stood at her gate and looked Mrs. Patterson in the eye and asked after her rheumatism. Such a small thing. Such an impossible thing, nine days ago.

And Vi. Vi, who had come to her door with empty hands and a serious face, asking to learn. Vi, who had played ‘Lightly Row’ this morning without a single mistake, her small fingers curved perfectly over the keys, her face lit with a quiet pride that Ada had never seen in her own mirror.

The distant, unmistakable clang of a tram bell cut through the terrace.

Ada’s fingers, resting on middle C, went perfectly still. The sound vibrated in the cold air of the room, a foreign intrusion that made the metronome on the piano’s edge seem like a relic from another age. One bell. Then another. Then a third, closer now, the metallic rhythm of the rails coming back to life after nine days of death.

She should have felt relief. The strike had been hard on everyone—the coal shortage, the disruption, the anxiety that hummed beneath every conversation. But all she felt was a hollow dread, a sense of something precious being eroded by the tide of returning normalcy.

She stood abruptly and went back to the window. The street was transformed. Where before there had been a handful of neighbours standing in doorways, now there was a crowd, gathering at the corner, craning their necks toward the sound of the approaching tram. Children were running. Dogs were barking. The whole world had come alive, and Ada stood apart from it, watching from behind the glass.

“Here it comes! The first one’s coming!”

The tram rounded the corner at the bottom of the street, its windows bright with morning light, its bell clanging in that familiar, forgettable rhythm. The driver wore a new uniform, or perhaps it was just that Ada had forgotten how they looked—crisp blue, the cap set at a proper angle. He looked exhausted, she thought. Or perhaps that was merely her imagination, projecting her own feeling onto a stranger’s face.

The passengers inside were leaning toward one another, talking, laughing, already forgetting the nine days of suspension that had briefly held their world apart. One woman near the front caught Ada’s eye—a young mother with a child on her lap, both of them waving at the crowd on the corner. The child was laughing, delighted by the novelty of it all, and the sound carried faintly through the glass.

Ada turned away. She could not watch.

She moved restlessly around the room, touching objects without purpose. The lid of the piano. The worn spine of her father’s lesson book. The cold metal of the metronome. Her hands were trembling slightly, though she could not have said why. The strike was over. The trams were running. This was what everyone had wanted, what the whole country had been waiting for.

And then she saw him.

Frank Hollis was walking up the street from the direction of the depot. His shoulders were slumped, his hands deep in the pockets of his worn coat. He walked slowly, deliberately, not joining the celebration at the corner but keeping to the edge of the pavement, his eyes fixed on the ground. Ada watched him pass Number Eight, Number Seven, Number Six. He did not look up. He did not look at her window. He walked as though he were carrying something heavy, something that bent his spine and pressed him toward the earth.

A woman from Number Four called out to him—Ada could not hear the words, but she saw the woman’s hand raised in greeting, a smile on her face. Frank raised one hand in response, a brief, mechanical gesture that contained no joy. His face, when he turned slightly toward the light, was grey with exhaustion. There was a hollow look around his eyes, a tightness in his jaw that spoke of something held carefully in check.

She had never seen him look so defeated. Not during the strike, when he had stood on the picket line with his head high. Not in the brief, awkward exchanges they had shared over the past weeks—the borrowed coal, the nods of acknowledgment, the unspoken understanding that passed between two people living alone in a world that had briefly stopped turning. Frank Hollis had always carried himself with a certain dignity, a quiet certainty that his cause was just.

Now he looked like a man who had won a battle and lost everything that mattered.

Ada’s hand pressed flat against the windowpane. The glass was cold against her palm, and she could feel the slight vibration of the tram bells ringing in the distance, the sound travelling through the frame of the house and into her bones. She wanted to open the window. To call out. To ask him what was wrong, what had happened, whether the victory they were all celebrating was worth the cost.

But she did not. The glass stayed between them, and Frank Hollis walked past her window without looking in.

He reached Number Twelve and stopped. For a moment, he stood at the gate, his hand on the latch, his back to the street. Ada could see the set of his shoulders, the way his coat hung loose on his frame, the grey at his temples that had not been there nine days ago. He looked, she thought, like a man returning to a house that was no longer his home.

The door opened before he could reach for it. A small figure appeared in the gap—Vi, her face pale, her wool cap still on her head. She stood in the doorway, watching her father approach, and Ada could see the question forming on her lips, the question she would not ask.

Frank Hollis stopped at the bottom of the front steps. He looked at his daughter, and for a moment, neither of them moved. The distance between them was perhaps three feet, but it might as well have been a mile. The celebration continued at the corner of the street, children still cheering the passing trams, neighbours still calling news back and forth, but in front of Number Twelve, there was only silence.

Then Vi stepped aside, holding the door open, and her father mounted the steps without a word. The door closed behind them, and Ada was left alone with the sound of the trams and the chatter of the celebrating street.

She turned away from the window and looked at her piano, at the empty bench, at the metronome that still had not been returned to its place. The room felt different now. Smaller. The silence that had filled it for nine days was gone, replaced by the noise of a world that had remembered how to turn.

She sat back down at the piano and lifted her hands to the keys. The chord that Vi had played that morning was gone, faded into the air, but she could still feel the shape of it in her fingers. A simple phrase. A minor key. A child’s hands learning to draw sound from silence.

Her father would have said it was time to return to proper order. The exercises first, then the scales, then the études. Discipline, structure, the comforting repetition of known patterns. But when she placed her fingers on the keys, she found herself playing something else entirely—a melody she had not thought of in years, a tune her father had played only late at night when he thought she was asleep. It had no proper structure, no discipline. It was only a feeling, given shape in sound.

Outside, another tram rattled past, its bell clanging, its windows bright with morning light. Ada watched it through the window, watched the passengers inside leaning toward one another, talking, laughing, already forgetting.

The metronome caught her eye. She reached for it, lifted it from the piano’s edge, and held it in her palm. The brass was cool against her skin. She wound the key slowly, feeling the mechanism tighten, the spring coiling inside. Then she set it on the mantelpiece, where it belonged, and started the pendulum swinging.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

The sound filled the room, measuring the silence that remained. But it was a different silence now. The quiet of the strike was gone, and in its place was something else—a hollow space where the old routines had not yet settled, where the fragile connections formed in suspension hung in the air, waiting to see if they would hold.

Ada thought of Vi, standing in the doorway, watching her father with that serious, knowing face. The girl would be sitting in their front room now, perhaps on the edge of a chair, perhaps still wearing her coat and cap, waiting for her father to speak. Or perhaps there would be no words at all. Perhaps they would sit together in silence, father and daughter, bound by a grief that had no name.

She thought of Frank Hollis walking past her window, his shoulders bent under the weight of a victory that felt like defeat. She thought of the coal she had given him, the transaction that had somehow become something more—a thread connecting their two houses, fragile as spider silk, impossible to see until the light caught it at just the right angle.

She thought of her father, long buried, and the rules he had taught her, and the way those rules had cracked open over the past nine days.

The trams were running again. The strike was over. The world was returning to its scheduled rhythm.

But something had changed. Ada could feel it in the stillness of her hands, in the way the metronome’s tick seemed to echo through an emptier room. She had opened a door that she could not close, and a child had walked through it, and nothing would be quite the same again.

She lifted her hands to the keys and began to play. Not the exercises her father had taught her, not the scales that Vi was learning, but something else—a melody that rose from somewhere deeper than memory, a phrase that had been waiting in the silence for nine days to be heard.

Outside, the street celebrated. The trams clanged. The world turned.

And Ada Penrose played on, alone in her front room, holding onto the last fragile echoes of a quiet that would never quite return.
