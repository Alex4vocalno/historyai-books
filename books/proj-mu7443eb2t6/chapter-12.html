Chapter 12

Delivering Kindness, Expecting Nothing

The metronome ticked alone in the grey front room, measuring an empty hour that stretched like a held breath. Ada Penrose sat at the piano bench, her back straight, her hands folded in her lap, watching the brass pendulum slice through the silence. The instrument had belonged to her father, and before him to his father—a lineage of precision passed down like bone china, too valuable to use and too sacred to discard. Outside, the tram strike had entered its second week, and the absence of wheels on rails had settled over the street like weather, a pressure that made the air feel thick, expectant. She had no pupils today. She had no pupils most days now. But the routine remained: the morning scale exercises, the afternoon repertoire, the evening’s quiet catalogue of dust motes drifting through the thin light that managed to pass the lace curtains.

She had just risen to adjust the metronome’s tempo when the knock came. Three precise raps against the front door, spaced with the deliberation of someone who had practiced the gesture. Ada’s hand paused above the brass stem. Visitors were rare. Pupils, rarer. And the hour was wrong for either—too late for a lesson, too early for the evening’s social calls, though she received none of those. The knock came again, softer this second time, as if the knocker had lost conviction between the first attempt and the follow-through.

Ada crossed the front room in seven measured steps, her slippers whispering against the worn carpet, and opened the door.

Vi Hollis stood on the threshold, her cheeks flushed from the cold, a wool cap pulled down to her ears. In her gloved hands she held an empty coal scuttle, its black iron body catching what little light the afternoon offered. The child’s face wore its usual seriousness—that earnest expression Ada had come to recognize over months of lessons, the look of someone much older peering out from behind young features. But today there was something else in that face, a tightness around the mouth that spoke of rehearsal, of words prepared and then second-guessed.

“Miss Penrose.” Vi’s voice was steady, but her grip on the scuttle’s handle shifted, her knuckles pale against the iron. “Father wonders if… if you might spare a scuttle of coal. Just ‘til the end of the week.”

The words hung in the cold air between them. Ada felt something shift in her chest—not sympathy, exactly, but a recognition, like seeing a familiar shape through frosted glass. She knew that tone. She had heard it before, in her own voice, on the rare occasions she had been forced to ask for anything. The careful neutrality, the way the request was framed as a small thing, a temporary thing, a thing that could be repaid. As if asking for less made the asking smaller.

“The end of the week,” Ada repeated, and the words came out flat, a statement rather than a question. She looked past Vi to the street, where the grey sky pressed down on the slate rooftops. At Number Twelve, the Hollis house, she could see the windows were dark. No lamplight.

No movement behind the glass. The strike vote had gone through—she had heard Frank Hollis’s raised voice through the thin walls the night before last, words she couldn’t quite make out but whose tone carried the weight of principle meeting necessity. She thought of her own coal scuttle, sitting in the narrow passage between her kitchen and the back door. She thought of the bag she had purchased three weeks ago, the weight of it against her hip as she’d carried it home from the merchant on Gloucester Road. How the lumps had clinked against each other like bones.

“He said to tell you it’s a loan,” Vi added, and now there was something else in her voice, a thread of desperation so thin it might have been imagination. “He’ll see you right when the vote settles.”

When the vote settles. As if votes settled anything. As if principle kept the cold at bay. Ada looked at the child’s face, at the way the wool cap had begun to slip back from her forehead, revealing hair that hadn’t been brushed that morning. She thought of her own father, of the way he had taught her to sit at the piano with her spine straight and her shoulders relaxed, the posture of someone who had earned the right to be there. She thought of the years after his death, when she had sat alone in this same room, playing scales to an empty house, maintaining the ritual because to abandon it would be to abandon him.

“There’s no need for that,” Ada said, and her voice surprised her with its steadiness. “Wait there. I’ll bring it round to the bunker.”

She closed the door before Vi could respond, before the child could offer thanks or protest or any of the other words that would make this moment more real than it needed to be. In the narrow passage, Ada lifted her own coal scuttle from its place by the back door. It was half-full. Perhaps less than half-full. She had been careful with it, rationing the lumps like coins, building fires only in the evenings and letting the mornings pass in a chill that seeped through the walls. Her father had taught her that, too—economy, the careful husbandry of resources. But looking at the black mound of coal, she could not calculate what a scuttle’s worth might mean for her own stores. She could only see Vi’s face, the flush of cold that wasn’t entirely from the weather.

The coal bunker sat at the back of the terrace, a brick-lined recess shared between the houses, accessible through a narrow passage that ran between Number Three and Number Four. Ada had never seen anyone else there when she’d gone to fetch coal for herself. It was a private space, or should have been. But as she rounded the corner, the scuttle heavy against her hip, she saw that she was not alone.

Frank Hollis stood by the bunker’s iron door, his hands hanging at his sides, his head slightly bowed. He wore his tram conductor’s uniform—the brass buttons gleaming in the grey light, the dark wool pressed and immaculate. Even now, even here, in this moment of asking, he had dressed as if for work. As if the uniform might preserve something that the empty scuttle had threatened to take away. He did not look up when her footsteps approached, though he must have heard them. He stood like a man waiting for a sentence he already knew.

“Miss Penrose.”

His voice was rough, stripped of the professional smoothness she had sometimes heard through the walls, the voice that called out stops and collected fares. This was a voice from somewhere deeper, or perhaps from somewhere that had been hollowed out.

“Mr. Hollis.” Ada stopped a pace away from him, the scuttle’s weight shifting in her grip. “It’s no trouble.”

She meant it as kindness, or something like it. But the words fell wrong, landing with a flatness that made her wince internally. It was trouble. They both knew it was trouble. The coal was not infinite, and the strike showed no signs of ending, and the winter was settling into the bricks of these houses like a tenant who refused to pay rent. To say otherwise was to pretend that this moment was ordinary, and it was not ordinary. It was the opposite of ordinary. It was the moment when the careful distance between neighbors collapsed into a single, weighted transaction.

Frank finally looked up, and she saw his face clearly for the first time. He was younger than she had expected—or perhaps he had seemed older in the glimpses she’d caught through windows, the figure moving behind glass. But here, in the cold light of the passage, she could see the lines around his eyes, the tension in his jaw. He looked like a man who had not slept well, or perhaps had not slept at all. His gaze met hers for a moment, then slid away, settling somewhere around her shoulder, on the brickwork behind her.

“The vote went through,” he said, and his voice had a rehearsed quality now, the words of someone who had practiced this explanation. “We hold the line. It’s… it’s a matter of principle.”

Principle. The word hung between them, heavy and hollow at once. Ada thought of her own principles—the rules she maintained in her teaching, the order she imposed on the front room, the metronome ticking through empty afternoons. She thought of the way principle could become a wall, something to hide behind, something that kept the world at bay. She thought of Vi’s face, the careful neutrality that had cracked just slightly at the edges.

“Of course,” she said, and she heard the tightness in her own voice, the restraint it took not to say more. Your principles won’t keep your daughter warm. She let the words die in her throat, unspoken. They would have been cruel, and she was not cruel. Or perhaps she was, but only in the ways that mattered—only in the small, daily cruelties of isolation, of keeping the world at arm’s length.

She extended the scuttle, the weight of it pulling at her wrist. Frank’s hands came up, slow and reluctant, and closed around the handle. His knuckles were white, the skin stretched tight over bone, and she saw that his fingernails were grimy, dark with something that might have been coal dust or grease or simply the residue of work that had stopped. For a moment, their hands occupied the same space, both holding the scuttle, neither quite willing to let go. Then Ada released her grip, and the weight transferred fully to him.

He staggered, almost imperceptibly, as if the coal were heavier than he had expected, or as if accepting it required more strength than he had left. The scuttle settled against his hip, and he stood there, holding it like something that might bite him.

“I’ll see this returned,” he said, and his voice was quieter now, the rehearsal abandoned. “You have my word.”

Ada looked at his face, at the way he still would not meet her eyes. She thought of the words she could say—reassurances, protestations, the social niceties that might smooth this moment into something bearable. But she could not find them. The silence stretched, filled with the distant sound of a bird calling somewhere beyond the terrace, the faint hum of wind through the narrow passage.

“When you’re able,” she said, finally, and the words were an offering and a dismissal at once. Take it. Just take it and let us both pretend this is simple.

Frank nodded, a single, sharp movement. He shifted the scuttle in his grip, adjusting to its weight, and for a moment his eyes flickered to her face. She saw something there—gratitude, perhaps, or shame, or some complicated alloy of both. Then he turned, and his footsteps retreated down the passage, the coal clinking softly in the scuttle as he walked. She watched him go, the straight line of his back, the gleam of brass buttons against dark wool. He did not look back.

Ada stood alone in the passage, her hands empty at her sides. The air smelled of damp brick and something older, the accumulated years of coal dust and rain and labor that had settled into these walls. She looked at the bunker’s iron door, at the dark opening where her own reserves waited in their diminishing pile. She would need to buy more soon, if the strike continued. She would need to count what remained and calculate how long it might last. But that was a problem for another moment, another hour. Right now, there was only the silence, and the cold, and the space where the scuttle had been.

She turned and walked back to her house, her footsteps measured on the worn stone. In the front room, the metronome still ticked, counting out its patient rhythm, indifferent to what had passed. Ada sat down at the piano bench and looked at the keys, at the ivory and ebony that her teacher had touched before her, that his father had touched before that. She thought of Vi’s hands on these same keys, the careful, halting scales that marked her early lessons. She thought of principle, and what it cost, and who paid the price.

The metronome ticked. The grey light shifted. Somewhere beyond the walls, in a house with darkened windows, a child would be warm tonight, or warmer than she might have been. And Ada Penrose sat in the silence of her front room, her hands folded in her lap, and waited for the hour to pass.

She did not move. She did not play. She sat in the precise posture her father had taught her, spine straight, shoulders relaxed, and let the silence wash over her like water, like weather, like the held breath of a world waiting to exhale.

Two days passed before the knock came.

Ada had been expecting it—not hoping, never that, but the ledger in her mind had been waiting for the entry to balance. She rose from the piano bench where she had been drilling finger exercises into the reluctant hands of a boy from the next street over, and moved to the door with the measured steps of someone who knew precisely what stood on the other side.

Frank Hollis held the empty scuttle in both hands like an offering, or perhaps a confession. His daughter hovered behind him on the path, her face pale above the collar of her coat, watching the exchange with the particular hunger of a child who had learned that warmth came from transactions between adults.

“Miss Penrose.” Frank’s voice was rough, scraped raw by gratitude he did not know how to carry. “I wanted to return this. And to—“

“There’s no need.” Ada kept her tone even, her hands clasped at her waist. The scuttle stood between them, its black iron mouth gaping like a question neither wanted to ask aloud.

“Please.” He stepped forward, and she had to retreat to allow him across her threshold. The front room swallowed him, made him smaller somehow, as if the precise arrangement of furniture and the gleaming surface of the piano measured him and found him wanting. He set the scuttle by the door with a soft clink of metal against wood.

“Your daughter’s lesson is tomorrow at four,” Ada said. “Punctuality—“

“Is the first lesson. She told me.” Frank’s mouth twisted—not a smile, something more complicated. “She practices on the kitchen table. Taps out the notes. Her mother would have—“ He stopped. The silence that followed was thick with old grief, fresh loss, the particular ache of a household learning to breathe around an empty chair.

Ada did not fill it. She had learned early that silence was not an emptiness to be patched but a space to be inhabited, and she stood within it now, waiting.

“Thank you,” Frank said finally, the words stiff and foreign in his mouth. “For the coal. For the lessons. Vi says you’re patient with her.”

“I am thorough.”

“That too.” He looked at the piano, at the metronome sitting silent on its lid, at the stacks of sheet music ordered precisely by composer and difficulty. “Vi seems to have a gift for it. Despite everything.”

Another offering, another piece of information handed across the invisible line that separated teacher from neighbor, professional distance from the tangled business of living. Ada received it without acknowledgment, without the small kindness of recognition that might invite more.

“Tomorrow at four,” she repeated.

Frank nodded, accepting the dismissal. At the door, he paused, his hand on the frame, his back to her. “If there’s ever anything I can—“

“There isn’t.”

He left. The door closed behind him with a soft click, and Ada stood in her front room, alone with the empty scuttle and the weight of obligation that had just settled into the space between them, heavy and irrevocable as the coal that had burned away to warmth in another house.
