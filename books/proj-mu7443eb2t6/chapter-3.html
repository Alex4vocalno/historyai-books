Chapter 3

The Silent Piano

The metronome on the piano was perfectly aligned with the edge of the sheet music, its brass-colored pendulum locked at an angle that suggested imminent motion but delivered none. Ada Penrose adjusted it a quarter-turn to the left, then back again, satisfied that the base sat flush against the mahogany lip of the music desk. The sheet music—Czerny, Op. 599, No. 12—was centered precisely, its pages smoothed flat, the spine creased just enough to hold it open without resistance. She had placed it there at ten minutes past two. It was now twenty to three.

The front room was ready. She had seen to that with the same attentiveness she brought to every Tuesday—every day, in fact, for the schedule permitted no exceptions. The stool had been pulled to the optimal distance from the keyboard, its height adjusted so that a child of ten or eleven might sit with forearms level. A clean handkerchief lay folded on the corner of the stool, in case of cold fingers or a runny nose. The room smelled of furniture polish and the faintest trace of lavender from the sachet she kept in the piano bench, and the light through the lace curtains fell in a pale, even wash across the ivory keys, catching the slight yellowing of the natural notes where decades of fingers had worn the veneer.

Ada smoothed the front of her skirt. She pressed her left thumb into the center of her right palm, a gesture so habitual she rarely noticed it—the small callus there, built up over years of demonstrating scales, received the pressure like a familiar handshake. The clock on the mantelpiece marked the seconds with a thin, persistent tick that she had long ago stopped hearing, so embedded was it in the architecture of her waiting.

Half past two was the time. Half past two, every Tuesday, for the Edworthy girl. Before the Edworthy girl, on Mondays and Wednesdays, the Poole boy at four o’clock. On Thursdays, the Collier twins, who were not twins but sisters born eleven months apart and shared a lesson out of their mother’s thriftiness. On Fridays, the Mackenzie boy, who was gifted and careless in equal measure and whose fingering Ada corrected with a patience she did not feel. Saturday mornings were reserved for the older pupils, the ones who practised seriously. Sunday was the Lord’s day and Ada’s day of rest, though she had not attended services in two years and her rest consisted of dusting the piano and polishing the keys and ensuring the room was ready for Monday.

One must keep to the schedule, you see. It’s what provides the structure.

The clock ticked. The metronome did not. Ada looked at it—the small wooden pyramid with its brass scale and its sliding weight, set to sixty beats per minute, which was Andante, which was walking pace, which was the tempo at which a child of ten or eleven might reasonably be expected to play Czerny’s Op. 599, No. 12. She had not started it. She would start it when the Edworthy girl sat down, when the small hands were positioned, when the first tentative phrase was ready to be shaped. The metronome was not about hurry. It was about agreement. A pact between teacher and pupil that time would be measured, that each note would occupy its rightful portion, that music was not merely sound but sound placed within boundaries.

She smoothed her skirt again. The gesture was automatic, her hands requiring occupation. The fabric was a dark grey wool, neat but slightly out of fashion—the cut was two seasons old, perhaps three, the kind of thing one might purchase in the reduced section of Mrs. Alcott’s drapery on Park Street and not feel ashamed of, because the quality of the material compensated for whatever the cut lacked in currency. Ada dressed as though she were a guest in her own time, which was to say she dressed with a respect for formality that the world had begun to relax. Gloves on the hallway table. A hat on the hook by the door. Skirts smoothed, collar straight, shoes cleaned on Saturday evenings even when she had no intention of leaving the house on Sunday.

Twenty to three. The Edworthy girl was late. This was not unprecedented. Children were late. The Edworthy girl’s mother was a seamstress who worked from home, and there were mornings when the pressing of a hem took precedence over the fetching of a child from school, and the girl would arrive flustered and apologetic at five to three, or three o’clock precisely, and Ada would receive her with the same composed expression and the same adjusted lesson plan, shorter by ten minutes but no less thorough. One adapted. The schedule was a structure, not a cage.

But the silence in the front room had begun to press against the walls in a way that was not altogether familiar. It was not merely the silence of an empty room—Ada knew that silence intimately, had lived with it for years, had arranged her life around it the way one arranges furniture around a piano. This was a different silence. It carried the weight of something outside, something beyond the lace curtains and the polished door and the hallway with its umbrella stand and its coat hooks. It carried, she realized, the silence of the street.

The trams had stopped. She knew this, of course. She had known it since that morning, when the 9: 05 failed to sound its bell and the absence of the familiar grinding and clattering on the track at the end of the road had registered as a kind of atmospheric shift, a change in pressure. The trams stopped and the street went quiet, and the quiet was different from the ordinary quiet of a residential terrace on a weekday afternoon. It was the quiet of suspension. Of something held and not released.

Ada crossed to the window. She did not open the curtains—she had drawn them to their usual width, a gesture of professional readiness that permitted light while maintaining a certain privacy, a certain containment of the room’s purpose—but she stood close to the glass and looked through the lace at the street. It was empty. Not merely quiet, but empty in a way that suggested not the momentary pause between passers-by but the genuine, protracted absence of traffic. No carts. No bicycles. No women with shopping bags. No children returning from school, because the schools had not closed but the parents were not sending, and the children who might have walked this street to reach the Edworthy house or the Poole house or the Collier house were elsewhere, kept close, kept in, kept warm if there was warmth to be had.

She pressed her thumb into her palm. The callus received the pressure.

A voice from the street. A man’s voice, raised not in anger but in the particular pitch of public address, the kind of voice that carries because it expects to carry, because it has been trained on picket lines and in meeting halls. She could not make out the words. The glass reduced them to a rhythm, a cadence, a pattern of emphasis and pause that was almost musical in its structure. She stood very still and listened, and the voice rose and fell, and she caught fragments—“the line” and “hold” and something that might have been “fair” or “fare” or simply a sound shaped by distance and glass into something she could not quite possess.

She knew the voice. Not well. Not in the way one knows the voice of a friend or a relation. She knew it the way one knows the sounds of one’s street—the particular cough of the man in number seven, the specific pitch of the baker’s boy’s whistle, the tread of the postman’s boots on the step at nine o’clock. This voice belonged to the tram conductor.

The one who worked the route that passed the end of her road. She had seen him from the window on countless mornings, standing on the platform of the green-and-cream tramcar, his cap set squarely on his head, his manner that of a man who took his position seriously and expected the tram to do the same. She did not know his name. She had never spoken to him.

But she knew his voice, the way one knows the sound of a bell that marks the hour—without intention, without intimacy, simply through the accumulated repetition of proximity.

The voice faded. The street resumed its silence. Ada stood at the window and the lace pressed its pattern against her forehead where she had leaned too close, and she could feel the cold of the glass through the thin cotton, the particular cold of October in a city built on water and stone.

She turned back to the room. The piano stood where it had always stood, against the interior wall, its fallboard open, its keys exposed in their alternating rows of white and yellowed, the dark wood of its case reflecting the afternoon light in a deep, liquid sheen that was almost but not quite a mirror. It was a Broadwood. It had been her father’s. It had stood in the shop on Park Street where he sold sheet music and instruments and reeds and rosin and all the small, necessary apparatus of musical life, and when the shop closed—when the debts that had been invisible until they were not became visible and insurmountable—the piano had been brought here, to the terrace house, and placed against the interior wall where the damp could not reach it and the sunlight could not fade its keys.

Her father had played it. Every evening, after the shop closed, he had sat and played—not the exercises, not the Czerny, not the little didactic pieces he assigned to his pupils, but other things. Schubert. Chopin. Sometimes hymns, quietly, as though he were at prayer. The piano had been an instrument then. It had been alive under his hands, responsive, speaking in a voice that was his and not his, the voice of the composer filtered through his particular sensitivity, his touch, his breath.

He had died on a Thursday. Ada had found him at the kitchen table on the Friday morning, sitting upright, his hands folded on the newspaper he had been reading, his face turned slightly toward the window as though he had been watching something outside. The coroner said it was his heart. Ada had thought, irrationally, that it was the debts—that the invisible weight of what he owed had pressed down on the organ until it simply ceased, like a metronome wound past its tolerance.

She had not played since. That was four years ago. The piano had become something else—a surface for sheet music, a stand for the metronome, an instrument she touched only to teach, and in teaching to keep her father’s practice alive in the only way she could bear. She did not play for herself. She did not play for pleasure. She played the notes the pupils needed to hear, the scales, the arpeggios, the simple melodies of instruction, and when the lesson was over she closed the fallboard and the piano returned to its silence, to its vigil, to its function as a reliquary for a man who had made music and could not make it any longer.

Three o’clock. The Edworthy girl had not come. The Poole boy was not expected until four, but Ada knew, with the certainty that arrives not through logic but through the body’s own intelligence, that the Poole boy would not come either. Nor the Collier twins. Nor the Mackenzie boy. No one would come. The street was empty and the trams were stopped and the man’s voice had faded and the clock ticked and the metronome stood silent on the music desk, its pendulum locked, its weight set to sixty beats per minute, which was Andante, which was walking pace, which was the pace of a world that had ceased to walk.

Her income had stopped. The realisation arrived not as a thought but as a physical sensation—a tightening in her chest, a coldness in her fingers, a sudden acute awareness of the weight of the house around her, its walls and floors and roof and the cost of each. The Edworthy girl paid two shillings a lesson.

The Poole boy paid half a crown. The Collier twins’ mother paid three shillings for the pair, which was generous given that she received, in effect, two lessons for the price of one and a half. The Mackenzie boy’s father paid three and six. On Saturday, the older pupils paid varying amounts, some more, some less, all in cash, all placed on the hallway table with a murmured courtesy that Ada received with a nod and a folded receipt. It was not a fortune. It was not even, by most measures, a living. But it was the structure, and the structure was what she had, and the structure had just stopped.

She sat on the piano stool. The leather was cool through her skirt. She placed her feet flat on the floor, adjusted her posture, squared her shoulders to the keyboard. The keys lay before her in their patient row, white and dark, waiting with the particular patience of inanimate objects that have been designed for a purpose they cannot fulfil alone. The Czerny was open on the music desk. No. 12. The first phrase. A simple melody in C major, the key of beginning, the key every child learns first because it asks only for the white notes and the white notes are where the hands naturally fall.

Ada lifted her hands. She positioned them over the keyboard—right thumb over middle C, left fifth finger over the C an octave below, the posture of a woman about to play, about to press the keys and release the sound that had been locked in this room for four years, the sound of her father’s piano, the sound that was not instruction but music, not duty but desire. Her fingers curled. She felt the familiar tension in her forearms, the preparation of the muscles that had been trained to this since childhood, the readiness that preceded the act.

She could not press them down.

Her fingers remained curled, trembling slightly, an inch above the ivory. She could feel the cool air between her skin and the keys, the small space that separated intention from action, and the space was not empty—it was full, full of something dense and resistant, something that held her hands where they were as effectively as if she had been bound. She breathed. She looked at the keys. She looked at the dark wood of the case and saw her reflection there—blurred, indistinct, a shape that might have been anyone, a woman or a ghost or a memory, something that belonged to the piano and not to herself.

The metronome stood silent. The clock ticked. The afternoon light had shifted, moving across the keys, and the shadows of the lace curtains lay across the ivory in a pattern that looked, from this angle, like bars.

Ada lowered her hands to her lap. She sat for a long moment, looking at the keyboard, at the Czerny, at the metronome with its brass scale and its sliding weight set to sixty. Then she reached forward and closed the fallboard, covering the keys with the dark wood panel, and the piano was once again a surface, a shape, a piece of furniture in a silent room.

She stood. She smoothed her skirt. She pressed her thumb into her palm. The clock ticked on the mantelpiece and the metronome did not tick on the piano, and the room was exactly as it had been before she sat down, as though nothing had happened, as though nothing had been attempted or failed, as though the silence had simply absorbed her effort and returned her to it, unchanged.

The shutters were not closed. That was the last thing she noticed before she left the room—the shutters on the front window were not closed, the lace curtains hung in their usual arrangement, and the street was visible through them, empty and grey and still. She would not close them. Not yet. Not today. She would leave the window open to the light, to the street, to the possibility that tomorrow might be different, that Tuesday might resume its shape, that the Edworthy girl might arrive at half past two with flushed cheeks and cold fingers and a ready smile, and the metronome would start, and the piano would speak, and the structure would hold.

But she knew, standing in the doorway of the silent front room, that it would not. Not tomorrow. Not this week. Perhaps not for some time. The trams were stopped and the street was empty and the man’s voice had faded and the room was a shrine and she was its keeper, and the keys were covered and the metronome was still and her hands hung at her sides, empty, the callus on her palm receiving nothing but the pressure of her own thumb.

She closed the door softly behind her.
