Chapter 5

The Unpaid Request

The silence in the front room had a quality that Ada could almost taste, like the stillness before a thunderstorm breaks, though no storm would come today. The General Strike had entered its third day, and with it the ordinary rhythms of the street had suspended. No milk cart rattled past. No newspaper boy called the headlines. Even the distant hum of the trams, that reliable pulse beneath all her mornings, had ceased. Ada sat beside the window where the light fell grey and diffuse, her hands folded in her lap, and watched the empty pavement.

A woman from Number 9 passed with a shopping basket, her steps quick and purposeful, but she did not look up. No one looked up anymore. The previous day’s brief acknowledgment through glass, that silent charged glance with the man from Number 3, had left something unresolved in the air between the houses, a question neither had spoken. Ada had turned away first. She was not certain why.

The front room held its breath. The piano stood against the inner wall, its black surface catching what little light the clouds allowed. Ada kept it clean, polishing the wood each morning, though the keys themselves she had not touched for her own playing in years. She did not play. She only taught. The distinction mattered. Pupils would come and go, their uncertain scales and halting arpeggios filling the space with purpose, and when they left, the silence returned, sacred and complete.

One must keep to the schedule, she thought. It’s what provides the structure. Nine o’clock, Mrs. Whitmore’s daughter. Ten, the Hartley boy. Eleven, a gap for preparation. But no one had come yesterday, and no one would come today. The strike had stopped more than the trams. It had stopped the entire machinery of obligation that held her days together.

She rose and moved to the coal scuttle by the hearth. Full. She had ordered extra before the strike was announced, anticipating disruption, and now the black heap rose above the rim, untouched. The fire had burned low through the night, and she knelt to feed it, the familiar motions steadying her. Coal clicked against coal. The flames caught. Outside, a bird called once and fell silent.

The knock came at half past ten.

It was too soft for the postman. Too irregular. Ada’s hands stilled on her skirt. She waited, thinking perhaps she had imagined it, that the silence had begun to play tricks. Then it came again, three taps, hesitant but deliberate.

She was not expecting anyone. The strike had emptied her calendar, suspended the appointments that gave each day its spine. Pupils did not call without notice. The grocer’s boy knew better than to knock at this hour. Ada straightened and moved toward the hall, her steps measured, her hand rising to smooth her hair before she caught herself doing it.

The front door was a heavy thing, painted a dark green that had faded to near-black in the recesses of the panels. Ada opened it halfway and stopped.

A child stood on the step. A girl, perhaps ten or eleven, with dark hair pulled back in a practical plait and a face that held an expression Ada recognized from the mirror: the careful blankness of someone trying not to show what they felt. The girl’s coat was thin, buttoned to the chin, and her shoes, scuffed at the toes, were planted firmly on the mat.

Ada knew this child. Vi Hollis, from Number 3. The house at the end of the terrace, the one with the peeling paint around the window frames. The one where the father worked at the depot and the mother had been absent for some time. Ada had noticed the missing figure months ago, the way the morning routines at Number 3 had shifted, a man and a girl emerging where once a woman had walked between them.

“Miss Penrose.” The girl’s voice was clear, pitched to carry across the threshold. She had rehearsed this, Ada thought. The clarity gave her away. “Can I learn the piano?”

The question hung in the cold air between them. Ada’s hand tightened on the door frame. She felt the paint rough under her fingertips, the chill from the street seeping past the gap.

“I beg your pardon?”

“The piano.” Vi did not flinch. Her eyes, dark and watchful, stayed fixed on Ada’s face. “I want to learn. Please.”

Ada’s mind moved through its familiar channels. The schedule. The fee. The requirement of a referral. The structure that had governed her teaching for years. “Lessons are by arrangement,” she said. “I would need to speak with your parents.”

“My dad’s at the depot.” The girl’s chin lifted slightly. “He’s on the committee. For the strike.”

Of course he was. Frank Hollis. Ada had seen him in passing over the years, a man with the kind of face that kept its own counsel, moving through the street with a purposeful stride that suggested he was always on his way somewhere else. She had seen him yesterday, through this same window, their eyes meeting for a moment before she had turned away.

“Then perhaps when circumstances permit,” Ada said. The words came automatically, the polite refusal dressed in the language of postponement. She had used them before, with other children, other parents. The structure held.

Vi Hollis did not move. She stood on the step with her hands at her sides, her thin coat already gathering the damp of the morning air, and watched Ada with an expression that suggested she had expected this answer and had prepared for it.

“I haven’t got any money,” she said.

The admission landed between them without ceremony. No apology. No hesitation. Just the fact, stated with the same clarity as her request.

Ada felt something shift in her chest, not a flutter but a weight settling. The girl’s words had cracked something open, a small fissure in the wall she maintained. “I’m afraid that presents a difficulty,” she said. The sentence sounded hollow even as she spoke it. “Lessons require payment. That is the arrangement.”

“I know.” Vi’s voice remained steady, but her hands had clenched at her sides, small fists forming and relaxing. “My dad’s got no coin for lessons. But I want to learn.”

Ada’s gaze moved past the girl to the street behind her. The empty pavement. The silent houses. The strike had drawn a line through every ordinary transaction, and here was a child standing on her doorstep asking to cross another kind of line entirely.

“There are other teachers,” Ada said. “Perhaps in better times—“

“You listen different.” The girl’s voice dropped, losing some of its rehearsed quality. “Than he does. My dad. You hear the ends of things.”

Ada did not know what to do with this. The observation felt too close, too accurate. She did listen. It was what made her teaching effective, or so she had always told herself. She heard where a pupil’s hesitation lived, where the uncertainty originated. But hearing had never extended beyond the front room. It had never required her to step outside the structure she had built.

“I couldn’t possibly,” she said. The words came from somewhere deeper than habit. “It wouldn’t be correct.”

Vi Hollis stood on the step a moment longer, her face held in that same careful blankness. Then she nodded, once, as if Ada had confirmed something she had already known. “I’ll come back tomorrow,” she said. “For your answer.”

The girl turned before Ada could respond. She walked down the path with her shoulders squared, her thin coat bright against the grey morning, and passed through the gate without looking back. Ada watched her cross the shared space between the houses, her small figure moving past the low wall that separated Number 5 from Number 4, past the hedge that marked Number 3, all the way to Number 12 where the house stood with its peeling paint and its silent windows.

The door opened before Vi reached it. A man’s figure filled the frame briefly, then stepped aside to let her in. Frank Hollis. Even at this distance, Ada could see the set of his shoulders, the way his hand rested on the door frame as his daughter passed him. He did not look toward Ada’s house. The door closed.

Ada remained in the doorway, the cold air pressing against her. She became aware of the chill on her face, the ache in her fingers where they gripped the frame. The street held its silence, but it was different now. It had been breached.

She closed the door slowly, letting the latch catch with a soft click, and turned back to the hall. The house stretched around her, familiar and strange at once. The stairs rose to the upper floors where her mother’s study sat untouched, her papers still arranged on the desk as she had left them. The kitchen waited at the back, the kettle cold on the hob. And the front room, the piano room, held its breath.

Ada walked into the room and stopped.

The piano stood against the wall, its black surface dulled by the dust that had settled since she had prepared the room for this girl. She had lifted the fallboard that day, aligned the sheet music, set the metronome on the lid—only to let the appointed time pass in silence. To play would be to admit something. She was not certain what.

The metronome still sat where she had placed it. She had carried it with her for years, a small brass weight she could not set down, though she never spoke of where it had come from. When the memory rose, unbidden, she pushed it down.

She moved to the instrument and stood before it. The dust lay on the lid, disturbed now where she had readied the room, where her hands had rested on the keys without pressing them.

Her hand rose without her willing it. One finger extended, and she traced a single line through the grey coating. The gesture felt like an admission. Beneath the dust, the black surface gleamed, polished and whole, waiting.

The coal scuttle by the hearth caught her eye. Full. Overfull. While at Number 3, Frank Hollis and his daughter made do with whatever they had. Ada had heard the talk in the shops before the strike, the murmured calculations about who could afford to stockpile and who could not. The Hollises had no reserves. The father worked at the depot, and the depot was closed, and the strike that had stopped the trams had stopped his wages as well.

Tomorrow, the girl would return. Tomorrow, Ada would have to give an answer. The structure demanded it. The schedule required it. But for the first time in years, the rules she had built around herself felt like something other than protection. They felt like walls.

She looked at the single line her finger had drawn through the dust. A clear bright streak in the grey. An opening.

Outside, the street remained silent. No trams. No carts. No voices. But somewhere in that silence, a child was waiting for an answer, and Ada Penrose, who had built her life on the certainty of never having to give one, found herself without a rule that could save her.

The fire crackled in the hearth. The clock on the mantle ticked through its familiar rhythm. And the piano stood in its corner, patient and unspeaking, its keys hidden beneath the fallboard, its voice held in reserve for a hand that might never touch it.

Ada withdrew her hand. She smoothed her skirt, a gesture that had become automatic over the years, and turned away from the instrument. But she did not leave the room. Instead, she moved to the window and stood where she had stood that morning, looking out at the empty street.

The light was changing. The clouds had thickened, and the grey had deepened toward something heavier. Rain, perhaps, before the day was done. The kind of rain that would settle the dust and fill the gutters and keep everyone indoors behind their closed doors.

At Number 3, a curtain shifted in the upper window. A face appeared briefly, a child’s face, looking toward Ada’s house. Vi Hollis, watching. Waiting. Then the curtain fell back, and the window returned to its blankness.

Ada’s hand found the windowsill. Her fingers curled against the wood, feeling the grain, the slight warmth where the weak sun had touched it. The structure was breached. The question was asked. And tomorrow, when the knock came again, she would have to decide whether to rebuild the wall or let it fall.

The clock ticked. The fire crackled. The dust settled slowly back onto the piano lid, drifting into the line she had drawn, beginning the slow work of filling it in.

But not yet. Not entirely. The streak remained, faint but visible, a mark in the grey. An answer that was not yet an answer. A question that was not yet a question.

Ada Penrose stood at her window and watched the empty street, and waited for tomorrow.
