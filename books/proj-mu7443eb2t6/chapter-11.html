Chapter 11

Sparks over Coals

────────────────────────────────────────

The hearth in Number Twelve stood like a mouth that had forgotten how to speak, the iron grate holding only a mound of grey ash that crumbled at the touch. Frank Hollis knelt before it with the poker in his hand, stirring the remains of yesterday’s fire as if some ember might have survived the night, hiding beneath the white powder like a small animal in snow. But the ash gave up nothing. It fell away from the iron tines in soft clumps and settled back into the shape of failure.

The lesson had ended an hour ago. He had heard Vi’s footsteps on the pavement, the front door opening and closing, and then the quiet that filled the house when she returned from Number Three. She had said nothing about the cold in Miss Penrose’s front room, and he had not asked. The never knowing was its own kind of warmth, he supposed. A man could almost believe his daughter was fine if she did not tell him otherwise.

He set the poker against the hearth and stood. His knees cracked in the silence, a sound like a small branch breaking. The front room smelled of old fabric and the ghost of coal smoke, a scent that used to mean comfort and now reminded him only of what was missing. Through the window, the street lay still. The tram strike had entered its second week, and the silence of the rails pressed against the rooftops like weather. No hum of electricity, no clatter of wheels on tracks. Just the wind moving between the houses, carrying the cold from one threshold to the next.

Frank crossed to the coal scuttle by the hearth. The brass band around its rim had tarnished to a dull green. He lifted it, and the lightness of it told him everything before he looked inside. Empty. A few fragments of coal dust at the bottom, black sand that would not burn.

He set it down again. The sound was louder than he intended, a hollow clang that seemed to reproach him. Vi was upstairs in her room, or so he had assumed. But when he turned, he saw her on the sofa by the window, wrapped in a grey wool blanket she must have pulled from her bed. A book lay open in her lap, and she was staring at the page without turning it.

“You’re back early,” he said.

She looked up. Her face was pale in the grey light, the kind of pale that came from cold rather than fear or illness. “Miss Penrose finished the lesson.”

“Did she.” He did not make it a question.

Vi pulled the blanket tighter around her shoulders. “She showed me the scales again. The proper fingering.”

“Good.”

He walked to the window and stood with his back to her, looking out at the street. The houses across the way were dark, their windows like sleeping eyes. No. 3, where Miss Penrose lived, showed no light either, but he knew she was there. She was always there, it seemed, a fixed point in the neighbourhood’s geography. The piano teacher who had never married, who kept to her rooms and her rituals. He had sent Vi to her because the girl had asked, and because asking for lessons was easier than asking for other things.

“She has a metronome,” Vi said. “It ticks the whole time. Even when she’s not playing.”

Frank did not turn. “That’s what metronomes do.”

“I know. But it sounds different than I thought it would. Like it’s counting something other than time.”

He had no answer for that. The cold was moving through the floorboards now, seeping up through the soles of his boots. He could feel it in his joints, a dull ache that had become familiar over the past week. The radiator in the hall had been silent for days, its pipes cold as stone. He had thought they might hold out until the strike ended, until the wages came through and the coal merchant would deliver again. But the strike showed no sign of ending. The men at the depot had voted to hold the line, and he had voted with them. It was the right thing. It was what a man did when his neighbours stood together.

But the right thing did not warm the hearth.

He heard Vi shift on the sofa. The blanket rustled against the upholstery, and then came a sound that cut through the quiet, a small chattering that she tried to muffle with her hand. Her teeth. The cold had gotten into her, past the blanket, past the cardigan she wore beneath it, past the resolve that children learned when they did not want to worry their parents.

Frank turned. Vi sat with the book still in her lap, her jaw clenched to stop the shaking. But her shoulders betrayed her, trembling in small spasms that made the pages of the book flutter. She was trying to read. She was trying to be a child who could sit quietly while her father failed to keep her warm.

“Vi.”

She looked at him. Her eyes were dry, but there was something in them that he could not name. Not accusation, not quite. Something softer and harder at once.

“I’m fine,” she said. “It’s not that cold.”

The lie hung between them. He could see the gooseflesh on her wrists, the blue tinge at the tips of her fingers. The room was freezing. It had been freezing when he woke, and it would be colder still by nightfall. And tomorrow would be colder yet.

He crossed to the sofa and crouched in front of her, his knees protesting again. Up close, he could see the tremor in her hands as she held the book. The pages shook, the words blurring with each shiver.

“Put the book down,” he said.

“I want to finish the chapter.”

“Vi.”

She looked at him, and something in his voice must have reached her. She closed the book and set it on the cushion beside her. Her hands lay in her lap, pale and thin, the nails bitten down to the quick. She was growing, he thought. Growing faster than the food he could put on the table, faster than the coal he could put in the grate. She was hungry in ways he could not name, and the cold was only the part he could see.

“Are we going to be all right?” she asked.

It was not the question he expected. He had thought she would ask for more blankets, or suggest they go to the kitchen where the stove might still hold some warmth from last night’s supper. But this question was larger, and he did not have an answer that would satisfy her.

“We’ll manage,” he said. “We always do.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

He looked at her. The grey light from the window fell across her face, catching the sharp line of her jaw, the way her hair lay flat against her forehead because she had not the warmth to spare for vanity. She was watching him the way she watched Miss Penrose during lessons, waiting for the instruction that would make sense of the music.

“The strike will end,” he said. “The trams will run again. We’ll have coal.”

“When?”

“Soon.”

“You said that last week.”

He had no answer. The words he might have spoken, the reassurances and promises, all caught in his throat like ash. He had voted to hold the line. He had stood with the men at the depot, and they had all agreed that the wages were not enough, that the hours were too long, that the company had to be made to see reason. And he still believed it. But belief did not warm a child’s hands.

Vi pulled her cardigan tighter, crossing her arms over her chest. The blanket slipped from one shoulder, and she did not bother to adjust it. She was still shivering, small tremors that moved through her like distant trains, invisible and relentless.

“The cold isn’t so bad,” she said. “I’ve been colder.”

“When?”

She did not answer. She did not need to. They both remembered the winter after she had gone, when the house had been so quiet that the cold had seemed like a presence, a fourth person in the rooms, sitting in the chairs and lying in the beds, taking up space that should have been filled with breath and warmth.

Frank rose. His legs were stiff, and he had to brace a hand on the arm of the sofa to steady himself. He walked to the hearth and picked up the coal scuttle. The emptiness of it weighed more than coal ever had.

“Get your coat,” he said.

Vi looked up. “Why?”

“We need coal.”

“But the merchant—“

“Not the merchant.” He turned to face her. “Miss Penrose.”

Vi stared at him. He saw the confusion in her face, and then the understanding, and then something that might have been pity. She knew what it meant, asking the neighbour for help. She knew what it cost.

“Dad—“

“Get your coat.” His voice was harder than he intended, and he softened it. “Please.”

She rose from the sofa, letting the blanket fall. She moved slowly, her body still stiff with cold, and he watched her cross to the hook by the door where her coat hung. She pulled it on, buttoning it with fingers that fumbled at the buttons. Then she reached for her wool cap and tugged it over her hair.

Frank crossed to her and held out the scuttle. It was an ugly thing, battered brass and black iron, the kind of object that announced need before a word was spoken. She took it from him, and he saw how light it was in her hands, how the emptiness of it made her seem smaller.

“You know where Number Three is,” he said.

“She’s my teacher. I know where she lives.”

He nodded. “Ask her for a scuttle of coal. Tell her—tell her we’ll replace it when we can.”

Vi looked down at the empty scuttle. Then she looked up at him, and her eyes were steady. “She’ll say yes.”

“She might.”

“She will. She’s not—she’s not like that.”

He did not ask what she meant. He did not ask what Miss Penrose was like, what Vi had seen in the front room during her lessons, what had passed between teacher and pupil in the hours he had not witnessed. He only nodded again and reached past her to open the door.

The cold from the street rushed in, a physical presence that seemed to push against them. Vi stepped out onto the threshold, the empty scuttle in her hands. She turned back once, her face pale in the grey afternoon light.

“I’ll be quick,” she said.

“Take your time.”

She looked at him, and he thought she might say something else. But she only adjusted her grip on the scuttle and walked down the path to the gate. He watched her open it, the hinges creaking in the silence, and step onto the pavement. She moved slowly, not from reluctance but from the cold, her shoulders hunched against the wind.

Frank stood in the doorway until she reached the end of the block. He saw her pause in front of Number Three, the white door with its brass knocker, the curtained windows that gave nothing away. She shifted the scuttle to one hand and reached up to knock.

He did not wait to see the door open. He turned back into the house and closed the door behind him, shutting out the cold and the grey light and the sight of his daughter standing on a neighbour’s step with an empty scuttle and a request he could not make himself.

The front room was silent. The hearth was dead. The ash in the grate lay still, a small mound of grey that would not catch no matter how many times he stirred it. He crossed to the sofa and sat where Vi had been sitting, and he felt the warmth she had left behind in the blanket, faint and fading.

Through the window, he could just see the top of Number Three’s door. It remained closed. Vi must have been admitted, must be standing in the front room now, speaking to Miss Penrose in that quiet voice she used when she was asking for something she needed.

He thought of the metronome, the tick that Vi had described. A sound that counted something other than time. He did not know what she had meant, but he understood the feeling. The hours in this house had begun to measure something other than minutes, something that had to do with endurance and pride and the slow erosion of both.

The coal would come. Vi would return with a full scuttle, and he would lay a fire, and the hearth would warm, and they would pretend that this was only a temporary trouble, a small dip in the road that would smooth out when the strike ended and the trams ran again. But the asking would stay. It would live in the empty space where his certainty used to be, a hollow feeling that no fire could fill.

He leaned forward with his elbows on his knees and stared at the dead grate. The iron rim was cold under his fingers, cold as the day he had bought the house, cold as the first night they had spent here, Vi small enough to carry, the rooms echoing with possibility.

Now the rooms echoed with something else. Not silence, exactly. The quiet, he thought. The quiet that comes after a sound has stopped, after the music has ended, after the metronome has counted its final beat. He did not know if Vi would find what she needed at Number Three. He did not know if Miss Penrose would open her coal store or her door. He only knew that he had sent his daughter to ask, and that the asking was a kind of surrender he had not planned to make.

The front room grew darker as the afternoon wore on. He did not light a lamp. He sat in the gathering dim and listened for footsteps on the path, for the gate swinging open, for the door that would announce Vi’s return.

The strike would end. The wages would come. The trams would roll again. But the cold would remain, he thought, long after the fires were lit. The cold of a front room where a father had knelt before a dead grate and sent his daughter out to carry his need across the street.

He rose at last and went to the window. The street was empty, the houses dark, and somewhere behind him, the ash waited in the grate, patient and grey. At the end of the block, the door of Number Three remained closed, holding within it the answer he could not bring himself to ask for.

The memory of Vi’s shiver returned to him—the sharp, involuntary sound she had made that morning, pulling her thin coat tighter. That sound had broken something in him, had driven him to his knees before a grate that held no warmth and never would again, not without coal.

He turned from the window and crossed to the hearth, where the empty scuttle stood like an accusation. He lifted it. The weight of it, the emptiness—this was what he had reduced their life to. A vessel with nothing to hold.

When Vi finally returned, her cheeks bitten red by the wind, she found him standing in the dark. She did not ask why he had not lit the lamp. She saw the shape of his intention before he spoke it.

“Take the scuttle to Number Three,” he said. His voice was flat, stripped of pride. “Ask Miss Penrose if she can spare coal. Tell her—“ He stopped. Tell her what? That her neighbor had failed? That the child standing at her door carried his shame in her small, cold hands?

“Tell her we’ll settle the debt when the strike ends,” he said finally. “When the wages come.”

Vi took the scuttle without a word. She understood what it cost him to send her. She understood that her father could not cross that street himself, could not stand before Ada Penrose and admit that the fire had died. But a child could ask. A child’s need was simple, forgivable. A child carried no history of silence between houses.

At the door she paused, and he thought she might refuse. But she only looked back once, her face half-lit by the grey light from the street, and then she was gone, the scuttle clutched against her chest, her footsteps fading toward the gate.

Frank sat again in the dark and waited. Somewhere across the street, a door would open. A question would be asked. And the answer, when it came, would arrive in his daughter’s voice, carrying back whatever mercy or refusal the winter allowed.
