Chapter 6

Inside a Still and Quiet Room

The front door had closed, and the sound of it stayed in the room long after the girl’s footsteps had faded. The parlour held that particular stillness that comes after something has happened—a quality in the air, a settling of dust that had been disturbed and was now finding its place again.

Light came through the lace curtains in thin, grey lines, striping the worn carpet, catching on the edge of the empty coal scuttle by the hearth. Yesterday’s ashes still lay in the grate, a pale mound undisturbed, and the poker stood where it had been left, tilted against the iron frame. On the mantelpiece, the clock ticked through its familiar rhythm, brass face catching what little afternoon remained.

The horsehair sofa bore the faint impression of someone who had sat there not long ago, a slight depression in the center of the seat, the antimacassar slightly askew. A cup sat on the side table—empty now, a dark ring marking where tea had been. The smell of the room was of cold ash and old polish, the particular scent of a house where the fire had not been lit in days and would not be lit again today. Outside, the street was silent. No trams. No hooves on cobbles. Just that held-breath quiet that had settled over Bristol since the strike began, as though the whole city were waiting for permission to exhale.

Frank Hollis sat in his chair by the window, hands resting on the worn arms, and watched the door. He had been sitting there since before Vi left, and he would be sitting there when she came back. That was what fathers did. They held the line.

The clock on the mantelpiece marked the minutes. Twenty past three. Twenty-five past. The light shifted, crawled further down the wall, abandoned the picture rail. Somewhere down the street a door opened and closed, and he found himself leaning forward, craning to see past the edge of the curtain, but it was only Mrs Patterson from number twelve, hurrying back from the shops with her basket over her arm. She glanced up at the window as she passed, and he drew back, not wanting to be seen watching.

At four o’clock, the lock turned.

He heard Vi’s tread in the hall, the shuffle of her boots on the mat, the pause—she would be removing her coat, hanging it on the hook—and then the door to the parlour opened, and she stood there in the fading light, hands clasped in front of her.

“You went,” he said. Not a question.

“Yes.”

“To number three.”

“Yes.”

He looked at her. She was thinner than she had been a month ago—they all were, with the strike dragging on and the union funds stretched thin—but her eyes were bright, her chin lifted in that way she had when she was determined not to show fear. She had her mother’s jaw, her mother’s stubborn set to her shoulders.

“What did she say?”

“She said she’d think about it.”

“Think about what?”

“Lessons. She said she’d think about lessons.”

He turned this over. The clock ticked. Outside, a bird landed on the windowsill, regarded the room with a black eye, and flew away.

“Did she give you anything?” he asked. “Tea? Cake? Money?”

“Tea. She gave me tea.”

“Did you drink it?”

“Yes.”

“Did you ask for it?”

Vi shook her head. “She offered. I said I shouldn’t, but she said—“ She stopped, her gaze dropping to her hands. “She said I looked cold.”

Frank’s fingers tightened on the arms of his chair. The thought of his daughter being pitied, being seen as someone who needed charity, sat in his chest. But he said nothing, only watched her, the way she stood with her weight on both feet, neither shrinking nor challenging.

“And what else?” he asked. “What else did you talk about?”

“The piano. She asked if I’d played before, and I said no, but I’d always wanted to. She asked why, and I said—“ Vi hesitated. “I said because it sounded like something worth learning.”

Frank said nothing. The light had almost gone now, the room settling into that grey indistinctness that came before true dark, when shapes lost their edges and colors drained away.

“Did you offer to pay?” he asked.

“I said we couldn’t pay. Not in money.”

“What did you say, then?”

Vi took a breath. “I said I could help. With chores. With cleaning, or errands, or whatever she needed. I said if she’d teach me, I’d work for it.”

The words hung in the air between them. Frank felt something shift—not in his chest, not in any place he could name, but somewhere in the room, in the space that separated his chair from where Vi stood.

“You offered to work,” he said slowly. “In exchange.”

“Yes.”

“For piano lessons.”

“Yes.”

He looked at her hands—her small, work-roughened hands, the nails bitten short, the knuckles red from the cold. She was twelve years old. She should not have to barter her labor for music. She should not have to negotiate, to scheme, to find ways around the poverty that had settled on their house like a second roof.

“She might refuse,” he said. “You know that.”

“She might. But she didn’t say no.”

He grunted.

The sound came from low in his throat, a noise that was neither agreement nor refusal, neither blessing nor condemnation. It was the sound of a man who had searched for something to object to and found nothing, who had looked for a reason to forbid and come up empty. It was the sound of a door neither opened nor closed, left ajar in the gathering dark.

Vi waited. When he said nothing more, she took a step back toward the door.

“I’ll start tomorrow,” she said. “If she says yes.”

He did not tell her not to go.

The door clicked shut behind her, and he heard her footsteps in the hall, the creak of the stairs as she climbed to her room. The parlour settled back into silence, the clock ticking, the ashes cold in the grate. He should light the lamp. He should rise from this chair, find the matches, bring some light into the room before the dark took hold entirely. But he did not move.

Instead, he sat and watched the window, the grey square of it, the last of the day retreating from the glass. The street outside was empty. The strike had emptied it, had stilled the trams and silenced the wheels and left the whole of Bristol waiting. He had voted for this. Had stood in the depot, surrounded by men he had worked beside for twenty years, and raised his hand for the motion to strike. He had known what it would cost—had seen it in his wife’s face before she left, in the way she looked at him as though he were a stranger, as though the man she had married would never have chosen a principle over a pay-packet. But he had held the line. That was what you did. You held, and you waited, and you trusted that the waiting would end.

But the waiting stretched on. And now his daughter was crossing the street to ask favours of a woman he did not know, offering to scrub floors in exchange for music lessons, and he was sitting in a dark room doing nothing to stop her.

He rose, finally, and went to the window. The glass was cold against his forehead when he pressed it there, looking out at number three. The shutters were closed, but a faint glow showed at the edges—lamplight, warm and steady. Someone was moving inside, a shadow passing across the gap. Miss Penrose, he supposed. The piano teacher. The woman Vi had sought out, had asked, had bargained with.

He had never spoken to her. Had passed her on the street perhaps a dozen times over the years, had nodded once or twice in that careful way neighbours nod when they do not know each other well enough to speak. She had always seemed the quiet type—kept to herself, had no visitors that he could see, went about her business with the same regular rhythm as the clock on his mantelpiece. And now, somehow, she had become the person his daughter turned to when her own father could not provide.

The shadow passed the window again. Then the glow shifted, brightened, as though a curtain had been drawn back, and for a moment he saw her—just a glimpse, a figure in the upper window, standing with her back to the glass, looking out at the street he could not see. Then she was gone, the curtain fell back, and the window returned to its uniform glow.

He stood there for a long time, his forehead against the cold glass, his breath fogging a small circle that faded and reformed. The street stayed empty. The clock in the parlour struck five. Upstairs, he heard Vi moving, the creak of floorboards, the soft thud of something being moved.

Tomorrow, she would go back. She would cross the street and knock on the door of number three, and Miss Penrose would either accept her offer or turn her away. And Frank would sit in this chair, in this room, and wait for her return. He would not follow. He would not intervene. He would hold the line, as he always had, and trust that his daughter knew what she was doing.

But as he turned from the window, as he fumbled for the matches to light the lamp, he found himself listening—straining to hear something from across the street, some sound that might tell him what was happening in that other house, what arrangement was being made, what door was being opened that he could not close.

There was nothing. Just the wind in the eaves, the tick of the clock, the settling of a house into night. He lit the lamp. The flame caught, wavered, steadied. The room came back into focus—the cold hearth, the empty coal scuttle, the chair where he had sat for hours, watching and waiting.

He sat down again. His hands found the arms of the chair, rested there, large and still. The lamp threw shadows up the wall, and he watched them, thinking of nothing, thinking of everything—of the strike, of the depot, of the vote he had cast and the line he had held. Of his wife, gone to her sister’s in Clifton with nothing but a case and a promise to return when the trouble passed. Of Vi, who had not gone with her, who had chosen to stay here in this cold house with a father who could not give her what she needed.

She had found another way. That was what children did—they found ways around the obstacles their parents could not move. They were resourceful, resilient, ruthless in their pursuit of what they wanted. And what Vi wanted was music.

He closed his eyes. The lamp hissed softly, a sound like breath, like the quiet that filled a room when all the talking was done. Tomorrow, he would sit here again. He would watch the door, wait for her return. He would not ask what she had learned, would not inquire about the lessons or the chores or the woman who had agreed to teach her. He would simply hold the line, as he always had, and trust that it was enough.

But even as he thought it, some part of him knew—had known since the moment Vi walked through the door with that bright, determined look in her eyes—that the line had already shifted. That something had changed, was changing still, in ways he could not see and could not stop. The strike would end, eventually. The trams would run again. Life would resume its familiar rhythms. But this—this small breach, this door opened between his house and number three—this would not close.

He opened his eyes. The lamp burned on. The clock ticked through the silence, and Frank Hollis sat in his chair, and waited for morning.
