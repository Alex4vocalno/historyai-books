Chapter 6

The Warnings That Were Filed Away

The letter lay on a desk in Pittsburgh in the spring of 1888. John Fulton had written it. He addressed it to the president of the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club. Fulton was general manager of the Cambria Iron Company. His mills stood below the dam, in the valley of the Little Conemaugh. He had looked at the river gauge. He had walked the streambed. He had measured what the dam’s spillway could carry and what it could not. His letter said the dam’s discharge capacity was insufficient. It said a heavy rain could fill the reservoir faster than the spillway could release it. It said the water would overtop the embankment. It said the embankment would fail.

The letter reached the club’s offices. Someone read it. Someone discussed it. Someone placed it in a file.

Beside the file, on the same desk or one like it, lay a club ledger. The ledger recorded expenditures for the season: boat repairs, landscaping, new furnishings for the clubhouse, wages for the caretaker and his men. The ledger showed what the club spent to maintain its grounds. It did not show what the club spent to repair the dam. The dam was not a line item. The dam was scenery.

The gap between Fulton’s letter and the ledger was the gap between knowledge and authority. Fulton knew what the dam could do. The club knew what the dam cost. The first was a matter of engineering. The second was a matter of accounting. The letter described a structure that could kill two thousand people. The ledger described a property that cost four hundred dollars a year to maintain. Both documents were accurate. Both were filed. Neither changed the dam.

Ignorance was not the problem. The dam did not fail because no one knew it was dangerous. It failed because the knowledge existed in writing, in the hands of men with the standing to act, and was absorbed into routine without consequence. The warnings were specific. The responses were vague. The specificity described culverts, spillways, embankment height, and discharge capacity. The vagueness consisted of maintenance, patchwork, and silence. The mechanism was simple: paper became a substitute for action. A letter acknowledged a risk. A filing acknowledged the letter. The risk remained.

The documentary trail began early in the decade. Cambria Iron’s interest in the dam was not abstract. The company’s mills, its rail yards, its coke ovens, and its workers’ houses occupied the flat ground along the Little Conemaugh below the South Fork reservoir. The river flowed through the works. A dam failure upstream meant a wall of water through the works. The company had every reason to watch the dam. It had every reason to write about it.

Fulton stood in the rolling mill in 1880 and looked at the river gauge. The water had risen after a storm. It rose fast. He noted the rate. He had been a miner, a superintendent, a man who understood how water moved through rock and earth. He understood that a reservoir above a steel mill was a structure, not a natural feature. Structures had capacities. Capacities had limits. He began to watch the gauge after every storm. He began to write letters.

The letters went to the club’s officers. The club’s officers were men Fulton knew. They were Pittsburgh men. They were steel men, railroad men, banking men. Fulton was a steel man. He spoke their language. He wrote in terms they could measure. He told them the spillway was too small. He told them the embankment had been lowered. He told them the old discharge pipes had been removed or had rusted shut. He told them the water level in the reservoir had risen to within feet of the crest during previous storms. He told them what a breach would do to the valley below.

The letters were not vague. They named the structure. They named the parts. They named the mechanism of failure. They said: the water will rise faster than the spillway can pass it. They said: the water will overtop the crest. They said: the embankment will wash away. They said: the flood will reach Johnstown.

The club’s responses are the second half of the record. The minutes of the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club survive in fragments. What they show is a pattern. A letter arrives. The secretary reads it. The members discuss it. The members authorize an expenditure. The expenditure is for patchwork. Someone is hired to dump stone and earth into a sag. Someone is hired to clear the spillway of debris. Someone is hired to cut brush from the upstream face. The work is recorded. The cost is paid. The letter is filed.

The pattern repeated. Each cycle consumed a season. The letter arrived in spring or summer, after a storm or before one. The club met. The club authorized repairs. The repairs were cosmetic. They addressed the appearance of the dam, not its capacity. They addressed the crest, not the spillway. They addressed the spillway, not the discharge pipes. They kept the dam looking sound. They did not make the dam sound.

The distinction between repair and remediation is the distinction that matters. Repair restores a structure to its previous condition. Remediation changes a structure to meet a new standard of safety. The club repaired. It did not remediate. It did not widen the spillway. It did not replace the discharge pipes. It did not raise the crest. It did not bring an engineer to the site to measure the embankment’s cross-section or calculate the reservoir’s capacity against a probable maximum flood. It spent money on the dam the way it spent money on the boat dock: to keep the season running.

The club’s defenders later insisted the dam was sound. They said the spillway was adequate. They said the embankment had stood for forty years. They said the warnings were exaggerated. They said Fulton and Cambria Iron were self-interested, their letters designed to justify some future purchase of the reservoir or some political maneuver against the club. The defenders made this argument in newspapers, in legal depositions, and in the corridors of the Allegheny County Courthouse after the flood.

The American Society of Civil Engineers investigated the dam after the disaster. Its committee published findings in 1891. The committee’s report weighed the same evidence the club’s defenders cited and reached different conclusions. The committee found the spillway grossly undersized. It found the embankment lowered below its original height. It found the discharge pipes removed and not replaced. It found the crest patched with earth and loose stone. It found the dam had been maintained as a road, not as a hydraulic structure. The committee’s language was professional. Its meaning was plain. The dam had been unsafe for years. The warnings were correct.

The disagreement between the club’s defenders and the ASCE committee is the chapter’s spine. The defenders said the dam was sound because it had stood. The engineers said the dam was unsound because it could not pass the water it was likely to receive. The defenders measured safety by the absence of failure. The engineers measured safety by the margin between capacity and demand. The dam had stood because no storm had tested it. The storm of May 1889 tested it. The margin was insufficient. The dam failed. The engineers’ standard predicted the failure. The defenders’ standard did not.

The mechanism by which knowledge became routine is the mechanism this chapter must expose. It worked through three parties. Each party received the warning. Each party processed it. Each party produced a consequence. The consequences were different. They were all inadequate.

For Cambria Iron, concern turned to frustration. Fulton wrote letters. The letters described specific dangers. The club acknowledged the letters. The club did not act. Fulton wrote again. The cycle repeated. Over years, the correspondence accumulated. Each letter was more specific than the last. Each response was more perfunctory. Fulton’s frustration is legible in the tone of his later correspondence. He stopped suggesting repairs. He began stating consequences. He told the club what would happen. The club thanked him. The club filed the letter. The frustration did not become action. It became documentation. Fulton’s letters are the most complete record of the dam’s condition before the flood. They are also the record of a man who knew and could not compel.

The company’s frustration had a structural cause. Cambria Iron owned the mills. It did not own the dam. The dam belonged to the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club. The club’s members were men of equal or greater standing in Pittsburgh’s industrial hierarchy. Fulton could write to them. He could not order them. He could not sue them before the flood. He could not inspect the dam without the club’s permission. He could not compel the club to hire an engineer, widen the spillway, or replace the discharge pipes. His authority ended at the property line. The dam was above the line. The mills were below it. The water would cross the line. The authority could not.

For the club, maintenance was a cost line. The dam appeared in the club’s books as an expense. The expense was small, comparable to the cost of maintaining the boat dock or the game trails. The club’s officers treated the dam as one element of the property among many. The property existed for fishing and hunting. The dam created the lake. The lake created the fishing. The dam’s function was to hold water for fish. Its function was not to hold water against a flood. The club’s understanding of the dam was recreational. The warnings described a hydraulic structure. The club managed a recreational facility. The mismatch was total. Fulton wrote about spillway capacity and probable maximum precipitation. The club wrote about fish stocking and guest accommodations. Both were talking about the same dam. Neither was talking about the same dam.

Absentee ownership compounded the mismatch. The members lived in Pittsburgh. They visited the lake in season. They did not live below the dam. They did not work in the mills. They did not walk the river gauge after a storm. The dam was a feature of their leisure, not a feature of their risk. The consequences of failure fell on people they did not know, in a valley they did not inhabit, against industries they owned but did not operate. The ledger balanced the club’s accounts. It did not balance the dam’s risk against the valley’s cost. The absentee ledger recorded what the club spent on its pleasures. It did not record what the valley would pay for the club’s neglect.

For the system, paper became a substitute for action. This is the most consequential mechanism. The letters, the minutes, the inspection reports, the correspondence between Fulton and the club’s officers: all of it constituted a record. The record was complete. It documented the danger. It documented the warnings. It documented the responses. It documented the gap. The record was so complete that after the flood, investigators could reconstruct the history of the dam’s condition from the papers alone. The papers existed. The papers were filed. The filing was the system’s final response. The system produced knowledge. It produced documentation. It produced correspondence. It did not produce enforcement.

The enforcement gap was structural. No law required the club to maintain the dam to a specified standard. No agency inspected the dam. No regulator had jurisdiction over a private reservoir on private land. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania had built the dam. The Commonwealth had sold it. The Commonwealth had no remaining authority over its maintenance. The dam passed from public ownership to private ownership. The public safety interest did not follow. The law treated the dam as private property. The dam held a public hazard. The law had no mechanism for the difference.

The warnings traveled through this gap. They moved from Cambria Iron to the club. They moved from the club’s secretary to the club’s members. They moved from the members to the minutes. They moved from the minutes to the files. At each step, the warning lost force. Fulton’s letter said the dam could fail and kill thousands. The minutes said the club authorized repairs to the embankment. The file said the matter was addressed. Each translation reduced the danger. Each translation made the warning smaller. By the time the warning reached the file, it was a line in a ledger. It sat beside the cost of landscaping and the wages of the groundskeeper. It was a cost of doing business. It was not a danger to anyone.

The dam’s deferred-maintenance debt compounded across the decade. Each year without spillway widening added to the risk. Each year without discharge pipes added to the reservoir’s rise during storms. Each year of patchwork added to the weakness of the embankment. The debt was not monetary. It was hydraulic. It was the difference between what the dam could hold and what a storm would deliver. The debt grew with every season the club did not remediate. The club paid for repairs. The repairs did not reduce the debt. They increased it. Each repair made the dam look sounder. Each appearance of soundness made the next warning easier to file. The appearance of maintenance substituted for the fact of maintenance. The club could point to its expenditures. The expenditures proved the club cared. Caring was not capacity.

The engineering record outside the club’s correspondence confirms the pattern. Engineers visited the dam during the 1880s. Some came at Fulton’s request. Some came independently. Their reports described the same conditions. The spillway was too narrow. The crest was too low. The discharge pipes were gone. The embankment was patched with loose material. The upstream face was brushy and degraded. The reports used numbers. They used measurements. They used the professional vocabulary of hydraulic engineering. They said the dam was unsafe.

The reports went to the club. The club received them. The club did not implement their recommendations. The club’s officers may have forwarded the reports to members. The members may have read them. The members did not act. The reports joined the letters in the files. The files grew. The dam did not change.

The asymmetry between the specificity of the warnings and the vagueness of the response is the chapter’s central fact. The warnings named the parts of the dam. They named the mechanisms of failure. They named the consequences. They were written by men who understood reservoirs, culverts, spillway capacity, and probable maximum precipitation. They were written in plain language. They said what would happen and how.

The responses did not address the parts. They did not address the mechanisms. They did not address the consequences. They addressed the appearance of the dam. They authorized expenditures for stone, earth, brush clearance, and labor. They did not authorize engineering. They did not authorize redesign. They did not authorize the reconstruction of the spillway or the installation of new discharge pipes. The responses were maintenance. The warnings demanded remediation. The difference was the space in which the dam failed.

The club’s defenders said the dam had stood for forty years. This was true. It was also irrelevant. The dam had stood for forty years because no storm had tested it. The dam’s survival was not evidence of its capacity. It was evidence of the weather. The engineers who inspected the dam understood this. The club’s officers did not. Or they did, and chose to disregard it. The distinction between ignorance and disregard matters less than it appears. Both produced the same result. The dam was not remediated. The dam failed.

The ASCE committee’s 1891 report made the distinction explicit. The committee found the dam’s design and maintenance deficient. It found the warnings justified. It found the club’s responses inadequate. The report did not say the club had ignored the warnings. It said the club had not understood them. The club had treated engineering warnings as maintenance requests. The committee’s finding was generous. It assumed the club’s officers had read the warnings and failed to grasp their meaning. The correspondence suggests an alternative. The club’s officers read the warnings. They understood them. They decided the risk was acceptable. They decided the cost of remediation exceeded the cost of the risk. The risk was a flood. The cost of the flood would fall on the valley. The cost of remediation would fall on the club. The club chose to bear the risk it did not own rather than pay the cost it did.

This is the calculation the absentee ledger recorded. The ledger balanced the club’s books. It did not balance the dam’s books. The dam’s books were the valley’s books. The valley would pay the cost of the club’s decision. The valley did not know the decision had been made. The valley did not know the dam was unsafe. The valley knew the dam was there. The valley had been told the dam was safe. The dam had stood for forty years. The standing was the evidence. The evidence was the weather. The weather was about to change.

On May 28, 1889, a low-pressure area formed over Nebraska and Kansas. By the time this weather pattern reached western Pennsylvania two days later, it had developed into what would be termed the heaviest rainfall event that had ever been recorded in that part of the state. The storm was moving toward the Little Conemaugh. The dam was not ready. The warnings had been filed. The files were in drawers. The drawers were in offices. The offices were in Pittsburgh. The rain was in the sky.

The rain would fall on the reservoir. The reservoir would fill. The spillway would not pass the water. The water would rise to the crest. The crest was too low. The embankment was patched. The patches were earth and loose stone. The water would find the patches. The patches would give way. The embankment would breach. The water would go through. The water would go down. The water would go to the valley. The valley was Johnstown. The warnings had said so. The warnings were in the files. The files were closed. The rain was not.

The storm was not yet over Pennsylvania. It was over the plains. It was moving east.

It carried moisture from the Gulf. It carried wind from the southwest. It carried the conditions that the engineers had described as a probable maximum flood.

The engineers had written the words. The club had filed the words. The storm was bringing the flood.

The filing had not stopped it. The filing had not widened the spillway. The filing had not raised the crest. The filing had not replaced the discharge pipes. The filing had preserved the warnings.

The warnings waited in the drawers. The rain waited in the clouds. The dam waited in the hills.

The gap between the knowledge in the files and the authority to act on it held open. The gap was wide enough for a flood to pass through.

The rain was three days away. The dam was not ready. The valley did not know.
