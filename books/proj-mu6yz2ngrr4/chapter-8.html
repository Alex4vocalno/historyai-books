Chapter 8

Alderman Rowes Summons

The summons, a crisp note on official board stationery, waited on her desk.

Evelyn Marsh read it twice before setting it face-down beside the tide ledger. The handwriting belonged to Alderman Rowe’s clerk, precise and unremarkable, but the seal at the bottom, the harbour’s anchor entwined with rope, carried weight enough. She had been summoned to the boardroom at eleven o’clock. No reason given. No reason necessary, when an alderman called.

She gathered her papers with methodical care. The quarterly tide forecasts lay in a neat pile, graphs and tables showing the expected patterns for the approaching equinox. If this were truly a review of her work, she would be prepared. If it were something else, she would still be prepared. The distinction might not matter.

The harbourmaster’s building rose at the head of the quay, stone weathered to the color of wet sand, windows watching the water like eyes that had seen too many tides. Evelyn climbed the steps with her ledger tucked beneath her arm, conscious of each footfall on the worn threshold. The door opened onto a corridor that smelled of polish and damp wool, familiar and unwelcoming in equal measure.

A clerk directed her toward the boardroom without meeting her eyes. That small avoidance told her something. Word had travelled, then. Her questions about the lock-gate log had not gone unnoticed, however quietly she had phrased them.

The boardroom door stood ajar. Evelyn knocked once and entered when a voice from within granted permission.

Alderman Walter Rowe sat at the head of the long oak table, his back to the window so that morning light silhouetted him, obscuring his expression. The harbour’s seal dominated the wall behind him, painted in oils that had darkened with age. Books lined the shelves to his left, spines cracked from use, and a clock ticked somewhere out of sight, measuring the silence.

“Miss Marsh. Thank you for coming promptly.” Rowe indicated a chair to his right, not at the table’s far end where petitioners usually sat, but closer. Within conversational distance. “Please, sit.”

Evelyn placed her ledger on the table and took the chair. Her hands rested in her lap, one thumbnail pressing against the pad of her thumb. The pressure grounded her.

“The quarterly forecasts,” Rowe said, nodding toward her papers. “I understand we may expect stronger tidal variations this season. The board appreciates your diligence in preparing the projections.”

“Thank you, Alderman. The calculations follow the usual methods. I can walk you through the significant variables, if you wish.”

“That won’t be necessary.” Rowe’s fingers drummed once against the tabletop, a sound like a gavel striking wood. “Your work has always been thorough, Miss Marsh. Thorough and accurate. The harbour relies on such reliability.”

Evelyn waited. The compliment hung in the air, too broad, too warm. Praise from men like Rowe never came without purpose.

“Reliability,” he continued, “is the foundation of trust. Merchants need to know their goods will move on schedule. Vessels need to know the lock gates will open when the tide requires it. The entire harbour operates on that certainty. When the ledgers match the reality, confidence follows. When they do not…”

He let the sentence trail. Evelyn watched his face, but the backlighting made reading him difficult. She shifted slightly, angling herself so the window’s glare no longer blinded her to his features. His jaw sat tight. His eyes held hers without wavering.

“The lock-keeper’s log for the night of the third,” Rowe said. His voice remained level, almost casual. “I understand there have been questions raised about its accuracy.”

Evelyn’s thumb pressed harder against her thumbnail. The pain focused her. “I noted a discrepancy during routine verification. The log records one opening at twenty-three forty-five. My tide ledger records two openings, the second at four-ten.”

“Yes. So I have heard.” Rowe leaned back in his chair. The movement creaked in the room’s stillness. “And you have made inquiries. Informal ones. Conversations with the lock-keeper, I am told. Visits to the quayside.”

“Verification is part of my duty, Alderman. The records must agree.”

“The records do agree. The official record, certified and filed, shows one opening. That is the record that matters.” Rowe’s voice carried no edge, no anger. Only certainty. “The lock-keeper’s log is the authoritative document. Your personal ledger, while useful for your own calculations, carries no formal weight.”

Evelyn said nothing. The clock ticked. A gull called somewhere beyond the window, its cry muffled by glass.

“Captain Trewin’s death was a tragedy,” Rowe continued. “A good man. An experienced seaman. The coroner’s inquest determined the cause. Death by misadventure. An accident, Miss Marsh. A terrible, regrettable accident, but nothing more.”

“The discrepancy in the log suggests—“

“It suggests nothing.” Rowe cut her off, not with volume but with precision. Each word dropped into place like a stone in a wall. “The discrepancy you mention exists only because you have chosen to interpret your personal notes as superior to the official record. They are not. They never were.”

Evelyn felt her throat tighten. She swallowed once, deliberately, and forced her voice to remain steady. “I recorded the second opening because the tide required it. The lock gates cannot remain closed when the water differential exceeds the safety margin. The opening at four-ten was tidally necessary. Someone must have operated the mechanism.”

“The lock-keeper’s log says otherwise.”

“The lock-keeper’s log is wrong.”

The words fell into the room like stones into deep water. Rowe’s expression did not change, but something behind his eyes shifted. A sharpening. A recognition.

“You are very certain,” he said.

“I have the tide tables. The calculations. The physical reality of the harbour does not alter to suit the paperwork.”

“No. But the paperwork is what the world sees. What merchants trust. What insurers rely upon.” Rowe leaned forward now, his hands flat on the table. “A harbour runs on trust, Miss Marsh. And ledgers. When you question the ledgers, you question the trust. You suggest that Porthkennick cannot keep its own records straight. You suggest that men who have served this harbour for decades are either incompetent or dishonest.”

“I suggest only that the records disagree. The reason for that disagreement warrants examination.”

“Why?”

The question was simple. It should have been simple to answer. But the way Rowe asked it, soft and curious, as if he genuinely wished to understand, made Evelyn pause.

“Because the truth matters,” she said.

“Does it?” Rowe’s head tilted slightly. “The truth of Captain Trewin’s death will not bring him back. The truth of a lock-gate opening will not change the morning’s tide. But the appearance of uncertainty, Miss Marsh, that changes everything. That suggests to every ship’s captain who might dock here that our records cannot be trusted. That suggests to every merchant that our harbour is mismanaged. That suggests to the wider world that Porthkennick is not a safe port of call.”

“I am not suggesting anything of the kind.”

“You are, whether you intend to or not.” Rowe’s voice dropped, intimate now, as if he were sharing a confidence rather than issuing a warning. “I am not asking you to falsify your records. I am asking you to understand the difference between a private working note and an official document. Your ledger serves your calculations. The lock-keeper’s log serves the harbour. They are not the same thing, and they are not meant to be the same thing.”

Evelyn’s hands had gone still in her lap. The thumbnail no longer pressed against its neighbour. A strange calm had settled over her, cold and clear.

“If the Harbour Board wishes the discrepancy to remain unexamined,” she said, “I will of course respect that decision.”

Rowe’s eyes narrowed. The smallest movement. A flicker of something that might have been surprise or might have been calculation.

“I am not telling you what to do, Miss Marsh. I am telling you what your actions already communicate. Whether you continue to make inquiries is your own affair. But I would ask you to consider whether the outcome you seek, whatever that might be, is worth the cost of achieving it.”

“And what cost is that?”

“Trust. Reputation. The stability of this harbour, which employs hundreds of men and feeds thousands of families. Those are not small things. They are not abstract. They are real, and they are fragile, and they depend on the appearance of certainty even when certainty is impossible.”

Evelyn looked at the harbour seal on the wall behind him. The anchor and rope, painted in oils that had cracked with time. The symbol of an institution larger than any single person, larger than any single truth.

“I understand, Alderman.”

“Do you?” Rowe studied her face. Whatever he saw there seemed to satisfy him. He nodded once and rose from his chair. “Good. Then we understand each other. The quarterly forecasts look excellent, Miss Marsh. Keep up the good work.”

The meeting was over. Evelyn gathered her ledger and left the boardroom, her footsteps measured on the polished floor. The clerk at the end of the corridor still would not meet her eyes.

The walk back to the records room took her along the quayside. The tide was out, leaving the mud flats exposed, the fishing boats tilted on their moorings. Men moved between the warehouses, loading and unloading, their voices carrying across the water. The harbour breathed around her, tidal and constant, indifferent to the small drama of a clerk summoned to an alderman’s office.

Her records room was a cramped space in the harbour office, a single window overlooking the water, a desk scarred by decades of use. She closed the door behind her and turned the key in the lock. Then she sat at her desk and did not move for a long time.

The receipt from Pengelly still lay in her drawer, where she had placed it after her visit to the warehouse. She took it out now and set it on the desk, smoothing the creased paper with her palm.

The date. The item. The price. The signature, hurried but legible.

She had read it before, many times. She had understood it as evidence of Pengelly’s grievance, proof that he had purchased new rope on the morning after Captain Trewin’s death, when he should have been occupied elsewhere. A detail that did not fit his story. A loose thread in the fabric of the official narrative.

But Rowe’s warning shifted the frame.

She looked at the receipt again. The date was the third. The morning after Captain Trewin died. Pengelly had bought rope at seven-thirty, the receipt showed. The chandler’s shop opened at seven. He must have waited at the door.

Why? What made the purchase so urgent that a man would wait on a shop’s threshold at dawn?

The rope on the lock steps had been examined as part of the coroner’s inquiry. It had been noted, described, and presumably stored somewhere as evidence. But if Pengelly had purchased new rope that same morning, then the rope on the steps had been new too. Or rather, new enough. New enough to pass scrutiny. New enough to look like the rope that should have been there.

But what if the original rope had been different? What if something about it had needed to disappear?

Evelyn turned to her tide ledger and found the entry for the night of the third. Two openings. Twenty-three forty-five and four-ten. The lock gates had opened in the small hours, when only the harbour’s night watch and the tide itself would know.

Captain Trewin had died on those steps. His watch had stopped at four-ten. The time of the second opening. The time the lock gate had operated, though the official log said it had not.

Someone had been there. Someone had opened the lock. And someone had needed Captain Trewin on those steps at that hour.

The receipt was not evidence of a crime. It was evidence of a cover-up. The rope had been replaced because the original rope had told a story someone did not want told. The same story the lock-keeper’s log had been altered to conceal.

Evelyn sat back in her chair. Captain Trewin’s watch lay on her desk, its cracked face still, frozen at four-ten—a silence that suddenly seemed very heavy. Rowe knew about her inquiries. Rowe wanted them to stop. Rowe had not asked her to stop directly, because direct orders left traces. Instead, he had appealed to her sense of duty, her loyalty to the harbour, her understanding of the greater good.

She understood now. She understood too well.

The system she served wanted the truth buried. It wanted the discrepancy to remain a clerical error, the death to remain an accident, the ledger to remain silent. It wanted her to be reliable, thorough, and above all, complicit.

Evelyn Marsh would not forget.
