Chapter 10

Nora at Dawn

Nora Keast sat in her cottage doorway, a grey shape against the grey stone, her hands moving through a tangle of net.

The afternoon had settled into a cold stillness. Behind her the cottage receded into shadow, one peat fire holding what warmth the dwelling had, and the smoke of it hung low over the roofline, unable to rise. Her fingers worked with the automatic precision of long practice, the netting needle passing through the mesh in a rhythm that had never required her eyes. She watched Evelyn approach along the flagged path without breaking cadence.

Evelyn had walked from the harbour office with the tide ledger pressed against her ribs, beneath her coat. The confrontation with Pengelly still moved through her—his refusal to explain the mooring rope receipt, the way his eyes had slid toward the window when she pressed him on his whereabouts, the pen in his hand that had stopped moving and then begun again, as though nothing had been asked. But the ledger demanded one answer, and she had chosen to follow a different thread first. The lock-keeper’s wife.

She stopped at the low stone wall that marked the boundary of the Keast property. Nora did not look up. The needle continued its circuit.

“Mrs. Keast.”

“Evelyn.” Nora’s voice carried no inflection. “You’ve walked a long way for a Wednesday.”

“I need to speak with you.”

“I can see that.” The needle pulled tight. “Whether I can help is another matter.”

Evelyn remained standing. The cottage sat at the edge of the fisherman’s row, set slightly apart, as though the tide of settlement had deposited it here and moved on. Whitewashed stone, slate roof, a small garden to one side holding the stumps of winter cabbages, their leaves curled black against the cold. The smell of tarred rope came off the nets stacked against the wall, thick enough to taste at the back of the throat.

“It’s about the morning Captain Trewin died.”

Nora’s hands did not pause. “I heard about Captain Trewin. A sad business.”

“You were on the quay that morning.”

The needle continued its motion for three more stitches. Then it stopped. Nora’s hands remained in the netting, the mending half-complete, but the movement had ceased. She did not raise her head.

“I don’t know what you mean.”

“I saw you, Mrs. Keast. At first light, standing near the lock steps. You were watching the water.”

The wind moved across the bare garden, stirring the dead cabbage leaves. Nora sat motionless in her doorway, her profile cut sharp against the dark interior. She was a woman of perhaps forty, though the harbour had weathered her past her years. Her hair was pulled back beneath a cap, and her hands were rough from salt and work.

“You were not the only one abroad that morning,” Nora said.

“No. I know that now.” Evelyn kept her voice level. “The lock gate opened at four-ten. The tide required it. But the official log records only the earlier opening, at eleven-forty-five the previous night. Someone falsified that record.”

Nora’s face gave nothing.

“Your husband signed that log,” Evelyn continued.

“Tom signs many logs. It’s his duty.”

“The log is incomplete. It omits a tidally necessary opening. That’s not a clerical error. That’s a deliberate falsehood.”

Nora finally raised her head. Her eyes were pale, washed-out blue, like sea foam under grey sky. She looked at Evelyn without expression, her gaze traveling from the younger woman’s face down to the shape beneath her coat where the ledger rested.

“You’ve made a long journey to tell me my husband made a mistake in his records.”

“It wasn’t a mistake.”

“Wasn’t it?” Nora’s voice remained flat. “I wouldn’t know. I don’t understand the tides the way you do, Evelyn. I don’t keep the harbour’s books. I mend nets and keep house and mind my own affairs.”

“You were there. At dawn. On the quay.”

“Was I?”

“I saw you.”

Nora’s hands, rough from salt and net-mending, went perfectly still in her lap. The needle paused halfway through a stitch, suspended in the hemp. She didn’t look up at Evelyn, but the admission was in that frozen moment, the truth of being seen, and the greater truth she would not yield.

The silence stretched. Somewhere below the cottage row, a dog barked twice and stopped. The wind shifted, carrying the smell of woodsmoke from a chimney further along the slope.

“What did you see, Mrs. Keast?”

Nora’s mouth set in a hard line. “I saw nothing that concerns you.”

“Captain Trewin is dead. His watch stopped at four-ten in the morning. The official verdict says accidental death, but the lock gate opened at that exact time, and someone chose not to record it. Someone needed that gate open, and they needed Captain Trewin on those steps.”

Nora’s face had gone pale, but her voice remained steady. “You’re speaking dangerous words, Evelyn.”

“I’m speaking the truth.”

“The truth.” Nora’s hands resumed their motion, the needle passing through the mesh with renewed deliberation. “The truth is a cold thing. It doesn’t warm you at night. It doesn’t put food on the table. And it doesn’t always set things right.”

“It can set the record right. The harbour’s records have to be accurate. You know that. Everyone who works here knows that. The tides don’t lie, and neither should the logs.”

Nora’s jaw tightened. For a long moment she worked in silence, the needle’s rhythm steady and precise. Then she spoke without looking up.

“It’s not my story to tell.”

Evelyn felt the words settle into her like cold water. “Whose story is it, then?”

No answer. Nora’s hands continued their work, her face turned toward the netting in her lap.

“Mrs. Keast. If you know something, if you saw something, you need to speak. A man is dead. The record has been falsified. Your husband’s signature is on that false record.”

The needle paused again. Nora’s voice came low and hard. “You think I don’t know what’s at stake? You think I don’t understand what it means, a false entry in the log? I’ve been a lock-keeper’s wife for twenty-two years. I know what those books are worth.”

“Then why—“

“Because some things are not for the telling.” Nora’s head came up sharply now, her eyes meeting Evelyn’s with a flash of something that might have been anger or might have been fear. “Some things have to stay buried. For everyone’s sake.”

Evelyn stood her ground. “Even murder?”

The word fell between them. Nora’s face did not change, but her hands had stopped again, the needle held frozen in the act of passing through a loop.

“You say that word,” Nora said quietly, “and you can’t take it back. You understand that? You accuse someone of murder, and the whole harbour hears it. The whole town. And the man you accuse, whether he did it or not, he carries that mark forever. So you’d better be sure. You’d better be very sure before you say that word again.”

“I have the tide tables. I have my ledger. I have Captain Trewin’s watch, stopped at the exact minute the gate had to open. And I have a log that says it never happened.” Evelyn took a breath. “Someone is lying. I need to know who, and why.”

Nora looked at her for a long moment. Then her gaze dropped back to her work.

“I was on the quay that morning. You’re right about that much. I went down before dawn. I couldn’t sleep. I walked the edge, near the lock steps. I saw the water rising.”

“And what else did you see?”

“Nothing.” The word came flat and final. “I saw nothing.”

“You’re protecting someone.”

Nora’s hands trembled slightly, then steadied. “I’m protecting no one. I told you, I saw nothing worth telling.”

“That’s not the same as seeing nothing.”

For a moment, Nora’s mask slipped. Evelyn caught a glimpse of something beneath, a weariness that went beyond fatigue, a knowledge held too long in silence. Then the face closed again, composed and watchful.

“You’re a tide clerk,” Nora said. “You keep the records. You note the comings and goings. That’s your place. But some things don’t belong in the books. Some things belong to the people who live them.”

“Captain Trewin can’t speak for himself.”

“No.” Nora’s voice softened almost imperceptibly. “He can’t. And maybe that’s the greatest mercy of all.”

Evelyn felt the weight of the ledger against her chest. She had come here expecting denial, perhaps even confession. She had not expected this stubborn wall of silence, this refusal to engage with the question at all.

“Mrs. Keast. If your husband—“

“Don’t.” Nora’s voice cut sharp. “Don’t say his name in that tone. Don’t come to my door and speak to me of my husband as though he were a criminal. Tom Keast has served this harbour for more than two decades. He’s opened those gates in storms, in darkness, in sickness and in health. Whatever mistakes he’s made, whatever’s written or not written in that book, he’s a good man. A better man than most.”

“Then why is his log false?”

Nora’s mouth tightened. She looked down at her hands, at the half-mended net, at the needle held motionless in the hemp.

“I’ve told you all I’m going to tell. I was on the quay. I saw nothing. Now go back to your office, Evelyn, and leave us be.”

Evelyn stood for a moment longer. The wind had died, leaving the afternoon unnaturally still. The cottage door faced the harbour, and below, through the grey light, she could see the water, the moored boats, the dark shape of the lock steps where Captain Trewin had been found.

“Thank you for your time, Mrs. Keast.”

Nora did not respond. Her needle had begun to move again, pulling through the mesh with careful, deliberate strokes.

Evelyn turned and walked back along the flagged path.

The cliff path led up from the cottages, climbing the slope toward the high road that rimmed the harbour. Evelyn took it slowly, her mind turning over the conversation. The smell of tar and salt faded as she climbed, replaced by the clean bite of the wind off the water. Below her the harbour spread out in miniature, the boats like toys, the buildings grey shapes against the grey stone.

Nora Keast had been on the quay. She had admitted as much, though the admission had come through stillness rather than words. But she refused to explain why, and that refusal was itself a kind of testimony.

Who was she protecting? The obvious answer was her husband. Tom Keast had signed the false log. He had omitted the second opening, the one that coincided with Trewin’s death. If Nora had seen something that morning, something that could explain or condemn, she would naturally protect him.

But Evelyn had seen something else in the woman’s face. Not just loyalty, though that was there. Something more complicated. A fear that went beyond the fear of exposure.

She stopped at the top of the path, where the cliff road opened onto the view. The wind was stronger here, cold against her cheeks, carrying the taste of salt spray. The tide was coming in, the water rising against the harbour walls. In a few hours the lock gate would need to open again. It was tidally necessary. Inevitable.

The harbour operated by precise calculations. The gates opened when the water demanded it. The records were supposed to reflect that reality, faithfully and completely. But someone had broken that faith. Someone had opened the gate at four-ten and chosen not to write it down. And Nora Keast had been there, watching, and had chosen to say nothing.

Evelyn’s hand moved to her coat, to the shape of the tide ledger beneath. Her records showed the truth. Two openings, recorded in her own careful hand. The official log showed one. Somewhere in that discrepancy lay the answer to Captain Trewin’s death. Her fingers found the other shapes she carried—the lock-keeper’s log, and the cracked glass of Captain Trewin’s stopped watch, still set to the minute the gate had opened. The evidence was here, gathered piece by piece, and it would not quiet.

Nora’s silence was a shield. But shields could crack. They could be worn down, or circumvented, or proven unnecessary if the truth came from another direction.

Tom Keast. She needed to speak with Tom Keast again. His wife’s refusal to speak only made his testimony more crucial. And if he had falsified the log, if he had opened the gate and then hidden the fact, he had to have had a reason. The harbour did not forgive such breaches. The harbour remembered.

She began to walk again, her steps measured and deliberate. The road curved along the cliff edge, the drop to the water below. In the distance the harbour office waited, its windows catching the grey light.

There was something else, something she had glimpsed in Nora’s hands as she worked. The netting needle was worn smooth from use, the hemp dark with age and handling. But in the cottage doorway, as Nora’s fingers had moved through the mesh, Evelyn had seen something else. A hesitation. A moment when the rhythm had faltered, not from guilt but from calculation.

Nora Keast was not simply protecting her husband. She was weighing something. Measuring the cost of silence against the cost of speech.

And that meant she knew something. Something specific. Something that could break this case open, if only Evelyn could find the right pressure, the right question, the right moment.

But pressure was dangerous. Evelyn had felt that danger in Nora’s warning. To accuse was to mark a man forever. The harbour’s system prioritized trust and ledger consistency over factual truth. To challenge that system was to challenge the foundation of Porthkennick’s working order.

Alderman Rowe wanted the case closed. He had made that clear enough, in his measured, indirect way. The official verdict was death by misadventure. The official record was closed. And here was Evelyn, walking the cliff road with a tide ledger that proved otherwise, preparing to question a man whose wife had just refused to speak.

The wind shifted. Below, the water continued its patient rise.

Evelyn walked on, her mind settling into the cold clarity of calculation. Nora Keast had given her nothing but silence. But silence was not nothing. Silence was a fact, a presence, a shape that indicated where the truth might lie.

The ledger demanded one answer. And Evelyn would find it, whether the harbour was ready or not.

The harbour canteen sat squat against the waterfront, its windows fogged with salt and breath. Evelyn pushed through the door into warmth that felt suddenly oppressive. The afternoon shift had thinned to a handful of men nursing cups of tea, their conversation dying as she entered.

Tom Keast sat at one of the corner tables, a half-eaten meal pushed to one side and a spread of chits and tallies before him. He was a heavy-shouldered man with the look of someone who had spent decades hauling nets before the work broke him down to this—checking harbour tallies and eating late. His eyes tracked Evelyn’s approach without welcome or alarm.

“Evelyn.” He set aside the chit he’d been marking. “Nora said you’d be along.”

“Did she.”

“Didn’t say what about.” He folded his arms. “But I can guess.”

Evelyn took the chair across from him, placing her bag on the scarred table between them. “Then you know why I’m here.”

“What I know is that a good man died bad, and folk won’t let him rest.” Tom’s voice carried the flatness of something rehearsed. “Official word’s given. My Nora’s said her piece. Now here you are.”

“Your wife said nothing. That’s rather the point.”

Tom’s jaw tightened, the heavy muscles cordning beneath weathered skin before the expression smoothed over. “Nora’s tired. She’s been tired a long time.”

“The tide ledger from the night Captain Trewin drowned shows entries in your handwriting, Tom. Water depth, cargo logged, times marked. All regular—except the water was too low for any boat to clear the harbour mouth the way the report claims.”

The canteen had gone quiet. The other men had stopped talking.

“Now,” Evelyn continued, her voice low and steady, “a man who knows tides as well as you do wouldn’t make that mistake. Which means either you falsified that record, or someone told you to.”

Tom Keast looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached for his cup, turning it slowly on the table.

“You should talk to the harbourmaster,” he said quietly. “But you already know that, don’t you?”
