Chapter 1

The Lock Steps at Dawn

The messenger’s knuckles were raw from the cold when he rapped on Evelyn Marsh’s office door.

She had been bent over the morning’s tide calculations, her pencil moving in the small, precise strokes that fifteen years of service had perfected. The harbour board required accuracy to the minute, and Evelyn had never given them cause to complain. High water at 6: 47. Low water at 12: 23. The spring tide would peak tomorrow, the highest of the month, and she had already noted the expected variance in her ledger with the quiet satisfaction of a clerk who anticipated irregularity before it arrived.

The knocking came again, sharper this time. She set down her pencil.

“Come.”

The door opened to admit a boy of perhaps twelve, his cap in his hands, his face pale above a muffler that had seen better winters. Evelyn knew him by sight—one of the Keast family’s younger relations, though she could not recall his given name. He worked the fish stalls on market days, and his presence here, in the harbour office, at this hour, meant something had gone wrong with the ordinary order of things.

“Miss Marsh.” His voice cracked on the second word. “You’re wanted. At the lock steps. It’s the Captain.”

Evelyn rose from her desk. The phrase “the Captain” needed no elaboration. In Porthkennick, there was only one man who answered to that title without further qualification. Captain Silas Trewin had served as her superior for the whole of those fifteen years, governing the harbour and its waters with a authority that long predated her own appointment. She reached for her coat.

“What’s happened?”

The boy’s eyes flickered to the window, towards the grey morning light that filtered through the harbour mist. “He’s fallen, Miss. They found him at the bottom of the steps. Mr. Pengelly’s been sent for.”

She stopped, one arm in her coat sleeve. “Mr. Pengelly?”

“The coroner, Miss. Tom Keast sent me for you specifically. Said you’d want to see.”

The boy had said it with the unthinking certainty of youth—that of course the tide clerk would want to see, that her role in the harbour’s workings was as natural and necessary as the tide itself. Evelyn pulled on her second sleeve and reached for her notebook. She did not correct him. She did not ask how bad it was. One did not send for the coroner and the tide clerk simultaneously unless the matter was already beyond remedy.

“Lead the way.”

They went out into the November air. The morning was cold, the kind of cold that settled into the granite and rose from the water in a low, hanging mist. The harbour lay still beneath it, the fishing fleet already gone out on the early tide, their berths empty and waiting. Evelyn could smell the familiar blend of salt and seaweed and coal smoke from the houses that climbed the hill behind the quay. On any other day, she would have paused to note the water level against the harbour wall, to calculate in her head whether the morning’s readings matched her predictions.

She did not pause today.

The lock steps descended from the quayside to the water gate, a narrow stone staircase worn smooth by generations of boots and sea water. They were slick at the best of times, green with a permanent film of algae that no amount of scrubbing could remove. In the dark, they would be treacherous. In the dark, with the spring tide rising—

A small crowd had gathered at the top of the steps, held back by nothing but their own reluctance to draw closer. Evelyn recognized faces: fishermen, dock workers, a woman from the chandler’s shop with her apron still tied around her waist. They parted as she approached, and she felt their eyes on her, curious, pitying, waiting to see what the tide clerk would do.

Tom Keast stood at the edge of the steps, his heavy coat buttoned to the chin, his cap pulled low. He was a broad man with the thick hands of someone who had spent his youth hauling nets before he won the position of lock-keeper, and his face now wore an expression Evelyn had never seen there: uncertainty, edged with something that might have been fear.

“Miss Marsh.” He touched his cap. “Thank you for coming.”

“What’s happened, Tom?”

He gestured downward, a short, stiff motion. “See for yourself.”

Evelyn moved to the edge. The steps descended in a narrow flight, thirty-seven of them—she had counted once, in her first week, because a clerk should know such things—and near the bottom, where the water lapped against the lowest stones, a shape lay crumpled against the iron railing. The tide was rising but had not yet reached him. It would within the hour.

Captain Silas Trewin lay on his side, one leg bent beneath him at an angle that suggested it had not been bent that way in life. His grey hair was matted with something darker than water. His face—

Evelyn looked away, briefly. Then she looked back. A clerk did not look away from the facts.

“Has anyone moved him?”

“Nobody’s touched him, Miss. I found him just like that, when I came to open the gate for the morning tide. Sent the boy straight away.”

“Who else has been down there?”

“Only Mr. Pengelly, and Dr. Hockin after him. They came to confirm—well. To confirm what we’re looking at.”

Evelyn began to descend. The steps were indeed slick, each one demanding attention, and she took them slowly, her hand on the iron railing that ran along the wall. The railing was cold and wet beneath her palm. At the twentieth step, she could smell the blood, mixed with the salt and the seaweed. At the thirtieth, she could see the Captain’s hand, frozen in the air before him, clutching something that caught the grey light.

She knelt on the thirty-fourth step. Not to mourn. To observe.

The Captain’s watch lay in his palm, the chain wrapped twice around his fingers as if he had gripped it in his final moments. It was a good watch, silver-cased, the kind a captain could afford and would need, for a man in his position lived by the hours and the tides. Evelyn had seen him consult it a hundred times, clicking it open with a practiced thumb, nodding at whatever it told him.

Now the glass was cracked. Through the fracture, she could see the hands, frozen in place.

4: 10.

She stared at them. The numbers did not change. They would never change again. The watch had stopped, she knew, at the moment of impact—or moments before, if the Captain had been checking the time when he fell. That was what watches did in such circumstances. They recorded the instant of catastrophe.

But 4: 10.

The sky would have been dark at 4: 10. The sun would not rise for another two hours. The spring tide would have been on the flood, the water rising towards its peak, but not yet high enough to cover these lower steps. Anyone standing here at 4: 10 would have been standing alone in the dark.

“Miss Marsh?”

She looked up. Josiah Pengelly was standing at the bottom of the steps, a document case tucked under one arm, his face professionally blank. He was a slight man, fastidious in his dress, with the careful hands of someone who had made his living attending inquests and recording the circumstances of the dead. Evelyn had never liked him—there was something in his eyes that suggested he found more satisfaction in tragedy than was quite proper—but she respected his competence.

Beside him, a shorter man in a stained medical coat was closing a battered leather bag. Dr. Hockin, the police surgeon from Penzance, had evidently just finished his own examination of the body.

“Mr. Pengelly. Dr. Hockin.”

“An unpleasant business.” Pengelly did not offer to help her rise. He simply watched, his gaze moving from her face to the body and back again. “Dr. Hockin has completed his preliminary examination. I’ve recorded his findings. I trust you’ll want to see for yourself.”

“I’m not a doctor, Mr. Pengelly.”

“No. But you’re the tide clerk. And Captain Trewin was your superior.”

It was not quite a question. Evelyn rose, brushing the damp from her coat, and moved down the final steps to stand beside the coroner. The water lapped at the stones a few feet away, rising slowly, inexorably. In an hour, it would cover this spot entirely.

“Tell me what you’ve found.”

Pengelly consulted the notes he had scratched onto his writing-slate, then gestured to the body with the dispassionate air of a man relaying a mechanic’s report rather than a human fate. “Dr. Hockin identifies a contusion to the right temple, consistent with a fall against the stone. The angle suggests he slipped on the steps and struck his head as he fell. Death would have been swift, in the doctor’s estimation. Unconsciousness followed by heart failure. He would not have suffered.”

Evelyn looked at the steps. The stone was slick, yes. The algae was treacherous. It was entirely plausible that a man of sixty-three, coming down these steps in the dark, might lose his footing. It was plausible that he might strike his head. It was plausible that he might die here, alone, with no one to hear him call out.

But 4: 10.

“Was there anyone with him?”

“Not that we’ve found. The night watchman—old Hitchens—says he saw nothing, heard nothing. But Hitchens is deaf as a post and was likely asleep at his post, as usual.” Pengelly shrugged. “A tragic accident, Miss Marsh. The steps are dangerous. We’ve all known it. The Captain knew it better than anyone.”

“Then why was he here at 4: 10?”

“I couldn’t say. Perhaps he couldn’t sleep. Perhaps he wanted to check the water level himself. Men of his generation—“ Pengelly’s voice took on that particular condescension that Evelyn had learned to recognize, “—they don’t always trust the records. They want to see with their own eyes.”

Evelyn said nothing. She was looking at the watch again, at the cracked glass, at the hands that pointed so precisely to a time when no honest man should have been on these steps.

“Miss Marsh.” Pengelly’s voice had shifted, grown slightly sharper. “I will record this as an accidental death. A fall, in the dark, on a known hazard. The coroner’s court will concur. There is no evidence of anything else.”

She met his eyes. He was watching her with an expression she could not quite read—something between warning and impatience.

“The watch,” she said. “It stopped at 4: 10.”

“Yes. As watches do, when they’re struck.”

“Was it struck? Or did it stop because the Captain—“

“I couldn’t say.” He cut her off, smoothly, as if she had not been about to say anything significant. “The mechanism may have been damaged in the fall. Or the Captain may have been checking the time when he slipped. It’s impossible to determine now. What we can determine is the cause of death: a blow to the head, sustained in a fall. The verdict will reflect that.”

He tucked the writing-slate more firmly under his arm and gave a curt nod to Dr. Hockin, who snapped his medical bag shut. The sound was loud in the quiet air.

Evelyn remained where she was. The water was closer now, creeping up the steps with the patient hunger of the tide. She could feel its presence at her back, cold and ancient, rising as it had risen for centuries before there were steps here, before there was a harbour, before there were men to name themselves captains and build quays and keep records.

“Miss Marsh.”

She turned. Alderman Walter Rowe was standing at the top of the steps, his greatcoat buttoned against the cold, a cigar smouldering between his fingers. He was a broad man, solid as the granite beneath his feet, with the heavy face and careful voice of someone accustomed to being listened to. He had been on the harbour board for twenty years, and he had never once, in Evelyn’s experience, allowed a matter of inconvenience to become a matter of controversy.

“Alderman.”

She climbed the steps to join him, Pengelly following. At the top, the small crowd still lingered, watching, waiting for someone to tell them what had happened and what it meant.

“A sad business.” Rowe shook his head, his voice carrying the weight of official sorrow. “Captain Trewin was a good man. A fine captain. He’ll be missed.”

“Yes, Alderman.”

“Mr. Pengelly has explained the circumstances, I trust?”

“He has. An accidental fall.”

“Precisely.” Rowe’s eyes moved to her face, and she saw in them the same warning she had seen in Pengelly’s—though softer, more reasonable, more dangerous for its reasonableness. “A tragedy, but an understandable one. The steps are treacherous. We’ve all known it. I’ve proposed a railing a dozen times, but the board—“ He spread his hands, a gesture of helplessness that was also, somehow, a claim of innocence. “Well. Perhaps now they’ll listen.”

Evelyn said nothing. Her fingers were tight around her notebook, her pencil cold against her palm.

“The harbour must go on, Miss Marsh.” Rowe’s voice had dropped, just slightly, so that only she could hear. “We depend on trade, on reputation. A death is sad, but it need not be a scandal. Do you understand?”

She understood. She had always understood. The harbour was more than a place; it was a system, an organism, a thing that breathed and moved and required certain conditions to survive. Scandal was a disease. Questions were infections. The board, and the aldermen, and the merchants who made their living from the water—all of them required the appearance of order, even when the reality was something else.

“I understand, Alderman.”

“Good.” He nodded, satisfied. “You’ll want to make a record, of course. That’s your duty, and I would never ask you to neglect it. But the record should reflect what we know: a fall, an accident, a sad loss. Nothing more.”

“Nothing more,” she repeated.

She was looking past him, down the steps, to where the water had nearly reached the place where Captain Trewin had lain. Soon it would cover the stone entirely, washing away the blood, the signs, the evidence of whatever had happened here in the dark. The tide was patient. It erased what it touched.

But the watch. The watch with its cracked face and frozen hands, pointing to 4: 10.

“Miss Marsh?” Rowe was watching her, his eyes narrowed slightly. “Is there something else?”

She thought of the ledger on her desk, the morning’s calculations, the neat rows of times and heights that she had recorded with such care. She thought of the lock gate, the great iron barrier that kept the sea at bay, that opened and closed on a schedule she knew as well as her own heartbeat. She thought of the Captain, who had walked these steps a thousand times, who knew their danger better than anyone, who would not have come here in the dark without a reason.

“No, Alderman,” she said. “There’s nothing else.”

Rowe nodded again, satisfied, and turned away to speak with Pengelly. The crowd began to disperse, murmuring among themselves, carrying the news up the hill to the houses and shops and taverns where it would be repeated and reshaped until it became the story that Porthkennick needed to hear: an accident, a fall, a tragedy, but nothing more.

Evelyn remained where she was. She watched them go, and she watched the water rise, and she watched the place where Captain Trewin’s body had lain until Pengelly’s men had come to carry it away.

Her notebook was open in her hand. Her pencil was poised above the empty page.

She wrote: Captain Silas Trewin, deceased. Found at foot of lock steps, 6 November 1923, approximately 6: 15 AM. Pocket-watch stopped at 4: 10.

She underlined the time. Twice.

Then she closed the notebook and turned back towards her office. The morning’s tide calculations were waiting on her desk, and there were records to be checked, figures to be verified, discrepancies to be noted.

The water continued to rise behind her, patient and cold, covering the steps where a man had died.
