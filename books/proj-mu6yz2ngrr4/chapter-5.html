Chapter 5

The Nephew's Debt

Daniel Trewin stood at the quayside, his silhouette a stark cut-out against the flat, grey sea. The wind carried the smell of dead fish and wet rope. Evelyn Marsh watched him from a distance. His shoulders were hunched, his hands deep in the pockets of a coat that hung too loose on his frame. He looked less like a man in mourning than a man waiting for something, though what that something might be she could not say.

She had spent the morning turning over Tom Keast’s lies. The lock-keeper’s alibi had crumbled the moment she checked the maintenance roster. No record of a sluice gate inspection. No reason for him to be anywhere near the lock at 4: 10 AM. Someone had opened that gate a second time, and Keast knew who. Or perhaps he was the who.

But Keast was a functionary. A man who followed orders and kept his head down. If he was lying, he was lying for someone else. That meant looking higher up the chain. That meant looking at who stood to gain.

She crossed the wet cobblestones toward Daniel Trewin. The gulls cried overhead, their voices sharp against the steady wash of the tide. The harbour was quiet at this hour, the fishing boats already out and the merchant vessels not yet due. A few figures moved along the far quay, but here there was only Daniel and the grey water and the boat that had belonged to his uncle.

The Tidewatch rode at her moorings, her paint faded, her decks showing the wear of a season without proper maintenance. Captain Silas Trewin had kept her tidy, but tidiness cost money, and money had apparently been in short supply.

Evelyn came to stand beside Daniel at the iron railing. He did not turn. His knuckles were white where he gripped the cold metal.

“Mr. Trewin,” she said. “I was sorry to hear of your uncle’s passing.”

His shoulders stiffened. He turned his head just enough to show his profile. The skin around his eyes was red, whether from grief or sleeplessness she could not tell. His jaw worked for a moment before he spoke.

“Miss Marsh.” His voice was hoarse. “I heard you’d been asking questions. About the lock. About that night.”

“I have. It’s my job.”

“Your job.” He let out a breath that was almost a laugh. “Uncle Silas always said you were thorough. He respected that, you know. Even when you found things people preferred stayed hidden.”

Evelyn let the silence settle between them. The wind tugged at her coat. She watched his hands on the railing, the way his grip tightened and loosened in rhythm with his breathing.

“Captain Trewin was well-liked,” she said. “The harbour will feel his absence.”

“He was the harbour.” Daniel’s voice cracked. “Forty years he gave to this place. Every tide, every vessel, every dispute between the fishermen and the merchants. He knew where the channels ran deep and where the sandbars shifted. And now—“ He gestured vaguely toward the water. “Now they’ll carve up his responsibilities and hand them to men who care more about ledgers than lives.”

“That must be difficult. To see his legacy treated that way.”

Daniel turned to face her fully. His eyes were bloodshot, his face drawn. “You didn’t come here to offer condolences, Miss Marsh. What do you want?”

She met his gaze. “I want to understand your uncle’s state of mind in his final days. Whether anything was troubling him. Whether he seemed himself.”

“Troubling him?” Daniel’s laugh was bitter. “The harbour board was squeezing him for every penny of berth fees. The fishermen were complaining about the lock schedule. Pengelly was—“ He stopped. His jaw clicked shut.

Evelyn let the name hang in the air. “Josiah Pengelly?”

Daniel’s face paled. The change was subtle, a slight draining of color from cheeks already pale with fatigue, but she caught it. His hands tightened on the railing.

“Pengelly,” he repeated, the name snatched by the wind. His eyes darted to the Tidewatch, as if calculating its worth against what he owed. “What about him?”

“I understand he and your uncle had dealings. Business matters.”

“Business.” Daniel’s voice was flat. “Yes. Business.”

“And you? Do you have dealings with Mr. Pengelly?”

The silence stretched. A gull screamed overhead. Daniel’s throat moved as he swallowed.

“The debt is manageable,” he said. The words came too quickly, rehearsed. “A temporary difficulty. Uncle Silas was helping me resolve it.”

“Helping you.”

“He was my uncle. Family helps family.” Daniel’s voice rose. “What are you implying, Miss Marsh? That I had something to do with his death? That I wanted him—“

“I’m implying nothing. I’m asking questions. That’s my job.”

He stared at her. His chest rose and fell with shallow breaths. For a moment she thought he might say more, might let something slip. But he pressed his lips together and turned back to the sea.

“You should talk to Pengelly,” he said quietly. “If you want to understand the pressures my uncle was under. He wasn’t the only one who owed money. Half the harbour is in debt to that man. He holds notes on three fishing boats, two warehouse leases, and half the nets on the east quay. When Pengelly calls in a debt, people pay. One way or another.”

“And your debt? Will you be able to pay?”

Daniel’s hands trembled on the railing. He pulled them away and thrust them deep into his pockets.

“Uncle Silas left me everything,” he said. “The boat. The harbour contracts. His share of the warehouse. It’s enough. It has to be enough.”

Evelyn heard the desperation beneath the words. Not grief, but fear. Not loss, but the terror of a man standing on the edge of ruin with only an inheritance between him and the void.

“I’m sorry for your loss, Mr. Trewin,” she said. “Truly.”

He did not look at her. “Are you?”

She left him standing at the railing, his silhouette shrinking as she walked back along the quay. The wind followed her, cold and salt-laden, carrying the cries of the gulls and the creak of the moored boats.

The harbour board office sat at the end of the main quay, a squat stone building with a slate roof and windows that looked out over the water. Evelyn climbed the steps and pushed through the heavy door. The clerk’s desk was empty, a half-eaten sandwich abandoned on a stack of forms. The clock on the wall showed just past one.

She moved behind the desk and through the door to the records room. The smell of old paper and lamp oil filled the space. Shelves lined the walls, crammed with ledgers, rosters, and logbooks bound in leather and cloth. A lamp burned on the central table, its flame steady in the still air.

Evelyn knew where to look. She had worked with these records for years, cross-referencing her own tide tables against the official logs. The harbour board loan register was kept in a green leather volume on the second shelf, between the berth assignments and the pilot rosters. As a harbour worker with routine access to these records, she had every right to consult them.

She pulled it down and carried it to the table. The pages fell open with practiced ease. She ran her finger down the entries, past the names of fishermen and merchants and harbour officials, until she found what she was looking for.

Trewin, Silas. Four hundred pounds, secured against the vessel Tidewatch and harbour income. Dated eight months prior. Witness mark: S. Trewin. Authorised: Alderman Rowe, Chairman, Harbour Board.

Four hundred pounds. A substantial sum. More than a year’s wages for a working man. She turned the pages forward, looking for any record of repayment. Nothing. The loan remained outstanding.

She sat back and studied the entry. Alderman Rowe’s authorisation was firm and clear, the harbour board stamp pressed neatly beside it. The harbour fund was meant for maintenance and improvements, not personal loans. Yet here was the chairman of the harbour board, authorising a loan to one of its own members.

Her eye caught another entry, further down the page.

Pengelly, Josiah. Loan called. Vessel Lizzie B — a second vessel, smaller than the Morning Star — seized for unpaid debt.

She turned back to the earlier pages, searching for any mention of Daniel Trewin. His name appeared nowhere in the harbour board loan register. But Pengelly’s name appeared often, a steady rhythm of loans made and debts called. The merchant was a moneylender in all but name, using his position to extend credit and then collecting with interest.

Evelyn closed the ledger and sat in the silence of the records room. The pieces were beginning to fit together, though the picture they formed was still unclear.

Captain Trewin had taken a substantial loan from the harbour fund. Daniel Trewin owed money to Josiah Pengelly. The Captain’s death would pass the boat and the harbour contracts to Daniel, giving him the means to pay his debts. But the harbour fund loan would also pass to Daniel, adding to his burden.

Unless someone else had an interest in that loan being repaid. Or in the Captain’s contracts changing hands.

She thought of Tom Keast’s false alibi. The lock gate opening at 4: 10 AM, unrecorded in the official log but present in her own tide tables. Someone had opened that gate. Someone had needed the lock cleared at an hour when no legitimate traffic moved.

The water didn’t lie. But men did.

Evelyn returned the ledger to its shelf and left the records room. The clerk’s desk remained empty. She stepped out into the afternoon light. The sky had darkened while she was inside, clouds rolling in from the sea. Rain would come before evening.

She walked back along the quay. Daniel Trewin was gone from the railing. The Tidewatch still rode at her moorings, her paint faded, her decks worn. A boat that had seen better days, like the man who had owned her.

Evelyn paused at the water’s edge. The tide was rising, the water slapping against the stone steps where Captain Trewin had died. The official verdict was accidental death. A slip on wet stone in the dark. A fall into water too cold to survive.

But the watch had stopped at 4: 10 AM. The same time her tide tables showed a second lock gate opening. The same time Tom Keast claimed to be checking a sluice gate that no maintenance roster recorded.

Coincidence was a convenient explanation. But Evelyn had learned to distrust convenience.

She turned away from the water and walked toward the harbour board office. The lamp was lit in the window, a warm glow against the grey afternoon. She could see figures moving inside, the silhouettes of men in discussion.

Alderman Rowe would be there. He had authorised the loan to Captain Trewin. He wanted the case closed, the verdict accepted, the harbour moving on.

But the harbour was built on secrets. Loans and debts and obligations that never appeared in the official record. Men like Josiah Pengelly, who held half the quay in his ledger. Men like Daniel Trewin, whose grief masked something sharper and more desperate.

And somewhere in the tangle of money and tide and lock gates, a killer waited.

Evelyn’s hand rested on her notebook through her coat pocket. She had copied the entry carefully — the numbers, the names, the dates, the harbour board stamp — all of it proving Captain Trewin had been in debt to the harbour fund. Four hundred pounds. Enough to ruin a man, or to save one.

The question was which direction the money flowed. Had Daniel needed his uncle alive to manage the debt, or dead to inherit the means of paying it? Had Captain Trewin been the victim of his own desperation, or of someone else’s?

She thought of Daniel’s face when she mentioned Pengelly’s name. The color draining away. The quick, rehearsed denial. The debt is manageable.

It was never manageable. Not with men like Pengelly. The debt was a weight that grew heavier with time, pressing down until something broke.

The rain began as she reached the office door, fat drops spattering against the stone. She pushed inside and shook the water from her coat. The clerk looked up from his desk, surprise flickering across his face.

“Miss Marsh. Can I help you?”

“I need to see the Alderman.”

“He’s in a meeting.”

“I’ll wait.”

The clerk hesitated, then gestured toward a bench by the wall. Evelyn sat. The office was warm, the air thick with the smell of pipe smoke and wet wool. Through the inner door, she could hear the murmur of voices, too low to make out words.

She opened her notebook and studied the entry she had copied. Four hundred pounds. Eight months ago. Authorised by Alderman Rowe.

The inheritance was larger than she had thought. But so were the debts. Daniel Trewin stood to gain his uncle’s boat and contracts, but he would also inherit the harbour fund loan and whatever he owed to Pengelly. The two motives pulled in opposite directions.

Unless there was a third option. A way to inherit the assets without the liabilities. A way that required Captain Trewin dead and the records of that night obscured.

The lock gate opening at 4: 10 AM. Someone had needed the harbour cleared at that hour. Someone with something to move, or something to hide.

The inner door opened. Alderman Rowe emerged, his face flushed, his collar loosened. Behind him, Evelyn caught a glimpse of another figure, a man in a dark coat, before the door clicked shut.

“Miss Marsh.” Rowe’s voice was carefully neutral. “I wasn’t expecting you.”

“I have questions, Alderman. About Captain Trewin’s loan.”

His eyes flickered to the notebook in her hand. For just a moment, something like concern passed across his face.

“This is hardly the time—“

“There’s no better time. You authorised a four hundred pound loan from the harbour fund. Eight months ago. Captain Trewin was in debt, and his nephew is in debt to Josiah Pengelly. I want to know what it means.”

Rowe’s jaw tightened. He glanced at the clerk, then back at Evelyn.

“Come into my office.”

She followed him through the door. The room was small, dominated by a desk piled with papers. A window looked out over the grey harbour, the rain streaking the glass.

Rowe sat behind the desk. He did not invite her to sit.

“Captain Trewin’s financial affairs are a private matter,” he said. “As is the harbour fund’s lending policy.”

“The harbour fund is public money. The loan register is a public record.”

“Then you already know what you need to know.”

Evelyn remained standing. “I know there was a loan. I know it was substantial. I know Daniel Trewin is desperate enough to lie about his debts. What I don’t know is what any of this has to do with a lock gate opening at 4: 10 AM on the morning his uncle died.”

Rowe’s face went still. The silence stretched between them, broken only by the rain against the window.

“I don’t know what you’re implying, Miss Marsh. But I would advise you to be very careful. The harbour board has accepted the coroner’s verdict. Captain Trewin’s death was a tragedy, nothing more.”

“And the loan?”

“Will be repaid from the estate. As all such loans are.”

Evelyn studied his face. The concern she had glimpsed was gone, replaced by the smooth mask of a man who had spent decades navigating the politics of the harbour.

“Who else knew about the loan?” she asked.

“Captain Trewin’s affairs were his own business.”

“And Daniel’s debt to Pengelly? Is that his own business too?”

Something flickered in Rowe’s eyes. “I cannot speak to Mr. Trewin’s private arrangements.”

“But you know about them.”

“I know many things, Miss Marsh. Knowing is not the same as discussing.”

She let the silence do its work. The rain fell. The lamp on the desk hissed softly.

“If Daniel Trewin owed money to Pengelly,” she said slowly, “and stood to inherit his uncle’s assets, he would have reason to want Captain Trewin’s affairs settled quickly. Quietly.”

“Daniel is a grieving nephew.”

“Daniel is a man with creditors at his door. And Captain Trewin was a man with a secret loan and a boat that was falling apart. Two men under pressure. Two men with something to lose.”

Rowe leaned back in his chair. His fingers drummed once on the desk.

“You are asking questions that could damage reputations, Miss Marsh. Including your own.”

“I’ve never cared much for reputations.”

“So I’ve observed.”

He folded his hands on the desk. When he spoke again, his voice was softer, almost conversational.

“Josiah Pengelly is a powerful man in this harbour. He holds debts on vessels, on warehouses, on livelihoods. When he calls in a loan, people pay. Or they lose everything. Daniel Trewin is not the first man to find himself in that position, and he won’t be the last.”

“And Captain Trewin? Did Pengelly hold something over him too?”

Rowe’s mouth tightened. “Captain Trewin was a proud man. He would not have wanted his affairs discussed.”

“Captain Trewin is dead. His affairs are the only thing that might explain why.”

The Alderman sighed. He looked older suddenly, the lines around his eyes deeper.

“Pengelly wanted the Tidewatch. Captain Trewin refused to sell. That’s all I know. What passed between them in private, I cannot say.”

Evelyn filed the information away. The Tidewatch. Captain Trewin’s boat. The asset that would pass to Daniel. The asset Pengelly might have wanted for himself.

“Thank you, Alderman.”

“Miss Marsh.” Rowe’s voice stopped her at the door. “Be careful. Pengelly is not a man who forgives interference. And Daniel Trewin is a man with nothing left to lose.”

She nodded once and stepped back into the rain. The cold air bit at her face, but she welcomed it. The grief she had witnessed in Daniel Trewin’s eyes had been genuine. But beneath that grief, older and sharper, was something else. A fear that calcified into calculation.

The inheritance would not be enough. The debt would swallow whatever the Captain left behind, and Daniel would be left with nothing but his name and a creditor who did not forgive.

Desperate men did desperate things. And Daniel Trewin, she now understood, was a man with both a motive and a lie to protect it.

But something still didn’t fit. If Daniel had killed his uncle for the inheritance, why open the lock gate at 4: 10 AM? Why falsify the log? The pieces were there, but they didn’t yet form a picture.

She needed to talk to Josiah Pengelly. She needed to understand what the merchant wanted, and what he was willing to do to get it.

The rain fell harder as she walked along the quay. The Tidewatch rode at her moorings, her faded paint dark with water. Somewhere out there, in the grey expanse of the harbour, the answer waited.

Evelyn Marsh had never let a question go unanswered. She wasn’t about to start now.
