Chapter 7

Tide Tables and Tampered Evidence

The brass handle of Pengelly’s office door was cold, and turned with a reluctant scrape. Evelyn stood on the threshold of the cluttered warehouse office, where ledgers stacked like precarious cliffs cast long shadows in the grey morning light. The receipt from the chandler’s window had settled into her understanding overnight. A fact, yes. But facts alone did not constitute guilt, and Pengelly’s anger, however loud, might yet prove to be nothing more than the bluster of a man who felt wronged by the world. Murder required a different kind of silence.

She had not taken the receipt. That would have been theft, and Evelyn Marsh was many things, but not a thief. She had memorized what she needed: the date, the time, the item. November 16th. 8: 15 AM. Heavy-duty mooring rope, twelve fathoms. The morning after Captain Trewin’s body was found on the lock steps.

“You’re early, Miss Marsh.”

Josiah Pengelly’s voice preceded him up the wooden stairs. He emerged from the stairwell with a ledger tucked under one arm, his weather-beaten face creased in what might have been suspicion or simply the habitual squint of a man who had spent decades reading the horizon. His hands, thick and knotted with old injuries, gripped the book with possessive strength.

“Mr. Pengelly. I need to verify a coal delivery invoice against the tide charts. The harbourmaster wants the accounts reconciled before the month ends.”

It was not precisely a lie. Alderman Rowe had been pressing for cleaner books, anything to present to the borough council that showed Porthkennick’s harbour operating with its usual efficient precision. The timing was Evelyn’s own addition.

Pengelly set his ledger on the crowded desk, shoving aside a tin of tobacco and a scatter of loose papers. “Coal delivery. That would be the SS Merriman’s run on the fifteenth.”

“The fifteenth, yes. I have the tide tables showing her docking at fourteen hundred, but the invoice lists an earlier arrival.”

“Rowe’s been at you about discrepancies.” It was not a question. Pengelly moved to the window, his back to her, and she watched his reflection in the grimy glass. “The man wants everything tidy. Doesn’t like loose ends.”

Evelyn did not respond to that. She moved to the desk, her eyes tracking across the surface. Papers, receipts, a half-empty ink bottle, a calendar open to November. “May I?”

“Go ahead. I’ll find the Merriman manifest.” Pengelly turned toward a filing cabinet in the corner, his back still largely to her. “Should be under M for merchant vessels, unless someone filed it under S for ship, which they shouldn’t have, but which they probably did.”

The office smelled of tar and damp wool, the accumulated scent of a working quay. A narrow window let in the grey Cornish light, and from below came the muffled sounds of the warehouse floor, men shouting orders, the creak of rope and pulley. Evelyn’s fingers moved across the desk with deliberate casualness, straightening a stack of receipts while her eyes read dates and items. November 14th. November 15th.

“Find it?” Pengelly called from the cabinet, his head half-buried in a drawer.

“Not yet.”

November 16th. The morning after. She did not need to look for it. She knew it existed. But finding it, seeing it in his possession, among his papers, that was something else. That was evidence of a different order.

A shout came from below. “Mr. Pengelly! The winch is jammed again!”

Pengelly swore under his breath, a low, practiced curse. He straightened from the cabinet, his face darkening. “Every bloody time. I told them to oil the gears yesterday.” He moved toward the door, then paused, looking back at Evelyn. “Five minutes. Don’t rearrange my files.”

“I wouldn’t dream of it.”

His footsteps retreated down the wooden stairs, heavy and deliberate. Evelyn counted to ten, then to twenty. The sounds from the warehouse floor grew more urgent, Pengelly’s voice joining the chorus of shouts. He would be occupied for several minutes at least.

She worked quickly but without haste. The ledger he had set on the desk when he arrived, the one he had been carrying under his arm, that was where she would start. Men kept their most recent concerns closest. She opened it.

Shipping manifests. Cargo lists. Dates and figures in Pengelly’s cramped handwriting. She turned pages with careful fingers, leaving no trace of her passage. November 12th. November 13th. November 14th. A receipt tucked between the pages for the fifteenth, a loose slip of paper that had no business being there unless someone wanted it hidden but accessible.

She slid it free.

Printed header: “Hitchens & Sons, Chandlers, St. Ives.” Date: “November 16th.” Time: “8: 15 AM.” Item: “Mooring rope, heavy-duty, 12 fathoms, hemp core, tarred.” Price: a sum that would sting a working man’s pocket. Payment: received in cash.

Evelyn’s thumb traced the embossed stamp of the chandlery. Her mind calculated distances. St. Ives was an hour’s sail in good weather, less if a man rowed hard with the tide. A purchase at 8: 15 AM meant leaving Porthkennick no later than 7: 00, assuming he sailed. Earlier, if he rowed. The body had been discovered at 6: 30 AM, when the harbour master’s assistant found it on his morning rounds. By 8: 15, Josiah Pengelly had been in St. Ives, buying rope.

She thought of Captain Trewin’s watch, stopped at 4: 10 AM. The official verdict, death by misadventure. The convenient lie that let everyone return to their business. But someone had opened the lock gates at 4: 10, and that opening was not in the official log. Only in her tide ledger, her private record of the harbour’s comings and goings. Two openings that night: one at 23: 45, when the evening tide permitted, and one at 4: 10, when the early morning tide made it necessary.

If someone had needed the lock opened at 4: 10, they had also needed Captain Silas Trewin on those steps.

The footsteps on the stairs returned before she could process the full implication. Evelyn slid the receipt back into the ledger, precisely where she had found it, and turned her attention to the coal delivery manifest that lay on the desk’s corner, the legitimate excuse for her presence.

Pengelly entered with a scowl. “Bloody fools. I told them the winch needed grease, but do they listen? They do not.” He crossed to the desk, his eyes moving across the surface, checking. His gaze lingered on the ledger he had set down earlier, still open to the page where she had replaced the receipt. “Find what you needed?”

“The Merriman invoice shows a discrepancy of forty minutes. I’ll note it in the harbourmaster’s report.” Evelyn closed her own notebook. “Though I wonder, Mr. Pengelly, if you might help me with something else.”

His eyes narrowed. “What’s that?”

“The morning after Captain Trewin died. November 16th. You were at Hitchens & Sons in St. Ives at 8: 15, purchasing a new mooring rope. Heavy-duty. Twelve fathoms.”

The silence that followed was not theatrical. Pengelly’s face did not change, not immediately. He stood very still, and in that stillness, Evelyn saw the calculation behind his eyes, the rapid assessment of what she knew and how she knew it.

“I don’t recall mentioning my shopping trips in the harbour reports.”

“No. But receipts have a way of being kept. And you keep yours in your ledger, Mr. Pengelly. Tucked between the pages.”

His hand moved, almost involuntarily, toward the ledger, then stopped. The mask of affable irritation he had worn since her arrival hardened into something colder. “You’ve been busy, Miss Marsh.”

“I’m a clerk. Keeping records is what I do.”

He picked up the ledger, found the receipt, pulled it free. He held it between his fingers, looking at it as if seeing it for the first time, or perhaps seeing it with new eyes, understanding what it looked like to someone who did not know his reasons.

“The old rope was frayed,” he said. “I noticed it the week before. Meant to replace it, but you know how it is. Things pile up. Then Trewin had his accident on the steps, and I thought, well, better safe than sorry. Don’t want anyone else going the same way.”

“Routine maintenance.”

“Exactly.”

“The timing seems urgent. You were at the chandlery when it opened. The morning after a man died on the lock steps.”

Pengelly set the receipt on the desk, his hand flat upon it. “I’m an early riser. Always have been. And I had business in St. Ives regardless. The rope was one item on a list.”

“What happened to the old rope?”

“Thrown it. Cut up for fenders, probably. Or burned. I don’t keep track of rubbish.”

Evelyn watched his hands. They were thick and capable, the hands of a man who had worked the sea and then worked the shore, hands that knew rope and net and winch. Hands that could have hauled a man up the lock steps and left him there, if that was what was required.

“And the debt Captain Trewin supposedly owed you,” she said. “The one you argued about in the canteen the week before he died.”

Pengelly’s face darkened. “That was between him and me. A private matter.”

“It seemed to have become a public one. There were witnesses to your argument.”

“He owed me money.” The words came out hard, bitten off. “A significant sum. For supplies I fronted on his behalf. He promised to pay, and he didn’t, and when I pressed him, he laughed. Said I could wait. Said I had no choice but to wait.” Pengelly’s voice dropped. “But I didn’t kill him for it. I wanted my money, not his death. A dead man can’t pay his debts.”

“No. But a dead man’s debts can be written off as a loss, and the harbour can move on.”

“Is that what you think? That I’m some kind of murderer over a few pounds?” Pengelly stepped closer, his anger rising, the theatrical fury she had seen before. “I’ve been at this harbour for thirty years, Miss Marsh. I’ve argued with half the captains on the Cornish coast and done business with the other half. I’ve shouted and sworn and threatened, and I’ve never laid a hand on any man in anger. You can ask anyone.”

“I might do that.”

“Ask away. Ask Tom Keast, if you can find him. Ask the harbourmaster. Ask anyone.” Pengelly’s voice was loud now, filling the small office. “But don’t come into my warehouse accusing me of murder based on a receipt for rope.”

Evelyn picked up her notebook. “I haven’t accused you of anything, Mr. Pengelly. I’ve asked questions. That’s my job, too. Keeping records. Noticing when things don’t add up.”

She moved toward the door. Behind her, she heard him breathe, a long, slow exhalation that might have been relief or might have been the regathering of control.

“Miss Marsh.”

She turned.

“The old rope. If you’re looking for it, you won’t find it. I cut it up myself, the morning I got back with the new one. Used it for fenders on the Morning Star. They’re hanging there now, if you want to see.”

“I might do that, too.”

She descended the stairs, her hand trailing along the rough wooden railing. The warehouse floor was busy now, men working the winch that had jammed, Pengelly’s shout still echoing in the rafters. No one looked at her as she crossed to the door and stepped out onto the quay.

The morning air was cold and tasted of salt and coal smoke. Gulls wheeled overhead, their cries sharp against the constant murmur of the harbour. Evelyn walked toward the water’s edge, her mind turning over what she had learned.

Pengelly had purchased rope the morning after Trewin died. He admitted it. He had a reason, or at least a story: frayed rope, routine maintenance. And he had disposed of the old rope, cut it up, turned it into fenders. If there had been evidence on that rope, evidence of what had happened on the lock steps at 4: 10 AM, that evidence was now gone.

But the timing gnawed at her. A purchase at 8: 15 AM, within two hours of the body’s discovery. That suggested urgency. That suggested a man who had seen something, or done something, and needed to remove the proof before anyone thought to look for it.

She thought of Tom Keast, the lock-keeper whose story did not fit the tide tables. He had claimed to be checking the sluice gate at the time of the second opening, but the maintenance roster showed no such check scheduled. He had lied, and his lie was provably false. Now Pengelly had his own lie, or his own convenient truth, about a rope replaced just in time.

The official verdict was death by misadventure. Alderman Rowe wanted the case closed, the books balanced, the harbour returned to its orderly routine. But Evelyn’s books did not balance. Her tide ledger showed two openings, and the official log showed one. Her memory showed a dead man’s watch stopped at 4: 10, and the official record showed an accident at some indeterminate hour, the details smoothed over for convenience.

She stopped at the quay’s edge, looking down at the water. The Morning Star rode low at its mooring, Pengelly’s fishing boat, its hull dark with recent catch. And there, along the gunwales, she saw them: the fenders he had spoken of, rough-cut sections of rope, tarred and knotted, hanging like forgotten sins.

The original rope was lost. But someone had opened those lock gates at 4: 10 AM. Someone had needed Captain Trewin on those steps, and someone had left him there to die, or had put him there after death, arranging the scene to look like an accident.

Evelyn turned away from the water. She had work to do. The coal delivery invoice was genuine, and the discrepancy was real, and she would report it as she had said she would. But she would also write something else, in her own private ledger, in the margins where she kept her observations.

Pengelly. Rope. November 16th, 8: 15 AM.

And beneath it, a question that would not leave her: Why was replacing it so urgent?

She began to walk toward the harbourmaster’s building, her steps measured, her face composed. Behind her, the harbour went about its business, the tide rising, the boats rocking at their moorings, the gulls crying overhead. The world did not stop for one dead captain, or for one clerk with too many questions. But somewhere in this harbour, in the gap between the official record and the truth, a murderer waited. And Evelyn Marsh intended to find them.
