Chapter 16

Scattered Evidence, Undeniable Sequence

The harbour outside her window had become a crime scene she could measure. Every fact she needed was already in her possession, scattered across the desk like wreckage from a single vessel. Tide charts, pinned at the corners by inkwells and a cold teacup. The lock-keeper’s log, open to the night of April 11th. Her private ledger, its pages soft with use. Captain Trewin’s watch, cracked glass over a face frozen at 4: 10. And the rope receipt, creased from being folded into a coat pocket, its ink faded but legible.

She had spent the morning arranging and rearranging these papers. The afternoon would be given to the work itself.

From her satchel she produced the remaining documents: the interview notes from her conversations with Tom Keast, the statements taken from Josiah Pengelly, the ferry schedules she had copied from the Penzance office, and the tide tables for the week of April 11th. These she laid in a row along the edge of the desk, leaving the center clear. Then she took a fresh sheet of foolscap and drew a horizontal line across it. Above the line she wrote times, beginning at 23: 00 and ending at 05: 00, in half-hour increments. Below the line she would place what each person had done, or claimed to have done, at each interval.

The first entry was simple. At 23: 45, the lock gate had been opened. This was recorded in both the official log and her ledger. Keast had operated the gate to allow the evening fishing boats in. Trewin had been seen alive on the quay at roughly that time, walking in the direction of the lock steps. That was where the night began.

She wrote “23: 45 — Lock gate opened (Keast). Trewin seen on quay.”

The next documented event was the discovery of Trewin’s body at dawn, roughly 05: 30. Between 23: 45 and 05: 30 lay the dark interval where the murder had occurred. Her tidal calculations, completed and verified the previous day, had narrowed that window with the precision of a closing vice. The two-knot ebb current, the position where the body was found against the harbour wall, the water temperature and its effect on lividity: all of it pointed to water entry between 03: 30 and 04: 15. The watch, stopped at 4: 10, sat at the center of that window like a stone in a stream.

She wrote “04: 10 — Trewin’s watch stops. Body enters water.”

And below that, in smaller letters: “Tidal window: 03: 30–04: 15.”

Now the suspects. She disliked the word. It implied suspicion without proof, a feeling rather than a fact. But she had three men whose accounts of that night did not satisfy her, and the tide would satisfy only one of them, or none.

Keast first. The lock-keeper had stated he opened the gate at 23: 45, logged it, and returned to his cottage. He claimed he did not hear the gate again and did not open it a second time. But her ledger recorded a second opening at 04: 10, tidally necessary to equalise the dropping water level in the inner basin. The gate had been opened. Keast had not recorded it. Either he had slept through it, which was possible but unlikely for a man whose cottage adjoined the gate mechanism, or he had opened it himself and failed to log it, or someone else had opened it. His story did not fit the tide table, but his failure to log the second opening was not, by itself, proof of murder. It was proof of negligence, or complicity, or fear. She set his name aside on the foolscap with a question mark.

Rowe next. The Alderman’s movements that night were documented in the harbourmaster’s building. He had been in his office until shortly after midnight, then had walked to the ferry terminal and taken the last Penzance crossing at 00: 30. The ferry logs confirmed his arrival on the far side at 01: 15. He had no documented return to Porthkennick until the 06: 15 morning ferry. The window of 03: 30 to 04: 15 found him, by every available record, across the bay. His motive was political: Trewin had opposed the harbour development scheme, and Rowe wanted the case closed. But motive without opportunity was merely a grievance. The ferry schedules, those impersonal timetables she had copied so carefully, placed Rowe beyond reach of the lock steps during the murder window.

She wrote “Rowe — Penzance, confirmed by ferry log. Eliminated.”

Her pen paused over the third name. Josiah Pengelly. The warehouse owner’s account of the night had been specific. He had stated that he was aboard his boat, the Morning Star, from approximately 23: 00 until dawn. He had been checking his nets and gear, he said, preparing for the morning tide. He had heard the lock gate open at 23: 45, which was consistent with Keast’s logged entry. He had not heard it again. He had not seen Trewin. He had not left the boat.

But the Morning Star, she now noted, rode low in the water that night. Pengelly had been loading something. And the rope receipt, which she had obtained from the chandler’s shop two streets back from the quay, was dated April 11th and timed at 03: 45 AM.

She picked up the receipt and held it beneath the green-shaded lamp. The chandler’s handwriting was cramped but precise. The entry read: “1 length 3-strand manila, 12 fathoms. Paid. 03: 45, 11 Apr.” The paper was stamped with the shop’s mark. There was no ambiguity in it.

Pengelly had claimed to be on his boat from 23: 00 until dawn. The receipt placed him at the chandler’s shop at 03: 45, on the quay road, on foot, fifteen minutes before the tidal window opened and twenty-five minutes before Trewin’s watch stopped. He had not been on the Morning Star. He had been ashore, within two hundred yards of the lock steps at 03: 45, inside the tidal murder window of 03: 30 to 04: 15.

She set the receipt down and pressed her fingertips against the desk’s edge. The wood was cool and solid under her hands. She stared at the timeline she had built and felt the competing explanations she had carried for days begin to collapse, one by one, under the weight of the tide’s arithmetic.

Could Keast have done it? He had opportunity. He was near the gate. But the rope receipt meant nothing to him, and his failure to log the second opening, while suspicious, was not corroborated by any physical evidence placing him on the lock steps at 04: 10. His story did not fit the tide table, but neither did it fit the receipt. A case against Keast would rest on the absence of a log entry. That was not enough.

Could Rowe have done it? The ferry logs were clear. She had checked them twice. The last crossing departed at 00: 30. The first morning crossing departed at 06: 15. No private boat could have crossed the bay in a two-knot ebb without being seen from the coastguard station, and the coastguard night log recorded no traffic between 01: 00 and 05: 00. Rowe was eliminated.

Pengelly remained. His alibi, that he had been on his boat, was directly contradicted by a dated, timed receipt from an independent shop. His presence at the chandler’s at 03: 45 placed him on the quay road at the opening of the tidal window. The lock steps were a four-minute walk from the chandler’s door. Trewin, who kept irregular hours and was known to walk the harbour at night, could have been met there by arrangement or by chance. The watch stopped at 04: 10. The tide placed the body in the water between 03: 30 and 04: 15. Pengelly was ashore, unaccounted for, within walking distance, during every minute of that interval.

No other suspect could be placed at the chandlery at 03: 45. No other suspect’s alibi was directly contradicted by a physical document. No other suspect had reason to be on the quay road, on foot, in the hours before dawn.

She wrote “Pengelly — chandler’s receipt, 03: 45. Alibi false. Present at quay during murder window.”

The foolscap was no longer a blank field. It was a sequence, each entry locked to the next by times and tides that no human authority could alter. She read it through, from 23: 45 to 05: 30, and the sequence held. There were no gaps. There were no contradictions. The tide had done what witnesses would not: it had fixed the moment of death within a window of forty-five minutes, and the receipt had placed the only unaccounted suspect inside that window.

But the second lock opening still troubled her. She pulled Keast’s statement toward her and read it again. He had said he did not hear the gate a second time. He had said he did not open it. Yet the gate had been opened at 04: 10, because the tide demanded it, and because her ledger recorded it. If Keast had not opened it, who had? And if Keast had opened it but failed to log it, why?

She closed her eyes and reconstructed the night from Pengelly’s perspective. He had been on the quay road at 03: 45. He had encountered Trewin at or near the lock steps. What happened next was not visible to her, but the outcome was: Trewin in the water, his watch broken and stopped at 04: 10, his body carried by the ebb to where it would be found at dawn. Pengelly needed to be elsewhere when the body was discovered. He needed to have been on his boat, as he claimed, all night.

The lock gate’s opening at 04: 10 was tidally necessary. It was also audible. The sound of the gate’s winch carried across the inner basin to the moorings where the Morning Star lay. If Pengelly opened that gate himself, or arranged for it to be opened, the sound would travel to the boats and to Keast’s cottage. Keast, hearing it, would log it as a matter of routine. The log entry would read: “04: 10 — Lock gate opened, tidal equalisation.” That entry would be a public record, a timestamp confirming that the gate had operated at 04: 10, and anyone aboard a boat in the basin could claim to have heard it.

That was the alibi Pengelly had intended to build. The sound of the gate at 04: 10 would place him on the water, hearing it, the way a man ashore could not. The log entry would confirm the time. The routine of the harbour would do the rest.

But Keast had not logged it. Whether he slept through the sound, or heard it and chose not to record it out of some fear or favour, the entry was never made. The official log showed only the 23: 45 opening. Pengelly’s intended alibi, the audible timestamp of the second gate opening, existed only in Evelyn’s private ledger. The mechanism he had built to place himself on the water at 04: 10 had failed because the one man who should have recorded it did not.

Pengelly had not planned to move the body with the second opening. The tide would carry it where it would. He had planned to create a sound, a public event, a logged timestamp that would say: the gate opened at 04: 10, and I heard it from my boat. Instead, he had opened a gate that no one recorded, and the only proof of its opening lived in a tide clerk’s private calculations.

She opened her eyes. The office had grown dark around her. The green lamp cast a circle of light across the desk, across the foolscap with its careful rows, across the receipt and the watch and the ledger. The harbour beyond the window was a field of dim shapes: the moored boats, the warehouse roofs, the dark line of the breakwater against a sky still holding the last grey light of dusk.

She stood and crossed to the window. The glass was cold against her forehead when she leaned it there. Her breath fogged a small circle on the pane, and through it she could see the lock gates, closed now, holding the basin water still against the ebb. Somewhere beyond those gates, in the channel where the tide ran its two-knot current, the evidence she had compiled had been moving since the night of April 11th. It had moved through the water, through the logs, through the receipts and the ferry schedules and the cracked face of a dead man’s watch. It had moved until it reached her desk, and she had caught it, and held it still, and read it.

The case was solved. Not by confession, not by witness, but by the tide’s mechanics and a shopkeeper’s dated receipt. The sequence was complete. Josiah Pengelly had been ashore at 03: 45. He had been at the lock steps when Trewin’s watch stopped at 04: 10. He had opened or arranged the second gate opening to create a sound he could claim to have heard from his boat. Keast’s failure to log it had broken the alibi, but the murder had already been done.

She turned from the window. The last shaft of cold light fell across her desk, falling on the foolscap timeline and the scattered evidence she had spent the day assembling. She sat down and opened her private ledger to a fresh page. The pages were faintly yellowed, the lines faint. She took up her pen and dipped it into the inkwell. The brass caught the lamplight.

She held the nib above the page for a moment. The room was silent except for the distant sound of water against the harbour wall and the settling of the building in the evening cold. Writing the name would make it real. It would transform a private conviction into a record, and a record could be presented, challenged, and used. Rowe’s ultimatum sat in the back of her mind like a stone: the case was closed, the official verdict stood, and her inquiries were to cease. She had been warned. She had been told, in the careful language of institutional pressure, that the harbour’s good depended on her silence.

The pen touched the paper. She wrote the name at the top of the page in clear, unhurried letters.

JOSIAH PENGELLY.

Below it she began to write the sequence, each entry dated and timed, each fact sourced. The 23: 45 opening, logged and verified. Trewin seen on the quay. The tidal window of 03: 30 to 04: 15. The rope receipt timed at 03: 45, placing Pengelly at the chandler’s, ashore, his alibi broken. The watch stopped at 04: 10. The second lock opening, tidally necessary, recorded in her ledger, absent from the official log. Pengelly’s intent: to create an audible, logged event that would place him on his boat at the time of death, claiming to have heard the gate from the water. Keast’s failure to log it: the broken mechanism of a cover-up. The body found at dawn, its position consistent with water entry at 04: 10 and a two-knot ebb.

The nib scratched across the paper. Each sentence was a stone placed in a wall, fitting against the last with no gap. She did not pause. She did not qualify. The tide did not qualify, and neither would she.

When she finished, she read the page through once. The ink was still wet, gleaming in the lamplight. She set the pen down and closed the ledger. The foolscap timeline she folded once and placed inside the front cover.

The office was dark now except for the lamp. The harbour had vanished into the night outside the window, but she knew its shape as precisely as she knew the page she had just written. Every mooring, every channel, every tidal current was fixed in her mind, and fixed on the page, in the order they had occurred on the night Silas Trewin died.

She extinguished the lamp. In the darkness, the room smelled of ink and cold stone and the faint salt of the harbour. She gathered the ledger and the foolscap and placed them in her satchel. The watch she left on the desk, where it would remain until morning, its cracked face still showing 4: 10, the hour the tide had fixed and the killer had failed to erase.

The case was solved. What remained was the cost of saying so, and she would pay that in the board room, before the men who had told her to be silent, with the tide as her witness.

She had reached the door when the doubt caught her—not in the evidence, but in the gaps between it.

Pengelly’s story held a plausible spine. He had been seen on his boat before midnight, and the rope receipt from Purdy’s chandlery was timed at 3: 45, which placed him buying line when Trewin was already dying. If the receipt proved anything, it proved his innocence as neatly as his guilt. A man does not purchase rope in the middle of murdering someone.

Except that the rope was hemp, three-strand, and the length on the receipt was four fathoms—exactly the length that had been found cut and weighted in the channel, where the tide had carried it after the 4: 10 slack. Pengelly had bought the rope to sink the evidence, not to commit the crime. The murder had happened earlier. The receipt’s timing didn’t place him at the killing. It placed him at the cover-up, and the cover-up required the rope to be purchased after the tide turned, because the weighted line had to catch the current at precisely the moment it would drag the body toward the deeper channel.

That was the break. The 3: 45 receipt was not an alibi. It was a timetable for disposal.

And the harbour opening? She had nearly dismissed it. An unlogged gate at 3: 30 should have been invisible—a hidden act, useless as an alibi. But Pengelly hadn’t wanted it hidden. He’d wanted Keast to hear it, to log it, to stamp a public timestamp proving Pengelly’s boat was leaving the harbour at 3: 30. Keast had dozed through it. The alibi had failed because the witness had failed, and the receipt was all that remained—a piece of evidence Pengelly had created to serve as proof, never imagining it would become the instrument of his conviction.

Evelyn stood in the hallway, satchel heavy against her hip. She had written the name. The ink was drying. There was no version of tomorrow in which she could unwrite it, no revision that would restore the safety of uncertainty.

Every clue had converged. The sequence was singular, and it ended with Josiah Pengelly.

She stepped into the night.
