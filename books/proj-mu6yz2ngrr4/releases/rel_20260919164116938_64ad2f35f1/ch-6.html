Chapter 6

Pengelly's Berthing Grudge

Rain beaded on the brass fittings of the Morning Star, the boat riding low with yesterday’s catch. Evelyn Marsh stood at the quayside, her notebook sheltered beneath the flap of her coat, and watched Josiah Pengelly work his deck. He moved with the practiced economy of a man who had spent thirty years hauling wet rope and heavier nets. His hands were thick, knotted with old injuries. He did not look up when her shadow fell across the gunwale.

“Mr. Pengelly.”

He straightened, one hand resting on the winch. “Harbour office.” His voice was flat, unsurprised. “Come to measure my berth again, have you? Count every inch like it was gold leaf.”

“I need to ask you about Captain Trewin.”

That made him turn. His face was weather-beaten, the skin around his eyes creased from decades of squinting against sun and spray. He looked at her with something between amusement and contempt. “Trewin’s dead. Everyone knows that. Coroner called it an accident. What’s the harbour office poking around for?”

“There are some irregularities in the records.”

“Irregularities.” He spat the word over the side, into the black water. “That’s one way to put it. Trewin was irregular, right enough. Refused me a berth three weeks running. Said my boats needed to wait their turn like everyone else. But the Lizzie May got a spot the same week he turned me away, and her owner’s brother-in-law sits on the licensing committee.”

Evelyn had checked the berth allocations. The Lizzie May had indeed received preferential treatment. But that was not why she had come. “I understand there was a dispute.”

“A dispute.” He laughed, a short, sharp bark. “That’s another way to put it. I told him what I thought of him. Right here on this quay, with half the harbour watching. I told him he was a petty tyrant with a memory for grudges and no head for fairness. I told him I’d see him ruined before I’d let him ruin me.” He stepped closer to the gunwale, looming over her. “And now he’s dead. So you’ve come to ask if I killed him.”

The rain was steady now, a fine mist that clung to her eyelashes. She blinked it away. “Did you?”

“No.” He said it simply, without heat. “I wanted him humiliated. I wanted him stripped of his post, exposed for the small man he was. Dead, he’s a martyr. The board will name a prize after him. They’ll put a plaque in the harbourmaster’s office. I couldn’t have designed a worse outcome if I’d tried.”

It was a plausible answer. It fit what she knew of Pengelly: a man whose anger was loud, public, theatrical. He had shouted his grievances to anyone who would listen. He had made threats in front of witnesses. A man who planned murder did not usually announce his motive in advance to half the harbour.

But something in his performance gave her pause. He was too ready with his answers. Each sentence landed like a rehearsed line, delivered with the timing of a man who had practiced this conversation in his head many times.

“Where were you on the night of the seventeenth?”

“Here.” He gestured at the deck. “Asleep in the cabin. The Morning Star was my home that week. My wife and I had a disagreement. I came aboard after supper and didn’t leave until dawn.”

“Did anyone see you?”

“I was alone. The boat was moored at the far end of the quay. No traffic, no passersby. Just me and the tide.” He shrugged. “You can ask at the chandler’s. I bought supplies that evening. Bread, cheese, a bottle of rum. Didn’t leave the boat until I heard the commotion the next morning.”

Evelyn made a note. The alibi was thin but not impossible. The far end of the quay was indeed quiet at night, especially in winter. A man could come and go unseen if he knew the shadows.

“The lock gate was opened twice that night,” she said. “Once at a quarter to midnight, and again at ten past four.”

Pengelly’s expression did not change. “I wouldn’t know about that. I’m a fisherman, not a lock-keeper.”

“The second opening wasn’t logged. Someone opened the gate and didn’t record it.”

“And you think I know something about it because I hated Trewin.”

“I think you had reason to want him out of the way.”

“I had reason to want him sacked. Not dead.” He turned away from her, bending to a coil of rope on the deck. “I’ve told you what I know. If you’re looking for a confession, you won’t find it here. I’m a man who speaks his mind. If I’d killed him, I’d have told the whole harbour why.”

It was the kind of thing a guilty man might say. It was also the kind of thing an innocent man might say. Evelyn watched him work, his hands moving automatically through the knots. His anger seemed genuine enough, but it was the anger of a man who felt wronged, not the fear of a man who had been caught.

Then he straightened, and something shifted in his posture. He reached into his jacket and produced a crumpled piece of paper, which he thrust toward her.

“You want to know what kind of man Trewin was? Look at this.”

She took the paper. It was a receipt, creased and smudged, smelling faintly of fish and engine oil. The ink was faded but legible. She read the date: the eighteenth of November. The morning after Trewin’s death.

“New mooring rope,” she said.

“Sixteen fathoms of manila. Cost me more than a week’s catch.” Pengelly’s voice rose, trembling with fresh indignation. “My old rope was worn, but it would have held another season. I was saving for new nets. Then Trewin refused me a proper berth and I had to moor out in the channel. The swell snapped my line like thread. If I hadn’t been aboard, I’d have lost the boat entirely.”

She studied the receipt. The vendor’s stamp was from a chandler in Penzance, not the local supplier. The time of purchase was recorded: 7: 45 AM. Early, but not impossibly so. The first ferry from Porthkennick to Penzance left at six. He could have made the crossing and returned by mid-morning.

“You bought this the morning after.”

“I had to. I couldn’t leave the Morning Star moored on a broken line. One good squall and she’d have been on the rocks.” He took back the receipt and folded it carefully, as if the paper itself were evidence of his grievance. “That’s what Trewin cost me. Not just a berth. A rope. A week’s wages. And for what? So he could play his petty games with the harbour he thought he owned.”

She understood what he was doing. The receipt was proof, or so he wanted her to think. Proof that he had suffered, that he had reason to hate Trewin, that his anger was justified. But it was also an alibi of sorts. A man who stopped to buy rope in Penzance at a quarter to eight was not standing on the lock steps at ten past four.

Unless he had left Porthkennick after the second opening and arrived in Penzance in time for the shops to open. The tide tables ran through her mind. The morning ferry departed with the flood. If he had been on the quay at four, he could have caught the six o’clock boat easily.

“Mr. Pengelly,” she said, her voice steady. “Where were you between three and five in the morning on the night Captain Trewin died?”

His eyes flickered. It was a small movement, barely perceptible, but she caught it. A moment’s hesitation before he answered. “I told you. Asleep in the cabin.”

“Alone.”

“Alone.” He met her gaze now, and there was something defensive in his posture. His shoulders had drawn up, his chin thrust forward. “I don’t have to account for every hour of my night. I was here. I slept. I woke up to find the harbour in uproar because Trewin had fallen down his own steps. That’s all I know.”

She believed that he believed it, or wanted to. But the hesitation lingered in her mind like the smell of diesel on his clothes. A man who had been asleep would say so immediately, without calculation. A man who had something to hide might pause, just for a moment, to remember which story he had told.

“Thank you for your time, Mr. Pengelly.”

“You’re welcome to it.” He turned back to his rope, dismissing her. “But if you’re looking for Trewin’s killer, you’re wasting your time in the wrong place. I’m not the only one who wanted him gone. Ask around the harbour. You’ll find a queue.”

She left him there, standing on the deck of the Morning Star with his coil of rope and his carefully preserved receipt. The rain followed her up the quay, drumming on the cobbles, running in silver threads through the gutters. She walked without hurry, her mind turning over what she had seen.

Pengelly’s anger was real. She had no doubt of that. He had been humiliated by Trewin, denied a berth that should have been his by right of seniority. His hatred was a matter of public record, shouted across the harbour for anyone to hear. But hatred was not murder. And the receipt, for all his faith in it, proved nothing except that he had bought a rope on the morning after.

The timing troubled her. He had presented it as evidence of his loss, proof that Trewin’s petty tyranny had cost him dear. But the purchase time undercut his claim of immediate crisis. A rope bought at a quarter to eight was a planned purchase, not an emergency. A man whose boat was truly at risk would have sought supplies closer to hand, would have borrowed from a neighbour, would have done anything rather than wait for the first ferry to Penzance.

Unless the rope was not for the Morning Star at all.

The thought came unbidden, and she turned it over carefully. Pengelly had shown her the receipt as proof of his grievance. But receipts could be kept for other reasons. To establish an alibi. To prove a purchase that might otherwise be questioned. To account for money spent on something else entirely.

She was speculating. She had no evidence, only a moment’s hesitation and a receipt that did not quite fit the story he had told. But the discrepancy nagged at her, a loose thread in the fabric of his performance.

The lock office was quiet when she returned. The lamp on her desk cast a small circle of light against the grey afternoon. She sat and opened her lock-keeper’s log, laying the pages flat, and began to write.

Josiah Pengelly, fish-merchant. Motive: public grievance over berth refusal. Alibi: asleep aboard the Morning Star, uncorroborated. Evidence: receipt for mooring rope, dated 18 November, 7: 45 AM, Penzance chandler.

She paused, her pen hovering over the entry. Then she added a notation beneath it, smaller and more precise.

Receipt presented as proof of financial loss. Purchase time suggests prior planning, not emergency. Hesitation when asked about hours 3-5 AM. Anger appears genuine but performative. May be concealing something, or may simply resent the inquiry.

She underlined the last sentence twice, then closed the ledger and sat back in her chair.

The rain had stopped. Through the window, she could see the quay stretching away into the mist, the boats bobbing at their moorings. Somewhere out there, the Morning Star rode the tide, and Josiah Pengelly stood on her deck, nursing his grudges and his carefully folded receipt.

He was a suspect. That much was certain. His motive was clear, his opportunity plausible, his alibi thin. But something about the encounter did not fit. His anger was too loud, too public, too ready for consumption. A man who killed in the dark did not usually announce his hatred in advance. A man who had pushed Captain Trewin down the lock steps would not be so eager to remind everyone of his grievance.

Unless the anger was the point. Unless he wanted everyone to remember how much he hated Trewin, so that when the question of murder arose, the answer would seem too obvious to be true.

She thought of the receipt again. The creases in the paper, the smudged ink, the careful way he had folded it and tucked it back into his pocket. He had kept it for a reason. He had shown it to her for a reason. And the reason, she suspected, had less to do with proving his loss than with establishing his presence elsewhere at a time he could not otherwise account for.

She would check the ferry schedules. She would ask at the Penzance chandler’s shop. She would find out whether Josiah Pengelly had truly crossed the harbour on the morning after Trewin’s death, or whether the receipt was a convenient fiction, a piece of paper bought and paid for to cover a darker truth.

For now, she had three names in her ledger: a lock-keeper with a false log, a nephew with a hidden debt, and a fish-merchant with a grudge and a receipt. Each had reason to want Trewin gone. Each had been somewhere unaccounted-for in the hours before dawn.

The lock gate had opened twice. The second opening had gone unrecorded. Someone had stood on those steps at ten past four, when the tide was turning and the dark was thick over the harbour. Someone had watched Captain Trewin fall, or had helped him on his way.

She closed her eyes and saw the watch again, the cracked glass, the hands frozen at ten past four. The water had been high enough to receive a body. The steps had been slick with winter rain. And somewhere in the darkness, a second opening had been necessary, tidally required, yet invisible in the official record.

The truth was in the ledger. She had always known that. The harbour kept its own accounts, and the numbers did not lie. Whatever stories the suspects told, whatever alibis they produced, the tide had been there before them, rising and falling with the inevitability of time itself.

She opened her eyes and picked up her pen. There was more work to do.
