Chapter 12

Rowe's Ultimatum

────────────────────────────────────────

A summons slip, crisp and official, waited on Evelyn’s ledger when she returned from the canteena square of cream card with the harbour board’s embossed seal and Alderman Rowe’s name typed in a precise, authoritative hand. She stood at her desk, the taste of weak tea still bitter on her tongue, and read the brief instruction: Please attend the Alderman’s office at your earliest convenience. Harbour business.

The words carried no threat in themselves. Harbour business was her business, had been for five years of tides and ledgers and the meticulous recording of Porthkennick’s comings and goings. But the timing prickled at her. Rowe had confiscated her tide ledger, but she still had her tide tables and the private notes she’d scrawled that morning, calculating currents and drift patterns, confirming what she already knew: that the ebb between 4: 10 and dawn on November fourth ran at nearly two knots, more than sufficient to carry a body from the lock steps to where Captain Trewin had been found. The water did not lie. It could not be persuaded or intimidated or bought.

She placed the summons beside her inkwell and removed her pen from her apron pocket. Her fingers were stained from the morning’s work, dark crescents beneath the nails. She ought to wash before presenting herself. Propriety mattered to Alderman Rowe. He had said as often enough at harbour functions, praising the “old-fashioned sentiments” that kept Porthkennick respectable in a changing world.

Evelyn walked to the window instead. The harbour lay grey beneath a November sky, fishing boats riding low at their moorings, the lock gates standing sentinel between the basin and the open channel. Somewhere out there, in the cold water she had studied so intently these past days, lay the answer to a question no one else seemed willing to ask.

She turned from the view, collected her ledger, and left the records room.

The harbourmaster’s building occupied the finest position on the quay, a sturdy granite structure with a slate roof and brass fittings that gleamed even on overcast days. Evelyn climbed the worn stone steps to the first-floor landing, her shoes clicking against flagstones worn smooth by decades of boots. The boardroom door stood closed, its polished oak surface broken only by a small brass plate that read Harbour Board — Private.

She knocked.

“Enter.” Rowe’s voice carried through the wood, muffled but clear.

The room beyond was oppressively warm. A coal fire burned in the grate, its heat radiating outward to meet Evelyn as she stepped across the threshold. The smell of polished oak and fresh ink filled the space, underlaid with the faint sweetness of coal smoke. Heavy curtains framed the windows, though a gap between them revealed the harbour beyond, grey water churning against the quay.

Alderman Walter Rowe sat behind a vast desk of dark wood, its surface clear except for a leather blotter, a fountain pen, and a single sheet of paper faced away from Evelyn. He did not rise at her entrance. His grey suit was immaculate, his moustache trimmed to precise edges, his expression one of patient expectation. A man comfortable with authority, long accustomed to its exercise.

“Miss Marsh.” He gestured to the chair opposite him. “Please sit.”

Evelyn remained standing. “You wished to see me, Alderman. Harbour business, the summons said.”

“So I did. And so it is.” Rowe folded his hands on the blotter, his wedding ring catching light from the fire. “Please. Sit. This may take a moment.”

She lowered herself into the chair, her ledger resting across her knees. The leather was stiff, unyielding. The seat placed her at a disadvantage, her eyes level with the desktop rather than the Alderman’s face. She straightened her spine against the discomfort.

Rowe studied her for a long moment, his expression unreadable. Outside, a gull cried against the wind. The fire popped, sending a spiral of sparks up the flue.

“You have been with us for five years now, Miss Marsh,” he said at last. “Five years of faithful service. Your records are meticulous. Your calculations, I am told, are beyond reproach.”

“Thank you, Alderman.” Evelyn kept her voice level. Praise from Rowe was never freely given. It preceded something else.

“The harbour board values your contribution.” He shifted slightly, settling back against the dark leather. “Which is why I find this recent… preoccupation of yours so troubling.”

Evelyn’s fingers tightened on her ledger. “Preoccupation, Alderman?”

“Your private inquiries. Your questions to harbour staff. Your visits to the lock-keeper, to Mr. Pengelly.” Rowe’s voice remained pleasant, conversational. “I am made to understand you have been conducting what might generously be called an independent investigation into Captain Trewin’s death.”

“I have been reviewing the records, Alderman. That is my duty.”

“Your duty.” Rowe repeated the word as though tasting it. “Yes. Your duty is to maintain accurate records of harbour operations. Tides, lockings, vessel movements. That is what you are paid for, Miss Marsh. That is what this board requires of you.”

“And I have performed that duty without fail.” Evelyn met his gaze. “The records are complete. They are accurate. They show what happened on the night of November third and the morning of November fourth.”

“What they show, Miss Marsh, is a discrepancy that the coroner’s inquest has already resolved.” Rowe’s tone hardened, the old-fashioned sentiment giving way to something colder beneath. “Captain Trewin died by misadventure. He slipped on wet steps in the dark and drowned. That is the official finding. That is the truth of the matter.”

Evelyn’s heart beat faster, but her voice remained steady. “The ledger shows two lock openings, Alderman. The water didn’t lie. The second opening at 4: 10 was tidally necessary. The official log records only one. Someone falsified that record. And Captain Trewin’s watch stopped at 4: 10, precisely when the tide required those gates to open. These are facts. They are recorded in my hand.”

“They are interpretations, Miss Marsh. Your interpretations.” Rowe leaned forward, firelight catching the planes of his face. “The lock-keeper made an error in his log. A regrettable oversight, perhaps occasioned by fatigue or distraction. Captain Trewin’s watch stopped when it struck the water, not before. These are reasonable explanations. The coroner heard them. The board accepted them. The matter is closed.”

“The matter is not closed while the records disagree.”

“The matter is closed because the proper authorities have closed it.” Rowe’s voice sharpened. “You are not a proper authority, Miss Marsh. You are a tide clerk. An excellent one, by all accounts, but a clerk nonetheless. Your function is to record, not to question. To maintain, not to investigate.”

Evelyn felt the words like cold water. She had known her position was precarious. She had felt the weight of Rowe’s attention since she first raised the discrepancy. But hearing it stated so plainly, having her role reduced to obedience, struck something in her that had been steady until now.

She looked past Rowe to the harbour beyond the window. A fishing boat was making its way toward the lock gates, its engine thrumming across the water. Men would be needed to operate the gates, to read the tide, to record the opening in the logs. The harbour’s business would continue regardless of what happened in this room.

“My function,” she said carefully, “is to ensure the accuracy of the harbour’s records. That is what I was hired to do. That is what I have done for five years. The discrepancy exists. It can be verified by anyone who cares to examine my ledger against the lock-keeper’s log. If I ignore it, I fail in my duty.”

“Your duty, Miss Marsh, is to the harbour board.” Rowe’s hand moved to the paper on his desk, his fingers brushing its edge. “The harbour board has determined that no discrepancy of consequence exists. Your continued insistence on this matter damages the reputation of this institution. It suggests impropriety where none exists. It undermines public confidence in the men who operate this harbour, men who have served faithfully for decades.”

“Captain Trewin served faithfully for decades.”

“Captain Trewin is dead.” The words fell like stones. “And nothing you can do will bring him back. Nothing you can discover will change what happened to him on those steps. The only thing your inquiry accomplishes is disruption. Disruption of harbour operations. Disruption of board business. Disruption of the public peace.”

Rowe drew the paper toward him, then slid it across the polished oak desk. The movement was deliberate, unhurried. He set the fountain pen beside it with a soft click.

“I have here a statement, Miss Marsh. A simple document. It affirms your understanding that Captain Trewin’s death was accidental, that any apparent discrepancy in the harbour records resulted from clerical error, and that you will cease all private inquiries into the matter henceforth. It requires only your signature.”

Evelyn looked at the paper. The type was precise, formal, the language careful. I, Evelyn Marsh, tide clerk of Porthkennick Harbour, hereby acknowledge… The words blurred slightly in the firelight.

“If I sign this,” she said slowly, “the discrepancy remains. The records still disagree. The second opening still occurred.”

“The second opening will be recorded as an oversight, corrected in the next edition of the official logs.” Rowe’s voice was patient now, almost kind. “A minor clerical matter, resolved. You may return to your duties with the board’s full confidence. Your position is secure. Your future here is assured.”

Evelyn’s gaze moved from the paper to the fountain pen. The nib gleamed, freshly filled. Rowe’s hand rested near it, waiting.

“And if I do not sign?”

The question hung in the heated air. Rowe’s expression did not change, but something shifted behind his eyes. A hardening, a closing off.

“Then I will be forced to conclude that your continued employment is not in the harbour’s best interest.” He spoke the words without emphasis, as though reading from a ledger. “Insubordination. Conduct detrimental to institutional reputation. Grounds for immediate dismissal.”

“Dismissal.” Evelyn repeated the word. It felt foreign in her mouth.

“The letter of dismissal has already been prepared.” Rowe nodded toward a second document, partially obscured by the first. “You may sign the statement and return to your duties, or you may leave this building as a former employee of the Porthkennick Harbour Board. Those are your options, Miss Marsh. I suggest you choose carefully.”

The fire crackled. The gull cried again outside, its voice thin against the wind. Evelyn sat very still, her ledger pressing against her knees, her ink-stained fingers resting on the leather cover.

Five years. Five years of rising before dawn to record the first lockings, of staying late to verify the final entries, of defending her calculations against the occasional challenges of captains and quartermasters. Five years of knowing that her work mattered, that the harbour depended on accuracy, that truth was not merely a virtue but a necessity for safe navigation. The tides did not forgive errors. The currents did not accommodate carelessness. What she did kept men alive.

And now she was asked to erase a truth she had verified with her own hand.

She looked from the crisp paper to the harbour view behind Rowe. The fishing boat had reached the lock gates. She could see men moving on the quay, preparing to operate the mechanism. The gates would open when the tide required it, whether anyone recorded the fact or not. The water would rise and fall according to forces beyond human control. The truth of what happened existed independent of what Rowe wanted her to say.

“My duty,” Evelyn said quietly, “is to the accuracy of the harbour’s records. I cannot sign a statement that denies a fact I have verified.”

Rowe’s face went still. “You understand what this means.”

“I understand.” She rose from the chair, her legs steady beneath her. “I understand perfectly.”

For a long moment, neither of them spoke. Rowe’s eyes searched her face, looking perhaps for hesitation, for some sign that she would bend. Evelyn met his gaze without blinking.

“Very well.” Rowe drew the dismissal letter from beneath the statement, his movements precise. “Your employment with the Porthkennick Harbour Board is terminated, effective immediately. Please return any harbour property in your possession and vacate the premises by the end of the day.”

Evelyn clutched her ledger to her chest. “This is my personal record. I compiled it myself, in my own hand.”

“It contains harbour data. Operational information. It remains board property.” Rowe’s voice was flat, final. “You may request copies through proper channels, should you have need. Now please go.”

She stood for a moment longer, looking at the man who had ended her career with a signature and a slide of paper across a desk. His face gave nothing away. No triumph, no regret. He had done what was necessary, in his view, to protect the institution he served. The truth was a casualty, but not one he would mourn.

Evelyn placed her ledger on the desk. The gesture felt like surrendering a limb. Five years of careful entries, of tidal calculations, of verified facts. The lock openings. The watch. The discrepancy that proved, beyond any reasonable doubt, that the official story was false.

She turned and walked to the door. Her hand was steady on the brass handle. The corridor beyond was empty, silent. She did not look back.

The stairwell was cool after the oppressive heat of Rowe’s office. Evelyn descended slowly, her fingers trailing along the stone wall. Each step took her further from the position that had defined her adult life. Tide clerk of Porthkennick Harbour. That title had given her purpose, standing, a place in the world. Now she was merely a woman without employment, without authority, without the right to ask questions in an official capacity.

But she still knew what she knew.

The main entrance opened onto the quay. Wind struck her face, cold and salt-laden, carrying the cry of gulls and the smell of seaweed and diesel. The harbour stretched before her, grey water churning against the stone walls, fishing boats rising and falling at their moorings. The lock gates stood open now, the fishing boat passing through into the basin.

Evelyn walked to the railing and gripped the cold metal. Her knuckles whitened. The wind pulled at her hair, her apron, the empty pocket where her pen had rested until moments ago.

She had no position now. No ledger. No official standing. The harbour board had closed ranks against her, protecting its own, preserving the story that kept the institution stable. Rowe had offered her a choice between truth and survival, and she had chosen truth. The cost of that choice was only beginning to settle over her.

But beneath the cost, beneath the loss and the uncertainty, something else stirred. A kind of clarity.

The water did not lie. The tide had carried Captain Trewin’s body to its resting place, and the tide could explain how it got there. She had calculated the current. She knew the drift. She understood what the harbour’s own records proved, even if those records now sat in Rowe’s office, beyond her reach.

The knowledge was hers. No dismissal could take that. No signature on a piece of paper could unmake what she had verified with her own hand.

Evelyn looked out at the lock steps, visible now as the tide receded. Wet stone gleamed in the grey light. The place where Captain Trewin had died, or where someone had needed him to be at 4: 10 in the morning, when the gates opened for the second time that night.

She would find the truth. Not as a tide clerk, not with the authority of the harbour board behind her, but as a woman who had given five years to the meticulous recording of facts. The official path was closed. The institution had made its choice.

Now she would make hers.

Evelyn turned from the railing and walked along the quay, the wind at her back, the salt air sharp in her lungs. Behind her, the Harbour Board office stood solid and implacable, its windows dark, its business concluded. Ahead, the water waited, patient and ancient, holding secrets in its depths.

The tide would turn again tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that. It would rise and fall according to forces no human hand could alter. And when it did, it would tell its story to anyone patient enough to listen.

Evelyn intended to listen.

She stopped at the harbour canteen, more out of habit than hunger. The fisherman’s stew was lukewarm, the bread stale, but she ate mechanically, her mind turning over the interview she had just endured. Rowe’s office—she could still see it: the polished mahogany, the framed charts, the calculated absence of anything personal. He had begun with harbour business, tonnage figures, the usual bureaucratic rhythm. Then the pivot, smooth as a tide change.

But listening would require access, and access required credentials she no longer possessed.

“Your diligence has been noted, Miss Evelyn. As has your… curiosity.”

The accusation hung between them. She had felt the weight of his silence, the stark authority of that office pressing against her composure. No raised voice, no overt threat—just the quiet certainty of a man accustomed to being obeyed.

“Accuracy is my duty, Alderman. The records show a discrepancy at the sea-gate. Two openings in a single night, unlogged. I cannot ignore what the figures demonstrate.”

His hand had moved then, sliding a single sheet of cream paper across the desk. Typed, formal. A letter of resignation, pre-dated, awaiting only her signature.

“The harbour board values loyalty above all else. Sign, and your service record remains unblemished. Refuse…” He had not finished the sentence. He had not needed to.

She had looked at the letter, then at him. Her pen had remained in her pocket. Five years of tide calculations, of meticulous entries, of pre-dawn mornings verifying depths and draughts—and now the cost of that devotion lay before her in black and white.

“My duty is to the truth, Alderman. Not to convenience.”

His expression had not changed. “Then you leave me no choice. Your appointment is terminated, effective immediately.”

The words had struck like a physical blow. She had gathered her things—her pen, her personal copy of the tide tables, Captain Trewin’s watch that she had kept at her desk—and walked out past the clerk who would not meet her eye.

Now, in the canteen, she felt the shape of that watch in her pocket. The morning tide would bring the fishing boats back through the sea-gate. And Evelyn would be waiting.
