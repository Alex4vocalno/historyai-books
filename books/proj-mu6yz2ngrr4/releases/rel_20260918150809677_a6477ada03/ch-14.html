Chapter 14

Keasts Confession, the Rivers Secret

────────────────────────────────────────

“You don’t understand what you’re pulling at.”

The tide tables proved it. The sea had risen and fallen on the night Captain Trewin died with a precision no human could alter, and the official time of death—resting on a single lock-gate opening at 23: 45—was physically impossible. Evelyn stood in the narrow corridor outside the harbour canteen with her ledger under one arm and the tide charts rolled tight in her hand, the proof of it carried like a blade.

The previous night’s work had stripped away every comfortable reading. She had laid the calculations out on her desk, hour by hour, foot by foot of water, and the numbers had done what numbers do: they refused to bend. Two openings were required that night—one at 23: 45, another near 04: 10—and the second one existed nowhere in the official log. The discrepancy was not an error. It was a removal.

She pushed through the canteen door without waiting.

The harbour canteen sat wedged between the sea-lock gate’s stone housing and the harbour’s outer wall, a low-ceilinged room barely large enough for a Formica table, a row of chairs, and the man who sat hunched at the far end of it. The room smelled of damp wool and machine oil—the wool from the coat draped over a hook by the door, the oil from the gate mechanism that shuddered through the wall every time the water pressure shifted. A single lamp with a brass burner cast a low, yellow glow across the table’s scarred surface. Keast sat behind it, hunched forward, a mug of something cold between his hands.

His face tightened when he saw her. The color beneath his weathered skin went grey, and his shoulders drew upward as though bracing against a draught.

“Miss.” He did not stand. “If it’s about the morning gate, I’ve already—“

“It’s not about the morning gate.” Evelyn pulled the door shut behind her. The latch caught with a sound that made Keast flinch. She placed her ledger on the edge of the table and unrolled the tide charts beside it, weighting the curling edges with his mug and the brass cap of his lamp. The charts spread across the surface, columns of figures in her own careful hand, annotated with times and water levels.

Keast looked at the charts. His eyes moved across them the way a man reads a warrant.

“I need you to explain something, Tom.” Her voice was level, stripped of accusation. She pointed to the first column. “The tide on the night of January the fourteenth. High water at 23: 40, give or take five minutes. The lock opened at 23: 45 to equalise. That’s in your log.”

“It is.”

“And in mine.” Her finger moved to the second column. “Low water at 04: 08. The ebb runs two knots on a spring tide—this was a spring tide. The water level outside the gate dropped four feet below the inner basin level between midnight and four. The gate cannot hold that differential. The hydraulic pressure exceeds the safety threshold by a margin of—“

“I know what the threshold is.”

“Then you know the gate must open again near low water. It is not discretionary. The mechanism fails if it doesn’t. The harbour’s own engineering specifications require it.” She withdrew her hand and let the fact sit. “Your log records one opening. The gate requires two. The tide tables confirm two. My ledger records two. Yours does not.”

Keast’s mouth opened. Nothing came out. His right hand, resting on the table, began a slow crawl toward the logbook that sat at his elbow—battered, oil-stained, its pages soft with use. His fingers found the cover but did not open it.

“You’ve made a mistake in the figures,” he said. “The second opening—sometimes the differential isn’t as bad as the calculations suggest. Wind, atmospheric pressure—“

“The wind that night was northeast, force three. Atmospheric pressure was steady at 1018 millibars. I’ve accounted for both.” She turned the chart to face him directly. “The second opening happened at approximately 04: 10. Captain Trewin’s watch stopped at 04: 10. His body was found on the lock steps at dawn.”

The lamp flame bent in a draught from the gap beneath the door. Keast’s hand still rested on the logbook. A muscle in his jaw worked against itself, and the skin around his eyes had gone tight.

“I don’t know what you think this proves.”

“It proves the gate opened at 04: 10 whether your log says so or not. The tide did the work. The water level is recorded at the monitoring station every fifteen minutes—it’s automatic, mechanical, nobody touches it. Those readings exist independent of either of us.” She paused. “Someone opened that gate at 04: 10. Someone was on those steps. And your log was altered to remove the record of it.”

Silence. The gate mechanism groaned through the wall, a low vibration felt in the floorboards. Keast stared at the tide charts as though they were written in a language he had forgotten.

When he spoke, his voice had changed. The bluster was gone, and what replaced it was something thin and stripped.

“Who sent you?”

“No one sent me.” Evelyn held his gaze. “I’m the tide clerk. These are my records. The discrepancy is mine to resolve.”

“Then resolve it and leave it.” His hand pressed flat on the logbook. “You don’t understand what you’re pulling at.”

“Then help me understand.”

Keast’s breathing had become shallow. His left hand remained on the mug, but his right slid from the logbook’s cover to its edge, and from the edge to the page. He opened it. The binding cracked softly, and the pages fell open to the night in question—January the fourteenth, the ink faded where his thumb had rubbed it, the entry in his careful, blocky hand.

One line. 23: 45. Gate opened for tide equalisation. No anomalies.

His finger found the line. It trembled as it traced the letters of the entry, following the ink from left to right, the pad of his forefinger dragging across the dried words as though trying to rub them out or confirm they were real. The trembling moved from his finger to his whole hand, and then his palm came down hard on the page, flat, as if pinning the lie to the table.

“There was another line,” he said. “Below that one. 04: 10. Gate opened for—.” He stopped. His hand stayed where it was.

“For what?”

“For tide equalisation. Same as the first. That’s all it said. That’s all I wrote.”

“And who removed it?”

Keast’s eyes left the page and found the door. He held there, staring at the planks of wood as if measuring the distance to the corridor and the harbour beyond.

“Tom.”

“I can’t.” His voice had dropped to nearly nothing. “You don’t know what this is.”

“I know Captain Trewin is dead. I know his watch stopped at 04: 10. I know the coroner returned a verdict of accidental death, and I know that verdict cannot be true if the gate opened at 04: 10 and Trewin was on those steps at the time.” She kept her voice even, clinical, the way she would read a tide measurement at a board meeting. “I know your log was altered. I need to know who altered it and why.”

Keast’s hand slid off the page. He sat back in his chair, and the wood creaked under his weight. The lines around his mouth had deepened, the skin hanging looser at his jaw. The lamp threw the hollows of his cheeks into shadow.

“He made me do it.” The words came out in a whisper, as though the stone walls might carry them somewhere he did not want. “After. Not before. After Trewin was found. That same morning. Before the coroner came.”

“Who, Tom?”

Keast swallowed. His eyes stayed on the door.

“Alderman Rowe.”

The name fell between them. It settled on the table like a stone dropped into still water, and the ripples were silent. Evelyn’s hand, resting on the edge of the tide chart, went still. The lamp flame bent again.

“Rowe came to the harbour office at half past six,” Keast said. “Before anyone else was in. He told me the second entry had to come out. Said it would confuse the inquest. Said the coroner only needed the routine opening—anything else would muddy the waters and delay the verdict. Trewin’s family wanted it settled quick. The harbour couldn’t afford a drawn-out investigation.”

“And you removed the entry.”

“I removed the entry.” His finger found the page again, pressing into the blank space where the second line had been. The paper was faintly discoloured there—a shade lighter where ink had been scratched away and the surface roughened. “With a knife. The blade of my penknife. Took the line out clean.”

Evelyn looked at the page. The abrasion was visible. A strip of the page’s surface had been lifted, the fibres disturbed, and though Keast had tried to smooth it back, the texture was wrong. Anyone who knew what to look for would find it.

“What did Rowe say when he told you to do it?”

“Nothing. He stood where you’re standing. He didn’t raise his voice. He said, ‘Tom, the harbour needs this clean. One opening, one accident, one verdict. You’ll do it because the harbour comes first.’ Then he left.” Keast’s hands were in his lap now, hidden. “I did it because I thought he was right. One opening, one accident. Simple. But then I heard about the watch.”

“The watch.”

“Stopped at 04: 10. That’s the time the second gate opened. That’s the time I wrote in the log before I scratched it out.” His eyes finally came back to hers, and what she saw in them was not guilt alone—it was terror. “I scratched it out because he told me to. But the time matches, Miss. The watch and the gate. And Rowe knew about the watch before he came to me. He knew the time. He came prepared.”

Evelyn let the implication form in the room’s cold air. Rowe had arrived at half past six, before the coroner, before the harbour staff, already knowing the watch had stopped at 04: 10. Already knowing the second opening matched it. Already knowing which entry to remove.

“When did Rowe learn about the watch?”

“I don’t know.” Keast’s voice cracked on the last word. He cleared his throat. “I don’t know when he learned. I don’t know what he knew before. I only know what he told me to do, and I did it, and I’ve been sick about it since.”

“Did Rowe say why Trewin was on the steps at that hour?”

“He didn’t say. He didn’t say anything about Trewin except that it was an accident and the verdict would confirm it.”

“And you believed him?”

Keast’s mouth twisted. “I wanted to.”

Evelyn gathered the tide charts and rolled them. She placed her ledger back under her arm. The room felt smaller than when she had entered, the walls pressing inward, the gate mechanism’s groan a constant reminder that the water outside was doing what water does—following rules no one could rewrite.

“I need more, Tom. I need to know exactly what Rowe said, what time he arrived, whether anyone saw him come to the harbour office.”

Keast’s gaze snapped to the door again. His right hand gripped the edge of the table, knuckles white.

“I’ve told you what I can.” The words came fast, stripped of the confessional quiet. “I’ve told you I did it and he told me to. That’s all. That’s all you’re getting from me.”

“If Rowe ordered the falsification, he did it to conceal something. The log is a public record. You’ve said yourself the time matches the watch. This isn’t a confusion of dates—“

“I know what it is.” Keast stood. The chair scraped back. “I know what it is, and I’ve told you, and now you need to go. If he finds out I’ve talked—“

“He’ll find out eventually.”

“Not from me.” Keast’s hand found the door frame. His body was angled toward the corridor, away from her, away from the logbook still open on the table. “Not from me, Miss. I’ve done what I can. The rest is yours.”

Evelyn looked at him. There was nothing left to say that would open him further—the fear had closed around him like the tide closing over a reef. She nodded once and stepped past him into the corridor, the door swinging shut behind her with a sound like a lock falling.

The harbour office was quiet when she returned. The records room held only the ticking of the clock and the faint percussion of water against the outer wall. She sat at her desk, opened her private ledger to a clean page, and uncapped her pen.

The nib rested on the paper. Ink gathered at its tip, a dark bead waiting.

She wrote the date. Below it, in letters more deliberate than any tide reading she had ever recorded: Keast Confession. Below that: Rowe. Below that: 06: 30, 15 January. Ordered removal of 04: 10 entry. Knew watch time before arrival. Knew which entry to target.

The pen moved with the same precision she gave to water levels and gate times, but her hand paused on the period after the last line. The nib held its position, a small dark circle spreading into the paper’s grain. The lamp’s glow caught her fingers—still, deliberate, the only part of her that was not calculating the weight of what she had just written.

She capped the pen. She closed the ledger. The name Rowe stared up at her through the cover as though the ink had soaked through, and in the silence of the records room, with the harbour’s water pressing against the outer wall, the name felt less like a clue than a warning.

She would need more than a dead man’s watch and a frightened lock-keeper’s word. She would need the kind of proof that could not be scratched out with a penknife.

Evelyn opened the ledger again and turned to the tide tables for the night of January the fourteenth. The numbers were already there, already proven. The water had risen and fallen, and the gate had opened twice, and no amount of institutional pressure could make the sea unsay what it had done.

She stared at the page. Somewhere between the two openings—between 23: 45 and 04: 10—Captain Trewin had walked to the lock steps and died. And Alderman Rowe had known the time before anyone should have, and had moved to erase it before anyone could ask why.

Evelyn closed the ledger, slipped it into her coat, and left the harbour office. The corridor outside was empty, and her footsteps echoed against the stone.

Back at the canteen door she stopped. Through the salt-streaked glass she could see Tom Keast still hunched over his untouched tea, exactly where she had left him. His eyes were already on the door. They darted sideways—toward the harbour office behind her—then back to the table, and he gripped the mug with both hands as though it were the last fixed thing in the world.

She sat across from him and kept her voice below the rattle of the kettle.

“Tell me what Rowe said. His words, Tom. Not what you thought he meant.”

Keast’s mouth worked. He looked at the door again, and his throat moved, and she could see the confession sitting right there behind his teeth—pressing to get out—and then she watched him swallow it back down.

“I’ve told you what I told you.” His voice had gone flat and small. “That’s all. That’s everything.”

“That isn’t everything.”

“It is.” He set the mug down with a clumsy knock that spilled tea across the Formica. His hand was shaking. “You don’t understand what kind of man—“

He stopped himself. Closed his eyes. When he opened them, whatever had been in them was gone, replaced by something blank and final.

“Please,” he said. “I can’t.”

Evelyn regarded him. She could press harder. She could threaten him with the falsified log, with conspiracy, with obstruction. She could see in the rigid set of his shoulders that it might even work—that one more question might crack the seal entirely. And she could see, in the way his eyes kept tracking the door, that it might cost him something she had no right to spend.

She took out her ledger and opened it flat on the table. The canteen light was hard and white and institutional. She wrote the date in a clean hand, then beneath it: Keast confession—log falsified under duress. She moved to the next line and wrote the name with the same deliberate, unhurried stroke, each letter set down like a marker on a chart.

Rowe.

She looked at the word. An alderman. A man who sat on the bench and signed warrants and shook hands with the Chief Constable at Remembrance services. A man whose name, once written in this book, could not be unwritten—who would learn that someone had written it, and would move accordingly, and would have the machinery to move with.

This was no longer an inquiry into a drowned harbourmaster’s death. It was an accusation aimed upward, at power, and it would be answered not with denial but with counterweight.

She closed the ledger and met Keast’s frightened, grateful eyes.

“Go home, Tom,” she said. “Lock your door.”
