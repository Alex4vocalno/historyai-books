Chapter 11

The Tide Table's Promise

Nora Keast’s silence was a locked door, and Evelyn had no key. She left the quayside with the taste of defeat sharp in her throat, the woman’s refusal to explain her presence echoing in her ears. The harbour stretched before her, grey and uncommunicative under the November sky, its waters holding whatever secrets they had swallowed in the dark hours of the fourth.

She climbed the stone steps to the records room above the harbour office two flights at a time. The room waited, familiar and patient, its green-shaded lamp unlit in the morning light. She pulled the tide tables from their shelf, the Admiralty charts for Porthkennick harbour, her own leather-bound ledger, and the lock-keeper’s log borrowed under pretense of routine audit. She spread them across the wide desk, four open books in silent contradiction.

Morning light fell on yellowed paper, catching the grain of charts older than her tenure. The dry scent of ink and aged vellum rose to meet her. Outside, gulls wheeled and cried, their voices muffled by the thick glass. Evelyn took up her pencil and turned to November the fourth.

The date stared back at her, a row of figures in her own careful hand. High water at 03: 47. Low water at 10: 22. The spring tide running strong, the ebb beginning just after four in the morning. She traced the column down to the lock gate entries. Two openings recorded: one at 23: 45 on the third, one at 04: 10 on the fourth. The second opening tidally necessary, the water equalising as the harbour drained toward the sea.

Her finger moved to the lock-keeper’s log. Tom Keast’s handwriting, cramped and serviceable. One opening only. 23: 45. The 04: 10 entry absent, a blank where a line should be.

She had known this. She had carried the knowledge for days now, turning it over like a stone in her pocket. But knowing a fact and understanding its implications were different provinces. The discrepancy was a locked room. She needed the key.

Evelyn pulled the Admiralty chart closer. It showed the harbour in meticulous detail: the lock basin, the sea-gate, the flight of stone steps leading down to the water where Captain Trewin’s body had been found. The steps sat at the eastern edge of the basin, sheltered by the harbour wall. The main channel ran west to east, the tide pushing through the lock gate twice daily, flooding and ebbing with the moon’s pull.

She began to calculate.

The tide tables gave her the times. High water at 03: 47 meant the turn came shortly after, the ebb beginning to run. By 04: 10, when the lock gate opened for the second time, the current was already moving. She consulted the tidal stream atlas, its pages showing the hourly progression of flow through the harbour approaches. The arrows for 04: 00 to 05: 00 told their story: a strong ebb running eastward at two knots, the water pouring out of the harbour basin toward the open sea.

Two knots. She wrote the figure in the margin of her notebook. Two nautical miles per hour. Roughly three thousand, five hundred yards in sixty minutes. Fifty-eight yards per minute.

Her pencil stopped.

She stared at the number. Fifty-eight yards per minute. If a body entered the water at the lock steps at 04: 10, caught in the ebb current, it would not stay at the lock steps. The tide would take it. By dawn, by the time the body was discovered at six, nearly two hours later, the mathematics were inexorable.

She turned to the harbour chart and measured with her dividers. From the lock steps to the point where Captain Trewin’s body was found, the distance was perhaps forty yards along the eastern wall. A plausible distance for a body to drift in the ebb, if it had entered the water at 04: 10 and been caught in the current.

But the body had been found at the base of the lock steps. Not forty yards downstream. At the steps themselves.

She checked her calculation. The ebb ran eastward. The body should have moved eastward with it. The harbour wall would have offered some shelter, perhaps, a slack area where the current weakened. She traced the chart with her finger, imagining the flow. The lock basin was protected, yes, but the main channel swept past its mouth. A body in the water would not stay still. It would turn and drift, caught by the larger movement of the tide.

She made a second calculation, factoring in the slack water near the wall. Even at half a knot, even at a quarter, the drift would be measurable. Ten yards. Twenty. Enough to move a body away from the steps in the hours between 04: 10 and dawn.

The pencil moved across her scratchpad. Figures accumulated. Times and distances, rates of flow, vectors of current. She worked in silence, the scratch of graphite the only sound in the room, her mind narrowing to the task. The human element fell away. Nora Keast’s silence, Josiah Pengelly’s broken alibi, Alderman Rowe’s veiled warnings. All of it receded. There was only the tide, the chart, and the body.

Her finger traced a line on the chart from the lock steps to a point fifty yards down-current. The numbers were incontrovertible. If Captain Trewin had entered the water at 04: 10, caught by the full ebb, his body should have been found fifty yards from where it was discovered. Further, perhaps. The tide was patient. It did not discriminate.

She sat back. The lamplight seemed brighter now, the room smaller. Outside the window, the harbour waters rose and fell with their ancient rhythm, indifferent to the questions she had asked.

The official verdict was death by misadventure. A fall in the dark, a slip on wet stone, a drowning at the lock steps. But the tide told a different story. The body at the steps at dawn, the watch stopped at 04: 10, the ebb running strong. The mathematics did not reconcile. A body that entered the water at 04: 10 would not be found at the steps at dawn. It would be elsewhere, carried by the current that Tom Keast’s missing entry had tried to erase.

Unless the body had not entered the water at 04: 10. Unless it had been placed there later, after the current slackened, after the tide turned. Or unless it had never entered the water near the lock steps at all.

She wrote a single line in her notebook: “The location is incompatible with the time.”

Then she gathered her papers, tucked the lock-keeper’s log under her arm, and went to find Tom Keast.

The harbour canteen sat at the landward end of the quay, a low building of stone and timber that smelled of tea and pipe smoke. She found Keast at a corner table, a mug of something steaming before him, his eyes fixed on the middle distance. He looked up as she approached, and something shifted in his face. Recognition, then wariness.

“Miss Evelyn.” His voice was flat. “Come to check my figures again?”

She sat across from him without invitation, laying the log on the table between them. “I have a question about November the fourth.”

“Thought you’d asked your questions.” He did not touch his mug. His hands rested on the table, thick-knuckled and still. “I gave my answers.”

“You logged one opening that night. At 23: 45.”

“That’s right.”

“But the tide tables show a second opening was necessary. High water at 03: 47. The ebb beginning. The lock gate would have needed to open again around 04: 10 to equalise the levels.”

Keast’s jaw tightened. “I logged what I was told to log. My job’s to keep the gate, not the book.”

“Your log is the official record. If it shows one opening, then officially, only one opening occurred. But the tide doesn’t lie, Mr. Keast. The water rose and fell regardless of what you wrote. A second opening happened. Someone opened that gate at 04: 10.”

“You’re the tide clerk. You’d know better than me.”

“I do know. My ledger shows the opening. The question is why yours doesn’t.”

He shifted in his seat. His left hand moved to the edge of the table, fingers drumming a slow rhythm against the wood. “Mistake, maybe. Forgot. Long night.”

“At 04: 10? In the small hours? What comings and goings would those be?”

The drumming stopped. Keast met her eyes, and for a moment she saw something beneath the wariness. Something like fear. “You’d have to ask the harbourmaster. Oh, that’s right. He’s dead.”

The words hung between them. Evelyn let the silence stretch. The canteen murmured around them, other conversations weaving a backdrop of ordinary noise. Someone laughed at a distant table. The smell of tobacco smoke drifted past.

“I’ve been calculating the tidal current that night,” she said, her voice even. “The ebb was running at two knots by 04: 10. Strong enough to carry a body a significant distance in the hours before dawn.”

Keast’s face showed nothing. But his right hand had moved to his mug, lifting it, setting it down without drinking. A small, purposeless motion. “Is that right.”

“If Captain Trewin fell from the lock steps at 04: 10, his body should have been found fifty yards down-current. At least. The tide would have taken him.”

“Maybe he got caught on something. A mooring line, a bit of wreckage.”

“The harbour was clear. The divers checked. There was nothing to catch him.”

Keast’s shoulders rose and fell. A shrug, elaborately casual. “Then maybe he didn’t fall at 04: 10. Maybe he fell later. Maybe his watch stopped before he hit the water. Watches do that.”

“The watch stopped at 04: 10. The second opening happened at 04: 10. You tell me if that’s a coincidence.”

“I tell you nothing.” His voice had risen slightly. A man at a nearby table glanced over, then away. Keast lowered his tone. “I keep the gate. I write what happens. If the book says one opening, that’s what happened. The official record.”

“The official record is wrong.”

“The official record is the record.” He pushed back his chair, the legs scraping against the stone floor. “You want to argue with the tide, Miss Marsh, you go ahead. But don’t come to me with your charts and your calculations. I know what I saw. I know what I wrote.”

“What did you see, Mr. Keast?”

He stood, looming over the table. His hands were fists now, held rigid at his sides. “I saw nothing. I was at the sluice gate, checking the mechanism. That’s where I was. That’s what the roster says.”

“The roster says nothing. The maintenance roster has no record of a sluice check that night.”

For a moment, his composure cracked. His eyes flickered to the lock-keeper’s log on the table, then back to her face. The fear was plain now, no longer hidden. “You’ve been busy.”

“I’m doing my job.”

“Your job is tides and tables. Not this.” He gestured broadly, taking in the log, the canteen, the harbour beyond. “Captain Trewin slipped. He fell. He drowned. It’s a tragedy, but it’s an accident. That’s what the coroner said. That’s what the alderman says. That’s what everyone says except you.”

“Everyone can be wrong.”

“Everyone isn’t wrong.” He bent, picking up his mug, draining it in a single swallow. “You want to chase ghosts, you go ahead. But leave my name out of it. I did my job that night. I opened the gate when it needed opening. What I wrote or didn’t write is my business.”

He turned and walked away, his boots heavy on the stone floor. The door swung shut behind him, cutting off the sound of the quay.

Evelyn sat alone at the table. The lock-keeper’s log lay before her, its single entry for November the fourth a silent accusation. She had struck a nerve. She had seen it in his hands, heard it in his voice. Tom Keast knew something. He was protecting something, or someone. His wife, perhaps. Or himself.

But the tide did not care about protection. The tide did not lie. The numbers were clear, the calculation simple. A body that entered the water at 04: 10, in a two-knot ebb, would not be found at the lock steps at dawn. The body had been moved. Or it had been placed. Or the official story was wrong from beginning to end.

She gathered her papers and rose. The canteen was emptier now, the morning shift heading to their work. She walked out into the grey light, the harbour stretching before her, its waters rising and falling with the patient rhythm she had studied all her life.

The harbourmaster’s building rose at the head of the quay. She climbed the steps again, passed through the familiar door, and returned to the records room. Her office waited, its window looking out over the water. She stood before it, her hands clasped behind her back, and watched the tide.

The ebb was running now, the water draining from the basin toward the sea. She could see the current in the movement of the buoys, the bend of the mooring lines. The harbour was a machine, its gears the moon and the water, its mechanisms ancient and inexorable. She had spent her career recording its workings, noting its comings and goings, trusting in the regularity of its patterns.

Now it had given her a witness. The tide itself, silent and immutable, speaking in numbers and vectors. It told her that Captain Trewin’s death was not what it seemed. It told her that the body at the lock steps was a lie, placed there by human hands, contradicting the evidence of the water.

She turned from the window and sat at her desk. Her notebook lay open, her calculations staring back at her. She wrote a second line beneath the first: “The tide is my key witness.”

Then she closed the notebook and began to plan.

The harbour office was quiet around her, the sounds of the building muffled and distant. Somewhere below, a door opened and closed. Somewhere outside, a boat horn sounded, long and low. The world went on its business, indifferent to the questions she carried.

But the tide. The tide remembered. It had been there in the dark hours of November the fourth, running its ancient course, carrying whatever secrets the harbour held. And it had left its mark, in the stopped watch, in the body at the steps, in the discrepancy between the ledger and the log.

Evelyn Marsh, tide clerk, keeper of records, reached for her pen. She had a theory now. A hypothesis, testable and precise. The body could not have been found where it was if it had entered the water when they said it did. The mathematics proved it. The tide proved it.

What remained was to prove it to someone else.
