Chapter 15

The Calculated Window

The lamplight pools on a tide table, its columns of numbers waiting to be interrogated.

Evelyn Marsh sat alone in the records room, the harbour office silent around her. Three documents lay spread across her desk: her own tide ledger, the lock-keeper’s weather log, and her tide tables. The green-shaded lamp cast its familiar circle, picking out the grain of the paper, the faded ink of old entries, the sharp black of her own recent notations. A half-empty cup of tea had gone cold at her elbow, a skin forming on its surface. She had not noticed.

Her finger traced the column for November 4th. The tide ledger showed two lock openings: 23: 45 and 04: 10. The official log showed only one. Somewhere between those two records lived a murderer, and she meant to find him by the only method that had never failed her: arithmetic.

The Admiralty chart gave her the baseline. High water on November 4th had occurred at 22: 47, reaching 5.8 metres above chart datum. Low water followed at 05: 12 the next morning, dropping to 0.9 metres. The ebb tide, then, had been running for approximately four hours and twenty-five minutes by the time the second lock opening occurred. She picked up her pencil and began to calculate.

The lock gates at Porthkennick served a singular purpose: they equalised the water level between the inner harbour and the outer basin. When the tide fell below the harbour’s maintained level, the gates had to open or risk structural damage from the pressure differential. The timing was not discretionary. It was governed by physics, by the moon’s pull, by the immutable laws that had moved water since before Porthkennick existed. The harbour’s founding charter specified it clearly: the lock-keeper opened when the tide demanded, and he recorded the time.

She turned to the weather log. Tom Keast had noted wind from the northeast at eight knots, veering to east-northeast by dawn. No precipitation. Good visibility. The barometric pressure had been steady at 1023 millibars. Nothing remarkable. A typical November night for the Cornish coast, cold and clear, the kind that sent men to their beds early and kept them there until the light changed.

But Keast had not recorded the second opening. His log showed only the 23: 45 entry, signed in his cramped, careful hand. Yet the tide ledger in Evelyn’s own handwriting proved the gates had opened again at 04: 10. The discrepancy was not a clerical error. She had checked and rechecked her figures. The water level differential at 04: 10 would have been 2.3 metres. The gates had to open. The harbour’s engineering permitted no alternative.

So Keast had lied. Or someone had lied for him.

She pulled the Admiralty chart closer, studying the approach to the lock steps. The inner harbour spread like an ink blot on the paper, its contours marked in fathoms and metres. The lock itself sat at the western end, a narrow channel connecting the maintained basin to the tidal outer harbour. The steps where Captain Trewin’s body had been found descended from the quayside into the water, used by the pilots to reach their boats in the predawn hours.

Evelyn’s pencil hovered over the chart. She opened the tide tables and the lock-keeper’s weather log side by side. High water at 22: 47: 5.8 metres. Low water at 05: 12: 0.9 metres. She interpolated between the two—by 04: 00, the tide had fallen to roughly 1.4 metres, and the ebb current was running at approximately two knots. The body had been recovered at 06: 30, approximately two hundred metres from the lock steps, caught against the pilings of the fish market. The coroner’s report had placed the time of death between 03: 00 and 05: 00, based on body temperature and rigor mortis. The stopped watch in Trewin’s pocket had narrowed that window to approximately 04: 10.

But the watch had been wrong.

She felt it before she could articulate it, that familiar tug of certainty that came when the numbers began to align. The tide was the silent witness, the one piece of evidence that could not be bribed or intimidated or persuaded to lie. It had been there. It knew what had happened, and it had left its record in the movement of the water.

The ebb current on that night would have been running at approximately two knots, she calculated. The flood had not yet begun; low water was still an hour away when the second lock opening occurred. A body entering the water at the lock steps at 04: 10 would have been carried westward on the ebb, toward the harbour mouth. The current would have been consistent for approximately three hours, until slack water at low tide, and then the flood would have begun to push back inward.

She marked the direction on the chart with a light pencil line. Westward from the lock steps, past the fish market, toward the harbour mouth. But Trewin’s body had been found against the fish market pilings, two hundred metres west of the lock steps—in the ebb’s path, yet far short of where the current should have carried it.

Her hand paused. The pencil point hovered above the paper.

A body could not have traveled against a two-knot current.

She pulled another sheet of paper toward her and began to work through the timeline in earnest. If the body entered the water at 04: 10, as the watch suggested, the ebb would have carried it westward. By dawn, it would have been near the harbour mouth, perhaps half a kilometre from where it was actually found. The math was unassailable. She checked it twice, then a third time, using different methods, calculating the drift rate based on body mass and water density. The result held.

The body had not entered the water at 04: 10.

Evelyn sat back, her breath coming more quickly. The lamp flickered as a draft found its way through the window frame, stirring the edges of the papers. Outside, the first grey light of dawn was beginning to soften the blackness of the sky. The harbour would be stirring soon. The fishermen would be preparing their boats. The lock-keeper would be checking his gauges. And somewhere in Porthkennick, a murderer would be waking to a day he believed was safe.

She returned to the documents. If the body had not entered the water at 04: 10, when had it? The coroner’s estimated window of 03: 00 to 05: 00 still held, but she needed something more precise. She needed to find the moment when the tide would have permitted the body to drift to its discovered location.

The flood tide had begun at approximately 06: 30, she calculated. By then, the current would have reversed, flowing inward from the sea. If the body had entered the water during the flood, it would have been carried eastward, toward the inner harbour. But the coroner’s report placed death well before dawn. The body temperature and liver mortis patterns were inconsistent with a morning entry.

So the body had entered during the ebb, but had somehow moved against the current. The only explanation was that it had been placed in the water at a location that would allow the ebb to carry it to the fish market pilings. She traced the chart again, her finger moving along the shoreline. If a body entered the water east of the fish market, the ebb would have carried it westward, past the pilings, where it might have been caught. But that would have required the body to enter the water much further east, near the boatyard, and there was no reason for Trewin to be there at that hour.

Or the body had been moved.

The thought arrived with the weight of physical evidence. Someone had placed Trewin’s body in the water after death, positioning it to be found near the lock steps, but the current had betrayed them. They had not accounted for the ebb. They had chosen the wrong time, or the wrong place, or both.

She pulled Captain Trewin’s watch from her pocket. The glass was cracked, the hands frozen at 4: 10. She had been carrying it since the inquest, a piece of evidence that had seemed damning and now seemed something else entirely. The watch was not a record of death. It was a stage prop, placed to suggest a narrative that the physical evidence contradicted.

The murderer had stopped the watch deliberately. They had set it to 04: 10, the time of the second lock opening, to create a false connection between Trewin’s death and the gate. They had wanted the death to appear accidental, a fall in the dark while the lock was operating. But they had not understood the tide. They had not calculated the current. They had left a witness they could not cross-examine, and that witness was now speaking to Evelyn in the only language it knew: the cold, irrefutable mathematics of water moving under the moon’s pull.

She needed to narrow the window further. The coroner’s estimate of 03: 00 to 05: 00 was too broad. She needed to know exactly when the body could have entered the water and still ended up where it was found.

Working methodically, she began to plot the current vectors at thirty-minute intervals throughout the night. At 23: 45, when the first lock opening occurred, the ebb was just beginning. The current would have been weak, less than half a knot, and the water level still close to high. By 01: 00, the ebb was running at full strength, approximately 1.8 knots. By 03: 00, it had reached its peak velocity of 2.2 knots. And by 04: 10, when the second lock opening occurred, it was still running strong at 2.0 knots.

A body entering the water at any point during this period would have been carried westward. The math was consistent. The direction was consistent. The only variable was speed.

She turned her attention to the flood tide. Low water occurred at 05: 12. Slack water, that brief period when the tide turned and the current approached zero, would have lasted perhaps fifteen minutes. Her own test at the lock steps had already proven this—a body placed during that window drifted far beyond where Trewin’s was found. If Trewin had been killed and placed in the water during slack water, his body would have been carried eastward by the incoming flood, potentially ending up near the fish market.

But the coroner’s estimated time of death was well before 05: 12. The body temperature suggested Trewin had been dead for several hours before dawn. Yet Evelyn could not fully trust that estimate, not when Rowe had already proven his willingness to pressure officials into falsifying records. If he had entered the water during slack water, the timeline would not match.

Evelyn frowned, turning the problem over in her mind. The contradiction was stark. The physical evidence pointed one way; the tidal evidence pointed another. Either the coroner was wrong about the time of death, or the body had been moved after entering the water, or there was some factor she had not yet considered.

She returned to the weather log. Wind from the northeast at eight knots. Veering to east-northeast by dawn. The wind would have created a surface drift, pushing floating objects in the direction of the wind, regardless of the current below. For a body in the water, the effect would have been minimal; most of the mass would have been submerged, subject to the current rather than the wind. But if the body had been partially buoyant, or if it had been moved by other means, the wind might have played a role.

She added the wind vector to her calculations. A northeast wind at eight knots would have created a surface drift of approximately 0.2 knots, pushing objects to the southwest. Combined with the ebb current, the net movement would have been westward, with a slight southward component. Still inconsistent with the body’s discovered location.

Unless the body had not been floating.

The thought struck her with the force of revelation. A body that sank would not be subject to the surface current. It would settle to the bottom, moved only by the bottom current, which could differ significantly from the surface flow. The harbour charts showed depths of three to four metres in the area near the lock steps. A body sinking to the bottom would have been in still water, unaffected by the surface ebb. It would have remained where it fell, until decomposition gases caused it to rise.

But Trewin’s body had been found floating, caught against the pilings. It had not sunk. Or if it had sunk, it had risen again before dawn. The timeline for that process was uncertain, dependent on water temperature and body mass and too many variables to calculate precisely.

She pushed back from the desk, rubbing her eyes. The grey light outside had strengthened. She could see the harbour now, the dark water catching the first reflections of the sky. A fishing boat was preparing to leave, its engine coughing in the morning air. The world was waking, and she was still sitting in the lamplight, trying to make the numbers speak.

But they had spoken. They had told her that the official story was impossible. They had told her that the watch was a plant. They had told her that someone had killed Captain Trewin and arranged his body to suggest an accident, but they had made a mistake. They had not understood the tide.

She pulled the documents together, aligning their edges with habitual precision. Her tide ledger. The Admiralty chart. The lock-keeper’s weather log. And Captain Trewin’s watch, still frozen at 4: 10. The evidence was incomplete, but it was enough. It proved that the death was not accidental. It proved that someone had lied. It proved that the murder had been planned with careful attention to the lock opening times, but not to the tidal currents that would follow.

She needed one more calculation. If the body had not entered the water at 04: 10, when had it? She returned to the timeline, searching for the narrow window that would satisfy both the physical evidence and the tidal mechanics.

The body had been found at 06: 30. The flood tide had begun at approximately 06: 00, with the current reversing around 06: 15. If the body had entered the water during the last minutes of the ebb, when the current was slowing, it might have drifted only a short distance westward before the flood caught it and pushed it back east. The window would have been brief, perhaps fifteen minutes, between 03: 50 and 04: 05. During that period, the ebb was still running, but weakening. A body entering the water at the lock steps would have been carried westward for perhaps one hundred metres, then caught by the turning tide and pushed back east, potentially ending up near the fish market.

She traced the path on the chart, her pencil marking the estimated drift. The numbers aligned. For that brief window, perhaps fifteen minutes, the tidal mechanics would have permitted the body to end up where it was found.

But that window was before the second lock opening. That window was before 04: 10. If Trewin had died between 03: 50 and 04: 05, the watch was wrong. The watch had been set to suggest a later time, a time when the lock opening would have provided a convenient explanation for his presence on the steps.

Evelyn sat very still, her finger resting on the chart where her penciled lines converged. The calculation was complete. The window was defined. The murder had occurred between 03: 50 and 04: 05, and the watch had been set to 04: 10 to implicate the lock opening and provide an alibi for whoever was elsewhere at that time.

She thought of Tom Keast, with his terrified eyes and his incomplete log. She thought of Josiah Pengelly, with his broken alibi and his boat riding low with catch. She thought of Alderman Rowe, with his smooth voice and his veiled warnings about the greater good. Each of them had a reason to want the case closed. Each of them had a connection to the harbour, to the tides, to the precise mechanics that had been used to stage this murder.

But only one of them had understood the tidal calculations well enough to plan it. Only one of them had known about the second lock opening, had known that the watch would need to be set to that specific time, had known how to arrange the body to suggest an accident.

The murderer had made a single mistake. They had calculated the lock opening times correctly, but they had not accounted for the current. They had assumed that a body entering the water at 04: 10 would be found near the lock steps, not carried westward by the ebb. They had not realized that the tide would betray them, would carry their carefully staged scene away from where they wanted it, would leave the body in a place that contradicted their narrative.

Evelyn looked at the watch again. The cracked glass caught the lamplight, the hands frozen at their false time. She had carried this watch for days, believing it to be evidence of the time of death. Now she saw it for what it was: a lie told in metal and glass, a false witness planted to mislead.

The harbour outside her window was no longer just a place of work. It was a crime scene she had mathematically defined. Every current, every tide, every movement of water was evidence, and she had learned to read it. The murderer had thought themselves clever, had thought the tide would hide their crime. But the tide did not hide. The tide remembered. The tide bore witness, in its own cold and patient language, and Evelyn Marsh had learned to translate.

She closed her notebook, the gesture final, like closing a coffin lid. The calculation was complete. The window was defined. The watch was proven false. What remained was the question of who had done this, and why they had needed Captain Trewin dead at that specific hour.

The answer lay somewhere in the records she had already examined, in the statements she had already heard, in the lies she had already uncovered. The tide had given her the when. The ledgers had given her the how. The rest was simply a matter of finding the person whose story could not accommodate the truth she now held.

She rose from the desk, her body stiff from hours of stillness. The lamp still burned, but the dawn had made it redundant. She extinguished the flame, and the room filled with grey light. The harbour office was waking. Soon there would be clerks and stevedores and fishermen, all going about their business, unaware that the tide had spoken, that the water had given up its secret, that a clerk in a back room had found the thread that would unravel the carefully constructed lie.

Evelyn gathered her documents. Her tide ledger. The Admiralty chart. The lock-keeper’s weather log. Captain Trewin’s watch. These were her witnesses now, silent and irrefutable. She would carry them to whoever would listen, and she would present her case in the only language that mattered: the cold, precise arithmetic of truth.

The water outside her window caught the first light of morning, its surface moving with the incoming flood. The tide was rising. The current had reversed. And somewhere in Porthkennick, a murderer was waking to a day that would not end as they had planned.

The numbers had converged. The window was defined. The watch was a lie. And Evelyn Marsh, tide clerk of the harbour office, had proven it with nothing but pencil and paper and the patient testimony of the sea.
