Chapter 4

Questions for the Lock-Keeper

The sanctity of her own record was violated. An unlogged event had occurred. Someone had opened the lock gate at 04:10, and that fact had been deliberately excised from the official account. Evelyn Marsh sat at her desk in the harbour office, the tide ledger open before her, its columns of figures marching with the precision she had trusted for seventeen years. Now that trust felt like a wound.

The harbour office was quiet, filled with the familiar smell of ink and damp wool. A single green-shaded lamp cast a pool of light across the varnished desk, leaving the corners of the room in shadow. Outside, the day had begun its grey business, cargo loading and vessels departing, the endless rhythm of a working harbour that had no patience for questions. The clock on the wall ticked steadily, indifferent to the irregularity she had found.

She had spent an hour comparing the two documents. Her tide ledger, which recorded water levels and lock operations for tidal calculations, showed two openings on the night Captain Trewin died: one at 23:45 and a second at 04:10. The official lock-keeper’s log showed only the first. The discrepancy was not a matter of interpretation. It was a matter of fact, recorded in two hands, in two books that should have agreed.

Evelyn closed her eyes. The second opening at 04:10 was tidally necessary. She had calculated it herself, the water levels demanding release at that hour to maintain the harbour’s equilibrium. The lock keeper had no discretion in the matter. The gate had to open. Which meant someone had opened it, and someone had then decided not to write it down.

She opened her eyes and looked at the clock. Half past seven. Tom Keast would be finishing his night shift about now, likely taking breakfast in the harbour canteen before heading home. If she wanted to speak to him privately, this was the hour.

She gathered the ledgers, placed them in her satchel, and walked out into the morning.

The air was cold and tasted of salt. Gulls wheeled overhead, their cries sharp against the wind that swept in from the open sea. Evelyn walked along the quay, past the fishing boats and cargo vessels, past the men who nodded to her with the casual respect reserved for harbour staff. She had worked here since she was twenty-three, first as an assistant to the previous tide surveyor, then in her own right after he retired. She knew every current, every eddy, every hidden snag in these waters. The harbour was her domain, and its records were her scripture.

Now that scripture had been falsified.

She found Tom Keast in the harbour canteen, a low building of stone and timber that smelled of tea and pipe smoke. He sat alone at a corner table, a half-eaten bread roll before him, his gaze fixed on the window and the grey water beyond. He looked tired, the lines around his eyes deeper than she remembered, his shoulders carrying a weight that had nothing to do with the night shift.

Evelyn approached and stood at the edge of his table. “Mr. Keast.”

He looked up, and for a moment she saw something flicker in his expression, a wariness that had not been there before. Then it was gone, replaced by the affable mask he wore for colleagues.

“Miss Marsh.” He gestured to the chair opposite. “Sit down. You’re up early.”

“I need to speak with you.” She did not sit. “In private.”

Keast studied her face. He was a man in his fifties, thick-set and weathered, with the look of someone who had spent his life outdoors. His hands were large and calloused, the hands of a man who worked with iron and timber. She had known him for a decade, had trusted him as a colleague, had never once questioned his word.

“Private,” he repeated. He pushed back his chair and stood. “We can use the records room. No one’s there at this hour.”

They walked in silence across the quay to the harbour office. The building was nearly empty at this time of morning, the day staff not yet arrived, the night staff departed. Keast led her down a corridor to the records room, a narrow space lined with shelves of ledgers and logbooks. He closed the door behind them and turned to face her.

“What’s this about, Miss Marsh?”

Evelyn opened her satchel and withdrew the two ledgers. She placed them on a table between them, her tide ledger and the official lock-keeper’s log, opened to the relevant pages.

“I’ve been reviewing the records from the night Captain Trewin died.”

Keast’s eyes went to the books, then back to her face. “Routine review, is it?”

“Routine.” She let the word sit. “I found something. A discrepancy.”

She turned the tide ledger so he could see. Her finger rested on the entry for 04:10. “My ledger shows two lock openings that night. One at 23:45. One at 04:10.”

Keast looked at the entry. His face gave nothing away.

She turned the lock-keeper’s log. “The official log shows only one opening. At 23:45.”

The room was very quiet. Dust motes hung in the grey light that filtered through the high windows. Somewhere beyond the walls, the harbour went about its business, cranes creaking, men calling, the water lapping against the stone.

Keast cleared his throat. “I logged what happened on my shift. If something came in after, that’s not mine to account for.”

Evelyn waited.

“My job’s to keep the gate and note what happens on my shift.” He spoke slowly, as if choosing his words with care. “I don’t account for every irregular opening.”

“There’s no error in the log. There’s an omission.” She kept her voice level. “The gate was opened at 04:10. My ledger proves it. The water levels required it. The gate had to open, Mr. Keast. You know that as well as I do.”

Keast looked at the window. His jaw worked, the muscles tightening beneath his skin.

“Someone opened that gate,” Evelyn continued. “And someone decided not to record it. I need to know why.”

For a long moment, Keast said nothing. Then he turned back to her, and his expression had changed. The affable mask was gone, replaced by something harder, more guarded.

“You’re asking me to explain something I don’t understand myself.”

“Then explain what you can.”

He ran a hand over his face. “That night… it was a strange one. The tide was high, higher than predicted. We had the first opening at 23:45, let the fishing boats out. Standard procedure. Then around four, I got a call from the sluice gate. There was a problem, they said. I went to check.”

“The sluice gate.” Evelyn made a note in her mind. “At what time?”

“Must have been around 03:30, 03:45. I was there for… half an hour, maybe longer. When I came back, the lock had been opened.”

“Opened by whom?”

Keast shook his head. “I don’t know. I assumed it was one of the other keepers. But when I checked the log later, there was no entry. I asked around, but no one admitted to it.” He met her eyes. “I let it go. I shouldn’t have, but I did. The Captain was dead, the coroner was calling it an accident, and I didn’t want to stir up trouble.”

Evelyn absorbed this. His story was plausible. The sluice gate was some distance from the main lock, and problems there would require attention. But something in his delivery made her pause. He was too controlled, too careful. A man telling the truth did not measure his words so precisely.

“You’re saying you were at the sluice gate at 04:10.”

“I’m saying I wasn’t at the lock. I didn’t open it. I didn’t see who did.”

“But you knew it had been opened. You knew the log was incomplete. You said nothing.”

“What was I supposed to say?” His voice tightened. “The coroner’s verdict was in. The Captain was dead. Nothing was going to bring him back. I kept my head down and did my job.”

Evelyn watched him. She had known Tom Keast as a reliable man, a steady presence at the lock for years. But she had also seen how he looked at the Alderman, how he deferred to authority without question. A man who kept his head down was a man who could be made to look the other way.

“The sluice gate,” she said. “Was there a maintenance call that night?”

Keast hesitated. Just for a moment, but enough. “I told you. I got a call.”

“A call from whom?”

“From the harbour. Someone reported a problem.”

“And you went alone.”

“I went alone. It was a simple check.”

Evelyn closed the ledgers. “Thank you, Mr. Keast. You’ve been helpful.”

He blinked, as if surprised by the abrupt conclusion. “That’s it? You’re not going to…?”

“I’m going to verify your account. Routine procedure.”

Keast’s face went still. Then he nodded, once, and moved toward the door. “I’ve got nothing to hide, Miss Marsh. You’ll see.”

He left. The door clicked shut behind him.

Evelyn stood alone in the records room, the two ledgers on the table before her. She did not believe him. His story was too convenient, his manner too guarded. But belief was not proof. She needed evidence.

She turned to the shelves that lined the walls. The harbour maintained records for everything: cargo manifests, personnel files, maintenance logs. If there had been a call about the sluice gate, there would be a record of it. If Keast had gone to check, there would be an entry in the maintenance roster.

She found the maintenance roster for the relevant month and pulled it from the shelf. The book was heavy, its pages filled with the neat handwriting of the harbour’s engineering staff. She turned to the date of Captain Trewin’s death and ran her finger down the entries.

The night shift had two recorded calls: a blocked drain on the east quay at 22:15, and a faulty winch on the cargo pier at 01:30. Both had been attended and signed off. There was no entry for the sluice gate.

Evelyn checked the previous page, and the page after. She checked the emergency call log, which recorded all incoming reports. Nothing for the sluice gate. No call, no report, no maintenance entry.

Keast had lied.

She closed the roster and returned it to the shelf. Her hands were steady, her breathing even. She had expected something like this, had prepared herself for the possibility. But the reality of it settled in her chest like cold stone.

Tom Keast had claimed to be at the sluice gate at the critical hour. The records showed no reason for him to be there. Which meant he had not been at the sluice gate. Which meant he had been somewhere else, doing something he did not want known.

The lock gate had opened at 04:10. Captain Trewin had died at approximately that time, his watch stopping when it struck the water. The coroner had ruled it an accident, a slip on the wet steps in the dark. But the missing entry suggested something else. Someone had opened that gate, and someone had erased the trace.

Evelyn gathered the ledgers and left the records room. The corridor was empty, the harbour office beginning to stir with the arrival of the day staff. She needed time to think, to decide what to do with what she had learned.

She walked out into the morning. The sky had lightened, the grey giving way to a pale blue that promised a clear day. The harbour was busier now, men and women moving with purpose, the cranes swinging into action, the water alive with the coming and going of vessels.

She made her way along the quay, past the lock gate where Captain Trewin had died. The steps were still there, wet and slick, descending into the dark water. She had walked past them a thousand times. Now they looked different, ominous, a threshold between the living world and whatever lay beneath.

Alderman Rowe wanted the case closed. He had made that clear from the beginning, pressing for a quick verdict, discouraging questions. The official story was convenient: a tragic accident, a respected man lost to the sea. But the numbers did not lie. The water remembered what the men had chosen to forget.

She continued walking, her mind turning over the facts. Keast had lied about the sluice gate. That much was certain. But why? Was he protecting himself, or someone else? What had happened at 04:10 that required the lock to open and the record to vanish?

She was passing the harbour master’s office when she heard voices. Two men stood in the doorway, speaking in low tones. She slowed, pretending to adjust the strap of her satchel.

“… borrowing again.” The voice was unfamiliar, one of the dockworkers. “Third time this year. Nora’s at her wit’s end.”

“Tom’s a good man.” The second voice was older, gruffer. “He does his duty. This harbour… it takes and takes.”

“Good men don’t always stay good, do they? Not when the debts pile up.”

Evelyn moved on, her heart beating faster. Money troubles. Tom Keast had money troubles. That was a motive, or the beginning of one. A man in debt was a man who could be bought, who could be persuaded to look the other way, to open a gate when he shouldn’t, to forget to write it down afterward.

She found a bench near the water and sat. The ledgers were heavy in her lap, their contradictory entries silent witnesses to a truth that someone did not want known. She opened the tide ledger again and studied the entry for 04:10. The numbers were precise, recorded in her own hand, verified by her own calculations. The gate had opened. The water did not lie.

The official log showed no such entry. The gate had not opened, according to the record. But the gate had opened. Someone had been there. And that someone had decided to erase the trace.

Captain Trewin’s watch had stopped at 04:10. The coroner had noted the time, had used it to establish the approximate hour of death. But if the lock gate had opened at that precise moment, the coincidence was too great. Trewin had been on the steps, in the dark, when someone had opened the gate above him. Had he seen something? Had he interrupted something?

Evelyn closed the ledger. She had enough now to suspect Tom Keast, but suspicion was not proof. She needed more. She needed to understand why he had lied, what he had been doing, who he might be protecting.

She stood and made her way back to the harbour office. The day staff had arrived, the corridors filled with the quiet hum of routine. She passed colleagues who nodded to her, their faces open and untroubled. They did not know what she knew. They had not seen the gap between the two records, the silence where an entry should have been.

She returned to her desk and sat. The green lamp cast its familiar glow, the clock ticked its steady rhythm. Everything looked as it always had, the harbour office a place of order and precision. But beneath that order, something had shifted.

She pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward her and began to write. She wrote down the facts: the two ledger entries, the missing record, Keast’s claim about the sluice gate, the absence of any maintenance call. She wrote down the questions that remained: who had opened the gate, why the log had been falsified, what Captain Trewin had been doing on the steps at that hour.

She underlined the final question: Why is Tom Keast lying?

The answer, she suspected, would lead her deeper into the harbour’s secrets. The water remembered everything. The men had forgotten, or had chosen to forget. But the numbers remained, patient and precise, waiting for someone to read them correctly.

Evelyn set down her pen. She had what she needed for now. A suspect. A motive, or the shadow of one. A path forward.

But she also had something else. A feeling, cold and persistent, that she was not the only one who had noticed the discrepancy. Alderman Rowe wanted the case closed. He had pressed for a quick verdict, had discouraged questions from the start. If he knew what she had found, how would he respond?

She thought of Tom Keast’s face when she had confronted him. The wariness, the careful control. A man who was afraid, but not of her. A man who was protecting something, or someone.

The harbour continued its business around her. The water rose and fell with the tide, patient and inexorable. The lock gate opened and closed, the vessels came and went, the men worked their shifts and collected their pay. But beneath the surface, something else moved. A current that was not recorded in any ledger. A truth that someone had tried to bury.

Evelyn Marsh had found the gap in the record. Now she had to decide what to do with it.

She could go to the Alderman, present her findings, let the official channels handle the rest. But Alderman Rowe had already shown his hand. He wanted the case closed, the verdict unchallenged. If she brought him evidence of wrongdoing, would he act on it, or would he bury it alongside the missing entry?

She could go to the police. But the police had accepted the coroner’s verdict. They had no reason to reopen the case, not without evidence of a crime. And what did she have? A missing entry in a logbook. A lie about a sluice gate. Money troubles and a dead man’s watch.

Or she could keep digging. She could follow the trail where it led, asking questions, checking records, building a case that no one could ignore. But that would mean crossing lines she had never crossed, challenging people she had once trusted, risking the position she had built over seventeen years.

She looked at the sheet of paper before her, the questions written in her neat hand. Why is Tom Keast lying? Who opened the gate at 04:10? What did Captain Trewin see?

The clock on the wall marked the passage of minutes. Outside, the harbour went on with its business, the cranes and the vessels and the men who worked them, all of it moving to the rhythm of the tide. The water rose, the water fell, the gates opened and closed. That was the way of things.

But somewhere in that rhythm, something had gone wrong. A man had died. A record had been falsified. And Evelyn Marsh, who had given seventeen years to the precise accounting of the sea, had stumbled on a truth that someone did not want known.

She folded the sheet of paper and placed it in her satchel, alongside the ledgers. Then she rose from her desk and walked to the window. The harbour spread before her, a maze of stone and water, of vessels and quays, of men and machines. Somewhere in that maze, the answer waited.

She would find it. The water did not lie.

But first, she needed to understand the shape of the thing she was chasing. Tom Keast had lied. His alibi was broken. But that was only the beginning. The lock gate had opened at 04:10 for a reason. Captain Trewin had been on the steps for a reason. And somewhere in the gap between those two facts, the truth waited to be found.

Evelyn turned from the window. The day stretched before her, full of hours that felt both endless and insufficient. She had work to do.

The lock-keeper had lied. That much she knew. The question was why, and what he was hiding, and how far he would go to protect it.

She walked out of the harbour office, into the light and the wind and the endless rhythm of the sea.

The harbourmaster’s building threw a long shadow across the quay. Evelyn was halfway to the police station when she heard the voices—low, urgent, leaking through an open window on the ground floor.

She stopped. Stepped closer.

Two dockworkers stood just inside the doorway, their voices carrying through the open window.

“Three months now,” the first one said. “The bank won’t wait forever.”

“I’m getting it together.” Keast’s voice, stripped of its morning affability. “Another week. Maybe two.”

“You said that in January. You said that before Christmas.” A pause, heavy with accumulated disappointment. “Eight hundred pounds doesn’t vanish into thin air. Unless he’s got a plan we haven’t heard.”

Evelyn pressed herself against the stone wall, heart knocking against her ribs. Eight hundred pounds. A substantial sum for a lock-keeper’s wages—impossible to save, easy to lose.

“The business with the trawler,” Keast said. “I told you. It’s coming through.”

“What business? What trawler?” The older voice cracked with frustration. “You’re borrowing from Peter to pay Paul, and sooner or later, both of them are going to want their due.”

Evelyn didn’t need to hear more. She moved away, footsteps quick and purposeful on the cobblestones.

The ledger discrepancy had been a thread. Now she had the whole cloth: a broken alibi, a hidden debt, a man whose affable smile had slipped the moment she’d pressed him. The sluice-gate roster sat heavy in her bag—ink and paper that proved Tom Keast had never checked the mechanism he’d claimed to inspect. He’d invented the task to explain away the missing hour, the gap where the lock gate had opened at 04:10 with no recorded keeper.

She thought of his hands, steady on the ledger. His easy laugh. The way he’d offered her tea as though hospitality could paper over the cracks in his story.

The police station rose ahead, its brick facade severe against the pale sky. Inside, Sergeant Hockin would listen. She would show him the roster, explain the discrepancy, tell him about the debt she’d overheard.

Tom Keast had become something more than a name in the harbour records. He was the first suspect. The first thread pulled loose from the fabric of the case.

And somewhere behind his easy smile, the truth waited to be found.
