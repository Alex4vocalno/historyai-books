Chapter 17

Proof Before the Board

The clock on the boardroom wall read half past two, and the tide outside the harbourmouth was on the turn. Evelyn Marsh stood at the long oak table where the harbour board held its quarterly reviews, her tide ledger closed beneath her hand, and waited for the men at the other end to stop talking among themselves. Alderman Rowe sat at the head, his gavel untouched beside a glass of water he had not sipped from. To his left, two board members she knew only as Mr.

Pellow and Mr. Vyvyan studied their folded hands. Tom Keast sat against the wall on a straight-backed chair, cap on his knee, his face the color of uncooked dough. Beside him, Josiah Pengelly occupied the corner seat, arms folded, his weather-beaten features arranged in an expression of practiced indifference, performed for the board but not reaching his eyes. Constable Hooper stood near the door, notebook closed, a man waiting for a meeting he expected to be brief.

Rowe cleared his throat. “Miss Marsh. You asked for this hearing. You have fifteen minutes.”

“I will need more than fifteen, Alderman.”

“You have fifteen. The board’s patience is not unlimited.” He did not look at her when he spoke. His gaze rested on the harbour seal mounted on the far wall, the painted anchor and intertwined rope that symbolised the port’s authority. “We have read your memorandum. The board’s position is unchanged. Captain Trewin’s death was ruled misadventure by the coroner. The case is closed.”

“The coroner’s ruling was based on incomplete information.” Evelyn opened her ledger. The pages fell to the entry for November the fourth, the ink dry and settled, her handwriting as controlled as she could make it. She placed the book in the center of the table, turned it so the board members could read. “On the night Captain Trewin died, the lock gates were opened twice. The official lock-keeper’s log records one opening, at twenty-three forty-five. My tide ledger records two: twenty-three forty-five and four-ten.”

Mr. Pellow leaned forward. His collar was too tight; his neck bulged over the edge. “Two entries in a personal ledger. One in the official log. That is a discrepancy, Miss Marsh, not a proof. Clerical errors occur.”

“The lock-keeper’s log is maintained by Tom Keast.” Evelyn did not turn to look at Keast. She could hear him breathing through his mouth, shallow and quick. “Keast’s log records the first opening because the first opening was routine. The tide on November the third required equalisation at twenty-three forty-five. I recorded it. Keast recorded it. The harbour operated normally. But the tide also required a second equalisation. The spring tide on November the fourth was 5.2 metres. The lock gate must open when the external water level drops to match the inner basin. That second equalisation occurred at four-ten. It is not discretionary. It is a function of the tide.”

She reached into her satchel and produced the tide tables, the Admiralty publication she consulted every working day of her life. She set it beside the ledger, her finger finding the column for November the fourth. “The predicted low water was four-fifty-three. The gate must open approximately forty-three minutes before low water to begin equalisation. That is four-ten. The gate opened at four-ten because it had to. The water demanded it. But the official log does not record it, because someone chose not to write it down.”

Rowe shifted in his chair. The leather creaked. “The lock-keeper may have omitted a routine entry. That does not constitute—“

“The lock-keeper did not omit it.” Evelyn raised her voice by a fraction, not enough to be called shouting, enough to stop Rowe’s sentence. “The lock-keeper did not record it because the lock-keeper was not present at four-ten. Keast left his post.” She paused. The silence was absolute. Keast’s breathing had stopped. “Keast’s statement to the harbourmaster’s office, given on November the sixth, asserts he was present at the lock house from midnight until six. His statement is false. The ferry schedule between Porthkennick and Penzance shows the last crossing on November the third departed at twenty-three fifteen. Keast was seen at the ferry ticket office at twenty-three ten. He could not have returned to the lock house before one in the morning. He was absent during the period that included the second opening.”

“That is circumstantial,” Rowe said. He had gone still. The water glass beside his hand caught the grey light from the window.

“Circumstantial evidence is still evidence.” Evelyn removed Captain Trewin’s watch from her satchel and set it on the table. The cracked glass caught the light from the high window. The hands had stopped at ten minutes past four. The watch sat between her ledger and the tide tables, a small, broken instrument that measured nothing now except the moment of its owner’s death. “Captain Trewin’s watch stopped at four-ten. It stopped because it struck the stone edge of the lock steps when he fell. The official explanation is that he slipped on the steps during the first opening, at twenty-three forty-five, and that his watch broke on impact. But the watch reads four-ten. Not twenty-three forty-five. Four-ten.”

She let the words settle. Mr. Vyvyan had begun to fidget with a cufflink. Pengelly’s arms remained folded, but his right hand had moved to grip his left elbow, the knuckles whitening.

“If Captain Trewin fell at twenty-three forty-five, his watch would read twenty-three forty-five. It does not. It reads four-ten. He fell at four-ten. He fell during the second, unrecorded opening. The official log was falsified to conceal that opening, and the coroner’s verdict of misadventure was based on that falsified log.” She straightened. Her hands were steady. She had rehearsed this in her office, in the cold, at her desk, with the tide tables and the charts and the smell of ink. She had traced the sequence until it was as fixed in her mind as the tidal curve itself. “The official record is closed. But the official record is wrong.”

Rowe placed both palms flat on the table. “Miss Marsh. You are a tide clerk. You record water levels and lock schedules. You are not a coroner, you are not a constable, and you are not this board. What you are presenting is speculation built on clerical entries. The watch could have stopped at any time. The mechanism failed. It is an old instrument.”

“The watch was serviced in October.” Evelyn produced the receipt she had obtained from Trewin’s estate, the jeweller’s note in Penzance confirming a new mainspring fitted three weeks before the death. “The mechanism was in working order. It did not fail. It was struck against stone and stopped. It stopped at four-ten because that is when it was struck.”

Rowe opened his mouth. He closed it. He looked at Constable Hooper, who had not moved from his position by the door. Hooper’s notebook remained closed. His expression was the careful blankness of a man who was listening more intently than he wished anyone to know.

Evelyn unfolded her tide chart. It was a large sheet, hand-drawn, the curve of the tidal prediction for November the fourth rendered in black ink. She had plotted the water level at fifteen-minute intervals through the night. The chart showed the high water at twenty-one thirty, the ebb beginning, the first equalisation at twenty-three forty-five, the continuing fall, the second equalisation at four-ten, and the low water at four-fifty-three. She spread it flat on the oak table, weighting the corners with her ledger, the tide tables, the watch, and her pen.

“The water was here, at four-ten.” Her finger traced the calculated curve, following the descending line to the point she had marked in red ink. “The external level was dropping past the basin level. The gate opened. Water flowed out of the basin through the lock, carrying a two-knot ebb current through the channel. If Captain Trewin had entered the water at twenty-three forty-five, during the first opening, the ebb current between twenty-three forty-five and dawn would have carried his body south-west, past the harbourmouth, toward the rocks at Carrick Point. That is where the current runs. I have verified this against the Admiralty tidal stream atlas for this section of coast.”

She paused. The room was silent except for the clock on the wall, its ticking measuring seconds that seemed longer than they were.

“Captain Trewin’s body was found at the foot of the lock steps. Not at Carrick Point. Not in the channel. Not past the harbourmouth. At the foot of the lock steps. The current could not have left him there if he had entered the water at twenty-three forty-five. The current would have taken him. The water does not lie, Alderman. It cannot be persuaded or intimidated or asked to protect the harbour’s reputation. It moves according to physical law, and physical law says Captain Trewin entered the water at four-ten, during the second opening, and that he did not move from the point of entry because the second opening’s current was brief and the body was recovered before the tide turned.”

Mr. Pellow sat back. Mr. Vyvyan had stopped fidgeting. Rowe’s face had acquired a greyish quality beneath the tan, the color of a man who has been presented with something he cannot argue against and is searching for a way to not argue at all.

“Miss Marsh.” Rowe’s voice was lower. “This is a serious matter. You are suggesting—“

“I am not suggesting. I am stating.” Evelyn turned to the tide chart again. Her finger rested on the red mark. “The lock gate opened at four-ten. Captain Trewin was on the steps at four-ten. His watch stopped at four-ten. The official log does not record the opening because the person who opened the gate did not want it recorded. That person needed Captain Trewin on the steps at four-ten, in the dark, during the only window in which the gate would open and no one would expect to see activity at the lock.”

She turned to face the table. Keast shifted at the far end, his cup halfway to his mouth, and set it down with a careful, deliberate steadiness — the gesture of a man ensuring his hands were seen and occupied. She did not look at him again. She looked at Pengelly.

“Josiah Pengelly.” She said the name the way she entered a figure in a ledger: precisely, without inflection, without heat. “On November the third, you submitted a berthing receipt to the harbour office claiming your vessel, the Morning Star, was moored at the outer quay from twenty-two hundred through the night. The receipt is time-stamped by the harbour watchman. The time-stamp is twenty-two fifteen.”

Pengelly’s arms unfolded. He placed his hands on the arms of his chair. He did not speak.

“The outer quay is visible from the lock house. If the Morning Star was at the outer quay all night, Keast would have seen it. Keast’s statement does not mention the Morning Star. Keast’s statement does not mention any vessel at the outer quay. Because the Morning Star was not at the outer quay.” Evelyn produced the second document: the harbour dues register, which she had cross-referenced with the ferry manifest and the watchman’s log. “The Morning Star departed the inner basin at twenty-one forty-five on November the third, transiting the lock during the first opening. It returned to the inner basin at four-ten, during the second opening. The second opening was not routine. It was not logged. It was opened so the Morning Star could re-enter the basin without record.”

Pengelly’s jaw tightened. The creased skin around his eyes deepened. His hands gripped the chair arms, the thick, knotted fingers pressing into the wood.

“Captain Trewin was harbourmaster. He held authority over berth assignments and harbour dues. You had a dispute with him regarding berthing fees. The dispute is recorded in the harbour office correspondence file, dated October the twenty-eighth. You owed dues in excess of forty pounds. Captain Trewin had threatened to revoke your berthing licence.” Evelyn set the correspondence file on the table. The letters were inside, Trewin’s handwriting and Pengelly’s, the exchange escalating from formality to hostility over three pages. “You had motive. You had means: you knew the tide, you knew the lock, you knew the second opening would occur at four-ten. And you had opportunity: the Morning Star was on the water, unrecorded, its position unaccounted for between twenty-one forty-five and four-ten.”

She placed the last document beside the others. It was the receipt from the Penzance chandler, the one she had found in Pengelly’s warehouse office during her visit: a purchase of rope and a weighted sinker, dated October the thirtieth. Not proof of murder by itself. But set beside the broken alibi, the falsified berthing receipt, the unrecorded lock opening, and the stopped watch, it formed a sequence that had only one reading.

“You returned to the basin at four-ten. The lock gate opened for you. Captain Trewin was on the steps. Whether he was there because he suspected unauthorised entry, or because he was lured there, I cannot say. But he was there. And he fell. And his watch stopped. And the gate closed, and the Morning Star was moored at the inner quay by four-thirty, and the body was found at dawn by a dockhand who raised the alarm.”

Pengelly stood. The chair scraped against the floor. His face was dark, the blood rising beneath the weather-beaten skin. “This is—“

“Sit down, Pengelly.” Rowe’s voice cut across the room, sharp and cold. Pengelly did not sit. He remained standing, his hands at his sides, the knotted fingers curling and uncurling.

“You have no proof.” Pengelly’s voice was loud, the way it always was when he was cornered: theatrical, filling the space, designed to overwhelm. “You have a clerk’s scribbles and a dead man’s watch and a lot of arithmetic. You were not there. You saw nothing. You know nothing.”

“The tide was there.” Evelyn’s voice did not rise. She met his gaze. “The tide saw you enter the basin at four-ten. The tide does not make clerical errors. The tide does not hold grudges. The tide was there, and it says you were there, and Captain Trewin’s watch says you were there, and the lock-keeper’s log says someone removed the record of your being there. That is not arithmetic. That is evidence.”

Pengelly’s mouth opened. Nothing came out. He sat down. The chair scraped again. His hands returned to the arms, gripping.

The room was silent. The clock ticked. Mr. Pellow coughed into his fist. Mr. Vyvyan stared at the tide chart. Tom Keast had not moved or spoken since Evelyn had named the second opening. His cap lay on his knee, crushed by white fingers.

Rowe sat motionless for a long moment. His palms remained flat on the table. He looked at the documents spread before him: the ledger, the tide tables, the watch, the chart, the berthing receipt, the correspondence file, the chandler’s receipt. He looked at them the way a man looks at something he has decided to ignore and has run out of ways to ignore.

“Alderman.” Constable Hooper stepped forward from the door. His voice was quiet, measured, the voice of a man who had been waiting for the correct moment and had found it. He opened his notebook. His pen appeared in his hand as though it had always been there. “I have heard the evidence presented. I note the discrepancy between the official log and the tide clerk’s ledger. I note the stopped watch. I note the tidal calculation regarding the position of the body. I note the berthing receipt and the correspondence regarding debt.” He looked at Rowe. “I note that the evidence, taken together, constitutes reasonable grounds to believe that a crime has been committed and that a specific individual may be responsible.”

Rowe did not respond immediately. His jaw worked. He looked at Hooper the way a man looks at a door he thought was locked and has found open.

“Constable, the board has not—“

“The board’s authority in this matter is administrative, Alderman. Criminal investigation falls to the constabulary.” Hooper’s tone did not change. He was not being insubordinate. He was being precise. “I am requesting all documents presented today. The tide ledger, the lock-keeper’s log, the tide tables, the charts, the berthing receipt, the harbour dues register, the correspondence file, and the watch. I will also require statements from Tom Keast and Josiah Pengelly, to be taken at the station.”

Rowe’s hands lifted from the table. He placed them in his lap, below the edge, where no one could see them. “This will be damaging to the harbour.”

“The harbour’s reputation is not my concern, Alderman. The law is my concern.” Hooper turned to Evelyn. “Miss Marsh. I will need your ledger and your charts. I will also ask you to provide a written summary of your tidal calculations, with references to the Admiralty tables, for the record.”

Evelyn nodded. She had expected this. She had prepared the summary already, three pages of figures and references, written in her clearest hand. She placed it on the table beside the other documents. Her fingers brushed the cover of her tide ledger. Five years of entries. Five years of water levels and lock schedules and the patient, unremarkable record of a harbour’s comings and goings. She had carried this ledger to the boardroom, and she would leave it here — for Hooper’s inspection only, with the understanding that she would reclaim it once he had noted its contents. The thought produced a sensation she did not examine. She let it pass.

Hooper collected the documents methodically, stacking them with the care of a man who understood that each piece of paper would be examined and argued over in a setting less forgiving than this one. He placed the watch in his breast pocket. The cracked glass pressed against his chest. He wrote in his notebook: date, time, location, persons present, documents received. His pen scratched in the silence.

Rowe stood. “This meeting is concluded.” His voice was flat. He did not look at Evelyn. He did not look at Pengelly. He looked at the harbour seal on the wall, the painted anchor and intertwined rope, and then he picked up his glass of water, untouched, and left the room. His footsteps echoed on the boards. Mr. Pellow and Mr. Vyvyan followed, neither of them speaking, their faces set in the careful blankness of men who had witnessed something they would prefer to forget.

Pengelly stood. He did not look at Evelyn. He looked at Constable Hooper. “I’ll not be making any statement without a solicitor.”

“That is your right,” Hooper said. “I will expect you at the station by four o’clock.”

Pengelly walked to the door. His shoulders were set, his gait stiff, the practiced economy of his movement gone ragged at the edges. He passed through the doorway and was gone. The door remained open. Cold air moved through it from the corridor.

Keast remained in his chair. His cap was still crushed in his hands. He looked at Evelyn. His mouth worked. He said nothing. Hooper touched his shoulder. “Keast. Come with me.”

Keast stood. He walked to the door. At the threshold, he paused. “I didn’t—“ he began. He stopped. He walked out.

Evelyn and Hooper were alone. The boardroom was cold. The window looked out over the harbour, where the tide was turning, the water shifting from ebb to flood, the current reversing direction with the slow, irresistible patience of a force that answered to nothing human. The oak table was bare now, its surface marked with the faint impressions where her documents had lain. The smell of old polish and salt air hung in the room.

Hooper closed his notebook. He looked at Evelyn. His face was not unkind. It was the face of a man who had been given a task and intended to perform it.

“You understand this will take time,” he said. “The tidal evidence will need to be verified by someone the court will accept. I am not that person. You may be, but the defense will argue you are compromised.”

“I understand.”

“You have done something here, Miss Marsh. Whether it holds is for the court to decide. But you have given me enough to open the case. I will need you available for questioning.”

“I will be at the harbour office. I am there every day.”

Hooper nodded. He gathered the documents under his arm. The tide ledger, the chart, the tide tables, the receipts, the correspondence, the written summary. He paused at the door.

“The watch,” he said. “You kept it all this time.”

“It was given to me for safekeeping. Captain Trewin’s effects were stored in the harbour office pending the inquest. The inquest concluded. No one claimed them. I kept the watch because it was evidence the inquest did not examine.”

Hooper looked at her for a moment. He did not comment on the irregularity. He nodded again and left.

The door closed. The boardroom was empty. Evelyn stood at the table. The clock on the wall ticked, measuring seconds that belonged to a different timepiece now, one that counted the hours of an official investigation rather than a private inquiry. She looked at the window. The harbour lay below, grey and ordinary, the water rising slowly against the quay wall. The Morning Star was at its mooring, riding the incoming tide. The lock gates were closed. The water moved as it always moved, according to laws that had nothing to do with the men who used it and everything to do with the truth it carried.

She picked up her satchel. It was lighter now, without the ledger, without the charts, without the watch. The weight she had carried for weeks had transferred to other hands. She did not feel lighter. She felt the absence the way one feels a removed tooth: a space where something solid had been, the tongue returning to it again and again.

She walked to the door. She opened it. The corridor was cold and empty. She walked through it, past the harbourmaster’s office, past the clerk’s room where her desk sat beneath the window, past the filing cabinet and the green-shaded lamp and the pen she had left in its holder. She walked outside, onto the quay, where the wind came off the water and the salt smell was strong.

The tide was coming in.
