Chapter 3

The Discrepancy in the Ledger

The green-shaded lamp cast a pool of light on Evelyn’s open tide ledger, the numbers marching in neat, inescapable rows. Outside the harbour office window, the November dark had settled over Porthkennick, and with it the quiet that followed the day’s business. The inquest was done. Captain Silas Trewin was officially dead by misadventure, a fall in the dark on a slippery step. The coroner’s verdict lay recorded in the town’s books, and the town itself seemed eager to turn the page.

Evelyn turned back to her work. The monthly reconciliation could not wait for grief or doubt. The tide ledger required her attention, and attention was what she had always given it. Her pen moved down the column of figures, checking each entry against the supporting documents, the habit of years guiding her hand. November 5th. The spring tide. The night Captain Trewin died.

Her finger stopped.

Two entries sat in her ledger, recorded in her own careful hand. The first: 23:45, lock gate opened, inward traffic, vessels entering from the outer harbour. The second: 04:10, lock gate opened, outward traffic, vessels departing to the open sea.

She had recorded both. She remembered recording both. The memory was clear in her mind—she had been at her desk in the pre-dawn dark, the lamp casting its circle of light just as it did now, her pen moving as the lock-keeper’s signal came through. The second opening had been necessary. The spring tide had brought a surge of water into the basin, and the pressure against the gates required careful management. An outward opening at the turn of the tide prevented damage to the mechanism. Every tide clerk in Porthkennick knew this. It was procedure.

Evelyn laid down her pen. The official lock-keeper’s log should confirm her entries. It always did. The system was simple: the lock-keeper on duty recorded each opening in his log, signed it, and filed the report with the harbour office. The tide clerk then verified the log against her own observations and the tidal data. Any discrepancy was flagged and investigated. In five years, Evelyn had never found a discrepancy that was not explained by a simple error—a blurred figure, a transposed number, a forgotten signature.

She rose from her chair and crossed to the filing cabinet in the corner of the office. The metal drawer slid open with a soft clatter, and her fingers found the folder marked “Lock Logs — November 1923.” The paper was crisp under her touch, the folder still holding the faint scent of the sea salt that seemed to permeate everything in this harbour town. She withdrew the log for November 5th and carried it back to her desk.

The pages fell open to the relevant date. Evelyn’s eyes moved down the column.

23:45 — Lock gate opened. Inward traffic. Two vessels entered. Signed: T. Keast.

She turned the page. The next entry, recorded hours later, bore a different time and a different signature.

Nothing.

Where her ledger showed a second opening at 04:10, the official log showed nothing at all. The line was blank, just the faint ruled grid waiting to be filled. The entries that followed belonged to the morning watch, hours later. The space where the 04:10 opening should have been recorded was simply empty.

Evelyn sat very still. The lamplight fell across the two open books side by side on her desk—her tide ledger on the left, the lock-keeper’s log on the right. The discrepancy was unmistakable. Her ledger showed two openings. The official log showed one. Either her record was wrong, or the log was incomplete.

She did not make errors of this kind. Her work was her reputation. The harbour board trusted her figures because they were always correct. Alderman Rowe himself had praised her accuracy more than once, noting that her tide tables never varied from the observed water levels by more than a minute’s worth of flow. She had built her position on precision, and precision did not allow for phantom entries.

But neither did the lock-keeper’s procedure allow for missing ones.

Tom Keast had been on duty that night. His signature was clear enough on the 23:45 entry, the familiar angular hand that Evelyn had seen in logs for years. If a second opening had occurred, he should have recorded it. If no opening had occurred, her ledger should show nothing. One of the two records was false.

She reached for her tide tables, the worn reference book she kept at her desk. The pages were soft at the edges from constant use, the binding giving. November 5th. Spring tide. High water at 02:47, height 5.8 metres. Low water at 09:15, height 0.9 metres. The tidal range was extreme—nearly five metres between high and low. The pressure on the lock gates during such a tide was significant.

Evelyn calculated the flow. The incoming tide would have filled the basin by late evening, the water rising against the gates with the weight of the Atlantic behind it. By 04:00, the tide would be beginning to ebb. The pressure differential between the basin and the outer harbour would shift. Without an outward opening to equalise the levels, the strain on the gate mechanism could cause damage. The harbour board had strict protocols for spring tides. The lock-keeper was required to open the gates at the turn of the tide to prevent structural stress.

A second opening at 04:10 was not merely possible. It was necessary.

Her ledger was correct. The log was wrong.

The implication settled over her like the November fog that rolled in from the sea. Someone had opened the lock gate at 04:10 and chosen not to record it. The procedure was clear: every opening had to be logged, signed, and filed. The omission was not an accident. It was a deliberate act.

And Captain Trewin had been found dead at the foot of the lock steps at dawn, his watch stopped at 04:10.

Evelyn’s hand moved to the small drawer at the side of her desk. She opened it and withdrew the object that lay inside—a pocket watch, its crystal cracked, its hands frozen. She had taken it from the evidence box after the inquest, and kept it, the rust still rough on its hands. The brass case was cool against her palm. The time stared up at her, unchanging.

04:10.

The same time her ledger recorded for the second lock opening. The same time the official log showed nothing at all.

She set the watch down on the desk between the two books. The three objects lay in a row—the ledger, the watch, the log. Evidence, though she was not yet certain of what. The verdict had been convenient—an accident, a slip on wet stone in the dark—and Alderman Rowe had seemed relieved. The harbour board could proceed with its business. The town could forget.

But the numbers did not lie. Evelyn knew this with the certainty that had sustained her through five years of tides and logs and endless columns of figures. The water rose and fell according to laws that no human hand could alter. The lock gates opened and closed according to procedures that the harbour board had established for good reason. And when those procedures were followed, they left records. The absence of a record was itself a record—an absence that spoke of something hidden.

She looked again at the blank line in the lock-keeper’s log. The ruled grid waited for an entry that would never come. Tom Keast had signed the 23:45 opening. His shift would have continued until the morning watch relieved him. If the second opening had occurred, he would have been the one to oversee it. He would have been the one to record it.

Unless someone else had opened the gate. Unless Tom Keast had been absent, or distracted, or prevented from witnessing what happened. Or unless he had chosen to conceal it.

Evelyn closed her eyes. The possibilities multiplied in the darkness behind her lids, each one more troubling than the last. She was a tide clerk, not a constable. Her duty was to the records, to the figures, to the unyielding truth of the harbour’s comings and goings. She had no authority to investigate a death, no standing to question a verdict. The coroner had spoken. The board had accepted. The matter was closed.

But her ledger remained open on the desk before her, its two entries clear and undeniable.

23:45.04:10.

The discrepancy would not resolve itself. It would sit in her records until she either corrected it or explained it. To correct it would be to falsify her own work, to admit an error she had not made. To explain it would require answers she did not have.

She opened her eyes and looked at the watch once more. The cracked glass caught the lamplight, throwing a small fractured shadow across the desk. Captain Trewin had carried this watch for years. She had seen him consult it on the quay, checking the time against the tide, calculating the hours until the next high water. He was a man who lived by the clock, as all harbourmen did. The sea waited for no one, and neither had he.

Now he was dead, and his watch had stopped at the precise moment her ledger recorded an event that the official log denied.

Evelyn drew a slow breath. The air in the office was cool, the coal fire in the grate having burned low while she worked. She rose and added a fresh lump of coal from the scuttle, watching the embers brighten, the flames lick at the new fuel. The routine action steadied her. She was overthinking. There could be a simple explanation. Tom Keast might have forgotten to record the opening—a grave error, but not impossible. He might have been called away, or taken ill, or simply neglected his duty in the exhaustion of a long night. The omission might be incompetence rather than malice.

But the watch. The watch stopped at 04:10, the very time the opening should have occurred. The coincidence was too precise. Captain Trewin had been on those steps at that hour, in the dark, when no one should have been near the lock. Why would the harbourmaster inspect the gate at four in the morning? He had a home, a bed, a wife who had looked bewildered and small at the inquest, her hands folded in her lap. He had no reason to be abroad at such an hour.

Unless someone had summoned him. Unless someone had needed him there.

Evelyn returned to her desk and sat. The two books still lay open before her, their contradictory entries accusing each other across the narrow gap. She picked up her pen and held it poised over the ledger, but she did not write. To alter her record was impossible. It was her work, her handwriting, her professional word. If the log was wrong, the log must be corrected, not her ledger.

But to correct the log would require an explanation. And she had no explanation to give.

She laid the pen down. The harbour office was silent around her, the only sound the soft crackle of the reviving fire and the distant lap of water against the quay wall. The town was settling into its evening quiet, the fishing boats secured, the merchants gone home, the harbour board members at their dinners. No one would disturb her here. The night was hers.

Evelyn turned back to the tide tables. The pages fell open to November, and she traced the columns with her finger, calculating the tidal flow for each hour of the night in question. The spring tide had brought a surge of water into the basin. The gates had been opened at 23:45 to allow inward traffic—two vessels, according to Tom Keast’s log. Those vessels would have ridden the flood tide into the harbour, seeking shelter or berth. By the time the tide turned, the basin would be full, the water pressing against the lock gates with tons of pressure.

A second opening at 04:10 would have allowed the excess pressure to equalise, the water flowing outward as the tide ebbed. It was standard procedure. Evelyn had recorded such openings dozens of times in her years as tide clerk. The necessity was beyond question.

She closed the tide tables and sat back in her chair. The discrepancy was real. The unlogged opening had occurred. And Captain Trewin had died at the precise hour when it should have taken place.

The official verdict was accidental death. The coroner had seen no reason to look further, and Alderman Rowe had been eager to close the matter. A slip on wet stone, a fall in the dark, a tragedy that could have befallen any man walking the harbour at night. There was no evidence of foul play, no sign of struggle, no witness to contradict the simple narrative of misfortune.

But the numbers told a different story. The numbers did not care about convenience or closure. They simply were, and they said that something had happened at 04:10 that someone had chosen not to record.

Evelyn looked at the blank line in the log once more. The absence of an entry was itself evidence. Someone had opened that gate. Someone had failed to record it. And that someone now had a secret to keep.

She closed the lock-keeper’s log and set it aside. Her own ledger she left open, the two entries staring up at her like eyes in the lamplight. 23:45.04:10. The figures were innocent, mere notations of harbour business. But taken together with the dead man’s watch, with the empty space in the official record, they became something else. They became a question.

The question was this: if the official path was closed, how would she proceed?

Evelyn Marsh was a clerk. She had no authority, no standing, no power to compel answers from anyone. But she had her records, and her records were accurate. The discrepancy would not disappear because it was inconvenient. It would remain in her ledger, in the harbour office, in the official archives of Porthkennick, waiting for someone to notice.

Someone already had.

She rose from her desk and went to the window. The harbour lay dark below her, the quay lanterns casting small pools of light on the wet stone. The tide was turning, the water beginning to rise against the harbour wall. Somewhere out there, in the black water of the basin, the lock gates stood silent. They had opened at 04:10 on November 5th. The water knew it, if no one else did.

The day had begun. The harbour was waking. And somewhere in the gap between two records, a truth waited to be found.

Evelyn turned back to her desk. She picked up the dead man’s watch—the one she had brought from the evidence box—and held it in her palm, feeling its weight, its stillness. The hands would never move again. But the time they marked—the time she had recorded in her ledger—was a moment that someone wanted forgotten.

She would not forget.

The official record was closed. Her private certainty was not.

She pulled the tide tables from the shelf and spread them across the desk. November 5th. High water at 02:47, low water at 09:15. The ebb current would have been running strong by 04:00—any vessel needing departure would have required the gates. Her fingers traced the calculations she knew by heart: the tidal window, the necessary depth, the timing that no experienced lock-keeper would misjudge.

The official log showed nothing.

Evelyn opened her ledger to the entry she had recorded that night. 04:10. Gate opened. Vessel departed, name unlit. She remembered the sound of the winch, the groan of the hinges, the way the black water had swallowed whatever had passed through. Her hand had written the truth because her hand knew no other way.

She placed the two documents side by side. The lock-keeper’s log—official, bound, certified—bore only the 23:45 entry. Her ledger—private, carbon-copied, stored in this room—told a different story.

A clerk could err. A clerk could sleep, could miss an entry, could forget. But the tide tables did not lie. The current did not forget. The water had risen that night according to laws older than any harbour master’s appointment, and those laws demanded the gates open twice.

Someone had opened them. Someone had recorded the opening in her presence, had called the time across the quay. And that same someone—or another—had later struck the entry from the official record, leaving only the clean, unremarkable first opening to stand as truth.

Evelyn closed both books. The discrepancy was no longer a suspicion. It sat before her in ink and paper, a gap where history should have been.

The lock-keeper’s log was a lie. Her ledger was the only proof that a vessel had departed the harbour in the dark hours of November 5th, unrecorded and unnamed.
