Chapter 9

Ink-Stained Fingers, One Truth

The receipt lay where she had left it, corner aligned with the grain of the desk.

Evelyn Marsh sat motionless in the pooled light of the green-shaded lamp. The harbour office had emptied hours ago. Footsteps had receded down the corridor, a door had clicked shut, and then silence—that particular silence of a government building after hours, when the stone walls seemed to absorb the day’s accumulated noises and hold them in suspension. The air smelled of lamp oil and old paper, of the coal fire long dead in the grate and the permanent damp that crept through Porthkennick’s foundations.

She had not moved since Alderman Rowe’s departure. His words still hung in the room like smoke from a pipe long extinguished. The public trust. The harbour’s reputation. The convenient fiction of an accidental death.

Her hands rested on the desk’s edge. The ink stains on her first two fingers had darkened with the day’s work, and she could feel the slight ridge where the pen’s grip had pressed into her middle finger. Small evidences. The body kept its own records.

Evelyn drew a slow breath and reached for the tide ledger.

The leather cover was cool and familiar under her fingertips. She opened it to the page in question—the night of Captain Trewin’s death—and smoothed the paper flat. Her own handwriting stared back at her, neat and precise: two lock gate openings recorded, one at 23: 45, one at 04: 10. The ink had dried to a deep brown, permanent and unambiguous.

Beside it she placed the official lock-keeper’s log. Tom Keast’s cramped hand recorded only the first opening. A single line where two should have been.

The discrepancy was no longer new. It had become a fixture in her mind, a thing she turned over and over like a stone in her pocket. But now she arranged the documents with fresh deliberation, creating a constellation of paper across the desk’s dark surface.

The tide tables came next. She opened them to the relevant date and traced the column with one finger. High water at 03: 58. Low water at 10: 22. The second lock opening at 04: 10 was tidally necessary—the gates would have opened to equalise the water levels between the harbour basin and the open sea. Captain Trewin’s watch, stopped at 04: 10, placed him on those steps at precisely the moment the gates should have been operating.

She drew Pengelly’s receipt from beneath the others. The paper was slightly warped from the morning’s rain, the ink smudged at one corner where a drop had fallen. The chandler’s stamp was clear enough: Josiah Pengelly, thirty fathoms of new mooring rope, paid in cash. The time stamp read 06: 45.

Evelyn’s finger rested on that time stamp. The ink had bled slightly into the paper’s weave, creating tiny tributaries of blue-grey that spread from each numeral. The chandler’s assistant had been hasty that morning, pressing the stamp down with uneven force. The “6” showed the pressure of the final digit, deeper at its upper curve. The “45” tilted slightly upward, as if the hand applying it had been moving away before the ink fully transferred.

She turned to her tide calculations and began to work.

The Morning Star, Pengelly’s boat, had been moored at the quay. To reach the chandler’s shop from his vessel required walking the length of the eastern jetty, crossing the harbour road, and proceeding uphill for approximately twelve minutes at a steady pace. Pengelly claimed he had been asleep on his boat until waking early to inspect his moorings. He had discovered the frayed rope, he said, and gone immediately to purchase a replacement.

Evelyn wrote the numbers in the margin of her notebook. If Pengelly had been asleep and woken at first light—which on that date occurred at approximately 06: 15—he would have needed to dress, assess the damage to his mooring, and reach the chandler’s by 06: 45. Possible. Barely.

But the tide tables told a different story.

She cross-referenced the tidal state at 04: 10. The lock gates would have opened to allow the ebbing water to equalise. Any vessel moored in the inner harbour would have felt the surge, the sudden pull as millions of gallons drained toward the sea. The Morning Star, tied fast at the quay, would have strained against her lines. The old rope, if already frayed, would have shown its weakness then.

Evelyn’s pencil moved across the page, calculating times and distances. If the rope had parted at 04: 10, Pengelly would have discovered it immediately. The Morning Star would have drifted. A boatman of his experience would not have waited until dawn to secure his vessel. He would have been on the quay within minutes, retying or seeking help.

Which meant he would have been on the quay at the very moment Captain Trewin fell.

She sat back. The lamp’s flame guttered slightly in a draft from the window, casting moving shadows across the documents. The calculation was crude, but it raised a question that would not settle: if Pengelly had been awake and on the quay at 04: 10, why had he claimed to be asleep?

And if he had purchased the replacement rope at 06: 45, what had he been doing in the intervening two and a half hours?

The receipt’s time stamp glared up at her. She could almost smell the rain that had fallen that morning, could picture Pengelly standing in the chandler’s doorway, water dripping from his oilskins. The purchase itself was unremarkable. Boats required new ropes. Frayed lines parted. But the timing—

Evelyn reached for Captain Trewin’s watch. The cracked glass caught the lamplight, throwing a small prism across the desk. The hands had frozen at 04: 10, the mechanism stopped by the impact of his fall. Or by water. Or by some other force entirely.

She had examined the watch before. The coroner had noted its stopped state and moved on. But now she turned it over in her hands, feeling its weight, the smoothness of the case where Trewin’s palm had worn it over years of use. The back was slightly dented. A small scratch near the winding crown suggested rough handling at some point.

The watch told her nothing new. But its stopped time aligned precisely with the second lock opening. The opening Tom Keast had failed to record. The opening that should not have happened without someone’s hand on the mechanism.

She drew a fresh sheet of paper toward her and began to construct a timeline.

23: 45—first lock opening, recorded in both her ledger and the official log. Normal operations. The evening tide equalising.

Sometime before 04: 10—Captain Trewin arrives at the lock steps. Why? The steps led down to the water, used by boatmen to access small craft moored in the inner harbour. Trewin had no such vessel. His own boat was dry-docked for repairs.

04: 10—second lock opening, recorded only in her ledger. Trewin’s watch stops. Pengelly’s mooring rope parts (possibly). A body on the steps.

04: 10 to 06: 45—the missing hours. Pengelly’s whereabouts unknown. The rope purchased in daylight, after the body was discovered.

Evelyn’s pencil paused. The timeline contained a gap that ached with absence. What had happened in those hours? If Pengelly had been on the quay at 04: 10, if he had seen—or caused—Trewin’s death, why wait until daylight to purchase the rope? Unless the rope was not a replacement but a concealment. Unless the original rope had been used for something other than mooring.

She thought of the old rope, the one Pengelly claimed to have replaced. Where was it now? He had said it was frayed, discarded. But no one had seen it. The harbour staff she had asked remembered only the new rope, clean and taut at the Morning Star’s bow.

The filing cabinet stood against the far wall, its brass handle catching the lamplight. Evelyn rose and crossed to it, her footsteps soft on the worn floorboards. The cold of the room had seeped through the soles of her boots. She could feel the harbour’s chill rising through the stone, the endless dampness of a building that stood at water’s edge.

She opened the cabinet and turned past the official ledgers, past the rosters and schedules and maintenance logs, to the drawer she kept for her private folios. The key was on its chain around her neck, warm from her skin. She drew it out and fitted it to the lock.

The mechanism turned with a soft click.

Inside, the drawer held only a few documents: personal notes, observations from her years of tide-watching, records she had kept for her own satisfaction rather than official necessity. The harbour office did not encourage private record-keeping. All knowledge belonged to the institution. All truth was subject to the board’s authority.

She looked down at the discrepant ledger page in her hand. The paper was slightly curled from having been removed and replaced multiple times over the past days. Her own handwriting recorded what the official log denied: two openings, not one. The water did not lie. The tide itself had demanded that second opening.

If she filed this page in the official record, it would disappear. Someone would notice the discrepancy—Alderman Rowe, or the harbourmaster, or one of the clerks who owed their positions to the board’s favour. The page would be removed, replaced with a clean copy that matched Tom Keast’s log. The record would be made consistent, and the inconsistency would become her word against the institution’s.

She had seen it happen before. Not often, but enough. A discrepancy noted, a quiet word exchanged, a document revised. The harbour’s records were supposed to be sacrosanct, but sanctity was a flexible concept when reputation was at stake.

Evelyn held the page over the open drawer. The lamplight fell across her hands, illuminating the ink stains, the small scars of her profession. She had given five years to these records, and she had spent every one of them trying to make the harbour’s documents worthy of the faith they demanded.

Now the records asked her to choose.

She lowered the page into the private drawer. It slid from her fingers and settled atop her other papers, its presence a weight she could feel even after her hand withdrew. She turned the key. The lock clicked shut.

The sound was very small in the silent room.

Evelyn stood for a moment with her hand resting on the cabinet’s cold surface. The act she had just committed was a breach of protocol. The ledger page belonged in the official record, accessible to inspection, subject to the board’s authority. By removing it to her private folio, she had made herself the sole keeper of its truth.

If Rowe discovered what she had done, her position would be forfeit. She could be dismissed, her reputation ruined, her five years of service rendered meaningless. The harbour office did not tolerate clerks who kept secrets.

But the alternative—filing the page where it could be altered or destroyed—was no alternative at all.

She returned to her desk. The documents still lay in their constellation: tide tables, lock-keeper’s log, Pengelly’s receipt, Trewin’s watch. The evidence was incomplete. She could not yet say who had opened the lock at 04: 10, or why Captain Trewin had been on the steps at that hour, or what connection existed between the lock opening and the stopped watch and the replaced rope.

But Pengelly’s alibi was broken. The receipt’s time stamp, combined with the tidal calculations, proved that he could not have been where he claimed at the hour he claimed. He had been awake, on the quay, at the time of Trewin’s death. And he had lied about it.

The lie transformed everything. An accidental death required no concealment. A slip on wet steps, a fall into the harbour—these were tragedies, not crimes. But if Pengelly had felt the need to fabricate an alibi, then whatever had happened on those steps was no accident.

Evelyn gathered the documents and restored them to their proper places. The tide ledger went back on its shelf, the lock-keeper’s log to the records room, the tide tables to their designated slot. Only the receipt and the watch remained on her desk—the one pointing toward Pengelly, the other toward the locked gate and the hour of 04: 10.

She looked at the watch’s stopped hands. The coroner had called the death an accident. Alderman Rowe had called the matter closed. The harbour board wanted only silence and consistency, the smooth surface of a record without blemish.

But the water had witnessed something else. The tide had opened the gate at 04: 10, and someone had been there to witness it—or to cause it. Captain Trewin had died at that hour, his watch frozen forever at the moment of his fall. And Josiah Pengelly, boatman and alibi-liar, had purchased a new rope two and a half hours later, as if a frayed line could explain away the blood on the steps.

Evelyn’s hands were steady as she placed the watch in her pocket. The receipt she folded carefully and tucked into her coat. These were her evidence now, her private burden. The official record would show only what the board wished it to show.

She rose from the desk and crossed to the window. The harbour stretched below, dark and glittering under the clouded sky. Somewhere out there, the Morning Star rode at her moorings, secured by a new rope that had cost Josiah Pengelly thirty fathoms and a morning’s errand. Somewhere, Tom Keast slept in his cottage by the lock, his omission recorded in his own cramped hand. Somewhere, Alderman Rowe sat in his warm parlour, confident that the tide clerk had understood her warning.

The window glass was cold against her palm. Evelyn pressed her hand flat against it, feeling the chill seep through her skin. Her breath fogged the surface, obscuring the view for a moment before fading.

The ledger demanded one answer. But the question of whom to confront first—with the broken alibi, with the evidence of lies—remained.

She turned from the window, collected her lamp, and made her way toward the door. The harbourmaster’s building settled around her, its stone walls holding the weight of years and records and small, necessary deceptions. Tomorrow would bring new calculations, new questions, new pressure from those who wished the truth to remain buried.

But tonight, Evelyn Marsh walked out into the darkness with the evidence in her pocket and a choice behind her. The ledger’s truth would not be silenced by authority or convenience. The tide had witnessed what men wished to hide.

And she, its humble clerk, would find a way to make it speak.
