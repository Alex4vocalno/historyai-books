Chapter 3

The First Discrepancy

The money from her first sale — four hundred escudos in used notes, counted twice in her flat with the curtains drawn — was folded into the lining of her handbag, where it had stayed for three days. She had not spent a single note. Each morning before leaving for work, her thumb found the folded edge of the bills through the leather, pressing once, twice, as though the texture of worn paper against her fingertips could confirm what her mind still doubted. The transaction had happened. The cash was real. She could do this again.

She was doing it again.

The telegram sat in the wooden tray marked INCOMING — FOREIGN POST, second from the top, its edges crisp from the morning sort. Ana had pulled it from the queue, read it once, and her fingers went still on the keys of her Remington. The typewriter’s carriage return lever hung in her right hand, forgotten. Around her, the censorship office hummed with its usual Tuesday sounds: the clatter of other machines, the murmur of Portuguese and English from the translators’ bench, the squeak of a filing cabinet drawer in the corner. The overhead light caught the fine dust that hung in the air. None of it reached her. She was somewhere else entirely.

The telegram was from the British naval attaché’s office in Gibraltar, routed through Lisbon, addressed to a contact code she did not recognize. It concerned a fisherman operating out of Sesimbra, thirty kilometres down the coast. The name was there in the third line, typed clean: Miguel Costa. The message identified Costa as a reliable informant passing coastal shipping movements to British intelligence. It gave his boat’s registration. It gave the nights he was most likely to be at sea.

She knew that name. She knew it the way she knew her own — through Rafael, through the years before they took him, through the long afternoons when her brother and Miguel Costa sat on the harbour wall at Sesimbra mending nets and arguing about politics while the Atlantic moved grey and patient beneath them. Costa had been Rafael’s friend. Costa had lent Rafael money for the pamphlet press that got him arrested. Costa had visited Rafael in custody once, before the authorities made visits impossible, and had told Ana afterward that Rafael was holding, that Rafael was strong, that Rafael had asked about the sea.

Ana set the telegram down on her desk. She picked it up again. She read it a third time, her lips moving around the words without sound.

The censorship office protocol was clear: telegrams containing intelligence of value to foreign agents were to be copied, filed, and passed to the supervising officer. The original went to the routing desk. No censor altered content. No censor made decisions about what the information might do to a man sitting in a harbour town with a boat and a name that someone, somewhere, had decided was worth writing down.

Her hands rested on the Remington’s keys. The letter M sat under her right ring finger. She could type the name as it appeared. She could file the copy, route the original, and let the machine do what the machine did — carry information from one hand to another, from one desk to another, until it reached whoever had use for it. That was the work. That was what she was paid her government salary to do.

The four hundred escudos were the sum of a single sale. Four hundred escudos. Twelve thousand was the figure she had been quoted by the lawyer who handled appeals for political prisoners — twelve thousand for a petition, a hearing, a chance. She was one-thirtieth of the way there. She knew the arithmetic the way she knew her heartbeat.

She also knew that Costa would be dead within a month if the telegram reached the people who wanted that kind of information. Not the British. The British wanted to protect their informant. But the telegram would be filed, and filed documents in Lisbon in 1942 had a way of travelling, of being seen by eyes that should not see them, of finding their way to desks where a fisherman’s name and a boat’s registration were not data points but coordinates.

Her right hand moved. She fed a blank sheet into the Remington. She aligned the carriage. She typed the date, the routing code, the first two lines exactly as they appeared. Her fingers hovered over the keys for the third line.

She typed: António Silva.

The clack of the keys was louder than she expected. Two women at the translators’ bench glanced her way. She pulled the carriage return lever, typed the rest of the message — boat registration, nights at sea, the contact code — and separated the carbon copy from the original. The original she placed in the OUTGOING tray. The carbon she filed under the day’s date in the bottom drawer of her desk, where the censor’s copies lived until the filing clerk collected them each evening.

The altered copy and the original now carried a name that meant nothing. António Silva was common as bread. There were fifty António Silvas in the Lisbon phone directory. There were probably twenty in Sesimbra alone. The name was invisible because it was everywhere.

She gathered her things at the end of the shift, nodded to the supervisor, and walked out into the Rua das Janelas Verdes. The river was gunmetal in the late afternoon light. She did not look at it. She walked to the tram stop with her handbag pressed against her side, her breath shallow, her ribs holding something she had not learned to name.

Three days passed. She did not hear from Keller. She did her work, she filed her copies, and she waited. The money in her bag did not change. The arithmetic remained: four hundred of twelve thousand. She was not close. She was merely closer than she had been.

On the fourth day, a note arrived at her flat, slipped under the door in a plain envelope with no postmark. The message inside was typed, three lines, no signature.

Thursday. Four o’clock. My office.

Keller’s office at the censorship building was a small, functional room on the second floor, past the translation bureau. Ana had been summoned there once before, for a routine briefing. This time felt different. The walk down the corridor seemed longer, the sound of her own footsteps louder.

She arrived at four minutes past four. The door was closed. She knocked.
“Enter.”
She pushed it open and stepped inside.

Otto Keller sat behind the desk. The room was dim, the blinds half-drawn against the afternoon sun, a desk lamp casting a cone of yellow light across the wood. The air held the faint, dry scent of his cigar, though none was lit. His face was pale, composed, the jaw set in its habitual line. His eyes, pale blue behind steel-framed spectacles, tracked her as she entered. His expression did not change, but there was a new tautness to his posture, a deliberate stillness that made the room feel smaller.

“Sit,” he said.

She sat in the chair opposite the desk. Her handbag went on her lap. Her hands went on the handbag. She watched Keller’s hands, which were resting on the desk on either side of a single brown folder. The folder was closed. Her throat tightened.

Keller did not speak immediately. He regarded her with the patience of a man who had learned that silence made people talk before they were ready. The desk lamp hummed faintly. Outside, the muffled sounds of the office continued.

He opened the folder.

Inside were two sheets of paper. Ana recognized both. The first was a telegram — her altered copy, the one she had filed four days ago, with the name António Silva in the third line. The second was also a telegram. She had never seen it before. It was not a copy. It was an original, or a photograph of an original, and in its third line, typed in the same clean font, was the name Miguel Costa.

Keller lifted the left-hand telegram — the one with Costa’s name — and placed it on the desk to his left. He lifted the right-hand telegram — the one with Silva’s name — and placed it to his right. He aligned them with the precision of a man who had done this before. He placed his index finger on the left-hand sheet, beside the name Miguel Costa. Then he placed his other index finger on the right-hand sheet, beside the name António Silva.

He looked up at her. His voice was low and controlled, but the skin around his eyes had tightened. He did not raise it, but the quiet carried a new, cold weight.
“Which one is correct?”

The desk lamp’s hum grew louder, or perhaps the silence around it did. Ana’s hands tightened on her handbag. The leather creaked under her fingers, a small sound that seemed enormous in the stillness. She could feel her pulse in her wrists, her temples, the hollow of her throat. She looked at the two telegrams, side by side, the same message with two different names, and understood with perfect clarity that she had been caught — not in a guess, not in a suspicion, but in a fact laid out on a desk under lamplight by a man who had another source inside the system, a source she had not known existed, a source who had given him the original while she had given him the lie.

Keller watched her without moving. His fingers stayed on the two names. The pale eyes behind the spectacles held no anger, no disappointment, no warmth. They held a focused, surgical attention. That was worse.

“I asked a question,” Keller said.

“It was a clerical error.” Her voice came out flat, stripped of inflection. She heard it as though someone else were speaking. “The original transmission was garbled in the third line. I corrected it to the nearest matching name in the registry.”

It was a lie. It was the first outright lie she had told Keller about her work, and it sat in the air between them like smoke, visible and acrid. She had lied before — to her supervisor about working late, to her landlord about the rent, to herself about what she was doing and why — but those had been small lies, social lies, the kind that oiled the machinery of daily life. This was different. This was a lie told to a man who bought information for a living and who had just shown her that he could tell the difference between a fact and a fabrication.

Keller did not respond immediately. He removed his fingers from the two telegrams, one at a time, and placed his hands flat on the desk, his knuckles pale. He looked at the telegrams for a long moment. Then he looked at her, his gaze sharpening.

“A clerical error,” he repeated. The German accent shaped the Portuguese vowels precisely, each syllable clipped. “You are telling me that a telegram identifying a British informant in a Portuguese fishing village was garbled, and that you, as censor, corrected it by substituting a common name for an uncommon one, and that this substitution was — what — an accident?”

“Yes.”

“And the original, ungarbled version — the one with Miguel Costa — this arrived at my desk from another channel. You are telling me that the other channel is wrong and you are right.”

“I am telling you the text reads as follows.”

Keller was quiet. The desk lamp hummed. A glass rattled somewhere in the building — a door closing on another floor, vibration travelling through the walls. Ana’s knuckles were white on the handbag. She loosened them deliberately, finger by finger, and forced her hands flat on the leather.

“Keller,” he said. It was the way he spoke — referring to himself in the third person when the weight of a statement required it, as though the name carried more authority than the pronoun. “Keller does not tolerate carelessness. Keller does not tolerate invention. What Keller tolerates is accuracy, delivered on time, without embellishment. You understand this.”

“I understand.”

“Then understand also this.” He picked up the two telegrams, folded them once, and returned them to the brown folder. He closed the folder. He placed his hand on it. “A second error of this kind — a second discrepancy between what you provide and what I can verify — and the arrangement ends. Not politely. Not with a conversation. It ends. You will consider what that means.”

She considered what it meant. It meant the twelve thousand escudos receding beyond reach. It meant Rafael in the stone yard at Tarrafal, under the sun that never softened, in the prison camp where political prisoners broke rocks and waited for pardons that did not come. It meant the lawyer’s fee unpaid, the petition unfiled, the hearing that would never happen. It meant her brother staying where he was until the regime decided he had served enough time, and the regime was not in the habit of deciding.

“I understand,” she said again.

Keller opened the desk drawer and placed the folder inside. He closed the drawer. The meeting was over. He had not raised his voice. He had not threatened. He had simply shown her two pieces of paper, asked her a question, and listened to her answer. The threat was in the structure — in the fact that he had another source, in the fact that he could cross-check, in the fact that her lie had been caught and displayed with the efficiency of a man checking a ledger and finding that the columns did not balance.

She stood. Her legs were unsteady. She locked her knees and walked to the door. Behind her, Keller’s voice came once more, quiet and precise.

“The material. Next time. Accurate.”

She closed the door behind her and descended the stairs to the main corridor. The censorship office was emptying for the day. The Rua das Janelas Verdes was full of people going home from work, and Ana walked among them, one more body in the current, carrying her handbag with the four hundred escudos in the lining. She reached the corner and stopped. A tram was approaching, its destination board unreadable in the failing light. She did not board it. She stood on the pavement and breathed, her chest rising and falling in a rhythm she had to remind herself to follow.

The clean business was over. She had known it would come to this — had known it from the moment she typed António Silva, perhaps had known it even earlier, when she first understood that selling information was not a single act but a practice, and that practices accumulated consequences the way riverbeds accumulated silt. She had told herself that the money was simple, that the transaction was clean, that she was moving paper from one side of a desk to another and receiving payment for it. She had told herself that she was a courier, not a participant.

Keller’s other source had disabused her of that. He had another line into the censorship office — or into the routing desk, or into the British naval attaché’s files, or into some channel she had not imagined. He had a second source. He was not buying from her exclusively. He was buying from her and from someone else, and he was comparing what they sold him, and when the comparison produced a discrepancy, he had called her in and laid the evidence on the desk with the calm of a man who had done this before and would do it again.

She boarded the next tram. It was crowded. She stood holding the leather strap, her handbag between her feet, and watched the streets of Lisbon slide past the window. A man in a grey suit sat reading a newspaper two seats away. He did not look up. Nobody looked up. She was invisible, which was what she had wanted, and which was also, she now understood, the condition under which she was most likely to be destroyed.

The tram carried her south and west, through the Baixa and along the river, to her stop on the Rua das Janelas Verdes. She walked home. The staircase was dark. She climbed it with her hand on the wall, the plaster cool and slightly damp against her palm, unlocked her door, and went inside. The flat was cold. She did not turn on the light. She sat on the edge of the bed and opened the handbag and took out the money — four hundred escudos in used notes, the sum of a single sale, the first installment of a price that had just become more expensive.

She counted the notes. She counted them again. She put them back in the lining and closed the bag and sat in the dark with her hands on her knees.

Rafael had said it to her the last time she visited Tarrafal, standing in the visiting yard with the sun on his shoulders and the dust around his feet: Ana, a country is its people, not its borders. He had said it as a fact verified through personal observation, a core truth delivered with quiet authority. She had carried that sentence home from the prison camp the way she now carried the money — folded into a private compartment, touched but not spent.

She had altered the telegram because of that sentence. She had typed António Silva because Miguel Costa was a man, not a data point, and because Costa had been kind to Rafael when kindness was expensive, and because the four hundred escudos in her handbag were supposed to be for Rafael’s freedom and not for the cost of Costa’s death. She had made a choice that had nothing to do with money and everything to do with the thing her brother believed, and the choice had been caught in four days, and the man who caught it had shown her the evidence and asked her to explain, and she had lied, and the lie had been accepted with a warning that was also a promise.

A mistake had been traced to her. The clean business was over. She knew, sitting in the dark with the money and the memory of Keller’s pale hands on two telegrams, that she would do it again — not the alteration, perhaps, but the act of choosing. She would choose again. The money demanded it. Rafael demanded it. And the space between what the money required and what Rafael believed had just become the ground on which she stood, and the ground was not solid, and the ground was moving, and she was already on it.
