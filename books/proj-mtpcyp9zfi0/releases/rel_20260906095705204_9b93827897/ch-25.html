Chapter 25

Two Hundred Million Escudos

The smell of Keller’s cigar smoke reached her before he spoke—a dry, sweetish haze that clung to the anteroom like a second occupant, patient and unnecessary. Ana closed the narrow stairwell door behind her and stood with her hands folded in front of her, the way one stands before a dentist’s chair or a customs desk, waiting for instructions that would not be kind. The release order was folded inside her handbag, a single sheet that had cost more than any document she had ever handled. Rafael had been on a steamer between the islands and the mainland coast, sitting in a cabin she had paid for with banknotes that had passed from Thorne’s careful hands to hers to a chain of intermediaries she would never meet again. She knew the exact figure the way one knows a wound—precisely, because the pain keeps the count fresh.

Keller sat behind the desk. He did not look up. The lighter clicked—that stark metallic sound she had come to associate with the moments before difficulty—and the flame touched the fresh cigar’s end, and he drew on it until the coal glowed evenly. Only then did his eyes settle on her.

“Sit,” he said.

“Sit,” he said.

She sat.

The room was as she remembered it from two weeks ago: the spare desk, the two chairs, the ashtray already holding a prior cigar’s remains, the deliberate cold of a space designed to make the visitor aware of her own discomfort. But something else occupied the desk now. Two sheets of paper, placed side by side with a precision that was itself a kind of accusation. She could not read them from where she sat—he had arranged them facing him—but she did not need to. The format was unmistakable. Telegrams. Censorship forms. The carbon-copy blue of the office’s triplicate paper.

Keller smoked. He let the silence do what silence does in a room where one person holds evidence and the other holds a story.

“You have secured your brother’s release,” he said at last. It was not a question.

“I have.”

“It was processed.”

“It was.”

He tapped the ash from his cigar into the tray. The gesture was small, exact, unhurried. Then he turned the two sheets around on the desk and slid them toward her, his index finger coming to rest beside a name on the left-hand document.

She looked. Keller’s finger tapped the unaltered name on the telegram now lying side by side with her copy, the quiet certainty of a man presenting proof he did not need to shout. The left telegram was the original—the unaltered transmission she had processed months ago, the one identifying a local fisherman as a British informant. The name was clear: Tavares. On the right was her copy, the version she had submitted to Keller, where the name read Costa. The alteration she had made—that half-second when the pen had hovered and her hand had chosen a different name, a safer name, the name of a man who was not Rafael’s friend—sat between them like a scar on clean skin.

She said nothing. There was nothing useful to say yet. Her mind was doing what it had taught itself to do under pressure: measuring distances, calculating which lie was still load-bearing and which had collapsed.

“Someone in your office,” Keller said, “provided me with the original. I will not tell you who. You will not ask.” He drew on the cigar. “What I will ask is this: explain the discrepancy.”

Her throat was dry. The sweat began at the base of her spine, beneath the clean blouse she had changed into at home that morning, a blouse that now felt like a costume worn for an audience that had already seen her undressed. She had prepared for this—not the specifics, but the shape of it. She knew a confrontation would come. She had known it since the night she sat in the dark with the first payment in her lap and the understanding that the clean business was over.

“A clerical error,” she said. The words came out with the practiced steadiness she had rehearsed alone, each syllable pressed flat, the way one presses a seam to keep it from unraveling. “The original telegram was damaged. Water stain across the surname. I could not read the final letters clearly. I—I knew a Costa in the area, a fisherman. I filled in what I thought I was reading.”

She paused. Then, because the excuse alone was not enough and she knew it, she added the thing that was true and therefore most dangerous: “The fisherman—Costa—he was known to my brother. Before Rafael was arrested. I recognised the first syllable and I—I panicked. I thought I was reading a name I knew and I wrote it down without checking the damaged section more carefully. It was stupid. It was a mistake born of—of sentiment. Of exhaustion. I had been working double shifts.”

Keller watched her. His expression did not change. The cigar smoke rose between them in a slow, unwinding ribbon, and she thought: he knows. He knows it is a lie, or at least that the lie is not the whole truth, and he is deciding what to do with that knowledge.

“Costa,” he repeated. “A friend of your brother’s.”

“Yes.”

“And you altered a classified telegram because a name reminded you of someone you knew.”

“I made an error in transcription. The name was partially illegible. I filled the gap incorrectly.”

He was silent again. She could hear the clock on the wall—a small, cheap thing, the kind one finds in government offices across every capital—ticking with the metronomic indifference of an instrument that does not care what happens to the people beneath it.

“The original is legible,” Keller said. “The name is Tavares. There is no water damage significant enough to obscure the surname. I have examined it.”

A cold thread ran through her, starting at the sternum and unspooling downward. The flooding. She had used the October flood as cover—the basement had taken on water, several documents had been affected. But the original telegram, the one Keller’s second source had retrieved, was intact. The name was clear. The water damage she had cited did not exist on this particular document. Her excuse was not merely thin; it was contradicted by the evidence on the desk.

She did not correct herself. To correct herself now would be to admit the lie, and to admit the lie would be to admit everything—the money, the dual operation, the fact that she had chosen to protect a man connected to her brother because she was already, even then, building toward the moment when she would need every thread of connection to pull Rafael free.

“I must have been thinking of a different telegram,” she said. “There were several damaged that week. I—I may have confused the extent of the damage across documents.”

“You may have.”

He let the words hang. Then he placed both palms flat on the desk, on either side of the telegrams, and leaned forward slightly. The cigar rested in the ashtray, still burning, sending its thin column upward.

“I will tell you what I believe,” he said. “I believe you are not incompetent. I have watched you work. Your translations are precise. Your recall is above average. You do not make errors of this kind—not from fatigue, not from sentiment, not from confusion about which document was water-damaged.”

She felt the floor beneath her chair become something thinner, a membrane stretched over a long drop.

“I also believe,” he continued, his voice level, not raised, carrying the weight of a man who has rehearsed his own patience, “that you are more useful to me free than otherwise. This is a calculation I make reluctantly. You should understand that it is a calculation, not a favour.”

She waited.

“If there is a next time—if I find another discrepancy, another name that does not match, another piece of intelligence that has been softened or shifted or made convenient—I will not have this conversation again. The material. Now. Everything that passes through your desk comes to me unaltered, or you will discover what the alternative looks like. And I assure you, it will not be a conversation.”

He picked up the cigar. He drew on it once, slowly, and the coal brightened and dimmed.

“You may go.”

She stood. Her legs were steady, which surprised her, because the rest of her felt as though it had been emptied and refilled with something colder and less substantial. She picked up her handbag. The release order crinkled faintly inside it—the paper that proved Rafael was free, that the money had done its work, that the chain of bribes and intermediaries and weeks of negotiation had achieved their single, brutal purpose.

She walked to the door. She did not look back. The stairwell was narrow and cold and smelled of damp stone, and she descended it with her hand on the wall, her fingers trailing the rough plaster, and she did not allow herself to shake until she reached the side entrance and stepped into the lane.

The lane smelled of drains and sea salt and cooking oil and the exhaust of a passing lorry. She leaned against the wall for a moment, the stone cold through her blouse, and she breathed. The air tasted of diesel and the river and something metallic she thought might be her own fear, though she could not be sure, having never been certain what fear tasted like until now.

She walked. Down the lane, onto Rua de São Bento, then west toward the Tagus, her heels on the pavement making a sound she could not seem to synchronise with her heartbeat. The riverfront was grey and choppy, the water dark, a steamer moving slowly toward the harbour mouth, its horn sounding low and distant. She watched it and thought of Rafael on his steamer, somewhere to the south, carrying the damage she could see in his eyes even through the relief—the way he had held himself on the prison steps, shoulders curving inward as though protecting a wound that had no external location.

She had paid for his freedom. She had paid with money from Thorne, money she earned by selling what passed through her desk—telegrams, intelligence, the small secrets of a neutral country’s censorship apparatus. And she had protected that operation by lying to Keller, by altering a name, by choosing to save a fisherman who was Rafael’s friend, and now Keller knew, or suspected enough to make knowing irrelevant. He had a second source inside the censorship office. Her alteration was not a secret. It was, instead, a documented flaw, a crack in the surface of her work that could be traced to her at any moment, by anyone who cared to look.

She reached the river wall and stood with her hands on the stone, the wind off the water pressing her skirt against her legs. The steamer’s horn sounded again, closer now, and she watched the vessel move through the harbour mouth and into the open water, and she thought: the brother is saved. Rafael is free. The money is spent. The release order is real and folded in her bag and means what it says.

But what has she become to save him?

She knew the answer. She had known it before she entered Keller’s office. She had become a woman who lies to both sides and is watched by one, who has spent the only currency she had—her reliability, her anonymity, her clean record—and has nothing left to trade except the things she has not yet been asked to give.

Keller was watching. The suspicion was now a wire, taut and visible. His patience was not indulgence, but a measured pause before a decision that would bind her further. That could change. That would change, if she made another error, if she protected another name, if she let sentiment or fear or the ghost of her brother’s suffering push her hand into another alteration.

She turned from the river. The wind carried the taste of salt and diesel, and somewhere behind her, in the narrow lane near Rua de São Bento, a door closed, and she did not look back to see whose it was.
