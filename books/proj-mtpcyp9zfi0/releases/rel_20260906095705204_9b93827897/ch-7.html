Chapter 7

The Price List

The sound of a pen scratching in a ledger filled the silence between heartbeats. Ana Ferreira sat at her desk in the censorship office on the Rua da Conceição, the morning telegrams stacked to her left in a pile that would grow until noon, and the small notebook open to her right, its pages ruled in faint blue lines she had drawn herself with a ruler borrowed from the supply cupboard. The pen was not the office pen. The office pen stayed in its inkwell, official, traceable, available to anyone who might check.

This pen — a Parker she had bought with the first notes Thorne gave her — wrote in a hand no one in this building would recognise as hers. She had trained herself to hold it differently, to slant the letters at an angle that bore no resemblance to the neat, vertical script required on censorship forms. The notebook was a school exercise book, bought from a stationer’s shop on the Rua das Flores for two escudos and fifty centavos. Its cover was blank. Inside, however, every page was becoming a ledger of a different kind of commerce.

She wrote the date at the top of a fresh page: 19 November 1942. Below it, she entered the items she had copied during the past week. Naval movement reports, three in total, each summarised in a single line of shorthand she had devised — grid references, vessel designations, dates of expected arrival. To the right of each line, a figure in escudos. This was what she had sold to Thorne. The prices were not fixed. She had learned, through the clumsy negotiations of the early weeks, that Thorne placed a higher value on diplomatic cable traffic than on raw naval movements. She had not been told this. She had inferred it from the way he received what she brought, from the small variations in the sums he offered without prompting, from the questions he asked that revealed what he already knew and what he did not.

She entered the final figure, added the column, and wrote the total at the bottom of the page: 4, 050 escudos. She stared at the sum, and then turned back several pages to where she had written a different number, circled it twice, and underlined it: 15, 000. The price. What a contact in the prison administration, reached through a cousin of a cousin of Rafael’s, had told her it would cost to arrange her brother’s transfer from Tarrafal to a hospital facility on the mainland, from which escape — or at least survival — might be negotiated. Fifteen thousand escudos. At the current rate of accumulation, she would need another month. Perhaps five weeks, if the volume of telegrams held steady and Keller did not grow suspicious.

She closed the notebook and slid it beneath a stack of routine correspondence — customs declarations, mostly, and a few personal letters that would pass through uncensored because they contained nothing of interest. The notebook disappeared from view. She placed a hand on it briefly, then turned to the next telegram.

The telegram was from a commercial firm in Hamburg to its Lisbon representative, confirming a shipment of industrial dyes. She read it, stamped it approved, and set it aside. The next was a personal message from a woman in London to her sister in Estoril, inquiring after the sister’s health. Ana read it twice, not because she suspected anything but because the ordinary language of family affection, travelling across borders at a time when borders had become lethal, struck her in a way she could not quite name. She stamped it and moved on.

She worked through the morning in this fashion, the notebook hidden beneath its camouflage of paper, the figures she had written in it already settling into her mind like a second set of accounts that ran parallel to the official ones. She had begun to think of Thorne in terms of his preferences, his habits, his predictability — the way a clerk in any trading house would think of a client. He was punctual, cautious, and never attempted to renegotiate a price once agreed.

He met her on Tuesday afternoons at a café near the British Embassy, always arrived first, and always left the envelope on the table beneath his handkerchief, a gesture so smoothly repeated that it had become a kind of ritual. Keller, her superior, was a different kind of problem. He required flawless reports, and his questions about discrepancies were sharp, unsettling. She had to be perfect for him. For Thorne, she had to be profitable.

By eleven o’clock the pile had been reduced to a third of its original size. Ana stretched her fingers, which had cramped slightly from the unfamiliar grip of the Parker pen, and looked around the office. Two other censors were at their desks, both older men who had held these positions since before the war and who regarded the telegrams with the weary suspicion of functionaries who had seen too much of everything. Neither looked up. The room smelled of ink, stale coffee, and the particular mustiness of paper that had travelled in cargo holds. A radiator beneath the window ticked irregularly, and outside, the sound of a tram passing on the Rua da Conceição rose and faded like a held breath released.

She reached beneath the correspondence and withdrew the notebook. Opening it to a clean page, she began to write what she thought of as the price list — a systematic record of what each type of intelligence was worth, based on the transactions of the past three weeks. Naval movement reports: 350 escudos per item. Diplomatic cables: 500. Commercial correspondence suggesting trade routes or shipping schedules: 200. Personnel transfers or arrivals of named agents: 600. The CORMORANT telegram, sold in those first frantic days before she had established any system at all, had fetched 800, a sum that now seemed arbitrary, driven by urgency rather than calculation. She would not make that mistake again. The notebook would ensure it.

She wrote the categories in a code of her own invention — naval reports became “blue,” diplomatic cables “white,” commercial traffic “green,” personnel items “red.” The prices were entered as numbers with no currency symbol. Anyone finding the notebook would see a list of colors and numbers that might mean anything — a shopping list, a schedule, the marginalia of a bored clerk. She was counting on this. She was counting on the fact that no one would look twice at a school exercise book hidden under customs declarations, and she was counting on the fact that even if someone did, the contents would be illegible to anyone but her. These were the calculations she made now, the calculations of someone who had begun to think of deception not as a series of desperate improvisations but as a system that could be maintained, refined, and scaled.

She finished the price list, reviewed it once, and then did something she had not done before: she wrote a note to herself at the bottom of the page. “Do not copy from the same batch of telegrams on consecutive days. Allow time between copying and sale. If questioned about a source, refer to the original telegram date, not the date of sale.”

These were operational rules, the kind of rules she imagined a professional would follow, though she had no way of knowing whether real professionals did anything of the sort. She was inventing the discipline as she went, and the fact that she felt the need to write it down told her something she did not entirely want to acknowledge: that the operation had grown beyond its original purpose, that the system itself was becoming a kind of structure she relied on, a scaffolding that held her days in place as firmly as the censorship office did, and that its collapse would leave her not merely exposed but unmoored.

She closed the notebook and returned it to its hiding place.

At half past twelve, she left the office for lunch. The afternoon shift began at two, which gave her ninety minutes — enough time to eat something, to walk to the café where Thorne would be waiting, to complete a transaction that had become, over the weeks, as routine as any other appointment in her day.

She walked along the Rua da Conceição toward the river, turning left at the Praça do Comércio, where the afternoon light fell in broad, flat sheets across the paving stones and the ferry terminals exhaled their smell of diesel and salt. She did not hurry. Hurrying attracted attention.

She had learned this from Thorne, who had told her once, in the flat, instructive tone of a man who had given this advice many times, that the secret of moving through a city unobserved was to match the speed of everyone around you — no faster, no slower, no more purposefully. She had practised it. She was practising it now, her stride aligned with the clerks and shopkeepers who walked the same streets at the same hour for ordinary reasons.

The café was on a narrow street that ran between the Praça and the river, a place with six tables, an owner who remembered faces but not names, and a window that looked out onto a wall. She had chosen it, or rather Thorne had chosen it, and she had understood why: there was nothing to see from the window, and therefore nothing to distract either of them from the transaction, and no reason for anyone to linger.

Thorne was already there, seated at the table farthest from the door, a bica before him, his overcoat folded on the chair beside him. He was reading a Portuguese newspaper, holding it in a way that obscured his face from anyone entering. She had noticed before that he did this — not to hide, exactly, but to reduce the surface area of his presence, to make himself smaller than the room. She sat down opposite him and ordered a coffee she did not intend to drink.

“You’re well,” Thorne said. It was not a question. He said it every time, but not exactly in the same way. Sometimes it was a statement, sometimes it carried the faintest inflection of inquiry, a subtle shift that made her feel he was assessing the space around her, the set of her shoulders, the quiet in her eyes. She had come to understand that it was his way of confirming that she was not under duress — that nothing had changed since the last meeting, that she was still operable. She nodded. He folded the newspaper and set it aside.

“The text reads as follows,” she said, using the phrase she had established at the second meeting, a formula that allowed her to present the intelligence without small talk, without the pretence of a social encounter that would have required her to perform a version of herself she did not have the energy to sustain. She placed her hand on the table, and beneath it, the folded paper. Thorne’s eyes dropped to her hand, then returned to her face. His expression did not change. It never changed during these moments. He was a man who had trained himself to receive information the way a desk receives a document — without reaction, without judgment, without any visible sign that the information had arrived.

She summarised the week’s items: two naval reports, one diplomatic cable concerning a shipment of tungsten ore from Portugal to Germany, and a personnel item — the expected arrival of a Spanish businessman in Lisbon whose name had appeared on a watch list compiled by the British Embassy. Thorne listened without interrupting. When she finished, he asked one question: the date of the diplomatic cable. She gave it to him. He nodded.

The envelope appeared on the table. It had been in his overcoat pocket, and the transfer was so smooth that she would not have seen it if she had not been watching for it — a movement of his left hand, a shift of the overcoat, and then the envelope was on the table beside his coffee cup, and his handkerchief was on top of it. She took it without looking at it, sliding it beneath the fold of her coat, into the interior pocket where it disappeared against the lining. The transaction was complete. It had taken less than four minutes.

“One more thing,” Thorne said. He did not look at her as he spoke. He was refolding the newspaper, his attention apparently absorbed by the task, and she recognised this as his way of saying something he did not want to say while facing her. “There will be an audit. Internal. Not ours — the Portuguese authorities. Someone has noticed discrepancies in the telegram logs. It may be nothing. It may be routine.”

The word audit landed in the silence between them like a stone dropped into still water. Ana felt the ripple move through her — a physical sensation, a tightening in her chest that she controlled by keeping her hands flat on the table and her breathing steady. An audit. Discrepancies in the telegram logs. She thought of the CORMORANT telegram, copied in her own hand on paper that did not belong to the office. She thought of the notebook hidden under customs declarations on her desk, with its figures and its code of colors.

“I see,” she said. Her voice was steady. She had discovered, in the early weeks of this work, that her voice remained steady even when her pulse did not, and that this was a skill she possessed without having cultivated it — a natural aptitude for concealment that she tried not to examine too closely, because examining it might reveal something about herself she was not prepared to know. “When?”

“Within the week, I’m told. Possibly sooner.” Thorne picked up his coffee, drank the last of it, and set the cup down with a small sound — the clink of porcelain on saucer that had become, for Ana, the period at the end of every transaction. “Your administrative standards will be under scrutiny.”

He left first, as he always did. She sat for a moment longer, the coffee she had ordered growing cold in its cup, the envelope pressing against her side like a weight. Through the window, she could see the wall opposite — grey stone, damp, a strip of sky above it that was the color of old pewter. She thought of Rafael in Tarrafal, where the sun was relentless and the ground was hard and the political prisoners were given nothing but the clothes they had been arrested in. She thought of the fifteen thousand escudos she needed, and the four thousand and fifty she had earned this week, and the audit that would arrive within days to examine the telegram logs for discrepancies she knew were there because she had created them.

She stood, left two coins on the table, and walked out into the afternoon. The envelope in her coat was heavier than it should have been, or perhaps it only seemed so because she was now aware of what it cost — not in escudos, but in the narrowing margin between the system she had built and the exposure that waited at its edges. She had been selling intelligence to one buyer and falsifying reports for her superior for weeks, treating each part as a discrete transaction.

But the transactions were not discrete. They were seeds — identical seeds, planted in two different gardens, each one capable of growing into the same plant, the same flower, the same evidence that someone had sold a secret and hidden the sale. She had known this, in the abstract, from the beginning. But knowing it in the abstract was different from feeling it now, in the weight of an envelope against her ribs, in the word audit settling into her mind like a crack in a wall that would widen with the next rain.

She walked along the river toward the office, the envelope secure in her coat, the notebook waiting on her desk with its price list and its rules and its careful, careful code, and she understood — as a clerk understands a column of figures that will not balance, as a sister understands the distance between what she has and what she needs — that every sale she had made had planted two identical seeds of risk, and that the ground beneath them was already beginning to shift.
