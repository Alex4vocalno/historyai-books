Chapter 5

The Altered Name

The mistake sat in her chest like a swallowed stone—heavy, insoluble, pressing against every breath. A mistake had been logged. The trap had a hair trigger now, and she could feel it in the way the city moved around her, ordinary and merciless, as though Lisbon itself were holding its breath alongside her, waiting for the mechanism to spring.

She had tried to sleep. Impossible. The ceiling of her rented room had become a map of everything she had done wrong, every line she had crossed, every small alteration that now seemed less like cunning and more like a rope slowly tightening around her neck. The coins Thorne had given her—three thousand escudos, counted and recounted in the dark—burned in her coat pocket like evidence.

Blood money. Her brother’s money. She corrected herself: her father’s money. Rafael Ferreira. Fifty-six years old, a schoolteacher who had taught children to read and write, who had quoted Pessoa to her as a child as though poetry were a form of civic instruction, who had been arrested two years ago in Praia and sent to the prison camp on Santiago—Tarrafal, the name itself a kind of dry wind that withered everything it touched.

The colonial authorities had called it administrative review. Rafael had called it something else, the last time she had seen him, through the wire, his face thinner than she remembered, his voice steady: “Ana, a country is its people, not its borders.” She had carried that sentence like a talisman, and now she carried the money that was supposed to buy his freedom, and both felt equally useless against the weight of what she had done.

She reached the Largo do Carmo at ten minutes past noon, as instructed. The square was sun-dappled and calm, the ruined Carmelite convent casting long shadows across the cobblestones. A café on the eastern side spilled the smell of roasting coffee into the open air, thick and sweet and cloying, coating the back of her throat like something she could not swallow. She touched the coins in her pocket—three thousand escudos, the exact amount, each note a fingerprint—and forced her hand to stay still. Her fingers found the edge of the bench and gripped it, the iron cold despite the heat, grounding her body in the present tense while her mind raced backward through every version of the report she had submitted, searching for the fracture.

Keller was already there.

He sat at a table near the café’s edge, a cup before him, a folded newspaper beside it. He wore the same grey suit, the same expression of calibrated patience. He did not wave. He did not stand. He simply looked up when she approached, and the look itself was the greeting—and the warning.

She sat across from him. The coffee smell was stronger here, almost aggressive, and she felt her stomach turn.

“Keller,” he said. Nothing more. His voice was level, neither warm nor cold, a instrument tuned to a frequency that made her spine straighten.

“You have something for me,” she said. Her own voice sounded wrong to her—too controlled, too even, a voice wearing a mask of itself. Subversive, someone had once called her tone, meaning it as an insult. She had kept it.

“I have a question.” He lifted his cup, sipped, set it down. The gesture was unhurried, deliberate, a man who measured time in increments of power. “A question about a name.”

The stone in her chest shifted. She kept her hands flat on the table.

“Which name?”

“Miguel Costa.”

The square held its silence. A tram clattered past on the street below, its bell cutting the air like a small, bright knife, and for a moment the sound filled everything, displacing even the coffee smell. Then it was gone, and Keller was still looking at her, and she understood that the silence between them was not empty but full—full of everything he already knew.

“You reported a fisherman. British informant. Operating out of Sesimbra.” Keller paused. “The name in your report was Miguel Costa.”

“That’s correct.”

“That is not correct.” He reached into the newspaper and produced two sheets of paper, placing them side by side on the table between them. The first was her report—she recognized her own typing, the slightly uneven margin, the ink smudge in the upper right corner. The second was something else: a carbon copy, cleaner, typed on a different machine, with a different rhythm. “The name in my other source is not Miguel Costa.”

She looked at both papers. The evidence was there, physical, undeniable, two versions of the same truth laid out on a café table like a verdict. She forced herself to study them with professional detachment, as though she were examining someone else’s work, as though the name she had changed—substituting Miguel Costa for the real fisherman, a man with a wife and two children who had spoken to the British because he was hungry and afraid—were a problem of typography rather than conscience.

“A clerical error,” she said. The words came out with practiced steadiness, but she felt the effort it cost, the way she had to press each syllable flat to keep it from shaking. “The original telegram was damaged. Water stain across the surname. I reconstructed it from context. If the reconstruction was inaccurate, I apologize. It will not happen again.”

Keller did not respond immediately. He picked up his cup again, sipped again, set it down again. The tram sound had faded entirely. Somewhere a pigeon landed on the convent wall with a soft, absurd sound, and Ana thought: even the pigeons are watching.

“A clerical error,” Keller repeated. The words sat between them, examined and found either acceptable or useful—she could not tell which, and the ambiguity was worse than accusation. “You understand that I have a network. Sources. Methods of verification. You understand that what you give me is checked.”

“I understand.”

“And you understand that a clerical error, if that is what it was, is still an error. And an error in this work is not a typo. It is a liability. It is a flaw that can be traced.” He tapped her report with one finger, once, the sound small and precise. “To a desk. To a typewriter. To a person.”

She said nothing. The coins in her pocket pressed against her thigh, three thousand escudos, Rafael’s freedom reduced to paper and metal, and she thought of the prison camp on Santiago, the wire, the dust, her father’s voice through it: “Do not waste your light on this dark place. Live.” She had tried. She had tried to live, to act, to become someone who could save him, and now she sat across from a man who was telling her, calmly, politely, that the path she had chosen was collapsing beneath her.

“I will accept the explanation,” Keller said. “Once.”

The word hung in the air. Once. A single-use pardon, a pass good for one transaction only, after which the account would be settled in a different currency.

“Keller,” he said, and she understood he was not introducing himself but reminding her of the name under which this conversation existed, the name that would appear on whatever ledger held her fate. “I do not say this to threaten you. I say it because the material must be clean. If it is not clean, it is useless. And useless things in my experience do not remain in the arrangement.”

“I understand,” she said again. The repetition disgusted her—she was shrinking, repeating herself, a parrot of compliance—but she could not find another word that felt safe.

Keller folded both papers and returned them to the newspaper. The motion was final, a drawer closing. “There is talk in your office,” he said, his tone shifting, conversational now, as though the confrontation were over and they had moved to another subject. “Administrative review. The censorship bureau is planning audits of outgoing correspondence. Internal inspections. Someone in Lisbon has decided that the office needs to be—how do they say it—brought into alignment.”

The information landed like a second blow, coming from the side. Audits. Inspections. If they examined the outgoing reports, if they compared what she had submitted to what had been received, the discrepancy would surface again—not in Keller’s hands this time, but in the bureaucracy’s, in the ledgers and filing cabinets of the state that had already taken her father.

“I had not heard,” she said, which was true.

“You will hear. Prepare for it.” Keller stood. He left a coin on the table for the coffee. “The material. Next time. Clean.”

He walked away across the square, his grey suit catching the light, and she watched him go, and the coffee smell thickened, and the tram sounded again in the distance, and she sat alone with the stone in her chest and the coins in her pocket and the knowledge that she had been given one chance and one chance only, and that the audit was coming, and that somewhere in Lisbon’s machinery—ThePortu, the shipping office, the docks where information moved like contraband between hands that never saw each other—someone else was watching, someone whose name she did not know, someone who had already provided Keller with the truth she had tried to bury.

She thought of her father. She thought of the fisherman whose name she had changed, the real name, the one that existed now in two places: in Keller’s other source and in the space she had erased it from. She thought of the money, how it was supposed to solve everything, how it was supposed to be simple—a transaction, an exchange, her skills for Rafael’s freedom—and how it had become instead a labyrinth with walls made of her own mistakes.

The square emptied. The pigeon departed. The coffee cooled in Keller’s abandoned cup, and Ana sat still for a long time, feeling the flaw settle into the record like a crack in plaster, invisible perhaps, perhaps not, but present, permanent, traceable to her desk, her typewriter, her hands.
