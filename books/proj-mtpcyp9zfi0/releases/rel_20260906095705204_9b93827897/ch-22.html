Chapter 22

Matters of Mutual Concern

The smell of dust and old paper reached her before she saw him.

Ana Ferreira stopped two steps inside the archive room of the British commercial attaché’s office, her hand still on the doorframe. Alistair Thorne stood at the far end of the table, his silhouette carved against the single lamp that threw everything else into amber half-dark. He had not turned on the overhead light. There was no need. A telegram lay flat on the table between them, weighted at one corner with a brass paperweight shaped like a lion’s head.

She had been summoned. Not asked. The note that arrived at her desk that morning had contained no pleasantries, no pretense of a social call. Just a time. An address she already knew. And the words matters of mutual concern.

Thorne’s face, as she approached, held the particular stillness of a man who had already arrived at his verdict and was now deciding only on the sentence. His grey suit was immaculate. His hands, folded behind his back, did not tremble. But there was something in the set of his jaw—a tightness she had never seen in their previous meetings, which had always been exercises in controlled courtesy, English restraint masking the sordid mechanics of purchase and sale.

“Close the door, Miss Ferreira.”

She did. The latch clicked. The room sealed itself around them.

“You wanted to see me,” she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. The subversive cadence she had cultivated—the flat, declarative tone that made every statement sound like a line read from a classified document—held for now. “The text reads as follows: matters of mutual concern. I’m here.”

Thorne pulled out the chair nearest him but did not sit. He placed both hands on the back of it, fingers gripping the wood until the knuckles showed white. “Sit down.”

“I prefer to stand.”

“Sit down, Miss Ferreira.”

She sat. The chair was cold. The room smelled of dust and something older—ink, perhaps, or the faint chemical tang of carbon paper left too long in a closed drawer. A filing cabinet in the corner stood with one drawer slightly ajar, as though he had been searching for something and had found it.

“Thorne,” she said, using the name like a key, testing whether the lock still turned. “What is this about?”

He unfolded his hands and reached into his jacket. A second paper—not a telegram this time, but a typed report on thin, onionskin stock—emerged between two fingers. He set it beside the telegram on the table. He did not push it toward her. He let it sit.

“Do you know what CORMORANT means, Miss Ferreira?”

The word landed in the room like a stone dropped into still water. Ana felt the ripple move through her chest, her throat, the backs of her hands. She kept her face still. She had been trained by necessity, if not by profession, in the art of keeping her face still.

“It’s a code designation,” she said. “I’ve seen it in intercepted traffic. A German agent operating in—“

“You’ve seen it because you sold it.” Thorne’s voice did not rise. That was the worst part. A shout she could have met with defiance. This low, measured cadence was something else. “You copied a telegram identifying CORMORANT’s cover name, his meeting schedule, his contact network. You brought that copy to me. I paid you for it. And then you brought the same copy to Otto Keller.”

Silence.

The lamp flickered. Somewhere in the building, a door closed—distant, muffled by stone and plaster. Ana’s fingers found the edge of the table. She pressed her fingertips against it, grounding herself.

“That’s an accusation,” she said.

“It is a fact.” Thorne shifted the onionskin report to the center of the table. “This is a debriefing transcript from a source inside the Abwehr’s Lisbon station. Dated eleven days ago. It documents the arrest and subsequent execution of a British agent whose operational designation was CORMORANT. The arrest was made on the basis of intelligence provided to the German legation by a Portuguese source inside the censorship office. The intelligence included the agent’s cover identity, his dead-drop locations, and the recognition signals he used to identify himself to his contacts.”

He paused. Not for effect—Ana could see that. For control. The muscles along his jaw worked once, twice.

“CORMORANT was my agent, Miss Ferreira.”

The room contracted. The walls, the filing cabinet, the brass lion’s head, the single lamp—all of it drew inward, compressing the space until she could feel the pressure against her sternum. She opened her mouth. Closed it.

Thorne continued. “His real name was João. He was twenty-six years old. He had a wife and a daughter, eighteen months old. He fished for sardines off the coast near Sesimbra and reported German naval movements to us. He was brave in a way that neither you nor I will ever be brave, because he did it for nothing—no money, no promise of safety, just because he believed the right thing mattered.”

Ana thought of Rafael. She could not help it. Rafael, whose broad smile and unruly curls existed now only in a single creased photograph she kept hidden inside the lining of her handbag. He had said it was about the people, not the borders, but where had it gotten him? To a prison camp on a Cape Verde island. It was a business transaction, a necessary one. She had told herself that many times.

She had sold the CORMORANT telegram to Keller for money. For the bribes that kept the channel to Tarrafal open, the intermediaries who carried messages in and out, the payments that ensured her brother received extra rations and was not beaten as badly as the others. She had sold it because the alternative was to let Rafael die, and she had chosen—she had chosen a logical, brutal piece of commerce. The justification was solid. It held.

“You sold him to Keller,” Thorne said, “and Keller’s people handed the intelligence straight to the Abwehr. João was arrested three days after your sale. He was executed six days after that. His wife was not informed. She learned of his death from a neighbour who had heard it from a fishmonger.”

“I didn’t know he was yours,” Ana said. The words came out before she could weigh them, and she hated herself for how defensive they sounded—how small. “I didn’t know CORMORANT was a British agent. I thought—“

“You thought he was a German asset. A name in a telegram. An abstraction.” Thorne’s voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture in the plaster of his composure. He sealed it immediately. “One must distinguish between the noise and the signal, Miss Ferreira. You sold the signal. You sold it to both sides. And the side that acted on it first was the side that killed him.”

Ana stared at the telegram on the table. She could see the familiar format now—the header, the routing notation, the cipher group that had identified CORMORANT’s cover name. She had copied it in her own hand, late one evening in the censorship office, her pen moving carefully across the page while the night staff smoked and talked in the corridor outside. She had wrapped the copy in a plain envelope and carried it to Thorne in a narrow lane near the German legation, the alley smelling of drains and sea salt and cooking oil. She had taken his money—British pounds, crisp and clean—and then she had walked to the side entrance of the legation and sold the same copy to Keller.

“I have known for some time,” Thorne said. He spoke the words as though they cost him something. “I let you believe our arrangement was unchanged because I needed to confirm the chain. The censorship office has many eyes. I needed to be certain it was you and not another leak.”

He straightened. His hands went to his pockets, then withdrew. He could not seem to find a place for them.

“You were not difficult to identify. Your copies arrived at the Abwehr within hours of your shift ending. The pattern was unmistakable. I confirmed it three weeks ago.”

Three weeks. She had sat across from him three times since then. She had taken his money, counted it in front of him, signed the receipt he always requested. He had known. He had looked at her, paid her, and known.

“Why did you keep seeing me?” she asked. Her voice was quieter now. The subversive edge had bled away. “If you’ve known for three weeks—“

“Because you are still useful.” The words fell like coins on stone. “And because I needed to understand what kind of person sells the same intelligence to both sides of a war and calls it commerce.”

Ana’s throat tightened. She could feel the weight of the cash in her handbag—Portuguese escudos and British pounds, the accumulated sum of months of sales. Enough, almost, for what she needed. The intermediaries had told her: another three payments, perhaps four, and the bribes would be sufficient to secure Rafael’s transfer from Tarrafal to a mainland facility, from which escape might be arranged. She was close. She was so close.

“He was a person,” Thorne said. “João. He had a name. He had a daughter who will never know him. And you reduced him to a line in a telegram and sold him for banknotes.”

“What would you have had me do?” The words came out sharp, sudden, edged with something that was not quite anger and not quite despair. “You think I don’t know the cost? You think I haven’t counted it? I count it every night. But I had—there were reasons. There are always reasons.”

“Reasons.” Thorne repeated the word as though it were a specimen he was examining under glass. “Tell me a reason, Miss Ferreira. Tell me one reason that weighs against a dead man and his daughter.”

She almost did. The name rose to her lips—Rafael, her brother, imprisoned for the crime of believing in a free Portugal, starving in a camp on a volcanic island because he had spoken at the wrong meeting and been overheard by the wrong informant. She could have told Thorne everything. The hidden ledger, the running tally of sums paid and sums still needed, the intermediaries and their escalating demands, the photograph she carried like a relic. She could have laid it all out and let him see that she was not a monster but a woman trapped between two impossible choices, one life weighed against another.

She did not. She looked at Thorne’s face—the controlled fury, the grief he wore like armour—and understood that telling him would change nothing. He would not forgive her. He might pity her, and that would be worse, because pity from a man like Thorne was just contempt with a human face.

“It was business,” she said.

The words dropped into the room and lay there, flat and cold and final.

Thorne stared at her. For a long moment, the only sound was the lamp’s faint electrical hum. Then he reached into his jacket again and produced an envelope. He set it on the table beside the telegram and the report.

“Then we continue as business.” His voice had changed. The grief was still there, but it had been sealed over now, buried under something harder and more durable. “You will bring me copies of everything you sell to Keller. Everything. Not selected items, not what you think I should see—everything. And the price will not increase. You will receive the same payment as before, and you will earn it by working for me as you have never worked before. You are no longer a source, Miss Ferreira. You are an asset. My asset. And if you fail to deliver, if you withhold a single page, a single line, I will ensure that Keller learns exactly what you have been selling me and to whom. Do you understand?”

She understood. She understood perfectly. He was not offering her a partnership. He was constructing a cage.

“I understand.”

“Take the envelope. Count it if you wish.”

She took the envelope. She did not count it. The weight was familiar—she knew by feel how many notes he carried, how the sums had grown over the months as the value of her intelligence increased. This was the same amount as last time. He had not raised the price. He had not lowered it. He had made it clear that money was all she would ever receive from him again.

“One more thing.” Thorne’s hand moved to the onionskin report. He folded it once, twice, and slipped it back into his jacket. “His name was João Fernandes. I want you to remember that name. I want you to carry it the way you carry whatever it is you carry in that handbag of yours. Not as punishment—as weight. So that the next time you sit at your desk and copy a telegram, you understand that what you are copying is not text. It is a life. Or a death.”

He turned off the lamp. The room went dark except for the grey light seeping through the shuttered window.

“You may go.”

Ana stood. Her legs were unsteady, but she made them hold. She took the envelope, her handbag, and the remnants of whatever self-respect had survived the last twenty minutes. She walked to the door and opened it.

“Miss Ferreira.”

She stopped but did not turn.

“If you ever sell my agents again—any of them—I will not come to this room. I will come to yours. And there will be no conversation.”

She left. The corridor outside was empty, dimly lit, smelling of floor wax and something else beneath it—old plaster, the mineral tang of limestone, the accumulated breath of a building that had stood through three centuries of secrets. She walked its length without looking back, descended the stairs, and stepped out into the narrow lane.

The alley smelled of drains, sea salt, damp stone, exhaust, and cooking oil. The same smells as every other time. The same city, the same war, the same trade. But something had shifted—some load-bearing wall inside the structure of her operation had cracked, and she could feel the entire edifice tilting, slowly, imperceptibly, toward collapse.

She pressed her handbag against her side. The envelope of cash was inside, beside the photograph of Rafael, beside the ledger she kept in the lining. Enough for another payment to the intermediaries. Another step closer to her brother’s freedom.

The money bought his freedom. But the name Thorne had given her—João Fernandes—sat inside her chest like a stone swallowed whole, and she knew it would not pass.

A man was dead because she had sold his name twice. She had known, in the abstract, that intelligence killed. Every censor knew that. But she had never before been forced to look at the specific weight of it—a face, a wife, a daughter eighteen months old. She had told herself she was selling information. She had not allowed herself to think she was selling people.

She walked the narrow lane toward Rua de São Bento, the evening air cold against her face, the cash heavy in her bag. Somewhere in Tarrafal, Rafael sat in a cell, dreaming of a free Portugal. Somewhere in Sesimbra, a widow sat with a daughter who would never know her father. And Ana Ferreira walked between them, carrying the money that connected one to the other—the price of a name, the cost of a life, the weight of a choice that could not be unmade.

The money bought her brother’s freedom, but at what permanent cost? She did not yet know. She feared she was about to learn.
