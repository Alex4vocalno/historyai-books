Chapter 10

The Unblinking Eye

The discrepancy had been traced. Not to her name, not yet, but to her desk, her station, the pile of forms she had processed on the afternoon she had changed Miguel Costa’s name to protect a friend of her brother’s. The log entry was specific: date, time, routing code. It sat in the audit ledger like a splinter working its way toward the surface. And now Senior Inspector Valente stood in the doorway of the telegraph room, clipboard in hand, and Ana understood that the mistake she had logged three weeks ago had finally found its way back to her.

She had known it would. She had told herself she had not, but she had. The moment she had pressed the time-stamp onto the altered form and watched the ink dry over a name that was no longer the real name, she had felt the small, definitive click of a lock engaging somewhere. A lock she could not see and could not pick. And now the door it secured was opening, and on the other side of it stood a man with a clipboard and the authority to inspect everything she touched.

The smell of Valente’s cigar reached her before his voice did. A thick, sweetish smoke that clung to the wool of his jacket and the grey bristle of his jaw. He chewed the stub of it even as he spoke, the words arriving around the obstruction like stones dislodged from a drain.

“Ferreira.”

“Senior Inspector.”

“I am conducting a spot-check audit of telegram handling procedures. Effective immediately. You will continue your duties as normal. I will observe.”

He said it the way a man reads a train schedule. No inflection, no emphasis, no suggestion that the words contained anything other than their plain meaning. That was what made it worse. A man who announces an audit with anger can be anticipated, managed, deflected. A man who announces it with indifference has already decided what he expects to find.

He stepped into the room. The space, never large, shrank. The telegraph machines occupied their usual positions along the far wall, their tape spools turning in idle loops, and between them and Valente’s broad, grey-suited frame there was barely room for the chair he pulled from the corner and placed directly behind Ana’s station. He sat. He crossed his legs. He clicked his pen once, twice, and looked at the clipboard.

“Begin,” he said.

Ana’s hands, which had been resting on the desk, did not move. She watched them as though they belonged to someone else. The fingers were still. The nails were clean. She willed them to reach for the first form in the queue, and they did, but the motion felt rehearsed, mechanical, as if she were operating her own body by remote control.

The morning’s traffic had been light. Commercial telegrams, mostly: shipping confirmations, price inquiries, a personal message from a man in Porto to his wife in Lisbon that read, in its censored form, as though all the warmth had been wrung from it by force. Ana processed each one. She read. She assessed. She stamped. The time-stamp came down with a loud, definitive clack that she felt in her wrist, and she moved the form to the processed pile, and reached for the next.

Valente watched. He did not speak. He did not shift in his chair. The cigar smouldered between his fingers, untended, and the smoke rose in a thin column that the overhead fan pushed in lazy spirals toward the ceiling. Every few minutes he made a note on his clipboard. The pen moved in short, precise strokes. She could not read what he wrote from her angle, and the not-knowing was its own kind of pressure.

She thought of the escudos in the tin box at the back of her wardrobe. Seven thousand, four hundred escudos, accumulated across weeks of sales: first to Thorne, then to Keller, then to both, the same intelligence sold twice, the price list she had developed through trial and error and the cold arithmetic of desperation. Each note in that box was a step toward Rafael. Each sale brought her closer to the sum she needed — the sum that the lawyer had named, that the fixer had confirmed, that the prison administration in Tarrafal would accept as the price of a transfer from the political section to a work detail, and from a work detail to a medical review, and from a medical review to the signed paper that said a man could go home.

Rafael. In her mind he was still in the sun-bleached linen shirt, still smiling, still saying, Ana, a country is its people, not its borders. But the photograph she carried was creased now, softened by handling, and the image it held was fading into the paper. She kept it in her handbag. She did not look at it at work. She did not need to. She carried the image inside her, and it burned there, a small, constant heat that did not go out.

The money was not enough. She knew the figure she needed. She had calculated it, recalculated it, lay awake in the dark recalculating it, and the sum in the tin box was perhaps a third of what was required. A third. And now Valente sat behind her with his clipboard and his cigar, and the telegrams she needed to copy, to sell, to convert into the next increment toward her brother’s life, were passing through her hands one by one, untouched, uncopied, unstolen, as pure and useless as water she could not carry.

She thought of Thorne. His professional stillness, his careful courtesy, the way he had counted the banknotes out in the café that first time — not with greed, not with reluctance, but with the precise attention of a man performing a transaction he had performed many times and would perform many more. He had not asked her name. He had known it already. That was what had unsettled her, that first meeting: the realization that she was not anonymous, that the man across the table had a file on her, or the equivalent of a file, some internal ledger in which her name sat beside a number, a risk assessment, a value.

But there had been something else. Once, between the exchange of an envelope and the counting of notes, a pause — barely a breath — when his eyes had gone somewhere else. Not to her, not to the café, not to the river visible through the window. Somewhere inward, somewhere that hurt. He had looked at the photograph on the table — not hers, his own, a small framed image he carried in his breast pocket, which he had set beside the saucer while he counted. A woman.

A child. Ana had seen it only for a moment before his hand had covered it, returned it to his pocket, and his face had closed like a door. He had not spoken of it. She had not asked. But she had recognized the look. She wore it herself, in the mirror, in the mornings before she left for the censorship office. The look of someone who has lost something they cannot recover and is trying to buy back something they still can.

Thorne had his grief. She had hers. And between them, the exchange of paper for money was a negotiation between two wounds, each pretending to be a business arrangement.

The morning crawled. Eleven o’clock. Eleven-thirty. The telegraph machines chattered, and forms emerged from the slot in the wall, and Ana took them, read them, stamped them, and set them down. Her hands had steadied. The initial trembling had passed, replaced by a numb precision that was its own kind of danger. She was being too careful. She could feel it. Each motion deliberate, each stamp placed with an exactness that her normal work did not possess. If Valente were watching for anomalies in behaviour rather than anomalies in paperwork, she was painting one across the surface of every routine action.

She forced herself to pause. She reached for her water glass. She drank. She set it down slightly off-center, the way she always did, the way the ring of moisture on the desk proved she always did. A small, deliberate imperfection. A signal to herself that she was still in control.

At eleven-forty, the high-value intercept arrived.

She knew it before she read it. The routing code told her: a prefix she had learned to recognize in her first week, the code for diplomatic traffic routed through the Italian embassy’s commercial channel, which everyone in the office knew was not commercial at all. The form was longer than usual. She drew it from the slot, and the first line of text told her what it was.

Naval movements. A convoy. Coordinates, escort composition, departure window. The kind of intelligence that Thorne would have paid four hundred escudos for — perhaps five hundred, given the detail, given the specificity of the escort count. The kind of intelligence that Keller would have matched, or tried to, because Keller had been growing more aggressive in his offers, more insistent, and the last time they had met he had leaned across the table and said, with a softness that was not kindness, “You understand, Fräulein, that reliability is not merely a virtue. It is a condition.”

The form sat on her desk. The telegraph machine had finished printing it. The ink was dry. The routing code was legible. The coordinates were precise. And behind her, Senior Inspector Valente shifted in his chair, and the leather creaked, and his pen clicked once.

She could not copy it. There was no way. The carbon paper she used was in the second drawer, the one she opened only when the station was unobserved, and Valente’s chair was positioned at an angle that gave him a clear sightline to every drawer in her desk. Her handbag was on the floor beside her, but reaching into it for the small notebook she kept there — the one with the shorthand she had developed, the personal cipher she had built from Portuguese verb conjugations and railway timetables — would be visible. Every motion would be visible. Every hesitation would be noted.

She read the intercept. She memorized what she could: the convoy designation, the first set of coordinates, the escort count. But the detail was too dense, the numbers too specific, and already the next form was emerging from the slot, and the one after that, and the rhythm of the work would not pause for her to stand with a blank expression and a form in her hand while the queue backed up.

She stamped it. The time-stamp came down with a loud, definitive clack, the sound of a door closing, and she placed the form on the processed pile, and it was gone. She reached for the next.

The coordinates she had memorized began to blur almost immediately. She held them for thirty seconds, a minute, but the next form required her attention — a personal telegram with a passage that needed redaction — and the act of focusing on the redaction pushed the naval coordinates aside, and when she tried to retrieve them they were already soft, already approximate, already useless. The escort count she retained: seven vessels. But the coordinates were gone. The departure window was gone. The convoy designation was a smudge in her memory, half-formed, already dissolving.

She had let it pass through her hands. Uncopied, unsold, unconverted into the escudos that would bring her one step closer to the sum, the figure, the number that meant Rafael could come home.

Valente made a note on his clipboard. The pen moved in its short, precise strokes. She heard it, the faint scratch of nib on paper, and her jaw tightened until she felt the ache in her teeth.

At noon, Valente stood. The chair scraped against the floor. He tucked the clipboard under his arm and looked down at her, and his expression had not changed since he arrived. The same flat, unhurried regard. The cigar, burned down to its stub, left a brown stain on his fingers.

“The audit will continue tomorrow,” he said. “Same time. Same arrangement.”

He turned and walked out. The cigar smoke stayed behind, hanging in the still air of the telegraph room, and Ana sat at her desk with her hands flat on the surface and her breath shallow and the sound of the time-stamp still ringing in her wrist.

The naval intercept sat on the processed pile. She could see the edge of it, the routing code visible, the first line of text beginning to disappear beneath the form that had been placed on top of it. She could still pull it. She could still copy it. But the doorway where Valente had stood was empty, and the corridor beyond it was not, and the sound of his footsteps had not yet faded.

She did not touch it.

She sat, and she breathed, and she counted the escudos she had not earned, and the sum in the tin box at home did not grow, and somewhere in the Cape Verde islands, in a prison camp at the edge of a salt marsh, her brother sat in a coarse uniform and did not know how close she was, or how far.
