Chapter 6

The Brother's Crime

The banknotes lay fanned across the kitchen table in Ana’s apartment, twelve of them, crisp and foreign, each one bearing the engraved face of a foreign monarch. Evening light from the single window fell across them at a shallow angle, catching the watermark threads and turning the paper translucent at the edges. Beside the notes sat a glass of water she had poured two hours ago and not touched — a skin of dust had already formed on its surface.

The ink stain on her right index finger had dried to a dark crescent, a mirror to the crescent of dried ink on the table’s edge where her carbon paper had bled through the night before. The smell of the notes was different from the escudos she knew — sharper, chemical, the scent of a different Treasury. From the building’s interior wall, the neighbour’s wireless bled a fado through the plaster, the singer’s voice flattened by the cheap speaker into something that sounded less like mourning and more like a complaint.

The room held its own weather: the radiator ticking in the corner, the damp stain on the ceiling spreading like a bruise, the calendar on the wall two days behind because she had forgotten to tear the page on the first of the month.

On the shelf above the radiator rested a single photograph in a bent tin frame: Rafael with unruly curls and a smile too wide for his thin face, squinting into a sun that no longer existed in the place where he was now. It had been taken on the Tagus waterfront, the river behind him flat and white, the April light so bright it had overexposed the left edge of the print, bleaching his shoulder into nothing. Below the photograph was a drawer she had not opened in four months. Inside it, she knew, were three things: a pencil stub, a tram ticket to Belém, and a folded leaflet printed on coarse paper, the ink still legible, the words still dangerous. The leaflets had been his crime. She had kept one.

The radiator ticked. The fado ended. The water remained, and the money smelled like someone else’s country.

Ana pressed her palms flat on the table on either side of the banknotes and stared at them until the engraved face dissolved into meaningless lines. Twelve notes. She had counted them three times. She knew the sum precisely — she had memorised it the way she memorised everything, with the translator’s fastidious need for accuracy that had become, in these last weeks, something else entirely. The money was not yet enough. It was a fraction of what she had been told the passage would cost — the bribes layered from the prison clerk to the colonial administrator to the man who controlled the boats at São Vicente. She had learned the architecture of corruption with the same precision she brought to her work at the censorship office, each payment a word in a sentence she was constructing, a sentence whose final punctuation would be Rafael’s name on a release order.

But the money refused to let her calculate tonight. It blocked the clean, compartmentalised thinking that had carried her through the first sales — the copy made, the telegram delivered, the cash received, the transaction closed and filed away. She could only follow the scent. The banknotes smelled of ink, which smelled of the censorship office, which in turn smelled of the printing shop on the Rua da Madalena. That was the shop Rafael had gone to on a Tuesday evening in November 1940, carrying a rucksack full of paper that would scatter across the cobblestones like autumn leaves when the PVDE officers threw him to the ground.

But tonight the money would not let her calculate. It would not let her think in the clean, compartmentalised way that had carried her through the first sales — the copy made, the telegram delivered, the cash received, the transaction closed and filed in her mind under a date and a code name. She closed her eyes. The memory was waiting. It had been waiting all evening, since she had placed the money on the table, since the smell of the notes had reached her — that sharp, chemical scent cutting through the room’s familiar damp — and she had understood, with a clarity that felt like vertigo, that the money was not abstract. It was not a figure in a ledger. It was the mechanism by which she would undo her silence, and her silence had begun on that Tuesday, at the door, in the hallway of the apartment on the Rua de São Bento, with the sound of boots on the stairs.

She had been twenty-six. Rafael had been twenty-nine. Their father had been dead for three years — broken, as Rafael said, by the Estado Novo police who had beaten him in a cell in 1934 for the crime of organizing a dockworkers’ meeting, and who had never walked without a cane afterward, and who had died in 1937 of something the doctor called pneumonia but which Rafael called grief. Their mother had died when Ana was eleven. It had been the two of them for six years, sharing the apartment, Ana at the censorship office by day, Rafael at the university library by night, reading and writing and printing the things he believed would save the country.

She had known. That was the part she could not forgive herself for. She had known what he was doing. She had seen the rucksack, heavy and square-cornered, when he left on Tuesday evenings. She had smelled the fresh ink on his hands when he came home. She had read the leaflets — he had left one on the kitchen table once, and she had read it, standing, in the grey morning light, the words plain and furious and true: A country is not its borders. A country is its people. And when the people are silenced, the country dies. She had read it and she had folded it and she had placed it in the drawer above the radiator, and she had said nothing.

Not to Rafael. Not to anyone. She had said nothing because she was afraid, and because she had told herself that her fear was a form of sense, and that her sense was a form of love — she was protecting him by not acknowledging what he did, protecting them both by maintaining the fiction that the rucksack contained books, that the ink on his hands was from the library’s stamp, that the leaflets were someone else’s concern.

The boots had come up the stairs at half past nine. Four men — two in the dark coats of the PVDE, two in the ordinary clothes of informants who had been promised something in return. Ana had been in the kitchen, washing a cup. Rafael had been in his room, reading. The knock was not a knock. It was a boot applied to the lower panel of the door, and the door opened because the lock was cheap and the frame was rotten, and the four men were in the hallway before Ana could set down the cup.

She had stood at the kitchen doorway. She had not moved. Her hands had been wet. The cup was still in her hand — she could feel it, the cold ceramic, the chip on the rim where Rafael had knocked it against the tap — and she did not set it down, did not drop it, did not throw it. She stood and held it and watched.

Rafael had come out of his room in shirtsleeves, the book still in his hand. He had looked at the men and then at Ana, and his face had done something she would never forget: it had settled. Not resigned — settled. As though he had been expecting this and had already moved past surprise into a kind of readiness. He set the book on the hall table, carefully, as though it were borrowed and he intended to return it.

“Ana,” he had said. Just her name. And then, because he was Rafael, because he could not leave a room without saying what he believed, even when the room contained men who would beat him for it: “A country is its people, not its borders.”

One of the PVDE officers had taken his arm. Rafael had not resisted. He had walked toward the door, and the other officer had gone into his room, and Ana had heard the sound of drawers being pulled out and papers being gathered, and then the sound she would hear for years afterward — the rucksack being emptied onto the floor, the leaflets scattering, the coarse paper sliding across the boards, the printed words facing upward, every copy of A country is not its borders exposed to the hallway light.

She had watched the leaflets scatter. She had watched Rafael’s back as they led him down the stairs, his head not bowed, his stride steady, and she had not moved from the kitchen doorway until the front door closed and the stairwell went silent and the only sound was the tap dripping into the sink where she had been washing the cup.

She had not screamed. She had not run to the door. She had not gone to the window to see which direction they took him. She had stood in the hallway and looked at the leaflets on the floor, and then she had knelt and picked up one — just one, the one nearest the wall, the one they had not bothered to collect — and she had folded it and placed it in the drawer above the radiator, next to the first one, and she had closed the drawer and washed the cup and gone to bed.

In bed, in the dark, she had made the promise. Not to God, not to Rafael, not to anyone who could hear it. She had made it to herself, in the silence of the apartment, in the silence that was her crime and her sentence: I will get you out. That was all. Four words. No plan, no money, no idea how. Just the promise, and the silence that followed it, which was the same silence she had maintained when she read the first leaflet, the same silence that had kept her at the kitchen doorway while they took him, the same silence she had been paying for ever since.

She opened her eyes. The banknotes were still on the table. The water was still untouched. The fado had been replaced by static, the wireless between stations, a sound like the sea heard from very far away.

She reached for the drawer above the radiator. She opened it. The pencil stub, the tram ticket, the folded leaflet. She took the leaflet and unfolded it on the table beside the money, and she read it again, standing, in the grey evening light, the way she had read it four years ago.

The ink had faded. The paper had yellowed. But the words were the same, and the hand that had set them — not Rafael’s hand, he had not printed them himself, but the hand of whoever had operated the press that night on the Rua da Madalena — had pressed hard enough to leave the letters raised on the reverse side, a texture she could feel with her fingertip, a braille of defiance that the PVDE had not managed to stamp out.

She looked at the leaflet and the money side by side. His crime and her crime. His crime was printing words that were true. Her crime was selling words that were secret. His crime had put him in Tarrafal. Her crime was meant to get him out.

She folded the leaflet and returned it to the drawer. She gathered the banknotes, squared them, and placed them in the tin box she kept behind the radiator, where the wall was damp and no one would think to look. She closed the box. She closed the drawer. She washed the glass of water and set it upside down on the draining board.

The money now felt heavier, stained with memory and purpose.
