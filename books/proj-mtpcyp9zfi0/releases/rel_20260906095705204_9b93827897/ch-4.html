Chapter 4

The Fisherman's Name

The scratch of pen nib on paper sounded like a bone breaking — small, precise, irreversible.

Ana Ferreira held the telegram under the cone of lamplight, the words sharp and black against the thin onion skin. The censorship office was empty at this hour, the other translators long gone, their desks holding only the ghost-impressions of the day’s work — blotting pads, half-empty inkwells, a cardigan draped over a chair back like a shed skin. The building’s heating pipes ticked in the walls. Somewhere above her, a door closed.

She read the telegram again. It had arrived on the afternoon wire from Faro, routed through the Lisbon exchange at 15: 42, and it concerned a fishing vessel — the Santa Beatriz — whose owner, one João Garção, had been observed making regular crossings to Gibraltar, returning with holds that smelled of diesel fuel though the manifests declared only sardines. The British naval attaché’s office had flagged the information to London, but the telegram had transited through Portuguese censorship first, because that was how neutrality functioned in Lisbon — every letter opened, every wire read, every secret shared with someone who had not been invited to hear it.

The name was there. João Garção. Fisherman. Fifty-three years old. Born in Setúbal, resident of Cacilhas. The telegram included his home address.

Ana set down her pen.

She knew that name. Not from the files — from Rafael’s letters, the few that had made it out of Tarrafal before the camp commandant restricted correspondence to monthly summaries of good behaviour. Her brother had written about Garção in the tone of a man remembering sunlight: He taught me the currents. He knew where the octopus hid. When I was sick he carried water from the well three times a day. A fisherman. A friend. A man who had visited Rafael in prison twice before the visits were cut, who had brought him a blanket that smelled of salt and engine oil, who had pressed coins into Rafael’s hand and said, Hold on, brother. Hold on.

And now João Garção was a name on a British telegram, identified as an informant, and the telegram would be copied and filed and the copy would sit in a drawer that Otto Keller’s money could open just as easily as Alistair Thorne’s money had opened it the week before.

She had sold the CORMORANT telegram to Thorne for seven hundred escudos. She had sold the same intelligence to Keller for the same amount. Fourteen hundred escudos in a tin behind the lavatory cistern in her apartment in Estrela — not enough, not nearly enough, but a beginning. The lawyers wanted twelve thousand for the petition to the Ministry of the Interior, the petition that was the only legal avenue to even begin a plea for his release from Tarrafal. After that would come the necessary bribes, the forged medical certificates, the costly transfer authorizations.

Rafael was wasting in that camp, sleeping on a stone shelf in a cell built for four men that now held eleven, and every week that passed shaved another kilogram from his frame, another degree of hope from his eyes. She had seen him once, through a wire fence, six months ago. He had been able to stand then. She did not know if he could stand now. The cash in hand was her first real step toward buying him back, but the distance still felt impossible.

Ana picked up her pen. She turned the telegram face-up and looked at the name — Garção — and she thought about the scratch of nib on paper, about how a single letter could be a door that opened or a door that closed. She pressed the nib down and drew a line through the ã and the o. In the careful hand the censorship office required, she wrote the name Garcão — no, not that either — she wrote Garçano, adding a stroke, a tail, a syllable that belonged to no one, that was no one’s name. It would send anyone searching for João Garção’s address in Cacilhas to a door where no one answered, because there was no João Garçano, there had never been, and the British agent CORMORANT had employed a man who did not exist.

The sound of the nib scratching the paper filled the empty office.

She held the telegram up. The alteration was clean. The ink matched. The hand was her own — the same careful Portuguese hand that stamped and approved and passed along a hundred wires a day, and which no one had ever questioned, because Ana Ferreira was quiet and competent and arrived on time and left on time and caused no one any trouble. She placed the original in the outgoing file for Keller’s contact and the carbon copy, unaltered, in the archive drawer that only she and the senior censor could access.

No. She stopped. She pulled the carbon from the drawer and tore it into strips and burned the strips in the office’s ashtray, watching the paper curl and blacken, and the smell of it was sharp and acrid and final.

She had burned the truth. What remained was the lie, and the lie was the only version that would leave this building.

Ana gathered her things. She took the altered copy and folded it into the lining of her handbag, where the edges pressed against the seven hundred escudos she had received from Thorne — no, that was at home, in the tin. What pressed against her hand now was only paper, but it was paper that could kill or paper that could save, and she had chosen, and the choosing felt like swallowing glass.

The café was in Alcântara, near the docks, a place where the clientele changed shift with the tide. Keller had chosen it, as he chose everything, with the precision of a man who understood that the right setting could make a confession feel like a conversation. The tables were marble-topped and stained with coffee rings. The light was amber and inadequate. A radio played fado somewhere behind the counter, the singer’s voice low and wounded, a complaint set to music.

Keller was already seated when she arrived. He did not stand. He was a compact man in a grey suit that fit him like a thought — precisely tailored, nothing wasted. His hair was fair, thinning at the crown, and he wore round spectacles that caught the lamplight and made his eyes difficult to read. He had the build of someone who had once been athletic and had replaced physical exertion with the more demanding labor of patience.

“Sit,” he said. Not a request.

Ana sat. She placed her handbag on her lap, her fingers resting on the clasp. The altered telegram was inside, and she could feel its weight — not physical weight, but the weight of what she had done to it, the single letter that was a door closed against a man who had carried water for her brother.

“You have the material,” Keller said. His Portuguese was fluent but accented, the consonants filed down to hard edges, as though he had learned the language from manuals and refined it through interrogations. “The material. Now.”

Ana opened her bag and produced the folded paper. She placed it on the table between them. Keller did not reach for it immediately. He looked at her first, and the look lasted long enough for her to understand that something had changed since their last meeting, that the easy transaction of the previous week — the CORMORANT telegram, the seven hundred escudos, the nod of professional acknowledgment — had been replaced by something more careful and less generous.

He unfolded the telegram. He read it. He placed it flat on the marble and smoothed the creases with the flat of his hand, and then he reached into his breast pocket and produced a second sheet of paper, which he placed beside the first.

Ana’s stomach dropped.

The second sheet was a carbon copy — not hers, not from the office, but a transcription, typed on different paper, with a different ribbon. She could see the name on it clearly from where she sat: João Garção. The correct name. The real name.

“One must distinguish between the noise and the signal,” Keller said, and the words were so precisely Thorne’s cadence that for a disorienting moment Ana thought the British attaché had somehow materialized in the café, had somehow followed her, had somehow — but no. Keller was quoting. He was quoting Thorne, or he was quoting someone who quoted Thorne, or he was simply using a phrase that any careful intelligence officer might employ. The effect was the same: he was showing her that he had access to language and information that should have been sealed.

“You gave me this,” Keller said, tapping the altered telegram. “Garçano. A name that appears in no civil registry, no fishing license, no port authority record. A name, in short, that belongs to no one.” He tapped the second sheet. “And yet my own sources provide me with this. Garção. A man who exists. A man with a boat and an address and a pattern of behaviour that the British have found interesting.”

The fado on the radio ended. In the silence, Ana could hear the clock behind the counter, the murmur of the man at the next table, the creak of the café’s old wooden floor as the barista shifted his weight. She could hear her own breathing.

“Keller,” she said, and her voice was steady because she had learned long ago that fear must be subverted, must be turned inside out and worn as something else — competence, boredom, mild irritation. “The text reads as follows. The name as transcribed in the outgoing file is Garçano. The original wire was damaged at the exchange — a carbon smudge on the third line. I rendered the name as I read it. If there is a discrepancy, it is a clerical matter, not a substantive one.”

She heard herself speaking and recognized the architecture of the lie — how it rested on a plausible detail (the smudge), a professional rationale (the damaged wire), and a tone of weary competence designed to make questioning feel like rudeness. She had built it in the seconds between Keller’s question and her answer, and it stood, she realized, on nothing at all.

Keller removed his spectacles and cleaned them on his tie. The gesture was slow, deliberate, a man buying time or a man pretending to buy time, and Ana could not tell which, and the not-knowing was worse than any accusation.

“A clerical matter,” he repeated. He replaced the spectacles. “I require confirmation. You are telling me that the British have an informant named Garçano — a man who does not exist — because of a smudge.”

“I am telling you that I transcribed what I could read. If your source has a cleaner copy, your source should provide it to you, and you should rely on that.”

The words came out harder than she intended, edged with the desperation she was trying to bury, and she saw Keller register the edge, saw his eyes narrow behind the round lenses, saw him file it away — not the excuse itself, but the tone, the defensiveness, the faint overreach of a woman who has been caught and is pretending she has not.

He folded both sheets of paper and placed them in his pocket. He did not push any money across the table. The absence of payment was more eloquent than any accusation.

“I will not apply pressure today,” Keller said. “Today I will accept your explanation. But I will remember this exchange, Miss Ferreira. I will remember that you gave me a name that leads nowhere, and that another source gave me a name that leads somewhere, and that you have asked me to believe these are the same name rendered differently. I will remember the quality of your explanation and the speed with which you produced it.”

He stood. The café’s amber light fell across his face and made his expression unreadable — not hostile, not friendly, simply opaque, the face of a man who has decided to watch rather than to act, at least for now.

“We will meet next week,” he said. “Bring me something clean. Something I can verify against what I already possess. If the material is good, payment resumes. If the material is not good —“ He paused, and the pause was a door opening onto a room she did not want to enter. “If the material is not good, we will have a different conversation. One in which I apply pressure.”

He left. The door of the café closed behind him, and the bell above it rang once, a small clear note that hung in the coffee-soured air.

Ana sat for a long time. The barista cleared Keller’s cup, untouched, and glanced at her with the incurious attention of a man who sees strangers every day and remembers none of them. The fado resumed on the radio, a different singer, a different complaint, the same wound.

She had lied, and the lie had held, but only barely, and only because Keller had chosen to let it hold — which meant it was not her lie that had saved her but Keller’s patience, which was a weapon she could not control and could not predict. He had a second source. Someone inside the censorship office, or inside the British wire room, or somewhere else along the chain that she had believed was hers alone to exploit. She had thought she was the only door. She was merely one of several, and the others had given Keller the truth while she had given him the lie, and now he knew both, and she knew that he knew.

Her handbag sat on her lap, empty of money, holding nothing but the weight of what she had done.

She walked home along the docks. The Tagus was black and flat, and the ships at anchor rode the current without sound, their riding lights trembling on the water’s surface. She counted her steps — a habit from childhood, when Rafael had taught her to count the paving stones between their apartment and the bakery, a game that had become a discipline that had become, in these last months, a means of holding herself together when the world felt as though it were pulling apart at the seams.

She thought about the money in the tin behind the cistern. Fourteen hundred escudos. Twelve thousand needed. At her current rate — seven hundred from Thorne, and if the payments from Keller resumed at all, seven hundred from him, per sale — she would need eight more sales to each, assuming the prices held, assuming neither man lost interest, assuming Keller did not decide that a source who lied once might lie again and was therefore a liability to be eliminated rather than an asset to be cultivated.

She thought about Garção, asleep now in his house in Cacilhas, unaware that his name had been on a telegram, that it had been altered, that a woman he had never met had chosen to protect him because a man in a prison camp had once written that this fisherman had carried water three times a day. She had saved him. She had also, she understood, endangered herself, and the arithmetic of that exchange — one life measured against another, one risk weighed against one reward — was the arithmetic that would define everything that followed.

She reached her apartment. The stairwell smelled of boiled cabbage and lye. She climbed to the third floor, locked the door behind her, and went to the lavatory and removed the tin from behind the cistern and counted the banknotes again. Fourteen hundred. She added nothing, because Keller had paid nothing, and the total remained what it had been, and the distance between what she had and what she needed was the same distance as before, except that now the path to closing it had a crack in it, a fracture line where her certainty had been.

She replaced the tin. She washed her hands. She looked at herself in the mirror above the basin — a woman of thirty-one with dark hair and tired eyes and a face that gave nothing away, that had been trained by grief and practice to reveal only what she chose to reveal — and she understood that the clean business was over. The first sale to Thorne had been simple: a telegram copied, a name passed, an envelope received. The second sale to Keller had been the same. But this — this alteration, this lie, this protection of a man whose only crime was kindness to her brother — this was different. This was not commerce. This was choice. And choice, in Lisbon in the winter of 1942, was a currency that spent lives instead of escudos.

A mistake had been logged. The trap had a hair trigger now.
