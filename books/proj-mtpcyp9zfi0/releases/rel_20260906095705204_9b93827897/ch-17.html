Chapter 17

What Rafael Did

The wine tasted of nothing — cheap vinho tinto cut with water, or perhaps just hours exposed to air — but Ana held the glass because her hands needed occupation that was not trembling.

She had chosen the tavern in Bairro Alto for its darkness: a single bulb behind a smoked glass shade, the walls sweating, the air thick with the breath of men who had nowhere better to drink at four in the afternoon. Three old men played cards beneath a calendar from 1938 that no one had thought to replace. The barman had glanced at her once — a woman alone — and then away, dismissing her as someone else’s problem.

She was waiting for a man who called himself Tio. Uncle. A ridiculous name for someone who moved in Rafael’s old circles, the remnants of student groups and trade union cells that the PVDE had not yet crushed. She had sent word through the usual intermediary: a bookseller on Rua Garrett who passed messages inside hollowed volumes of Pessoa and never asked questions. Three days of silence, and then a reply: the back table at Tasca do Chico, Thursday, the hour when the light was worst.

Tio arrived without ceremony. A small man in a damp coat, cap pulled low, his face a topography of caution — deep grooves around the mouth where suspicion lived, eyes that checked the room before they settled on her. He sat, did not greet her, signalled the barman with two fingers. They waited in silence until two glasses arrived and the barman retreated.

“You are Ferreira’s sister.”

Not a question. Ana nodded.

“I require confirmation of who you are,” she said. The formal phrasing steadied her. “The text reads as follows: Rafael sent a message through the bookseller in March. The word was jacarandá.”

Tio’s shoulders loosened a fraction. He drank, wiped his lips. “Your brother. He is alive, or he was in November. The camp does not announce deaths quickly, but I have a man who still — “ He stopped, looked at her. “You know about the camp.”

“I know he is in Tarrafal. I know he was arrested,” she said, the official charge she had clung to for months rising automatically to her lips. Then Tio’s expression shifted, a flicker of something heavier. A cold knot tightened in her stomach. “What… what more should I know?”

Tio’s glass paused midway to the table. He set it down carefully, as if the sound it made against the wood might carry.

“Distributing material,” he repeated. The word sat between them, and she watched him taste it — the way one tests something found on the ground to see if it is food or filth. “Is that what you think?”

The tavern noise — the slap of cards, a cough from the corner, the barman’s radio leaking fado through a cracked speaker — seemed to recede. Ana felt the pressure change in her ears, the way it does before a door slams shut.

“I was there the night they took him.” Her voice was level. She had rehearsed this. “The PVDE broke through the door. They found pamphlets. Sedition laws. It was — “

“It was not pamphlets.” Tio spoke quietly, the way one speaks to someone standing on a ledge. “Your brother was not arrested for pamphlets. The pamphlets were — how do you say — the cover story. The story for the family. The story for the newspapers.”

Ana’s stomach turned. The wine, the airless room, the smell of damp stone and unwashed wool — all of it pressed inward. “What do you mean, cover story?”

Tio leaned forward. His breath was sour. “I knew Rafael. We were in the same cell. Not the student group — the other one. The one that did not write letters and hand out leaflets at the university.” He paused, and she saw him decide something. “Your brother was brave. Braver than the rest of us. But what he did — what he was convicted of — it was not printing.”

“Then what?”

The barman’s radio changed. A news bulletin, the flat voice of the Emissora Nacional reading shipping figures. Tio waited until it filled the silence, covering their conversation like a blanket over a wound.

“There was a man in the PVDE—what some people still called PIDE from before they reorganized names, but officially it was PVDE then. A man named Correia. Inspector Correia. He was — how do you say — the one who came to the houses. The one who knocked on doors at three in the morning. He had a reputation. Women, men, it did not matter. He was thorough.”

Ana knew the type. She had stood frozen in a doorway while men like that took her brother. Her hands, pale and ink-stained, gripped the edge of the table.

“Rafael killed him.”

Two words. Two words that struck the air from her lungs. The tavern seemed to tilt — the single bulb swaying though there was no wind, or perhaps it was her own vision swimming. She gripped the table harder. The glass jumped, spilling a dark line across the wood.

“That is not — “ she began, but the sentence had no ending. She had come here expecting — what? Confirmation that Rafael was a pamphleteer, a dissident, a political prisoner? A man of words, not of blood?

“On the seventeenth of June, 1937. Correia left his office on Rua António Maria Cardoso. Your brother was waiting. A knife.” Tio mimed a short, upward motion. “It was quick. Correia died in the street. They took Rafael two hours later — he did not run. He stayed. He sat on the curb beside the body and waited for them to come.”

Ana’s memory reassembled itself with terrible precision. The night of the arrest: she had been twenty-three, reading by the window, when the door splintered inward. Rafael standing calm — too calm, she had always thought, for a man caught with seditious papers — his hands open, his face carrying an expression she had read as resignation. Now she understood it was something else entirely. It was the face of a man who had already done the thing he was willing to die for.

The pamphlets had been on the table, yes. But they were not why the PVDE came with six officers and a car. They came because Rafael Ferreira had murdered an inspector of the political police, and the state wanted him alive only so it could make him suffer slowly in the Cape Verde sun.

“He never told me.” Ana’s voice was barely audible. “In every letter — every message through the intermediary — he wrote about the pamphlets. About free speech. About — “

“What would you have done if he had told you?” Tio’s question was not unkind. “Would you have been less determined to free him? Would you have stopped sending money to the intermediary, stopped bribing the guards for his food and medicine?”

Ana’s mouth opened, but no sound came. Because the answer, rising like bile in her throat, was: she did not know.

“The regime needed the pamphlet story,” Tio continued. “A murderer is a criminal. A pamphleteer is a political prisoner. Portugal sells itself to the world as a gentle dictatorship — no blood, no executions, just quiet men in quiet prisons. If the foreign press learned what Rafael actually did, it would change the story. So they kept it quiet. The trial was closed. The file is sealed.”

“But you know.”

“I was in the cell next to his for three months before they sent him to Tarrafal. He told me everything. He told me because he needed someone on the outside to know — not for propaganda, not for the cause. Just to know.” Tio finished his wine. “He said: tell my sister, when she is ready to hear it. I have been waiting for you to ask.”

The fado on the radio ended. In the silence, Ana heard her own breathing — shallow, rapid, the breathing of a woman who has been running without moving.

She placed both hands flat on the table. The spilled wine had reached her fingertips, cold as something dead. She stood, and the room swayed but held.

“Thank you,” she said. The words were stones in her mouth.

Tio did not rise. “One more thing. Correia was not just an inspector. He was the one who — before Rafael — had been closing the student cells. One by one. He had a list. Rafael’s name was on it. Your brother did not kill Correia for politics, Ferreira. He killed him because Correia was coming for him. It was not sacrifice. It was survival.”

Ana walked out.

The lane behind the tavern opened onto a hillside of crooked cobblestones descending toward the Tagus. The river wind hit her — cold, salt-edged, carrying the distant groan of a freighter moving out toward the Atlantic. She pressed her back against the stone wall of a building and breathed until the nausea subsided, and then she walked.

She walked past the German legation without turning her head, past the sentry box and the black Mercedes parked at the kerb, past the side door to the press office where she had met Keller a dozen times with telegrams folded inside her handbag. She walked to the riverfront, where the stone quay was slick with spray and the wind cut through her charcoal-grey suit as if the fraying collar were an open wound.

Rafael. Her brother, who had taught her to read English with a battered copy of David Copperfield, who wept at fado, who carried spiders outside rather than crush them. Rafael, who had sat on a curb beside a dying man and waited for his own arrest with the patience of someone who had already crossed a line and knew there was no path back.

She had been selling secrets to fund the release of a man she did not know.

The money — Thorne’s escudos, Keller’s reichsmarks, the sums she counted in the dark of her rented room — had always been blood money in a technical sense. Intelligence work killed people at a distance. She knew this. She had accepted it the way one accepts weather: without protest, without the illusion of control. But she had told herself, every night, that the purpose was pure. Rafael was a political prisoner. A pamphleteer. A man imprisoned for words.

Now the words were a lie, and the man was a killer, and the money she had been accumulating with such careful, treacherous precision was meant to buy the freedom of someone who had opened another human being’s throat with a knife.

She thought of Keller’s face in the anteroom that morning — the way his eyes had tracked her hands as she set the documents on the desk, the way he had said “You seem distracted, Ferreira” with a softness that was worse than accusation. He was watching her now. One mistake, she had understood in that moment, and everything would collapse. But what was everything? A life built on the fiction of her brother’s innocence, the money earned by selling the same telegrams to both sides, the fragile architecture of double deception that had always been, she understood now, not a tower but a bridge — a bridge over nothing.

If she bought Rafael’s freedom — if the bribes and the intermediaries and the years of risk and the slow corrosion of her own honesty finally succeeded — she would not be rescuing a political prisoner. She would be unleashing a man who had killed and sat down beside the body to wait. A man whose gentleness had been, perhaps, the mask, and whose violence had been, perhaps, the truth.

The river wind pressed against her. A ship’s horn sounded, low and mournful, from the direction of Belém. Ana stood at the quay and felt the cold enter her bones, and could not tell whether it was the wind or the knowledge that had finally found her.

She had been selling her soul to save a murderer.

She would do it anyway.

And that, she realized, was the worst part — not the revelation, not the nausea, not the shattering of every image she had carried of the brother who had called her “little owl” when she was small and afraid of the dark. The worst part was that knowing what Rafael had done changed nothing. She still needed the money. She still needed Keller’s trust and Thorne’s envelopes and the careful, deadly dance between them. She still needed to be the woman who altered telegrams and sold them twice and lied to men who would have her killed if they knew.

The wind shifted. Somewhere behind her, in the narrow lanes of Bairro Alto, a door closed. She turned toward it, instinct overriding grief, and began to walk back toward the legation, toward the desk and the carbon paper and the encrypted telegrams that would not wait for her to finish falling apart.

If she bought his freedom, what kind of man would she unleash?
