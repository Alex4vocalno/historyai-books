Chapter 8

The Unreliable Source

The routine was profitable, but every sale planted two identical seeds of risk — one in Thorne’s files, one in Otto Keller’s — and both were now growing toward the same moment of comparison, the same collision, the same exposure. Ana had understood this from the beginning. She had simply assumed the collision would come later, on some Tuesday she hadn’t reached yet, when the sums had accumulated and her brother was free and the risk no longer mattered.

She was wrong.

The sound of paper scraping wood was her only greeting as Otto Keller slid two pieces of paper across the desk.

Otto Keller slid two pieces of paper across the desk, and the sound of paper scraping wood was the only greeting she received.

Ana stood in the doorway of the hotel room for a beat too long, a sharp, subversive thought cutting through her: This isn’t the script. She had come expecting the usual: the envelope, the quiet count, the brief exchange of pleasantries that weren’t pleasant at all. Tuesdays. The Hotel Metrópole. Room 411. The routine had become almost liturgical, and like any liturgy, its power lay in its predictability. Now the ritual was broken.

Keller did not look up. He sat in the chair behind the desk with his coat still buttoned, as though he might leave at any moment, and the two sheets of paper lay between them like an accusation waiting to be read.

“Close the door,” he said.

She closed it. The latch clicked with a finality that tightened her stomach. The room was small, impersonal, stripped of anything that suggested a person lived in it voluntarily. The curtain was drawn. The overhead light was the kind that turned everything the color of old newspaper.

“Sit.”

She sat. The chair was cold through her dress. She placed her handbag on her lap and kept her hands on it, because she did not know where else to put them and she needed somewhere to anchor her fingers that wasn’t the desk, wasn’t the papers, wasn’t the space between them where Keller’s gaze now rested with the flat patience of a man who had all night.

He said nothing. The silence did the work. It stretched past the point of politeness, past the point of discomfort, into the territory where silence itself became a tool of extraction. She had watched him use it before — on the hotel clerk, on a waiter who had brought the wrong order, on the world in general. Keller did not raise his voice. He simply removed sound until the other person rushed to fill it.

She looked at the papers between them.

The top sheet was hers. She recognized her own handwriting, the careful block capitals she used for the intelligence summaries, the particular way she formed the letter G. The second sheet was typed, and it took her a moment — a moment she would later be grateful for, because it gave her face time to settle into a mask of mere confusion — to understand what she was looking at.

The same report. The same date. The same coastal village, the same fishing boat, the same description of a man meeting a British officer at the harbour. Identical in every particular except one.

The name.

Her sheet read Miguel Costa. The typed sheet read João Pereira.

The fisherman’s real name. The one she had changed before passing the report to Keller, because Rafael had mentioned Costa in a letter from the prison — a friend, a cellmate, someone who had carried messages between political prisoners and their families. Costa was not an informant. Costa was a man who had helped her brother. And she had seen his name on a telegram and understood, in the time it took the censor’s stamp to fall, that passing it on unaltered would put him in the same kind of trouble Rafael was already in. Worse trouble. The kind that ended in a different building, with a different door, one that did not open from either side.

So she had changed it. One name. One letter in one report. She had told herself it was a small thing, a single grain of sand removed from a beach, and Keller would never notice because Keller received dozens of reports and cross-referenced none of them, because that was the entire premise of her operation — she was a primary source, not a corroborated one.

But here was the corroboration. Here was the second sheet, typed and precise, with the real name — Costa. A flaw, her single grain of sand removed, was now on record. Traceable back to her hand and her pen.

He has another source. The realization moved through her like cold water flooding a hold. Not panic — something colder, more structural, the feeling of a floor that had been solid a moment ago and was now revealed to be a trapdoor. She kept her hands on her handbag. She kept her eyes on the papers. She did not look at Keller because looking at him would mean showing him whatever was on her face, and she did not yet know what was on her face, and so she needed a moment to decide.

He had another source. Someone inside the same censorship office, or close to it. Her alteration had been immediately detected because someone else had seen the original telegram before she altered it, someone who had reported independently and whose report had now surfaced on his desk beside hers with the two names sitting side by side like a before and after photograph of her own betrayal.

“You see the problem,” Keller said. Not a question. A statement, delivered with the same flat patience, the same absence of emphasis. He might have been reading a weather report. The problem was laid bare. Keller had another intelligence source within his network, and it had found her out.

She saw it. She saw all of it — the two names, the implication, the fact that he had placed them in front of her instead of simply acting on them. If he had already decided she was compromised, she would not be sitting in this chair. She would not be sitting anywhere. The fact that she was still here, still breathing, still being spoken to, meant he had not yet decided. He was testing. He was offering her the chance to explain, which was also the chance to incriminate herself, and the difference between the two depended entirely on what she said in the next ten seconds.

Her mouth was dry as dust. She could feel the prickle at the nape of her neck, the skin tightening along her scalp like a drumhead. The room smelled of stale tobacco and furniture polish and something else — the particular nothing-smell of spaces where people did not stay long enough to leave a scent. Think, you fool. Think past the fear.

She needed a reason. Not the real reason — the real reason would end everything. She needed a reason that was boring, bureaucratic, the kind of explanation that made the listener’s eyes glaze and their attention drift. Something that implied incompetence rather than intention. Something that made the error so mundane, so uninteresting, that pursuing it would feel like more trouble than it was worth.

Water damage. The word surfaced from somewhere — not a plan, not a preparation, just a memory rising at the moment she needed it. The censorship office basement flooded in October. She had seen telegrams come through with the ink bleeding, the paper warped, names half-obliterated. Everyone in the office knew about it. It was the kind of detail that was too banal to invent and too boring to question.

“There was water damage,” she said.

She heard her own voice and it sounded wrong — too careful, too measured, the voice of someone choosing words from a shelf rather than speaking. She pressed on before the silence could swallow the explanation. She saw his face, his eyes resting on her with a flat, unblinking scrutiny that accepted nothing.

“The original telegram. October — the basement flooded, you may have heard. Several sheets were damaged. The name was partially obscured. I reconstructed it from what was legible.” She paused. Not a calculated pause — a real one, the kind that happened when a lie was being built in real time and the builder needed a breath to check the structure before adding the next layer. “I must have read it wrong. The ink had bled. P— the letter P can look like a G when the paper is warped. I transcribed what I saw.”

It was, she realized, a good lie. Not because it was clever, but because it was specific. The flooding was real. The damaged telegrams were real. Anyone who checked would find that the basement had indeed taken on water in October and that several documents had been affected. The detail about the letter P and the letter G was the kind of thing that only someone who had actually tried to read water-damaged paper would think to say, and it had the additional virtue of being true — she had seen P and G blur together on smudged sheets, had squinted at them herself, had made the same kind of judgment call in good faith dozens of times.

Keller studied her. His eyes were pale, the color of slate washed in rain, and they did not move. They rested on her face with the steady, unblinking attention of a man who had spent years learning to read the difference between truth and its convincing imitation.

“Clerical error,” he said. He said it the way a doctor might say interesting — not agreeing, not dismissing, simply placing the word on the table beside the two reports and letting it sit there.

“Clerical error,” she confirmed. The echo cost her something. She could feel it in her chest, a slight catch, as though the repetition had tightened something around her ribs.

Keller picked up the typed sheet — the one with the real name, the one from his other source — and folded it once, precisely, along the center line. He placed it inside his coat pocket. Then he picked up her sheet, the one with Miguel Costa written in her careful block capitals, and he placed it on top of the folded note, and he buttoned the coat.

Both versions. Both names. He was keeping them both.

“The flooding,” he said. “October.”

“Yes.”

“And you reconstructed the name from damaged text.”

“Yes.”

“And it did not occur to you to flag the uncertainty.”

The question landed like a hand on the back of her neck. She had not anticipated it. The lie had been built to explain the discrepancy, not to explain why she hadn’t mentioned the discrepancy, and for a moment the structure wobbled — she could feel it, the way a table wobbles when one leg is shorter than the others, the slight tilt that tells you something is wrong before you look underneath.

“I should have,” she said. Not an excuse. An admission. She offered it because admitting a small failure was better than inventing a new lie to support the first one, because lies compounded and she had learned — was learning, was learning right now, in this room, under these eyes — that the compounding was where the structure collapsed.

Keller regarded her for a long moment. The overhead light hummed. A car passed on the street below, its tyres hissing on wet cobblestone. When he spoke again, his voice was colder, lower, the tone of a man no longer assessing but delivering a verdict.

“This discrepancy is now logged,” he said. The word logged carried a weight that recorded would not have. In his world, it meant a note in a personal file, an entry in a private ledger kept for assets that had shown a flaw. A flaw is now on record, traceable back to her and her alone. A mark against a name that would not be erased by time or good behaviour or the passage of however many Tuesdays she survived after this one. “Your alteration was immediately detected. My source provided the original text within the hour. You see, I have another intelligence source within my network, precisely for verifying such things. The explanation. The date.”

He paused. When he spoke again, the texture of his voice had shifted further, like stone grinding against stone.

“The next error ends our arrangement. Not the next discrepancy. Not the next inconsistency. The next error. Whatever its cause. Whatever its explanation. You will not receive a second opportunity to reconstruct a name from damaged text. Do you understand the distinction?”

She nodded. The nod was small, controlled, the kind of nod that acknowledged without agreeing, that accepted the terms without accepting the premise. She was not incompetent. He knew she was not incompetent. She knew he knew. The fiction they were maintaining — that this was a clerical error, that she was a careless but honest source, that the explanation was sufficient — was a fiction they were both choosing to inhabit, and the fact that he was choosing it told her something she filed away for later examination: he needed her. Not enough to trust her, but enough to keep her. The access she provided was worth more than the lie she had told, at least for now, and that calculation was the only thing standing between her and whatever came after arrangement ended.

Keller reached into the desk drawer and produced the envelope. He placed it on the desk between them without ceremony. She did not count it. She picked it up, placed it in her handbag, and stood.

“The fisherman,” Keller said, as she turned toward the door. “Costa. Or Pereira. Whichever he is.”

She stopped. Her hand was on the doorknob. The metal was cold against her palm.

“He’s nobody,” she said, the words coming out more sharply than she intended. “A fisherman. A name on a telegram.”

“Everyone is a name on a telegram to someone,” Keller said. He was looking out the window now, or rather at the curtain, which was the color of dust. His voice was quiet, but it carried a new, implicit threat. “The question is whose. And how many copies that telegram has.”

She left. The corridor was empty. The carpet was thin and patterned in a design that had probably been fashionable in 1935 and was now simply tired. She walked to the stairwell because the lift required an operator and she did not want an operator, did not want another person, did not want anyone to see her face in the fluorescent light of the lift car and form an opinion about what they saw there.

On the stairs, she pressed her back against the wall and stood for a moment in the dark, her heart hammering a frantic rhythm against her ribs. A source. He has another source. The envelope was in her handbag. The money was real. The sum she needed for Rafael’s release — the lawyer’s fees, the bribes, the payments that moved through hands she had never seen to places she had never been — was still distant, still months away, still measured in Tuesdays she could not afford to lose.

But the ground had shifted. Keller had another source. Someone in her office, or near it, someone who had seen the original telegram and reported it independently, and that person’s existence meant her operation was not the closed system she had designed. There was a second pipe feeding the same reservoir, and the two streams were now visible to the man who controlled the valve.

She pushed off the wall and descended. The hotel lobby was busy with the early evening crowd — men in suits, women in hats, a clerk behind the desk who did not look up. The revolving door deposited her onto the street, and the Lisbon air hit her face, cold and damp, carrying the smell of the river and the salt of the Atlantic beyond it. She breathed. The air was a relief against her skin, which was still too warm, still carrying the heat of that room and that light and those eyes.

She had survived. The lie had held. But the lie had also changed things — had moved her from the category of trusted source to the category of source-with-a-notation, and notations in a spy’s private log did not fade. They accumulated. They were compared. They were placed beside future notations until the pattern became unmistakable and the arrangement ended and the envelope stopped appearing on Tuesdays and the sum she needed retreated further into the distance.

A mistake had been logged. The next one would cost her everything — not just the income, not just the access, but the thing the income was for, the reason she had started all of this, the brother in the prison cell who had written to her about a man named Costa who carried messages and deserved better than to have his name given to the people who would make him disappear.

The tram arrived at the stop on the corner. She boarded. The conductor called the route. She did not hear it. She was counting — not the money now, but the weeks, the days, the distance between the sum she had and the sum she needed, and the narrowing, treacherous space between the lie she had told and the lie she would have to tell next.
