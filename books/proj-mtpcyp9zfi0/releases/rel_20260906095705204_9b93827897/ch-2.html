Chapter 2

The First Sale

The banknotes sat against her hip like a second heartbeat, and each one was a small, folded confession.

Ana had counted them three times in the lavatory of the censorship office before she’d left, standing in the narrow stall with the door bolted, her hands trembling so badly she’d had to press the notes flat against the tiled wall to separate them. Fourteen hundred escudos. She did not yet know the price of what she had done, but she knew the weight of it — the exact weight, the faintly starched stiffness of paper that had never been folded, never been creased, never passed through another human hand before Thorne’s man had pressed it into a plain envelope and left it beneath the napkin at the table in the Café A Brasileira, between the sugar bowl and the small glass ashtray, while she had sat there pretending to read a newspaper she could not see.

That had been forty minutes ago. She was still trembling now, sitting on the hard bench of the post office in the Praça dos Restauradores, waiting for the clerk to call her number. The hall smelled of shellac and old varnish, and beneath it the particular staleness of a public building where too many people had stood too long in too close a space. The marble was cracked near the base of the far wall, and a fine grey crack ran up through it like a vein beneath skin. She watched it because watching it kept her from looking at the envelope in her handbag.

She had taken the first irreversible step. She understood this in the way one understands that a window, once broken, cannot be unbroken. The telegram — the CORMORANT telegram, as she had come to think of it, though she did not know why that word had attached itself to the intelligence, only that it had — was no longer hers. It was Thorne’s now. And the money was hers, and Rafael’s, and Tarrafal’s, and that was the geometry of the thing: she had traded a secret for a sum, and the sum would travel across the sea to a prison camp on an island off the coast of Africa, and somewhere in that transaction her old self — the translator, the clerk, the woman who had only ever observed and never acted — had been left behind at the café table, next to the cold coffee she had not drunk.

She had replayed the meeting so many times in the past hour that it had begun to lose its edges, to flatten into a series of still images, like the photographs in the newspapers. Thorne arriving. Thorne sitting down. The careful, unhurried way he had placed his gloves on the table, as though he were a man with nowhere more important to be and no one more important to meet. She had expected someone furtive, someone who would glance over his shoulder and speak in half-sentences. Instead he had been composed, precise, almost courteous — the demeanor of a man conducting a business transaction over a ledger rather than a dead drop over coffee.

“You have something for me, Miss Ferreira,” he had said, and the use of her name — her true name, the one she had not given him — had landed like a stone dropped into still water. She had felt the ripples move through her, and she had understood, in that single moment, that she had never been anonymous. That she had never been the hidden observer she had imagined herself to be. That he had known her before she had ever walked through the door of the café, and that the meeting was not a negotiation but an audition she had already passed.

“The text reads as follows,” she had said, because those were the only words she could find, and they had come out steady, which surprised her. She had slid the folded paper across the table beneath the newspaper, and he had taken it without looking down, without any change in the even rhythm of his breathing, and she had thought: he has done this before. He has done this many times. I am not the first, and I will not be the last.

He had studied the paper for a moment, his expression unreadable. “This will do,” he had said finally, his voice measured and professional. “It’s clear. Precise. The sort of thing I can use.”

She had not known whether this was a compliment or a warning, whether he was praising the quality of the intelligence or cautioning her against the illusion that what she had done was simple, or safe, or without consequence. His voice had been that of a man who treated espionage not as adventure but as administration.

She had not asked what he meant. She had not needed to. The danger was already sitting in her chest, a tight bright knot beneath her ribs that she would carry for days, that she could feel even now, sitting on this bench in the post office, waiting for her number.

He had paid her without ceremony. The envelope had appeared from beneath his coat — he had not opened a wallet, had not counted notes, had done nothing so vulgar or visible as to exchange money in public — and he had placed it on the table with the same unhurried precision he had used for the gloves, and he had said, “Fourteen hundred escudos. I trust you’ll find it sufficient. I trust you’ll understand that sufficiency is not the same as finality. There will be more, if the quality holds. There will be nothing, if it doesn’t. And there will be consequences, if I find that what you’ve sold me has also been sold elsewhere.”

She had almost laughed then, though not from humour. The idea of selling the same intelligence to someone else — to Keller, to the Germans — had not yet occurred to her, and the fact that Thorne had raised it unprompted told her something about him that she would not fully understand until later: that he thought in contingencies, in second-order possibilities, in the moves that had not yet been made but might be. He was not a man who reacted. He was a man who anticipated.

“I’m not a merchant, Mr Thorne,” she had said, and the subversion in her own voice had surprised her — the quiet, coded insistence of it, the way it had refused to accept the framing he had imposed. She was not selling. She was not trading. She was — what? Saving her brother. That was all. That was the whole architecture of it, and if the architecture happened to resemble espionage, then the fault lay not with her but with the world that had put her in a position where this was the only door left open.

“Fourteen hundred,” he had repeated, as though confirming a detail in a contract, and then he had stood, and drawn on his gloves, and left a coin on the saucer for the coffee he had not drunk, and he had walked out of the café with the unhurried gait of a man who had no reason to hurry because he had already arrived wherever he intended to be.

Now the post office clerk called her number, and Ana rose and walked to the counter, and the envelope came out of her handbag, and the notes came out of the envelope, and she counted them once more — fourteen hundred escudos, each one a piece of a life she was trying to reassemble — and she asked for a money order form.

“Destination?” the clerk said, without looking up. His pen was poised over the form, and his voice carried the flat indifference of a man who processed a hundred such forms a day and could not afford to be curious about any of them.

“Tarrafal,” she said. “Campo do Tarrafal. Chão Bom.” The words came out steady, but her hands, filling out the form, were not. She wrote Rafael’s name in the space for the recipient, and her own in the space for the sender, and she thought: he will know it’s me. He will know I have not forgotten him. He will know that whatever this money costs me — whatever it costs — I have chosen to pay it. And then she thought: he would not want me to pay it like this.

Rafael, who had said, the last time she had seen him, in the courtroom, before they had taken him away, “Ana, a country is its people, not its borders.” He had said it with such conviction, such unshakeable, impractical, beautiful conviction, and she had wanted to scream at him that borders were all that separated him from her, that the border between the courtroom and the street was the border between a brother and a sister, and that she would cross any border, pay any price, betray any trust, to bring him home.

The clerk took the form, stamped it, counted the notes, and wrote out the money order with the mechanical efficiency of a man who had no interest in the story behind the transaction. Ana watched the ink dry on the official paper and thought: this is what it feels like. This is what it feels like to cross a line you cannot uncross. The money is gone. The telegram is gone. And in their place is a receipt and a promise and a fear that will not leave me, not today, not tomorrow, not until Rafael is free and I can stop.

She folded the receipt and put it in her handbag, next to the empty envelope, and she walked out of the post office into the January light, which was thin and pale and did not warm her. The Praça dos Restauradores was busy — people moving with the purposeful indifference of a city at war with itself, neutral on paper but divided in every other way, the British and the Germans and the Portuguese all occupying the same streets, the same cafés, the same hotels, all pretending that the pretence of neutrality was the same thing as peace.

She had done it. She had sold a secret and sent the money to her brother, and the world had not ended, and no one had stopped her, and Thorne had known her name and had not used it against her but had used it to draw her deeper in, to make her understand that she was no longer invisible, that she was a participant now, a piece on a board she could not see the edges of.

And she thought: I can do this again. The thought came to her with a clarity that frightened her, because it was not a question. It was a recognition. The money was already spent, and Rafael needed more — more for bribes, more for food, more for the lawyers who had promised to file an appeal and who required payment in instalments that would stretch on for months. Fourteen hundred escudos was a beginning. It was not an ending. And the censorship office, where she sat every day reading the private correspondence of a continent at war, was full of telegrams, full of secrets, full of signal hidden in the noise.

She could do this again. She knew this now, in the way she knew the weight of the envelope, the smell of the ink, the crack in the marble. She knew it in her hands, which had stopped trembling. She knew it in her chest, where the knot of fear had not loosened but had become something else — something harder, something she could carry, something that felt less like terror and more like purpose.

She turned up her collar against the wind and walked toward the Avenida, where the trams were running and the cafés were full and the war, that vast and distant thing, was being fought not with guns but with telegrams and money and the quiet, irreversible decisions of people who had no other door left open.

The money was in her handbag no longer. But the knowledge of what it could buy — what it had already bought — sat against her hip like a second heartbeat, and each pulse said the same thing: again, again, again.
