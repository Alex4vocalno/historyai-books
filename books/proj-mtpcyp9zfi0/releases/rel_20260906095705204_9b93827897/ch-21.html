Chapter 21

File 14-B on the Desk

Now she sat at her desk in the censorship office, the anticipated horror pressing against her ribs, and waited for something worse.

She had been waiting for this particular worse thing since the rumours first surfaced three weeks ago. A departmental audit. Internal review of censorship logs, interception records, processing compliance — the phrase had circulated through the office in lowered voices, passed between desks like a coin no one wanted to hold. Not a surprise, then, but an anticipated horror, the kind you watch approach from a great distance and still aren’t ready for when it arrives.

For three weeks, since those first whispers began, she had expected this dread. A full review of the department. Scrutiny of every censorship log, each interception record, all procedural compliance. The very idea had moved through the office quietly, handed off like an unwanted token. It was no surprise, yet remained a dread observed from afar, a thing for which one can never truly be prepared.

The sound reached her before the sight of them. Two pairs of shoes on the tile corridor — one heavy, deliberate, one lighter, quicker, both with the measured cadence of people who had been told exactly where to go and what to do when they got there. A third set of footsteps followed at a distance, unhurried. Ana kept her pen moving. The German text she was rendering into Portuguese sat half-finished on the page, its syntax broken open like a watch with its back removed.

Two men entered the office. The lead auditor was broad, grey at the temples, with the compressed mouth of someone who found error personally offensive. He wore a dark suit that carried the stale tobacco smell of a hundred closed rooms before this one. His voice, when he finally spoke, was flat and bureaucratic, devoid of inflection. The second was younger, thin, with spectacles that he adjusted by touching only the bridge — a small, precise gesture he repeated as he surveyed the room. A third figure, a woman in a brown cardigan, followed and placed a cardboard box on the nearest empty desk without speaking.

No announcement. No greeting. The lead auditor looked at Ana, then at the stacked files on her desk, and pulled the first log binder toward him without asking permission. The sound of pages turning began — dry, rhythmic, each page lifted and set down with the care of someone counting banknotes.

Ana kept translating. The pen moved. Her breathing stayed even because she made it stay even, each exhale counted to four, each inhale held for two. This was it, then: the Censorship Office Audit the office rumors had warned her was coming. The second auditor took the binder beside the first and worked from the back, cross-referencing dates against a typed list on a clipboard. The woman in the cardigan sat at the empty desk and began unpacking carbon-copy sheets from the box, laying them in chronological rows.

The carbon copies. Ana’s pen paused for half a stroke, then continued. Those were the office’s duplicate copies of processed telegram forms — the ones kept in the central archive, not the original intake sheets. The originals had been transferred to the Foreign Ministry archive six months ago, part of a records consolidation that had caused weeks of complaint across the department. The auditors were working from the duplicates and from the log binders her unit maintained. Internal consistency. Her logs against the carbons.

That was the scope. Not originals against logs — duplicates against logs. The same material she had access to, which meant they weren’t checking for a master list from initial receipt. They wouldn’t find her method: she had never altered existing documents or forged signatures directly on intake sheets. Instead, she had worked within this very system, creating false entries in her own log binders that mirrored perfectly legitimate traffic—entries whose corresponding carbons she had studied and could reproduce in her sleep, matching their spacing, their stamps, their very wear patterns. A fraudulent layer built atop an authentic bureaucratic process. She focused on keeping her jaw relaxed, her gaze fixed on her translation pad, as the understanding seeped into her muscles.

For twenty minutes, only paper sounds filled the room. The lead auditor turned pages. The second auditor’s pen made small ticks on the clipboard. The woman in the cardigan matched sheets to entries with a ruler, her finger sliding down columns like a blunt needle seeking a flaw in a weave she herself had helped create. The thoroughness was methodical, relentless — each entry checked against its carbon, each date verified, each routing notation examined for completeness. Nothing was skipped. Nothing was treated as too small to matter.

Then the rhythm broke.

Then the rhythm broke.

The lead auditor stopped turning pages. His thumb held a single page flat, and he leaned forward, bringing his face closer to the surface. The fluorescent light caught the sheen of old carbon ink, faded in patches. He didn’t speak. He simply held the page and looked, and the silence expanded until it filled the space between the desks like something solid.

Ana’s fingers tightened around her pen. She watched him from the corner of her vision while keeping her translation moving, the German sentence she was writing now meaningless — she was operating on muscle memory, the hand producing correct shapes while the mind was elsewhere entirely.

He was looking at the entry for March 9th. She knew because she recognized the edge of the page — a slight tear in the upper corner she had noticed when she made the alteration. March 9th, the day she had changed Costa to Martins, smoothing one name into another with a careful blade and a steady hand, then re-copying the carbon to match.

The auditor looked up. “This entry.” His voice was flat, neither accusatory nor curious. Bureaucratic. “March 9th. The sender name here — it’s difficult to read. The carbon impression is uneven.”

He slid the log binder across the desk toward her, his finger marking the line. Ana set down her pen. She leaned forward, bringing her own face close to the page, and let her eyes move across the entry as though seeing it for the first time.

March 9th. Sender: A. Martins. Recipient: Banco Ultramarino, Lisbon branch. Content summary: routine commercial correspondence, currency transfer request. Routing: standard. Her handwriting. Her formatting. Her alteration, invisible to anyone who hadn’t watched her make it.

But the carbon — the carbon copy she had re-made to match. She had used the office’s standard carbon paper, pressed hard enough to leave a clean impression, and then aged the sheet by rubbing it lightly with a cloth to remove the telltale sheen of fresh carbon. The name was clear. Too clear, perhaps, for a copy that was supposed to be the third in a stack of five, where pressure faded and ink thinned.

“The carbon copies from that week were poor quality,” Ana said. She didn’t hesitate. The words came with the easy precision of someone who had handled bad carbons a hundred times and knew exactly how they failed. “We had a batch in March — the supplier sent a lighter grade, almost tissue paper. Half the copies came out smudged or incomplete. I re-copied the affected entries myself to ensure legibility. You’ll find a note in the supply requisition log if you check — I requested replacement stock on March 11th.”

She pointed to the entry without touching it, her fingertip a centimetre above the page. “Here — you can see the impression is heavier than the surrounding entries. That’s because I re-ran it. The original carbon was barely legible. A smudge across the sender’s name. I made a second copy from the original form to preserve the record.”

The auditor looked at her. Then at the page. Then at the second auditor, who consulted the clipboard and found, after a moment’s searching, a corresponding tick mark beside a supply note dated March 11th. The requisition was real — she had filed it herself, not as cover, but because the carbon paper had genuinely been terrible that month. The note existed. The bad batch existed. The explanation was not a lie but a truth reshaped around a lie, the most dangerous kind.

The lead auditor made a small notation on his clipboard — not a cross, not a flag, something in shorthand she couldn’t read from her angle. Then he turned the page.

The sound resumed. Pages turning, one after another, the rhythm restored. Ana picked up her pen. The German sentence on her page ended in the middle of a word and she finished it, the stroke slightly heavier than before.

They worked through the morning. The lead auditor finished the first binder and started the second. The second auditor moved to the carbon copies from April, laying them beside the log entries with methodical patience. The woman in the cardigan began re-boxing the sheets they had already checked, filing them back in order, her hands moving with the unconscious efficiency of someone who had done this a thousand times.

At half past eleven, the lead auditor closed the second binder. He spoke briefly to the second auditor, who nodded and made a final notation on the clipboard. The woman in the cardigan sealed the box with a length of packing tape.

The lead auditor turned to Ana. “Your logs are in order. The processing compliance is consistent. We may need to follow up on the March carbon issue — supply records are being reviewed separately — but nothing here requires further immediate attention.”

“Of course,” Ana said. “I’m happy to provide any additional clarification.”

The second auditor was already walking toward the door. The woman in the cardigan lifted the box and followed. The lead auditor gathered his clipboard and binder and stood, and for a moment his gaze moved across Ana’s desk one final time — the stacked files, the translation work, the pen lying across a half-finished page. Then he left.

The office exhaled. Chairs scraped. Someone across the room laughed, a short, nervous sound. The fluorescent lights hummed their constant, indifferent hum.

Ana sat still. Her hands lay flat on the desk, and she watched them — steady, no tremor, as though they belonged to someone who hadn’t just gambled everything on the quality of office stationery. The pen lay where she had set it down. She picked it up and finished the German sentence she had abandoned, and the writing was the same as it always was, because she would not let it be otherwise.

She had passed. The audit had been thorough — exhaustively, mercilessly thorough — and she had passed. Every altered entry, every shifted name, every re-made carbon had survived inspection. The explanation had been plausible because it was rooted in fact, and the auditor had accepted it because it was the kind of mundane, bureaucratic problem that existed in every office, too dull to be suspicious.

But as the relief settled, it carried something else with it — something she noticed only because she was watching the auditors leave. The second auditor, the thin one with the spectacles, paused at the door. He adjusted his glasses — the same precise gesture, bridge only — and looked back. Not at the room. Not at the lead auditor waiting in the corridor. At her desk. At the stack of files. His gaze lingered for two seconds, perhaps three, on the file second from the top — File 14-B, the one Keller’s order concerned — and then he was gone.

Ana stared at the closed door. The file sat where it always sat, unremarkable among the others, its tab identical in size and color. She hadn’t moved it. She hadn’t touched it since placing it there yesterday. There was no reason for anyone to look at it specifically, no reason for it to draw attention, and yet the auditor had looked, and the looking had been deliberate, and the deliberation sat in her stomach like a stone she couldn’t dissolve.

She thought of Rafael. Not the Rafael of the photographs she kept in a drawer she never opened at work, but the Rafael who existed now — behind wire, in heat, in the particular darkness of Tarrafal, where bribes bought messages and messages bought proof of life and proof of life was the only thing standing between her and the possibility that her brother had already ceased to exist. The money she had taken — the first payment, then the second, each amount exact and agreed — sat in a place she couldn’t think about while sitting at this desk, in this office, under this light.

It was meant for him. Every escudo was meant for him. The intermediary in Setúbal who handled the bribes had raised the price again last month, and the money she had was no longer enough, and the gap between what she had and what she needed was the space in which her entire life now operated.

Keller’s order in her pocket. Thorne’s money in its hiding place. Rafael’s name on a prison register she couldn’t reach. And now, a file on her desk that an auditor had looked at twice too long.

She placed her hand on File 14-B. The cardboard was cool and slightly rough under her fingertips. She left it where it was. Moving it now would be worse than leaving it. If the auditor had marked it — if the notation on his clipboard had been more than routine — then moving it would confirm what looking at it had only suggested.

The afternoon light shifted across the office, turning the tile floor from grey to pale gold and back. Ana finished her translation, filed the completed pages, and sat with her hands flat on the desk until the clock reached the hour she always left.

She stood. She put on her coat. The folded paper from Keller pressed against her ribs as she buttoned it, a small rectangular weight she could feel with each breath. She picked up her bag, and as she did, she looked down at File 14-B one more time.

The tab was the same as every other tab. The file was the same as every other file. Nothing about it was different, except that someone had looked at it who should not have been looking, and the looking had lasted long enough to mean something, and what it meant was a question she could not answer and could not afford to leave unanswered.

She walked to the door, and the corridor swallowed her, and behind her the office settled into its evening silence, and the file sat on the desk in the last of the light, unremarkable, untouched, and marked.
