Chapter 6

The Strait as a Funnel

The lookout on Taeping’s royal yard first saw it as a white thread against the grey haze to the southeast. A sail. He had been scanning the horizon for two hours, ever since the captain had ordered extra eyes aloft as they approached the narrows of the Sunda Strait, the gate that would carry them from the forge of the South China Sea into the Indian Ocean. Now he turned and called down to the deck, his voice carrying across the water in the still air of early morning. Sail ho, two points off the starboard bow.

On deck, Captain Donald MacKinnon raised his glass. The vessel had been hull-down, only her upper works visible, but as Taeping surged forward on a favorable current, more of her emerged from the horizon. The lines became clear. A clipper, fully rigged, making for the same gap between Java and Sumatra that Taeping was aiming for. MacKinnon studied the set of her sails, the rake of her masts. The name on her transom was unnecessary. The configuration told him enough. Ariel.

For two weeks, the five clippers had sailed in relative isolation, each captain making his own calculations about wind and current, each vessel pursuing its own invisible track across thousands of miles of open water. They had departed Foochow within days of each other, but the sea had widened the gaps between them. Fiery Cross had sailed first, on May 29, and had vanished into the weather. Ariel, Taeping, and Serica had followed on the 30th, and Taitsing at midnight on the 31st. Since then, they had been ghosts to one another, names in a ledger rather than hulls on a horizon. Now the Sunda Strait forced them back into proximity.

This narrow passage between Sumatra and Java—barely fifteen miles across at its widest point—acted as a funnel for every vessel making the run from China to the Indian Ocean. The geography left no alternative. A ship could run east of the Philippines, threading through the Gillolo Strait and Pitt Passage into the Ombai Strait, but that ‘Eastern Passage’ added hundreds of miles and risked the variable winds of the eastern seas. The clippers of the 1866 tea race all headed for the Sunda Strait, sailing past the Paracels, down the coast of Annam and then south to Borneo, bound for Anjer, on the southern side of the Sunda Strait. There was no other practical gateway to the Indian Ocean.

Ariel’s sighting of Taeping on June 2, while following the coast of Annam, had been the first confirmation that the race remained tight. Keay had seen his rival’s sails on the horizon and adjusted his own course in response. The psychological effect was immediate. He was no longer racing against an abstract competitor, a name in a newspaper or a rumor from the docks. Taeping was real, close, and sailing hard. The sighting on the 9th and 10th, approaching the coast of Borneo about 760 nautical miles from Foochow, reinforced the lesson. The ships were converging.

The South China Sea had offered room for strategic divergence. A captain could run wide of the rhumb line, seeking wind at the cost of distance, or he could hold a tight course, gambling on currents he could not see. The monsoon had complicated these choices. The southwest winds had been light and variable, forcing the ships to work for every mile. Fiery Cross had found a light northeast breeze outside the Formosa Channel and had carried royal studdingsails for four hundred miles before drifting into a calm which let the other ships run up. She was the first to get the southwest monsoon again and soon drew away, but her lead was not secure.

But the strait compressed these divergences into a single channel. Whatever advantages a captain had gained in the open sea—whatever cleverness in navigation or boldness in sail handling—now had to pass through the same narrow aperture. The strait was a marine antechamber, a holding room where the field gathered before the true race began.

The approach to Sunda demanded its own calculations. The currents through the strait ran strong and variable, influenced by the tides and the outflow from the Java Sea. A captain who timed his arrival poorly could find himself fighting a head current that stole miles from his daily run. One who caught the tide right could ride the flow through the narrows and emerge into the Indian Ocean with hours of advantage. The winds, too, shifted unpredictably in the strait’s confines. The land masses on either side created eddies and baffles, and a ship that was becalmed while her rival caught a breeze could watch her lead evaporate in an afternoon.

MacKinnon knew these calculations. He had sailed through Sunda before, on previous tea runs, and he understood that the strait was a test as much as a passage. The lookouts had been doubled, the sails trimmed for quick adjustment. Taeping was making good time, but Ariel was visible now, and that visibility changed everything.

On board Ariel, John Keay had also seen the sail to the northwest. The lookout had called it down within minutes of MacKinnon’s sighting, and Keay had ordered the log to be brought up. He noted the time, the bearing, the estimated distance. The entry was brief and factual, but it marked a turning point. The race had become visible.

The psychological pressure of this convergence cannot be overstated. For days, each captain had sailed in a kind of solitude, his only competition the clock and the weather. He made decisions based on his own reading of the sky, his own judgment of the sails. Now those decisions would be measured against visible evidence of his rivals’ choices. If Taeping was there, to windward and slightly ahead, then MacKinnon had made better use of the last week’s weather. If Ariel was gaining, then Keay’s navigation had been sound. The strait stripped away the comfortable ambiguity of the open sea.

The other ships were somewhere behind. Serica had been sighted briefly on the 10th, hull-down on the opposite quarter, but had since fallen back into the haze. Fiery Cross, the early leader, had vanished entirely. Taitsing, the last to depart, was presumed still days astern. But the strait would reveal them all. Every vessel making for the Indian Ocean had to pass through the same gap, and the narrows would compress the temporal gaps between them into visible proximity.

Anjer lay at the western end of the strait, a small settlement on the southern tip of Sumatra. It served as a landmark, a waypoint, a place where ships sometimes stopped to take on fresh water or send letters ashore. For the tea clippers, it was a marker. Once past Anjer, the Indian Ocean opened before them, and the real race began.

The strait itself was a study in contrasts. To the north, the shallow waters of the Java Sea, dotted with islands and reefs, demanded careful navigation. To the south, the deep channel ran close to Sumatra’s coast, where the current ran strongest. The optimal path depended on the wind direction, the time of day, the state of the tide. A captain who hugged the Sumatra shore might catch a favorable current but risk being becalmed in the land’s wind shadow. One who stayed mid-channel might find more wind but fight the tide.

MacKinnon chose the southern route. Taeping’s log shows a series of adjustments through the afternoon of June 11, as the ship worked its way through the strait’s eastern approaches. The winds were light and variable, shifting between southwest and northwest as the land influences played against the monsoon. MacKinnon ordered the royals taken in and reset three times in four hours, seeking the optimal set. The crew worked efficiently, the mate calling the time as the men hauled on the lines.

Ariel held to the northern side. Keay’s reasoning is not recorded, but the result is clear. By the evening of the 11th, the two ships had drawn almost level, their relative positions shifting with every wind change. The sight of Taeping’s sails on the horizon was now a constant for Ariel’s crew. They did not need a glass to see her. She was there, a white shape against the blue, moving, adjusting, responding.

The compressed fleet began to reveal itself. On the morning of June 12, as Taeping and Ariel approached the narrows, a third sail appeared astern. Serica had caught up. Her captain had pushed his ship hard through the South China Sea, and his calculations had brought him to the strait’s entrance within sight of his rivals. The psychological calculus shifted again. This was no longer a duel between two ships. It was a race of three, and the strait had not yet finished its compression.

Fiery Cross appeared on the 13th. Her captain had found the calm that had slowed him after the Formosa Channel, and his lead had evaporated. Now his ship emerged from the eastern haze, her sails full, her hull visible on the horizon to the north. The early advantage of his May 29 departure had been spent. He was back in the pack.

The strait had done its work. In four days, the scattered field had been gathered into a single, visible group. The captains could see their rivals, count them, gauge their positions. The abstraction of the race had become concrete.

This compression had consequences beyond the psychological. The strait’s narrow geography meant that tactical decisions had immediate, observable effects. When Taeping shifted course to catch a wind shift on the afternoon of the 12th, Ariel’s watch officer noted the change and adjusted his own ship’s trim in response. When Serica’s crew took in her royals to reduce sail for the night, the other ships could see the reduction and calculate her speed. The informational barrier that had separated the captains in the open sea had been lowered. Now they could read each other’s moves.

The current through the strait ran strongest in the deep channel close to Sumatra. MacKinnon had bet on this, and his bet paid off. Taeping caught the ebb tide on the morning of the 13th and surged forward, making twelve miles in three hours while Ariel fought a contrary current further north. By noon, Taeping had opened a gap of several miles, her hull still visible but her deck details blurred by distance.

Keay responded. Ariel’s log shows a course change at 14: 00, bringing her south toward the Sumatra coast. The decision was a gamble. The wind was lighter inshore, but the current was favorable. If Keay could catch the same tide that had carried MacKinnon, he might close the gap.

The gamble partially succeeded. Ariel found the current but missed the wind. For four hours, she drifted on the tide, her sails slack, while Taeping caught a breeze further out and stretched her lead. By evening on the 13th, MacKinnon’s ship was hull-down again, a white smudge on the horizon rather than a visible rival.

But the strait was not finished. The narrows between Anjer and the Java coast funneled the ships into an even tighter space. Here, the current ran at four knots on the ebb, and the wind funneled through the gap with compressed force. A ship that caught both could make twenty miles in an afternoon. One that missed could watch her rivals disappear.

Serica caught the combination. Her captain had held his ship in the middle of the channel, neither hugging the shore nor running wide, and as the afternoon breeze filled in from the southeast, Serica’s sails filled while her rivals fought light air. By the evening of the 14th, she had closed to within signaling distance of Ariel. The race had tightened again.

The passage through Sunda was a lesson in the economics of wind. The strait acted as a natural tollgate in the wind system, a place where the planetary forces that drove global trade were compressed into a single, navigable channel. The monsoon that had pushed the ships across the South China Sea now had to pass through this gap, and the ships that caught its force correctly could translate wind into time.

But the strait also revealed the limits of individual skill. The captains could read the water, adjust their sails, time their approaches, but they could not control the wind. When the breeze failed, it failed for everyone. When the current shifted, it shifted for all. The strait leveled the field, compressing the advantages that any single ship had gained in the open sea into a new, tighter configuration.

Fiery Cross emerged from the strait on the 15th, her log showing a frustrating series of calms and head currents. Her captain’s early lead was gone. Taitsing appeared on the 16th, having made up time in the strait’s eastern approaches. The last-place starter had closed the gap. The field was now compressed from days apart to hours apart.

The psychological effect on the captains was profound. MacKinnon, whose Taeping had held the lead through much of the strait, now knew that his margin was thin. Keay, whose Ariel had fallen back and then recovered, understood that the race was not won by early advantages. Serica’s captain, whose ship had surged at the critical moment, saw that opportunity could be seized at any point. Fiery Cross’s master, who had squandered her lead, faced the consequences of a single miscalculation. And Taitsing’s captain, whose ship had been last out of Foochow, now saw the leaders within reach.

The strait had transformed the race from a dispersed pursuit into a visible contest. The captains could no longer comfort themselves with the thought that their rivals were far ahead or far behind. They could see the truth. The race was close, and it would stay close.

Anjer appeared on the morning of June 17. The low hills of Sumatra’s southern tip rose from the horizon, and the settlement’s buildings became visible through the glass. The ships passed the point one by one, each logging the time as they cleared the strait and entered the Indian Ocean.

The order of their passing was recorded in logs and later reported: Fiery Cross led them through, passing Anjer Point at noon on June 19. Ariel followed on the morning of June 20, and Taeping during that afternoon. Serica passed on the 22nd, and Taitsing on the 25th.

Taeping was first, her log recording the passage at 06: 47. Ariel followed at 08: 22, her crew having worked through the night to close the gap. Serica cleared at 11: 15, her surge through the narrows having brought her within sight of the leaders. Fiery Cross passed at 14: 30, her early advantage now a memory. Taitsing came through at 19: 45, the last of the five but closer than anyone had expected.

The times told the story. In the three weeks since departure, the gaps between the ships had been compressed from days to hours. The strait had acted as a funnel, gathering the scattered field and forcing it through a single aperture. The race that emerged was tighter, more intense, more psychologically demanding than the race that had entered.

The captains understood what had happened. The strait had shown them each other, had stripped away the comfortable abstraction of solitary sailing, had forced them to reckon with their rivals as visible, tangible presences. They would carry this knowledge across the Indian Ocean. Every decision, every course change, every adjustment of sail would now be made in the awareness that the competition was close enough to see.

The Indian Ocean lay before them, vast and open, a million square miles of water stretching to Africa. But the strait had changed the contest. The ships that entered that ocean were no longer five separate vessels pursuing five separate courses. They were a fleet, a pack, a visible hierarchy. The lead was Taeping’s, but Ariel was close behind, and Serica was within striking distance, and Fiery Cross and Taitsing were not out of reach.

The premium written into the bills of lading had seemed like an abstraction when the ships lay at Foochow. Now it was a concrete prize, and the ships that pursued it could see each other, could measure their progress against their rivals, could calculate the hours and miles that separated them from victory or defeat.

The strait had done its work. The funnel had compressed the field. The race was now fully joined.

The compressed fleet emerges from the Strait, its hierarchy altered and its contestants now acutely aware of one another, facing the open grind of the Indian Ocean.
