Chapter 12

The First Lookout’s Cry

Dated September 5, 1866, the Lloyd’s signal station ledger recorded a timed entry at 2: 47 PM. The clerk, having watched two ships draw near against the gray horizon, noted first their course and sail, then, upon closer identification, inscribed the names Ariel and Taeping. The incomplete telegraph form on his desk held the brief chronicle of a race’s end. The ships had been sighted running in for the Lizard, about five miles apart, beam and beam, steering on slightly converging courses.

The message traveled north through the Cornwall relay stations, dots and dashes flowing along the copper wire toward London. By evening it reached the Lloyd’s listening post at Falmouth, where the shipping intelligence was collated and dispatched to the counting houses. The tea fleet was coming. Two ships, close together, racing up the Channel. The betting at Lloyd’s Coffee House, dormant since the June departure reports from Foochow, flickered back to life.

The London tea trade had been waiting for this signal for three months. The Pall Mall Gazette on June 11 had named the starters: Fiery Cross, Ariel, Taeping, and Serica. Sixteen clippers had ridden high in the Min River that May, waiting for the tea to come down from the hills. The competition among them had generated its own market in wagers. But the telegraph had transformed speculation. The ships were still in the Atlantic, but their progress had become information, flowing ahead of them toward Mincing Lane.

The bill of lading had established the terms. A premium of ten shillings per ton would go to the first vessel to dock with the new season’s tea. The clause was brief, but it had launched five ships within three days of each other at the end of May. Fiery Cross departed Foochow on the 29th. Ariel, Taeping, and Serica sailed on the 30th. Taitsing followed at midnight. The race had begun with pilots dropping away in the Min River. It would end here, in the English Channel, under the eyes of signal stations and pilots and merchants who had committed capital to the outcome.

John Keay had been awake since midnight. The captain of Ariel stood on the quarterdeck in the darkness, watching the eastern horizon. His ship had been making good speed through the night, carrying every stitch of canvas she could bear. The southwest breeze was not strong but steady, and Ariel had been riding it like a vessel scenting port. At 1: 30 in the morning of September 5, the lookout’s cry came down from the masthead. Land. The Bishop Light, bearing northeast. With all possible sail set, she sped toward the mouth of the English Channel.

Keay ordered the course adjusted. The mouth of the English Channel lay ahead, and beyond it the North Sea and the final run toward London. He had sailed this route for fifteen years. He knew every current, every tidal gate, every shift of wind that could make or break a passage. The Bishop Light told him he was on time. His calculations in the cabin, the daily runs logged in his notebook, the noon sights—all of it pointed to a landfall off the Lizard on the fifth or sixth of September. The arithmetic had held.

What the arithmetic could not tell him was where his competitors were. He had not seen another tea clipper since the Atlantic, when Taeping had been a speck on the horizon, hull-down and falling behind. Keay had pushed Ariel hard through the night watches, driving her through squalls and calms, trusting his ship and crew to find wind where other captains might have shortened sail. The premium was not won by caution.

The dawn came gray and wet. Keay was still on deck, his oilskins slick with spray, when the lookout called down again. Sail on the starboard quarter. The captain raised his glass and trained it on the distant shape. A ship, making all sail, steering the same course. Even at this distance, through the morning haze, he could see the set of her sails and the rake of her masts. Captain Keay later said “Instinct told me that it was the Taeping,” and he was right. Donald MacKinnon’s ship had closed the gap in the night.

The two ships raced up the Channel now, within sight of each other, within sight of the English coast. The telegraph stations would be watching, sending reports ahead to London. The merchants would be waking to the news. The pilots would be preparing their cutters. The captains pushed their ships as hard as they dared, knowing that every mile counted, knowing that the premium would go to the first ship to dock, knowing that the difference between first and second might be measured in minutes.

The southwest wind that carried the clippers had been blowing for a week. It was fair for a ship making for London, but not strong. The captains would have to work for every knot, find the currents and ride them, avoid the calms and fight through the squalls. Decisions came in real time, with no second chances.

The telegraph reports followed the ships. By afternoon, the signal station at the Lizard had logged their passage. By evening, the news had reached London. Ariel and Taeping, in sight of each other, racing up the Channel. The betting at Lloyd’s tightened. The merchants who had consigned their tea to one ship or the other watched the prices move. The premium remained uncertain, but the field had narrowed.

The newspapers picked up the story the next morning. The Times carried a brief notice in its shipping column: the tea clippers Ariel and Taeping had been sighted off the Lizard, making for the Channel, and the first cargo of the new season was expected within days. The market, sluggish through the summer, stirred. The tea auctions were scheduled for autumn, and the first lots to arrive would set the tone. The merchants who had bought forward contracts, who had committed to prices based on the assumption of early arrival, calculated their positions. The premium was a prize for shipowners, but it was also a signal for the entire trade.

Keay pushed Ariel through the night of September 5, driving her hard on a starboard tack, trying to squeeze every fraction of speed from the dying breeze. MacKinnon matched him, keeping Taeping within sight, never falling behind, never gaining ground. The two ships were like riders in a horse race, neck and neck, neither willing to yield. A strong west-southwesterly wind carried these two ships up the channel at 14 knots. The Lizard was abeam at 8: 00 am and Start Point at noon. They were off Portland towards 6: 00 pm and St. Catherine’s Point was due north at 7: 25 pm. Beachy Head was abeam just after midnight. The relative positions of the two ships barely shifted all this while – Ariel kept her lead.

At 3: 00 in the morning of September 6, Keay signaled for a pilot. Ariel was approaching Dungeness, and the tide was turning. He needed a pilot to take him through the Thames estuary and up to the docks. At 4: 00, he hove to and began firing rockets and flares, the traditional signals for a pilot in the dark. The flares arced up from Ariel’s deck, bursting over the water, illuminating the ship’s rigging and the white foam of her wake. Taeping, also signalling for a pilot, was coming up fast and was close astern of Ariel at 5: 00 am.

MacKinnon was doing the same. There was no sign that Taeping would heave to, so Captain Keay ordered Ariel’s sails to be filled to keep ahead of the other ship, to be sure of getting the first pilot. On Taeping, Captain MacKinnon conceded and also hove to. At 5: 55 am, the pilot arrived on board Ariel. He saluted Captain Keay with congratulations at being the first ship from China that season. He got the reply “Yes, and what is that to the westward? We have not room to boast yet.” At 6: 00 am, both ships were under way again.

The pilot cutter Magpie was the first to reach them. Her captain, a man named Callender, had been watching the flares and calculating his approach. He came alongside Taeping first, MacKinnon’s ship being slightly closer to shore. The pilot jumped from the cutter to the clipper’s deck, and Taeping turned toward the Thames. But Ariel was not far behind. Callender hailed another pilot, a man named Smith, and directed him toward Ariel. Smith came alongside Keay’s ship and boarded her, and Ariel turned toward the Thames as well.

The two ships were now under pilotage, making their way up the estuary with the flood tide. The wind was still southwest, light but fair, and the tide was with them. They would make London on the same tide, docking within hours of each other. The question was which one would dock first.

The Thames was busy with shipping. Colliers from Newcastle, steamers from continental ports, coastal traders making their way to the Pool of London—the river was a highway of commerce, and the tea clippers were two more vessels among thousands. But they were the first of the new season, the ships that would set the market, the ships that had raced across the world to be here. The crowds on the riverbanks watched them pass. Dockworkers paused in their labors. Merchants in counting houses looked up from their ledgers. The telegraph had prepared them. The ships were coming.

The docks at London were the terminus of a commercial system that stretched halfway around the world. The tea that Ariel and Taeping carried had been picked in the hills of Fujian, processed in the factories of Foochow, loaded into chests and stowed in the holds of the clippers. It had crossed the China Sea and the Indian Ocean, rounded the Cape of Good Hope and traversed the Atlantic, passed through the English Channel and up the Thames to the warehouses of the East India Docks. The journey had taken ninety-nine days. The premium clause had set the terms. The race was about to end.

Taeping reached the dock first. MacKinnon’s ship came alongside the East India Dock at low water on the morning of September 6, her sails furled, her lines made fast, her cargo ready to unload. Twenty minutes later, Ariel docked beside her. The two ships had left Foochow within hours of each other on May 30. They had arrived in London within hours of each other on September 6. They had raced across the world and finished within sight of each other, within the same tide, within the same morning. After 99 days and almost 16, 000 miles (about 25, 750 km) the leaders were still tied and raced within sight of each other the full length of the English Channel and into the Thames.

But the owners had foreseen this possibility. The race had been so close, the margins so narrow, that a disputed result was almost inevitable. The consignees, fearing that anything resembling a dead heat might lead the underwriters to call the race void—there being no outright winner—and refuse payment, had agreed in advance that whichever ship docked first would claim the premium and share it with the second. The agreement had been made before the ships left China, a recognition that the race might end too close to call.

In 1866, there was a problem – the first cargo of tea had arrived over two weeks earlier in the steam auxiliary ship Erl King, a ship that was not considered part of the race. The tea merchants were committed to a payment when their tea would sell at a loss.

To add to this, the first two tea clippers had signalled off Deal early on the morning of 6 September, followed by a third (Serica) at &nbsp; 1: 00 pm. These three ships would create a glut of new season tea, so prices were bound to fall further.

The agreement was honored. Taeping claimed the premium, and the owners of Taeping and Ariel split it between them. The merchants who had consigned their tea to either ship received their cargo, and the market adjusted to the new supply. The first lots of the new season’s tea would go to auction, and prices would be set. The premium had served its purpose. It had driven the ships across the world at maximum speed, and it had delivered the first tea to London in record time. Taeping divided her winnings of 10 shillings per ton with the owners of Ariel and Captain MacKinnon divided the captain’s £100 with Captain Keay.

The third ship, Serica, arrived that night. Captain Innes had pushed his ship hard, but he had not been able to match the pace of the leaders. Serica docked about two hours after Taeping and Ariel, still within the same tide, still among the first of the season. The fourth ship, Fiery Cross, arrived twenty-eight hours later. The fifth, Taitsing, arrived the day after that. The race had been a contest among five ships, but it had been won by two.

The newspapers reported the finish the next day. The Times noted that Taeping had docked twenty minutes ahead of Ariel, with Serica arriving that night. The close finish, the paper observed, was evidence of the skill of the captains and the quality of the ships. The premium had been split, the cargo was being unloaded, and the tea would soon be at auction. The race was over.

But the race was a commercial transaction, a mechanism for delivering a commodity to a market at a specific time. The premium had been the incentive, and the ships had responded. They had sailed faster, pushed harder, taken more risks, because the premium had made it worth their while. The market had set the terms, and the market had received its delivery.

The telegraph had followed the ships from the Lizard to London, and the newspapers had followed the telegraph. The public had watched the race unfold in the shipping columns, a daily chronicle of sightings and reports. The merchants had watched the market, calculating positions, adjusting expectations. The underwriters had watched the risks, assessing the likelihood of loss, setting their premiums accordingly. The whole apparatus of the tea trade—the growers in Fujian, the merchants in Foochow, the shipowners in London, the auctioneers in Mincing Lane—had been oriented toward this moment.

The finish of the 1866 tea race was the logical conclusion of a system that prized narrow margins above all. The premium had been set at ten shillings per ton, a significant sum but not a fortune. The ships had raced for it anyway, because the premium was money, but it was also reputation. It was proof of speed, of reliability, of commercial value. A ship that won the premium could command higher freight rates in future seasons. A captain who won the premium could write his own terms. The race was about the money, and it was about position.

The narrowness of the finish was itself a product of the system. The ships had been built for speed, their hulls designed to cut through the water with minimum resistance, their rigs designed to carry maximum sail. The captains had been selected for their willingness to push, to take risks, to drive hard. The crews had been hired for skill and endurance. The tea had been loaded with care, stowed tight to prevent damage, the chests arranged to allow rapid unloading. Every element of the operation had been optimized for speed, because speed was the variable that mattered.

The fact that Ariel and Taeping finished within twenty minutes of each other was not coincidence. A competitive process had pushed both ships to their limits. The captains had matched each other move for move, tack for tack, sail for sail. They had sailed the same route, encountered the same weather, faced the same choices. And they had arrived at the same destination at almost the same time. The race had been so close because the ships were so evenly matched, and the ships were so evenly matched because the market had selected for speed.

The owners of Taeping and Ariel had agreed to split the premium before the ships left China. They had recognized that a disputed result would be bad for business, that a fight over the prize would damage the reputation of both ships and undermine the confidence of the merchants. The agreement was a recognition that the race was not a zero-sum game. The premium was a bonus, not the main event. The main event was the delivery of the tea, and both ships had delivered.

The arrival of Serica that night, and of Fiery Cross and Taitsing in the following days, completed the picture. The five ships that had left Foochow within three days of each other had arrived in London within three days of each other. The race had been close throughout, and the finish had been the closest of all.

The merchants who had consigned their tea to Taeping and Ariel could be satisfied. Their cargo had arrived first, their tea would be the first to auction, their prices would set the market. The merchants who had consigned to Serica could be satisfied too. Their tea would arrive in time for the autumn auctions, and the quality would be good. The merchants who had consigned to Fiery Cross and Taitsing would have to wait, but their tea would arrive eventually, and the market would absorb it.

The pilots who had brought Taeping and Ariel up the Thames had done their job. Callender and Smith had boarded the ships in the dark, navigated them through the estuary, and brought them to the dock. They had been part of the race too, their skill and local knowledge essential to the final leg. The dockworkers who unloaded the tea, the clerks who logged the arrivals, the carters who moved the chests to the warehouses—all were part of the system, their labor contributing to the delivery.

The premium had been paid, the tea delivered, the race concluded. But the consequences were just beginning. The finish of the 1866 tea race would be remembered as one of the closest in history, a contest so tight that the ships had docked within minutes of each other. The story would be told and retold in the shipping columns, in the memoirs of the captains, in the lore of the tea trade. The race would become a legend, a symbol of the age of sail.

But the legend would obscure the mechanism. The race was a story of a commercial system that had set an incentive and watched the market respond. The premium had been the catalyst, the ships the instrument, the tea the commodity. The race had been a transaction, a delivery, a fulfillment of a contract. The drama had been real, but it had been produced by the logic of the market.

The ships that had raced in 1866 were the product of decades of development. The tea clipper was a specialized vessel, designed for speed and capacity, optimized for the China trade. The yards that built them, the owners that commissioned them, the captains that sailed them—all had contributed to the creation of a fleet capable of racing across the world in less than a hundred days. The premium had been the final element, the incentive that turned a commercial voyage into a contest.

The telegraph had changed the nature of the race. The ships had been tracked from the moment they were sighted off the English coast, their progress reported in real time to the merchants in London. The betting at Lloyd’s had flickered and shifted with each report. The public had followed the race in the newspapers, a daily drama played out in the shipping columns. The telegraph had made the race a spectator sport, a public event followed by thousands who would never see the ships or taste the tea.

The arrival of Ariel and Taeping on the same tide, within twenty minutes of each other, was the climax of a narrative that had begun ninety-nine days earlier in the Min River. The ships had left Foochow on the same day, May 30, 1866. They had sailed across the China Sea, through the Sunda Strait, across the Indian Ocean, around the Cape of Good Hope, across the Atlantic, through the English Channel, and up the Thames to the docks. They had encountered the same weather, the same currents, the same challenges. And they had arrived at the same destination at almost the same moment.

The closeness of the finish was the product of a competitive process that had pushed both ships to their limits. The captains had matched each other, the crews had worked to exhaustion, the ships had been driven hard. The premium had been the incentive, and the race had been the result.

The tea that Ariel and Taeping carried would be unloaded, weighed, sampled, and auctioned. The prices would be set, the contracts fulfilled, the profits calculated. The merchants would move on to the next season, the next cargo, the next race. The ships would be refitted, reprovisioned, sent back to China for another load. The cycle would continue, year after year, until steamers made clippers obsolete and the telegraph made the premium irrelevant.

But for now, in September 1866, the race was over, and the system had delivered. The first lookout’s cry had marked the beginning of the end, the moment when the ships came within sight of land and the final sprint began. The telegraph had carried the news, the newspapers had spread the word, the merchants had prepared their bids. The ships had docked, the tea had arrived, the premium had been paid.
