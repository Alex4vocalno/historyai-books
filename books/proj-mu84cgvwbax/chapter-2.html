Chapter 2

The Logbook’s First Page

John Keay stood on Ariel’s quarterdeck and watched the Foochow pilot’s boat pull away toward the Pagoda Anchorage. The morning of May 30, 1866, offered a light northeast breeze, barely enough to fill the royals. Behind him, the anchorage still held the vessels that had not yet loaded: hulls waiting at their moorings, their captains impatient for the tea chests to come down from the hills. Keay could not see his rivals yet, but he knew where they were. Taeping and Serica had dropped their pilots within twenty minutes of each other that same morning. Fiery Cross had sailed the previous day, May 29. Taitsing would follow at midnight on May 31.

The logbook lay open on the cabin table below. The first entry would record the departure, the wind, the sail configuration, and the ship’s position. These details, entered daily at noon, would become the documentary record of the race. But the log was more than a register. It was the instrument by which owners in London would measure their investment, by which underwriters at Lloyd’s would assess risk, and by which competing captains would gauge their rivals’ performance through the newspapers that published abstracts. The first entry started the market countdown.

Keay went below and recorded the departure. Light northeast breeze. Royal studdingsails set. The simplicity of the entry concealed the complexity of the decision it recorded. A light northeast breeze in late May meant the summer monsoon had not yet established itself firmly. The prevailing wind should have been southwest, blowing hard against ships trying to beat their way south through the Formosa Strait. But the weather was in transition. The northeast breeze was a gift, temporary and fragile. The captain who pressed his ship hardest in these first hours might gain miles that would compound across the next three months.

On Taeping, Captain MacKinnon faced the same calculation. His departure at 10:50 a.m. Mirrored Keay’s timing, with light winds and all sail set to royals. But MacKinnon chose a different course. Where Keay stood out toward the eastern edge of the Formosa Strait, keeping the deep water under his keel, MacKinnon hugged the coast of Formosa. The inshore route offered shelter from the worst of the monsoon when it came, but it also risked calms in the lee of the mountains and the danger of hidden currents.

Serica’s captain, George Innes, split the difference. His track ran between Ariel’s offshore line and Taeping’s inshore gambit. Three ships, three judgments, all made within hours of each other in waters where local knowledge mattered more than ship design.

The Formosa Strait was a bottleneck through which every tea clipper had to pass. To the east lay the island of Formosa, its mountains rising to twelve thousand feet, creating a wind shadow that could trap a ship for days. To the west lay the Chinese coast, with its maze of shoals and the treacherous outflow of the Min River. Between them ran the channel, forty miles wide at its narrowest, where the monsoon funneled with particular violence. A ship caught in the strait when the southwest monsoon blew full could spend a week beating against headwinds, watching its competitors disappear over the horizon.

Fiery Cross had left on May 29, a day before Ariel and Taeping. Captain Robinson knew the strait better than any man in the trade. He had sailed it for years, had learned its currents and its calms, and had won the premium twice before in earlier seasons. His decision to leave a day early was itself a calculation: the weather window was closing, and he preferred to take his chances with light winds rather than wait and risk a full monsoon. By the morning of May 30, Fiery Cross was already south of the Pagoda Anchorage, hull down below the horizon.

The race’s first hours established a pattern that would hold for the next three months. Each captain made decisions based on incomplete information: the weather he saw from his own deck, the sails he could spot on the horizon, the memory of previous voyages. The logbook recorded the result, but it could not capture the reasoning. That remained locked in the captain’s mind, revealed only through the track he chose.

On the second day out, May 31, the wind shifted. The light northeast breeze died in the morning, and by afternoon a southerly had begun to build. Keay recorded the wind veering south and freshening. He ordered more sail made. The monsoon was asserting itself earlier than expected. Ariel was still north of Formosa, and the southerly wind meant she would have to beat against it. Keay ordered the watches to trim the yards, bringing the ship onto a starboard tack that would take her southeast toward the open Pacific before she could turn south again.

Taeping faced the same shift from a different position. MacKinnon’s inshore route had taken him closer to Formosa, where the mountain range created a wind shadow. The southerly built later there, giving him a few more hours of favorable breeze. His log for May 31 showed a better day’s run than Ariel’s, closing the gap that Keay’s early departure had opened.

Serica, running the middle track, found the worst of both. Too far offshore to benefit from Formosa’s shelter, too close inshore to clear the channel quickly, she spent the day in flukey winds that shifted unpredictably. The log recorded a series of sail changes as Innes tried to find a consistent breeze. Each change cost time, each tack lost miles.

Taitsing left Foochow at midnight on May 31. Captain Nutsford brought his ship out into a building monsoon, the wind now firmly established from the southwest. His departure was the latest of the five, and the conditions were the worst. Where the earlier departures had caught the last of the favorable weather, Taitsing sailed into the teeth of the monsoon from the first hour.

The logbooks tell the story in compressed form. A log entry is a single line: position, wind, course, sail configuration. But behind each line lies a watch’s worth of decisions. The mate on deck calls for a sail to be set or taken in. The men climb the rigging, work the ropes, trim the yards. The captain walks the quarterdeck, watching the wind, the sails, the horizon. He looks for other ships. He looks for signs of weather changing. He thinks about the competition and the premium and the owners who have entrusted him with their capital.

The loan agreements that financed these voyages were structured around predictable durations. A tea clipper was expected to make the passage from Foochow to London in about a hundred days. The premium for the first arrival created an additional incentive, but the basic economics of the trade depended on the regularity of the wind. Ships sailed on schedules determined by the monsoon. They loaded when the tea was ready, departed when the weather allowed, and arrived when the winds brought them. The capital tied up in the cargo earned its return only when the tea reached the London market. This was wind capitalism: an economic system where profit depended on the reliable patterns of planetary weather.

The first three days of the 1866 race revealed how little reliability there was in the transition season. The weather window that had allowed Fiery Cross to depart on May 29, Ariel and Taeping and Serica on May 30, and Taitsing on May 31 was narrower than anyone had calculated. By June 1, the monsoon had established itself. The southwest wind blew steady at twenty knots, and the ships were beating into it.

Keay’s log for June 1 recorded strong southwest winds, close-hauled on the starboard tack, with a heavy sea. Ariel was making progress, but slowly. The ship’s best point of sailing was a reach, with the wind abeam, her elegant hull designed to slide through the water with minimal resistance. Close-hauled, she could point high, closer to the wind than most ships, but every degree of angle cost speed. Keay was trading distance for direction, trying to make southing while the wind pushed him east.

MacKinnon on Taeping had chosen well. The inshore route had taken him into the lee of Formosa, where the mountains blocked the worst of the monsoon. His log for June 1 showed a better day’s run than Ariel’s, with smoother water and less wind. Taeping was closing the gap.

Robinson on Fiery Cross was ahead of them all. His day’s lead had become three days’ advantage by June 1, the ship already clear of the Formosa Strait and running south through the South China Sea. He had sailed in the last hours of the favorable breeze and had used the transition period to build a lead. The logs of the other captains would not record his position—they could not see him—but they knew he was there, somewhere over the horizon, making miles while they fought the wind.

The abstract of the logs, published later in the newspapers, would reduce these first days to a series of numbers. Ariel: 21 days from Foochow to Anjer. Taeping: 21 days. Serica: 23 days. Fiery Cross: 21 days. Taitsing: 26 days. The numbers suggest a close race, with most ships reaching the Sunda Strait within two days of each other. But the numbers conceal the drama of those first seventy-two hours, when positions were established and gaps opened.

Fiery Cross reached Anjer, at the western exit of the Sunda Strait, on June 19. Ariel and Taeping arrived on June 20. Serica on June 22. Taitsing on June 25. The pattern established in the Formosa Strait had held. Fiery Cross’s early departure and superior local knowledge had given her a lead that lasted through the entire South China Sea passage. Ariel and Taeping, despite their different tactics, arrived at Anjer on the same day. Serica, caught between routes, lost two days. Taitsing, sailing into the full monsoon from the start, lost five.

The premium clause in the charter parties created a structure that shaped every decision. Ten shillings per ton for the first ship to dock in London with the new season’s tea. On a cargo of a thousand tons, that was five hundred pounds—a significant sum, but not the only incentive. The real value of winning was the reputation. A captain who brought the first tea could command higher wages and choose his ships. An owner whose vessel won could negotiate better freight rates for the next season. The premium was a fraction of the total profit, but it concentrated attention.

The five captains in the 1866 race were experienced men. Robinson had won the premium twice before. Keay was known as a careful navigator who pushed his ships hard. MacKinnon had a reputation for aggressive sailing. Innes and Nutsford were less famous but equally determined. Each had his own style, his own approach to the trade, his own relationship with his owners and crew.

The logbooks recorded their decisions without explaining them. A course change, a sail configuration, a position at noon. The reasoning behind each entry was invisible, recoverable only through the pattern of choices over time. Keay’s track showed a preference for offshore routes, deep water, and steady winds. MacKinnon’s showed a willingness to risk the inshore passage for the chance of calms and currents. Robinson’s showed a deep knowledge of the China coast, a willingness to sail early and accept risk for the chance of a lead.

The first three days of the race established an early form that would be tested over the next three months. Fiery Cross led, having sailed earliest and chosen the best route through the strait. Ariel and Taeping followed, close together, their different tactics having canceled each other out. Serica trailed, having chosen poorly. Taitsing brought up the rear, having sailed into the worst conditions.

But the race was not won in these first days. The South China Sea was a proving ground, not a finish line. The ships would have to navigate the Anambas and Natuna islands, thread the Sunda Strait between Sumatra and Java, cross the Indian Ocean, round the Cape of Good Hope, and beat north through the Atlantic. The lead that Fiery Cross established in the first week would be challenged, lost, and regained. The positions would shift as winds changed and luck intervened.

What the first three days established was a pattern of relentless pursuit. The captains knew where their rivals were, roughly, and they knew that every mile mattered. The premium concentrated their attention on speed, but the structure of the trade ensured that speed was always calculated against the competition. A fast passage was good; a faster passage than the other ships was better.

The logbooks became the medium through which the race was recorded and compared. Each captain kept his own log, entering the ship’s position and conditions at noon each day. When the ships reached port, the logs would be abstracted and published in the newspapers. Readers in London could follow the race day by day, comparing the positions and speeds of the different vessels. The logs turned the race into a public spectacle, a contest that could be followed and judged from half a world away.

The publication of the logs also served an economic function. Freight rates depended on a ship’s reputation for speed. A captain who had made a fast passage could negotiate better terms for his owners. The logs provided the evidence. They were the record by which the market valued the ships and the men who commanded them.

In the first three days of the 1866 race, the logs recorded the establishment of a competitive pattern that would hold for the entire passage. Fiery Cross led, Ariel and Taeping chased, Serica followed, Taitsing struggled. The gaps were small—a day here, a day there—but they were visible, measurable, and consequential. The captains knew the stakes. They pushed their ships and their crews, trading risk for speed, hoping that the weather would favor their choices.

The light northeast breeze that had allowed Ariel, Taeping, and Serica to depart on May 30 was a brief respite in the seasonal transition. By June 1, the monsoon had reasserted itself. The ships beat southward, close-hauled against the southwest wind, making southing by degrees. The Formosa Strait funneled the wind, increasing its force. The currents ran against them. Progress was slow.

Robinson on Fiery Cross had escaped the worst of it. His early departure had taken him south of the strait before the monsoon built. He was running free now, the wind abaft the beam, making miles while the others fought for each degree of latitude. His lead would prove crucial in the weeks ahead.

Keay on Ariel had accepted the gamble of the offshore route. He was east of the main channel, in deeper water but also in stronger wind. His ship was fast, designed to excel in these conditions. He pushed her hard, setting all sail, driving her southward. The log recorded the daily runs: 180 miles, 200 miles, 220 miles. Good numbers for a ship beating into the wind.

MacKinnon on Taeping had chosen the inshore route, and it was paying off. The Formosa mountains created a wind shadow that extended miles to leeward. Taeping sailed in lighter airs, making better progress than Ariel despite being closer to the wind. The two ships were invisible to each other, separated by the width of the strait, but their logs would later reveal how close they were in position and time.

Serica, running the middle track, found neither the deep water of Ariel’s course nor the shelter of Taeping’s. The log recorded frustration: variable winds, squalls, lightning to the south. Serica was losing ground.

Taitsing had the worst of it. The late departure meant sailing into the full monsoon from the first hour. Taitsing was a fast ship, well-designed and well-manned, but she could not overcome the weather. The log for June 1 recorded strong southwest gale conditions, close-reefed topsails, heavy sea. Taitsing was making southing, but slowly, each mile hard-won.

The China Sea was a confined space, its geography forcing the ships into narrow channels where tactics mattered more than pure speed. The Formosa Strait, the Sunda Strait, the approaches to Anjer—these were the places where captaincy and local knowledge created advantages. Once the ships reached the open Indian Ocean, the distances would flatten the competition. A faster ship would gain miles steadily, but a shrewd captain could steal an advantage in the coastal waters.

The race of 1866 would later be remembered for its dramatic finish, with Ariel and Taeping arriving in London on the same tide, separated by twenty minutes. But the race was won and lost in places like the Formosa Strait, where decisions made in the first hours compounded over ninety-nine days. The premium that the owners offered, ten shillings per ton, was a small fraction of the cargo’s value. But it concentrated attention, forced aggressive tactics, and created a public spectacle that sold newspapers and captured the imagination of a nation.

The captains knew this. They sailed not just for the premium but for the reputation, the future employment, the pride of being first. The logbooks recorded their efforts in dry prose: position, wind, course, sail. But behind each entry was a watch of anxious calculation, a day of pushing ship and crew to the limit, a night of watching the horizon for the lights of a rival.

The first three days established the pattern. The ships sorted themselves: Fiery Cross leading, Ariel and Taeping close behind, Serica trailing, Taitsing struggling. The gaps were small but visible. The pursuit was relentless. The logbooks had begun to record the race, and they would continue to record it, day by day, until the ships reached London or the race was lost.

On the evening of June 1, Keay stood on Ariel’s quarterdeck and watched the sun set into the China Sea. The wind was steady from the southwest, the ship close-hauled and making good progress. He could not see his rivals, but he knew where they were. Fiery Cross ahead, somewhere over the horizon. Taeping to the west, in the lee of Formosa. Serica behind, struggling with flukey winds. Taitsing far behind, fighting the full monsoon.

The logbook lay open below, the day’s entry complete. Strong southwest winds, close-hauled on starboard tack, heavy sea, 220 miles. It was a good day’s run, better than he had expected in these conditions. But it was not enough. Fiery Cross was ahead, and the premium was slipping away.

Keay turned and walked to the rail. The sea stretched out before him, dark and endless. Somewhere beyond that horizon lay London, and the market, and the premium that would make his reputation. But between here and there lay weeks of wind and water, of tactical decisions and calculated risks, of pushing ship and crew to the limit.

The first logbook pages recorded small but potentially decisive gaps, establishing a pattern of relentless pursuit as the fleet headed into the open South China Sea.
