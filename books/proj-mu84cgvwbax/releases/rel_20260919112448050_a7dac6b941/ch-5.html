Chapter 5

Into the Teeth of the Monsoon

The ships were loaded, their holds sealed, their decks cleared for sea. The tea chests sat in their tiers, a commercial proposition reduced to physical weight. The race that had begun in the counting houses of London now awaited the wind.

On the morning of May 29, 1866, Fiery Cross dropped her moorings and caught the ebb down the Min River. Captain Robinson had secured his cargo a day before the others, and he meant to make that advantage count. The river current carried the clipper past the Pagoda Anchorage, where Ariel, Taeping, and Serica still rode at their moorings. Taitsing would not depart until midnight on the 31st. Robinson had clear water ahead, and he intended to extend his lead into something decisive.

The Fiery Cross found a light northeast breeze outside, and passed through the Formosa Channel with royal studdingsails set, followed by the other four ships. They all carried this breeze for four hundred miles, when the Fiery Cross drifted into a calm which let the others close the gap. The advantage Robinson had fought to secure on the wharf began to dissolve almost immediately. The sea does not respect commercial priority. The wind answers to no bill of lading.

What followed in those first days of June revealed each captain’s philosophy of the sea. Every man faced the same water, the same weather, the same geography. Each chose a different path through them. The monsoon was coming, its southwest winds already stirring the South China Sea. How a captain positioned his ship to meet those winds—hugging the coast for slants and local breezes, or standing offshore where the heavier swell ran and the gusts blew longer and harder—would shape the entire race.

The premium of ten shillings per ton, written into every bill of lading, had created the race. The monsoon would decide who won it. Engaging the monsoon meant entering the capital circuit at its most elemental level. The tea chests locked in the holds represented time itself—time compressed into cargo, time that would dissolve into price differentials the moment the ships reached London. A faster passage translated directly into higher profit.

John Keay aboard Ariel understood this as well as any man in the trade. When Ariel dropped her pilot on the morning of May 30, trailing Taeping and Serica by mere minutes, Keay made his first significant decision of the race. Rather than follow Fiery Cross directly through the Formosa Channel, he stood further east, seeking clearer water and what he hoped would be more consistent breezes. The northeast wind that had carried Robinson south was already beginning to shift. Keay could feel it in the rigging.

Ariel’s log for those first days recorded runs that would have satisfied any owner in ordinary times. But this was not an ordinary passage. On June 2, following the coast of Annam, Keay sighted Taeping off his port quarter. The two ships had been at sea three days, and already they were within visual distance of each other. The proximity was both reassuring and maddening—reassuring because it confirmed that Ariel had not fallen behind, maddening because it meant Taeping had matched her pace almost exactly. Donald MacKinnon, Taeping’s master, had chosen a similar offshore route. The two captains were now locked in a duel that would last the entire passage.

The southwest monsoon brought conditions that tested the clippers at every level of their design. These ships were built for speed, not comfort. Their sharp entries and fine runs meant they drove through waves rather than riding over them. In heavy weather, the bow would plunge, sending sheets of spray across the forecastle. The decks ran constant wet. The crew lived in a state of perpetual dampness, their oilskins never quite dry, their hands raw from handling rope and canvas that had absorbed the tropical humidity.

The work never stopped. In light airs, the crew went aloft to set every stitch of canvas the masts could carry—royals, topgallants, studdingsails spread wide. When the wind freshened, they stripped the sails just as quickly, reefing down to preserve the spars and rigging. The monotony of the tropics could shift to a squall in minutes. A black line on the horizon meant men running to their stations, the boatswain’s pipe shrill above the wind, the captain’s voice steady on the quarterdeck. The sails had to come in before the gust hit, or the ship would lose a topmast. Then the squall would pass, and the crew would go aloft again to shake out the reefs.

The logbooks recorded this rhythm in their spare, functional prose. Daily runs were entered alongside wind direction and force. The language was uniform across the fleet because the conditions were uniform. Every captain faced the same monsoon. Every crew performed the same backbreaking labor. What differed was how each ship responded to the test.

Fiery Cross, the first to depart, had established an early lead. But Robinson’s choice to hug the coast carried risks. The inshore route offered protection from the full force of the monsoon, but it also meant dealing with variable breezes, land effects, and the constant need to navigate around headlands. The China coast was not empty. Fishing junks, coastal traders, and the occasional steamer all shared these waters. A clipper racing for London had to thread through them all, maintaining speed without running foul of some slow-moving junk in the night.

On June 8th, as recorded in the logs, the Fiery Cross and Ariel met on opposite tacks, both ships having a strong southwest breeze, and the Fiery Cross passed three miles to windward. She kept her lead through the Straits of Sunda, passing Anjer Point at noon on June 19th.

By the time the fleet reached the coast of Borneo, the monsoon was at full strength. On June 9 and 10, Ariel and Taeping sighted each other again, roughly 760 nautical miles from Foochow. The two ships had been racing for ten days, and still they remained in visual contact. Neither had broken clear. The monsoon had compressed the race, bringing the vessels together even as it drove them southward. The wind that should have scattered them had instead concentrated the competition.

Serica sailed somewhere to the east. Her captain had chosen a different tactical approach, standing further offshore than Keay or MacKinnon. The logic was sound: deeper water meant steadier breezes, fewer calms, less interference from land. But it also meant longer distances and greater exposure to the full fury of the monsoon. A ship too far offshore might catch stronger winds, but she also risked being driven past her intended waypoint. The navigation became more complex, the margins for error narrower.

Taitsing, the last to depart, had ground to make up. Her master had left Foochow nearly two days after Fiery Cross, and a full day after the main group of Ariel, Taeping, and Serica. In an ordinary race, that deficit would be insurmountable. But the monsoon did not reward the early starters automatically. The wind shifted, the calms settled, the squalls struck without warning. A ship that departed first might find herself parked in a windless patch while her rivals overtook her on either side.

The physical strain on the crews mounted with each day. A tea clipper carried thirty men, give or take, to handle every sail, every watch, every emergency. The work was divided into four-hour watches, with two hours of dog watch in the late afternoon to rotate the schedule. Men stood watch on watch, catching what sleep they could in the forecastle, where the air was thick with the smell of wet wool and tobacco smoke and the eternal damp that no amount of ventilation could clear.

The food offered little comfort. Salt beef and salt pork, preserved in barrels, were the staples. Hard bread—hardtack to the sailors—supplemented the meat, along with dried peas and beans that could be soaked and boiled into something resembling a meal. Fresh provisions lasted only a few days out of port. After that, the diet was a monotonous round of preserved rations, varied only by what the cook could manage with his limited ingredients and the rolling of the ship.

The officers fared somewhat better. The captain ate alone in his cabin, or occasionally invited the chief mate or the supercargo to join him. The mates and the boatswain took their meals in the roundhouse, a small space aft of the main hatch. Their food was the same as the crew’s, perhaps of slightly better quality, and they had the advantage of a table and the possibility of conversation. But the hierarchy of the ship meant that they could never fully relax. The captain was always the captain, even at dinner. The mates were always on call, their meals interrupted by any change in wind or weather.

The supercargo was a figure unique to the China trade. He was the owner’s representative, responsible for the cargo and the commercial aspects of the voyage. He kept the accounts, managed the bills of lading, and would oversee the sale of the tea once the ship reached London. On a tea clipper, the supercargo’s role was somewhat anomalous—the cargo was already loaded, the bills already signed. His main function was to ensure that nothing happened to the tea during the passage. He checked the holds regularly, monitoring the temperature and humidity, looking for any sign of leakage or spoilage.

For the sailors, the supercargo was another officer, another mouth that did not pull ropes or steer the ship. His presence reminded them what the race was really about. The tea in the hold was a specific quantity of a specific commodity, purchased at a specific price, to be sold at a specific time in a specific market. The supercargo carried the paperwork that connected the ship to that market. When the vessel docked, he would go ashore and the captain would remain aboard. Their worlds were complementary but distinct.

The monsoon forced a reckoning between these worlds. The wind that drove the ships south was the same wind that would determine the profit on the cargo. Time arbitrage, the profit captured by exploiting the time lag between markets, depended entirely on the vessel’s speed. A fast passage meant arriving when the market was still hungry, when the first teas of the season could command premium prices. A slow passage meant arriving into a glutted market, when prices had already settled and the premium was meaningless.

By mid-June, the fleet had settled into the pattern that would carry them through the South China Sea. Fiery Cross held the lead, but her margin had shrunk. The calm that had let the others close the gap had cost Robinson the advantage of his early departure. Ariel and Taeping continued their duel, never more than a few miles apart, matching each other tack for tack, reef for reef. Serica sailed to the east, her position known only from the logbooks that would not be compared until the ships reached London. Taitsing had closed some of her deficit, the later start offset by better winds in the first week.

The log entries from this period reveal the daily rhythm of the race. Runs of two hundred miles were common when the wind held steady. Runs of less than a hundred miles marked the days of calm or light airs. The monsoon was not a constant force. It built and eased, shifted and settled. A ship might make two hundred and fifty miles one day and eighty the next. The cumulative effect was what mattered. The total distance from Foochow to the Sunda Strait was roughly three thousand miles. At an average of two hundred miles per day, the passage would take fifteen days. But the monsoon rarely allowed such consistency.

The physical design of the clippers imposed its own logic on the race. These were extreme vessels, built for speed above all else. Their hulls were narrow, their bows sharp, their sterns fine. They carried an enormous spread of canvas—more sail area per ton of displacement than any other merchant vessels of their time. In the right conditions, a clipper could outsail almost anything afloat. In the wrong conditions, she could be a handful.

The captains knew their ships intimately. John Keay had commanded Ariel since her launch in 1865. He understood how she handled in light airs and heavy weather, how she responded to the helm, how she heeled under a full press of sail. This knowledge was not theoretical. It came from thousands of miles of ocean, from countless watches and maneuvers and close calls. A captain who did not know his ship was a danger to everyone aboard.

Donald MacKinnon had similar experience with Taeping. Keay and MacKinnon were the central figures in the race. Their ships were the fastest in the fleet, their skills the most closely matched. The rivalry between them was professional, not personal. They were both servants of the same commercial system, both pursuing the same premium, both subject to the same constraints. But the race had a way of making everything personal. The sight of the other ship on the horizon, gaining or losing ground, was a constant reminder of what was at stake.

The crew felt this pressure as well. The sailors on a tea clipper were participants in a race that would be reported in the London newspapers, discussed in the counting houses, celebrated or lamented in the taverns. They knew what the premium meant. They knew that their labor, their skill, their endurance would translate directly into the ship’s arrival time. The owners did not offer bonuses for fast passages—that was not the custom. But the pride of the ship, the reputation of the crew, the knowledge that they had beaten the other vessels—these were rewards of their own.

The monsoon tested that pride. The southwest wind brought heat and humidity that made every task harder. The tropical sun beat down on decks that held the heat long after sunset. The nights offered little relief. The air below decks was stifling, the air above thick with moisture. Men worked with sweat running into their eyes, their hands slipping on wet rope, their footing uncertain on decks that were never truly dry.

The routine of the watch offered some structure. Four hours on, four hours off, with the dog watches breaking the cycle and rotating the schedule. The men learned to sleep in short bursts, to eat quickly, to manage their fatigue. The officers learned to read the weather, to anticipate changes, to position the ship for the best advantage. The captain learned to trust his mates and his crew, to delegate what could be delegated, to reserve his attention for what mattered most.

What mattered most was speed. Every decision was filtered through that single imperative. When to set more sail, when to reduce it. When to steer closer to the wind, when to fall off. When to hold the course, when to tack. The premium clause had reduced a complex commercial enterprise to a simple proposition: arrive first. The monsoon made that proposition difficult. The wind that drove the ship south also pushed her west, away from the Sunda Strait and the passage to the Indian Ocean. The captain had to balance speed against course, distance against direction.

The coast of Borneo marked a turning point. The ships had been running south for nearly two weeks. The monsoon had tested them, shaped them, revealed them. Now they would turn west, threading through the islands toward the Sunda Strait. The race would enter a new phase. The open water of the South China Sea would give way to the confined channels and passages of the Indonesian archipelago. The tactics would change. The challenges would shift.

But the monsoon had already done its work. The initial ocean leg had revealed the fundamental sailing philosophy of each captain and crew. Robinson of Fiery Cross had chosen the inshore route, conservative and steady. Keay of Ariel and MacKinnon of Taeping had matched each other offshore, aggressive and closely contested. The master of Serica had stood further east, gambling on cleaner air and longer runs. The captain of Taitsing had pushed hard to close the gap, making up for lost time with bold decisions.

The logs recorded the results. The daily runs, the wind shifts, the weather observations—all entered in the spare language of professional seamen. But behind those entries lay the real story. The wet decks and raw hands. The sleepless nights and endless watches. The moments of triumph when the ship surged forward under a full press of sail, and the moments of frustration when the wind died and the canvas hung limp.

The transformation was complete. What had begun as a commercial speculation had become a maritime ordeal. The ships and their crews were no longer merely carrying cargo. They were engaged in a battle against the elements, a contest that would strip away pretense and reveal the true character of each vessel and each man. The South China Sea had served as a forge, heating and hammering the fleet into something harder and clearer than they had been at departure.

The tea chests in the holds remained silent through all of this. They did not know that they were the subject of a race, the object of a premium, the reason for the endless labor above their stowage. They merely sat in their tiers, packed tight, waiting for the journey to end. But the men who tended them, who checked the holds and monitored the temperature, who worried about leakage and spoilage—they knew. Every time they descended into the darkness of the cargo space, they were reminded of what they were carrying.

The monsoon began to ease as the ships approached the southern end of the South China Sea. The wind shifted, the seas moderated, the air grew marginally less oppressive. The worst of it was behind them. But the race was far from over. The Sunda Strait lay ahead, the narrow passage that would carry them into the Indian Ocean. The ships are scattered by wind and choice across the South China Sea, but their paths are now converging on a single, narrow gate.
