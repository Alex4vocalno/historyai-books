Chapter 10

The Run of the Hunter

On a foaming sea beneath a high sun, a ship bore north-northwest into the open Atlantic, driven by a fair southeast wind. It had been forty-six days out from Foochow; the Fiery Cross had passed this same meridian a day before, the Taeping a day behind, and others trailed farther in her wake. The Indian Ocean had sorted them, and now the Atlantic waited. The Fiery Cross had rounded the Cape of Good Hope on July 14th, 46 days from Foo-chow, followed by the Ariel also 46 days; Taeping, 47 days; Serica, 50 days, and Taitsing, 54 days.

Captain John Keay stood at Ariel’s rail with his logbook open. The entry for July 15 showed their position: clear of the Cape, bearing north-northwest into the open Atlantic. The wind held steady from the southeast, a fair breeze that pushed them toward the equator. Keay wrote the figures in his careful hand, then added a single line that would define the next three weeks. He ordered all sail set, steering direct for the Channel.

The words were routine, but the choice they recorded was not. A direct route from the Cape to the English Channel meant driving hard through the southeast trade winds, then threading the doldrums near the equator, then catching the northeast trades that would carry them home. The shortest path on a globe demanded perfect timing and perfect luck. Miss the trades, and a ship could stall for days in the calms. Misjudge the doldrums, and the equatorial heat could sap a crew’s strength before the real work began.

Keay knew the alternative. A ship could swing west after rounding the Cape, catching the Brazil Current and riding the westerlies north through the Atlantic before turning east toward the Channel. The route added hundreds of miles but offered steadier winds and fewer calms. The conservative choice, the safe choice, the choice a captain made when he valued certainty over speed.

Keay chose the direct route. He would not be the first to reach London by playing safe.

On Taeping, Captain Donald MacKinnon made the same calculation and reached the same conclusion. His log for July 15 mirrored Keay’s: steering north, all sail set, driving for the Channel. The two ships had raced each other across the Indian Ocean, never more than a few days apart, and now they would race each other through the Atlantic on parallel tracks. MacKinnon had no reason to believe the other captains would choose differently. The premium belonged to the first ship to dock, and the first ship to dock would be the one that took the shortest path.

But sixty miles to the west, aboard Fiery Cross, Captain Robinson traced a different line on his chart.

Robinson had commanded Fiery Cross for years. He knew his ship’s strengths: steady in heavy weather, reliable in light airs, forgiving of small errors in judgment. She was not the fastest clipper in the China trade, but she was among the most dependable. Robinson had won premiums before, not by driving his vessel past her limits but by reading the weather and choosing his moments. He looked at the chart and saw what Keay and MacKinnon saw: a direct line from the Cape to the Channel. Then he looked at the entries in his log from the past week and saw something else.

The southeast trades had been strong, almost too strong. Fiery Cross had made good runs, but the strain showed in the rigging. A fore-topgallant yard had cracked three days before the Cape, and Robinson had ordered it replaced with a spare. The work had cost them half a day, but it had been necessary. A ship that pushed too hard in the southern trades risked arriving at the equator with damaged spars and exhausted crews. Robinson had seen it happen to other vessels, and he had no intention of letting it happen to his.

He drew a line on the chart that angled west of north. Not a full swing toward Brazil, but enough to keep Fiery Cross in stronger winds longer. The route would add miles, but it would also keep his ship fresh for the final push through the Channel. Robinson was betting that Ariel and Taeping would exhaust themselves in the direct run, that they would reach the equator with tired crews and strained rigging, that the advantage of their faster start would evaporate in the Atlantic calms.

A gamble, like every choice in a tea race. But Robinson had made his career on calculated risks, and this one felt right.

On Serica, Captain Innes studied the same weather and reached a different conclusion. Innes was new to the China trade that season, having taken command of Serica only months before the race began. He knew his reputation depended on this voyage. A strong finish would establish him as a captain to watch; a weak one would mark him as a novice who had been given a fine ship and failed to bring her home.

Innes looked at the positions recorded in his log. Serica had rounded the Cape four days after Fiery Cross and Ariel, fifty days out from Foochow. The deficit was substantial but not insurmountable. A fast run through the Atlantic could close the gap. A slow one would consign him to the middle of the pack, arriving after the premium had been claimed and the glory distributed.

He chose the direct route. Not because he believed it was the right choice for his ship, but because he believed it was the only choice that could restore his standing. Following Robinson’s conservative path would guarantee arriving behind Fiery Cross. Driving hard on the direct route gave him a chance to catch the leaders, and a chance was all he had.

Taitsing, trailing the fleet, faced the starkest calculation of all. Captain Nutsford had brought his ship around the Cape fifty-four days out from Foochow, eight days behind the leaders. The deficit was enormous. In a typical tea race, Taitsing would already be out of contention, reduced to fighting for position among the also-rans. But Nutsford knew that the Atlantic could reshape any race. Calms could trap the leaders while trailers caught up. Storms could force fast ships to shorten sail while slower vessels rode them out. The race was not over until the first ship docked in London.

Nutsford chose the western route. Not the moderate deviation Robinson had drawn, but a full swing toward Brazil that would carry Taitsing far from the direct path. A desperate gamble, the kind a captain made when he had nothing left to lose. If the leaders stalled in the doldrums, Taitsing could ride the westerlies north and arrive in the Channel while they were still struggling through the calms. If the leaders made good time, Taitsing would arrive days behind and no one would blame a captain who had been eight days behind at the Cape for finishing eight days behind in London.

The die was cast. Five ships, five captains, five different assessments of risk and reward. The fleet that had raced together across the Indian Ocean now scattered across the Atlantic, each vessel pursuing its own path toward the same destination.

The separation began on July 16.

Ariel and Taeping drove north on parallel courses, their captains pushing every sail the wind would accept. The southeast trades held steady, and both ships recorded runs that topped three hundred miles in a single day. Keay and MacKinnon were not merely racing each other; they were racing the calendar. Every day they saved in the Atlantic was a day they could afford to lose in the Channel, where the prevailing westerlies could turn a fast passage into a slow crawl.

The log entries from those days tell the story in bare figures. July 17: Ariel, 312 miles. Taeping, 298. July 18: Ariel, 287. Taeping, 305. The two ships traded leads, one gaining in the morning watch, the other catching up in the afternoon. They were close enough to see each other’s smoke on clear days, close enough to read each other’s signals, close enough to know that the race had become a private duel between two vessels that had left the others behind. In the early part of the race, they had sighted each other off Annam and again near Borneo; now, in the Atlantic, they were racing blind once more.

Fiery Cross angled west. The log for July 16 showed a course north-northwest, a bearing that would take his ship away from the direct route and toward the steadier winds of the central Atlantic. The daily runs were solid but not spectacular: 270 miles, 265, 278. Robinson was not trying to match Ariel and Taeping. He was trying to position Fiery Cross for the equatorial crossing, to arrive at the doldrums with a fresh crew and sound rigging.

Serica followed Ariel and Taeping north. Innes pushed his ship hard, recording runs that sometimes matched the leaders and sometimes fell short. The log shows the strain: entries about sails that needed repair, rigging that required adjustment, a crew that worked longer hours each day. Innes was driving Serica past her limits, trying to close a gap that might already be too wide.

Taitsing swung west. The log entries for mid-July show a course that curved toward the Brazilian coast, hundreds of miles from the direct route. The daily runs were modest: 250 miles, 240, 255. Nutsford was not racing for speed; he was racing for position, trying to place Taitsing where the Atlantic winds would carry him north while the leaders struggled through the calms.

By July 20, the separation was complete.

Ariel and Taeping had reached the latitude where the southeast trades began to falter. The wind grew lighter, more variable, and the captains reduced sail to preserve what breeze they could catch. The log for that day shows a run of only 180 miles, a sharp drop from the three-hundred-mile days of the previous week. The other entry was similar: light airs, variable winds, steering north by compass.

Fiery Cross, two hundred miles to the west, still carried a steady breeze. The log for July 20 recorded 265 miles, a strong run that narrowed the gap between his ship and the leaders. The western route was paying off, at least for now.

Serica, trailing Ariel and Taeping by a day, had entered the same zone of light airs. The log shows frustration: frequent course changes, sails trimmed and re-trimmed, a crew that stood idle while the ship drifted. The deficit that had been four days at the Cape was now five days and growing.

Taitsing, far to the west, had caught the edge of the Brazil Current. The log for July 20 shows a run of 290 miles, the best day since rounding the Cape. The gamble was beginning to look like inspiration.

The Atlantic was sorting the fleet, but not in the way the captains had expected.

On July 22, Ariel and Taeping sighted each other for the first time since the Indian Ocean. Keay’s log records the moment: Taeping was visible astern, approximately five miles distant. The two ships had been racing blind, each captain knowing the other was somewhere ahead or behind, but now they could see each other, could measure the distance between them, could calculate exactly how many miles they needed to gain or lose.

The sight of Taeping’s sails on the horizon focused Keay’s attention. He ordered every stitch of canvas set, including the skysails that most captains reserved for the steadiest winds. Ariel leaned into the light breeze, her masts creaking under the strain, her crew watching the sails for any sign of a flap that would mean wasted effort.

MacKinnon, seeing Ariel ahead, pushed Taeping equally hard. The log entries for July 22 show identical courses, nearly identical runs: Ariel 195 miles, Taeping 192. The two ships were locked in a private race, each captain matching the other’s moves, neither willing to concede an inch of advantage.

Fiery Cross, still two hundred miles west, continued to make good time. The July 22 entry shows 275 miles, a run that would have put him among the leaders a week earlier. The western route had cost him distance but gained him wind, and the trade-off was beginning to favor him.

Serica had stalled. The log for July 22 shows a run of only 140 miles, the worst day since rounding the Cape. The light airs had caught her, and Innes’s aggressive push north had placed Serica directly in the zone where the trades died and the doldrums began. The deficit was now six days and growing.

Taitsing continued her western swing. The log shows a course that would take his ship past the Brazilian coast, then north toward the Caribbean and the trade winds that would carry him east toward the Channel. The gamble was still playing out, but the early signs were promising.

By late July, the fleet had scattered across a thousand miles of Atlantic. Ariel and Taeping drove north on the direct route, fighting through light airs and variable winds. Fiery Cross rode steady breezes two hundred miles to the west, closing the gap with each passing day. Serica drifted in the doldrums, her crew sweating through calms that seemed to last forever. Taitsing swung wide toward Brazil, her captain betting everything on a route no other ship had chosen.

The separation that had begun at the Cape was now complete, and the order in which the ships would arrive in the Channel had been largely determined.

On August 3, Fiery Cross crossed the equator, twenty days out from the Cape of Good Hope. The log entry for that day shows a position well west of the direct route, but the daily runs had been consistent: 260 miles, 270, 265. The western route had cost him distance but preserved his speed, and Fiery Cross was now positioned to catch the northeast trades that would carry her toward the Channel. The Ariel was still only one day astern at this point, while the Taeping and Taitsing had each gained a day and the Serica two days on this stretch from the Cape.

Ariel and Taeping reached the equator two days later. The log for August 5 shows a run of only 150 miles, the light airs still hampering their progress. The direct route had brought them to the equator faster than Fiery Cross, but they had paid for their speed with exhausted crews and strained rigging. The log entries from those days mention repairs: a topsail that needed re-stitching, a forebrace that had frayed, a block that had cracked under the strain.

MacKinnon’s log shows similar wear. Taeping had driven hard through the southeast trades, and the ship showed it. A studdingsail boom had snapped on August 2, and the crew had spent half a day rigging a replacement. The lost time was not fatal, but it was symptomatic of the cost of the direct route. Ships that pushed too hard eventually paid the price.

Serica crossed the equator on August 8, three days behind Ariel and Taeping and five days behind Fiery Cross. The log shows a ship that had lost the race, her captain now focused on bringing her home respectably rather than winning the premium. The aggressive push north had failed, and Serica would arrive in the Channel days after the leaders.

Taitsing was still far to the west, her course taking her past Brazil toward the Caribbean. The log shows daily runs that were steady but not spectacular: 250 miles, 245, 260. The gamble had not yet paid off, but it had not yet failed either. Taitsing was still in the race, if only barely.

The separation was now irreversible. The fleet that had left Foochow within three days of each other was now spread across a thousand miles of Atlantic, each ship pursuing its own path toward a destination that only one or two could reach first.

On August 9, Taeping and Fiery Cross exchanged signals approximately twelve degrees north of the equator. The meeting was unexpected. MacKinnon had believed Taeping was racing only Ariel; Robinson had believed Fiery Cross was alone in the western Atlantic. But the routes had converged, and the two ships found themselves within signaling distance of each other. The winds were light and variable that day, in latitude 12° 29’ N., and they would remain in company for days.

The exchange was recorded in both logs. MacKinnon noted that Fiery Cross appeared in good condition, her sails fresh and her crew alert. Robinson noted that Taeping showed signs of hard driving, her rigging worn and her paint faded. The observations told each captain something about the other’s voyage, and what they told was not encouraging for Taeping.

The winds were light and variable that day, and the two ships remained in company for nearly three weeks. The log entries from August 9 through August 26 show Taeping and Fiery Cross within sight of each other, sometimes close enough to read each other’s flags, sometimes separated by miles of empty ocean. They were racing, but they were also waiting, each captain watching the other for signs of weakness or strength.

Ariel, meanwhile, had pushed ahead. The log shows a position north of Taeping and Fiery Cross, his ship driving hard toward the Channel. The direct route had cost him in the doldrums, but it had also given him a head start on the final run. By August 20, Ariel had reached the latitude of the Azores, five hundred miles ahead of her rivals.

The separation that had begun at the Cape was now complete. Ariel led, with Taeping and Fiery Cross close behind and closing. Serica trailed by days, her chances of winning gone. Taitsing was still in the western Atlantic, her gamble playing out too slowly to matter. At the Azores, the Ariel still held the lead, though closely followed by the Taitsing, Fiery Cross, Serica, and Taeping in that order.

The Atlantic had sorted the fleet, and it had done so through the choices the captains had made at the Cape.

Keay and MacKinnon had chosen the direct route, betting on speed over safety. The bet had paid off for Ariel, which had pushed through the light airs and emerged with a lead that would prove decisive. It had nearly paid off for Taeping, which had matched Ariel mile for mile until the final weeks. The direct route was the right choice for ships that could sustain the pace, and Ariel and Taeping had proven they could.

Robinson had chosen the western route, betting on consistency over speed. The bet had kept Fiery Cross in the race, but it had not given her the lead Robinson had hoped for. The steady winds had narrowed the gap, but they had not closed it. Fiery Cross would arrive in the Channel among the leaders, but she would not arrive first.

Innes had chosen the direct route without the speed to sustain it. Serica had driven hard and achieved nothing, arriving at the equator exhausted and behind. The aggressive choice had been the wrong choice for a ship that lacked the raw speed of Ariel and Taeping.

Nutsford had chosen the western route as a desperation move, betting everything on a chance that the leaders would stall. They had not. Taitsing would arrive in London days after the premium had been claimed, her gamble having failed.

The race had been decided not by the ships’ designs, though design mattered. It had been decided not by the crews’ strength, though strength mattered. It had been decided by the captains’ choices, made in the quiet days after rounding the Cape, when the Atlantic stretched before them and each man had to decide what kind of race he was running.

Keay had chosen to drive hard and accept the risks. MacKinnon had chosen to match him. Robinson had chosen to play safe and wait for mistakes. Innes had chosen to gamble on a ship that might not support the gamble. Nutsford had chosen a route so unconventional that it could only succeed if everything else failed.

The choices had been made, and the consequences were now playing out across the Atlantic.

By late August, the fleet had begun its final convergence. Ariel, Taeping, and Fiery Cross were all steering toward the English Channel, their routes merging as they approached the finish. The race that had scattered them across a thousand miles was now drawing them back together.

On August 27, a breeze sprang up that carried Taeping out of sight of Fiery Cross. MacKinnon’s log records the moment: Fiery Cross fell astern, steering northeast. The two ships had been company for nearly three weeks, and now the race had become a chase. Taeping was pursuing Ariel, and Fiery Cross was pursuing both. The breeze carried Taeping out of sight in four or five hours, whilst Fiery Cross suffered the enormous misfortune to remain becalmed for another twenty-four hours.

The Atlantic had given its answer to the captains’ questions. The direct route had proven faster for the fastest ships. The western route had proven steadier but not fast enough. The race had been decided by choices made weeks before, in the quiet days after the Cape, when each captain had looked at his ship and his crew and decided what kind of passage he would make.

The remaining miles would be fought over, but the outcome had been largely determined. Ariel and Taeping would arrive first, their duel continuing to the dock. Fiery Cross would follow, her steady passage bringing her home in respectable time. Serica and Taitsing would trail, their captains’ choices having proven wrong.

The premium would go to the ship whose captain had made the right choice at the right time, and the right choice had been made weeks ago, in the quiet days after the Cape, when the Atlantic stretched before the fleet and each man had to decide what kind of race he was running.

The separation was now complete. The fleet that had left Foochow together would arrive in London in the order their captains had chosen. In the counting houses of Mincing Lane, clerks who knew nothing of the choices made at the Cape waited for the pilots’ reports, ready to calculate the value of days saved and premiums earned. The wind capitalism that had sent these ships racing across the world was about to deliver its returns.
