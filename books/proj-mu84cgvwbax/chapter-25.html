Chapter 25

The Printers’ Devils at the Dock Gates

Seen from above, autumn in London was a cityscape of brick and soot and the smoke of countless chimneys, through which the mechanical rhythm of its commerce was as constant as the hours marked by church bells. Within this wider landscape, Fleet Street formed a narrow canyon where the business of news flowed ceaselessly, with paper and ink moving in and printed sheets moving out. Here, in one building along that canyon, a compositor worked his fingers over the type cases, pulling lead letters to set the words that would tell an old story.

The article he was setting concerned a subject that had once commanded front-page attention. But now, more than a decade after Ariel and Taeping had fought their way up the Channel within sight of each other’s lights, the story demanded a different treatment. It had ceased to be news. It had become memory.

The compositor reached for an italic font to set off a quotation from a captain’s log. The practice had become common enough that the men in the printing houses had developed a feel for it. A logbook entry, with its terse notation of latitude and wind direction, could be made to carry dramatic weight with the right presentation. The compositor did not know Captain John Keay of the Ariel, had never seen the man or his ship, but he knew how to make the type tell a story. The anniversary article would run in the morning edition, and readers would see the race not as a commercial event but as a chapter in the national story of maritime prowess.

This shift from commerce to commemoration had happened gradually, then all at once. The telegraph had seen to that. When the underwater cable from Shanghai to London had finally come online in 1871, information that had formerly required ninety-nine days to travel now crossed the globe in hours. The tea merchants of Mincing Lane received word of the new crop’s availability within hours of its arrival at the Foochow warehouses. The premium for the first cargo, that ten shillings per ton that had launched five clippers on the same tide in 1866, had evaporated. There was no longer any profit in racing wind against wind when the market knew the cargo’s location before the ship had cleared the Sunda Strait.

The Illustrated London News had prepared a special engraving for the anniversary. The artist had rendered Ariel and Taeping as they had appeared on that September morning, hulls nearly touching as they raced up the Channel, their sails filled with a wind that the artist had taken care to make look dramatic. The image bore little resemblance to the reality that either captain would have recognized. In 1866, the two ships had been close, yes, but close enough that the pilots at Deal had remarked on it, not so close that a man could have thrown a biscuit from one deck to the other. The engraving sold the romance. The compositor at The Times, setting his type a few streets away, was doing the same thing with words.

The anniversary article opened with a flourish. The great tea race of 1866, the author had written, stood as evidence of British seamanship and the clipper’s final, glorious hour. The compositor set the phrase “final, glorious hour” in a larger font. The readers would see it. They would remember it that way.

What the readers would not see, what the anniversary articles carefully omitted, was the commercial ledger behind the race.

The premium that Ariel and Taeping had split in September 1866 had amounted to a few hundred pounds, a fraction of what either ship’s owners had hoped for when they had sent their vessels to Foochow that spring. The real money in tea had always been in the cargo itself, not in the premium for arriving first. And the cargo that year, like the cargo in most years, had been carried by steamships as often as by clippers.

The Erl King, a steam auxiliary vessel, had arrived in London with the first tea of the 1866 season more than two weeks before Ariel and Taeping had docked. The newspapers at the time had noted this fact in their shipping columns, but the anniversary articles in 1879 left it out. The romance required a clean narrative.

The Illustrated London News engraving showed the two clippers under full sail, their white canvas gleaming against a blue sea. The artist had added a few details that no logbook would have recorded: a sailor in the rigging of Ariel, pointing toward the finish; a pilot boat in the foreground, its crew watching the race with expressions of awe. The inventions served a purpose. The readers of 1879 wanted to believe that the great age of sail had been a time of heroism and skill, not a time of commercial calculation and thin margins.

The compositor at The Times finished setting the first paragraph of the anniversary article. He pulled the galley proof and examined it under the gas lamp. The type was clean, the lines even. The article would run on page four, below the fold, a placement that would have been unthinkable in 1866 when the tea race had commanded headlines. But the year was 1879, and the tea race had ceased to be news. It had become history.

The printing press began its run around midnight. The steam engine that powered the machine hissed and clanked, a sound that the men in the press room had learned to read like a language. A steady rhythm meant the press was running smoothly. A stutter meant a problem with the feed or the ink. Tonight the press ran steady. The anniversary editions would be on the street by dawn.

Across London, in the offices of the tea merchants, the ledgers told a different story. The men who had financed the clippers, who had paid for the tea chests and the crews and the repairs, had mostly moved on. The trade had changed. Steamships now carried the bulk of the cargo, their schedules predictable, their arrival dates printed in the shipping circulars weeks in advance. The premium for the first tea had become a relic, a practice that belonged to an earlier era of commerce. The men who remembered the race of 1866 remembered it as a financial event, a moment when the wind and the market had aligned in a way that would never happen again.

But the newspapers had found another value in the race. The anniversary article in The Times would sell copies. The engraving in the Illustrated London News would attract readers who had never seen a clipper ship but who wanted to believe in a time when men had sailed across the world for glory. The race had become a story, and stories had their own economy.

The compositor thought about the type, the spacing, the way the letters sat in the composing stick. His task had changed. In 1866, the printers had set type for news: arrival dates, cargo manifests, betting odds. In 1879, they set type for memory. The difference was subtle, but it was real.

The steam-powered press rolled on. The anniversary editions piled up in the loading bay, ready for distribution. By morning, the story of the great tea race would be in the hands of readers across London and beyond. They would read about Captain Keay and Captain MacKinnon, about the close finish and the split premium. They would see the engraving and imagine the scene. And they would believe, as the article encouraged them to believe, that the race had been a contest of skill and daring rather than a commercial transaction shaped by wind and market pressure.

The telegraph had killed the premium, but it had created something else. The speed of information had made the race obsolete as a financial instrument, but it had also made the race available as a story. The same cables that carried the price of tea from Shanghai to London also carried the narrative of the clipper era to a reading public hungry for tales of British triumph. The printers at Fleet Street were not just setting type. They were manufacturing memory.

The morning edition of The Times hit the streets just after six. The Illustrated London News followed a few hours later, its engraving catching the light in shop windows across the city. Readers bought copies, read the articles, examined the images. The tea race of 1866 entered the collective consciousness not as it had been but as the printers had made it.

This transformation from event to legend had taken less than a generation. The sailors who had manned the clippers, who had stowed the tea chests at Foochow and rounded the Cape of Good Hope and fought their way up the Channel, were still alive. Some of them were still working. Captain Donald MacKinnon, who had commanded Taeping in the great race, had died in 1867 on the homeward passage from China, landed at Algoa Bay with an illness that took his life before he reached England. He was forty-one years old. The anniversary articles mentioned his death, but they placed it in the context of sacrifice, as if the sea itself had claimed him as the price of his skill. The reality was more prosaic: illness had taken him, not the race, not the wind, not the premium he had helped to win.

John Keay, who had commanded Ariel, had left the tea trade. The clipper he had sailed to such glory had been sold out of the fleet, her new owners putting her to work in trades that required less speed and more capacity. The anniversary articles did not mention this. They presented Keay as a figure from a golden age, a master who had achieved something that could never be repeated.

The readers of 1879 had no way to check the facts against the legend. The logbooks existed, filed away in shipping offices and private collections, but the average reader of The Times had no access to them. The newspaper itself had once printed abstracts of the logs, dry columns of numbers that showed the daily runs of each ship: Ariel, Taeping, Serica, Fiery Cross, Taitsing, each vessel reduced to a line of figures that told the story of the race in the language of commerce. Twenty-one days from Foochow to Anjer. Twenty-five days from Anjer to the Cape. The numbers were still there, in the archives, but the anniversary articles did not quote them. The numbers did not make for good reading.

What made for good reading was the image of two ships racing side by side, their captains matched in skill and daring, the outcome uncertain until the final moment. The reality had been more complex. Taeping had arrived at the dock first, twenty-eight minutes ahead of Ariel, because her shallower draft had allowed her through the outer gates of the London Docks when the tide was still too low for Ariel to enter. The premium had been split between the two vessels because the consignees had feared that anything that might be considered a dead heat would lead to disputes. The romance of the close finish had been real enough, but the commercial outcome had been the product of practical calculation.

The printers at Fleet Street did not concern themselves with these details. Their job was to sell newspapers, and the anniversary of the great tea race was an opportunity. The story wrote itself, or rather, the story had already been written by the passage of time. The race had become a symbol, and symbols did not require accuracy.

The engraving in the Illustrated London News showed the two ships approaching the docks, their sails still drawing as they neared the finish. The artist had rendered the scene with an eye for drama rather than precision. In reality, the ships would have been under reduced sail as they approached the Thames, pilots aboard, the crews standing by to take in canvas at a moment’s notice. The engraving showed them as if they were still in the open sea, driving hard toward a finish line that existed only in the imagination of the artist and his readers.

Commemoration worked this way. The event was transformed by the act of remembering. The race of 1866 had been a commercial transaction, a bet on wind and time that had paid off for some and not for others. The race of memory was something else entirely. It was a story that served a purpose, a narrative that connected the readers of 1879 to a past they could feel proud of.

The compositor at The Times finished his shift as the morning light began to filter through the windows of the printing house. He had set the type for a dozen articles that night, but the anniversary piece was the one he remembered. The words had a rhythm to them, a cadence that suggested importance. The great tea race of 1866. Evidence of British seamanship. The final, glorious hour of the clipper ship. The phrases stayed with him as he walked home through the waking streets of London.

He did not know that he was participating in something larger than the production of a newspaper. He did not know that the type he had set would help to create a memory that would outlast the ships themselves. He was a working man, doing a job, earning a wage. But the job had consequences beyond his knowing.

The anniversary editions sold well. The readers of London and beyond consumed the story of the great race, and the story entered the culture. The tea race of 1866 became, in the telling, something that it had never quite been in the living. It became a symbol of an age that was passing, a reminder of what had been lost in the rush toward steam and speed and certainty.

The men who had built the clippers, who had sailed them, who had financed them, knew the difference between the story and the reality. But their voices were fading. The new generation, the readers of 1879, knew only what they read in the papers. And what they read was romance.

The printing presses of Fleet Street had created something that the tea merchants of Mincing Lane had never intended. The premium for the first tea had been a financial instrument, a way to extract value from the uncertainty of wind and weather. The anniversary articles were something else: a way to extract value from memory itself. The readers paid for the papers, and the papers gave them a past they could believe in.

The clipper era had entered a new economy. The ships themselves were fading, their hulls rotting in distant ports or serving out their final years in trades that required neither speed nor beauty. Ariel had been sold at auction in 1877, Lot 47 at the sales in Limehouse, her value reduced to the timber in her frames and the copper on her bottom. The men who had sailed her had scattered, some still at sea, some ashore, some dead. But the story of the race lived on, printed and reprinted, engraved and displayed, consumed by a public that had never seen a tea clipper but that wanted to believe in the world she represented.

The compositor walked home through the streets of London, the morning light warming the buildings, the city coming alive around him. He thought about his breakfast, his bed, the next shift that would come in the evening. But the type he had set would outlast his shift, would outlast the day, would outlast the ships themselves.

The anniversary article ran in The Times. The engraving appeared in the Illustrated London News. Readers across the country saw the story of the great tea race, and the story entered the culture. The race had been transformed from a commercial event into a national memory. The transformation had happened in the printing houses of Fleet Street, one letter at a time.

The telegraph had made the race obsolete, but it had also made the story possible. The same technology that had killed the premium had created the audience for the legend. Information now traveled instantaneously, and the readers of the newspapers expected to be informed, to be entertained, to be given stories that connected them to a world beyond their immediate experience. The tea race of 1866, stripped of its commercial context and dressed in the language of romance, served that purpose perfectly.

The sailors who had raced the clippers had understood their trade as a commercial enterprise. They had sailed fast because speed meant money. They had pushed their ships and their crews because the market rewarded those who arrived first. The romance of the race, the drama of the close finish, the skill of the captains and the courage of the sailors: all of these had been real, but they had been real as components of a financial calculation. The wind had been a source of profit, not a symbol of freedom.

The printers had changed that. They had taken the raw material of the race and turned it into something else. The story they told was not false, exactly. Ariel and Taeping had raced up the Channel within sight of each other. The finish had been close. The captains had been skilled, the crews brave. But the story left out the context that had made the race meaningful to the people who had actually run it. It left out the premium, the market, the calculation. It left out the fact that the race had been a response to a specific set of commercial conditions, conditions that no longer existed.

The readers of 1879 did not know what they were missing. They read the anniversary articles and saw the engravings and believed that the great tea race had been a contest of skill and daring, a moment when the best of British seamanship had been put to the test and had prevailed. They did not see the ledgers, the insurance policies, the bills of lading. They did not see the commercial logic that had sent five ships to sea on the same tide.

The printers had created a past that the present could use. The race of 1866 had become a symbol of a lost age, an age of sail and skill and uncertainty. The steamships that now carried the tea, reliable and predictable, did not lend themselves to romance. They arrived on schedule, their captains following the routes laid out in the shipping guides, their crews working the engines rather than the rigging. The romance belonged to the clippers, and the clippers belonged to the past.

The anniversary passed. The newspapers moved on to other stories. But the memory remained, fixed in type and image, available to anyone who cared to read. The race had entered the culture as legend, and legends had a durability that commercial events could never achieve. The ships would rot, the sailors would die, the ledgers would be discarded, but the story would persist.

The pressure of the clippers’ new role as artifacts measured against printed legend hands off to the process of their physical preservation.
