Chapter 3

Tea, Salt, and Men

The mate’s hand rested on the last tea chest as two Chinese stevedores waited for his signal. Below, in the dimness of the hold, a million pounds of tea lay stacked in rows that stretched from the keel to the lower deck beams. The chest measured roughly two feet by three, woven of bamboo and lined with lead paper to keep the leaves dry. Each one bore the chop of the broker who had bought it at auction. The mate calculated the weight in his head. A full cargo for a ship like Ariel ran to just over a million pounds, packed tight enough to shift the center of gravity six inches lower than she would ride in ballast. He raised his hand. The stevedores lowered the chest into the hatch. It settled with a thud that echoed through the timbers.

The loading had taken five days. Sixteen clippers had waited in the Pagoda Anchorage at Foochow that May, their captains watching each other’s spars for signs of readiness. The new crop of tea became available here earlier than at any other Chinese port, which was why the ships trying to be first back to London had gathered at the Min River’s mouth. Each captain wanted his hull filled before his rivals’ so he could catch the first favorable breeze. The race began before the ships left the anchorage. It began with the loading.

A clipper’s hold was not a simple warehouse. The tea had to be stowed so that the ship would sail fast and handle well in heavy weather. A layer of clean shingle ballast covered the bottom, leveled and packed tight. Above this, the chests went in tiers, the lower courses packed closer than the upper to keep the center of gravity low. The stevedores worked from the wings inward, filling the space between the frames before laying the central tier. Gaps meant shifting. Shifting meant damage. Damaged tea meant claims against the owner. The mate supervised every chest, checking the alignment, rejecting any that looked crushed or damp. The hold smelled of dry, grassy leaves, tar on the seams, and salt air seeping through the ventilators. The smell of money.

The premium written into the bills of lading added ten shillings per ton to the ordinary freight rate. For Ariel, carrying perhaps 1, 100 tons of tea, that meant an extra £550 if she arrived first. The money went to the ship’s owner, but the captain and the crew had their own arrangements. A fast passage meant better wages and better prospects for the next berth. The sailors knew the stakes. They had signed articles in Shanghai or Hong Kong, agreeing to the run for a lump sum or a share of the earnings. They were not hired for the voyage. They were hired for the race.

The crew of a tea clipper varied by design and owner. Ariel carried about thirty men, including the master, the mates, the boatswain, the carpenter, the sailmaker, the cook, and the stewards. The working hands divided into able seamen, ordinary seamen, and apprentices. The able seamen were the backbone of the crew. They could hand, reef, and steer. They knew the ropes, the sails, and the routines. Years at sea, often in the China trade, had taught them what a passage in the monsoon season meant. The ordinary seamen had less experience. They could pull and haul, but the finer work required supervision. The apprentices were boys of fifteen to eighteen, sent to sea by their families to learn the trade. They slept in the half-deck, a cramped space forward of the main hatch, and they ate what the cook gave them.

The cook was a provisioner and a fire-watch, not a chef. His domain was the galley, a wooden box with a brick-lined stove bolted to the deck. He issued the rations: salt pork on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday; salt beef on Tuesday and Saturday; dried fish on Thursday; and a pound of biscuit per man per day. The meat came in barrels, packed in brine. Water came from casks stowed in the hold, tapped through a pump that ran to the galley. A man needed a gallon a day in the tropics, more if he was working hard. The cook boiled the water for tea and coffee, which was how the crew took most of their fluids. The food was monotonous, but it was adequate. The owners had no interest in starving their crews. A weak crew meant slow work. Slow work meant lost time.

The provisions were part of the cargo in one sense and separate from it in another. They occupied space that could have held tea. They added weight that had to be carried. They represented a cost that had to be paid whether the ship made a fast passage or a slow one. But they were also essential. A ship could not sail without them. The calculation of how much to carry was a matter of judgment. Too much meant extra weight and extra cost. Too little meant short rations or a stop for resupply, which cost time. The captains of the tea clippers erred on the side of sufficiency. They carried enough for a hundred days with a margin for storms and calms. The race might last ninety. The margin was insurance.

The crew’s work began before the ship left the anchorage. Before Ariel had even crossed the bar of the Min River, Captain Keay had the men working on the fore and aft trim. A clipper sailed best when she was balanced, with her bows slightly lower than her stern. The trim affected her speed and her handling. A ship that was down by the head would pitch heavily and lose way in a seaway. A ship that was too high forward would gripe and fall off. Keay had the men shift stores and water casks to adjust the balance. The process continued for over two weeks. Adjustments were needed later in the voyage as stores and water were used. The loaded depth of a tea clipper was about twenty feet, and every inch of that depth was calculated.

The men who did this work were not automatons. They were individuals with names, histories, and prospects. The muster roll listed them: John Smith, able seaman, age 28, shipped at Shanghai; William Jones, ordinary seaman, age 22, shipped at Hong Kong; Thomas Brown, apprentice, age 16, signed by his father in London. The names were common because the trade was large. Hundreds of men signed on for the tea season each year. Some came back. Some did not. The China run was not as dangerous as the whaling grounds or the North Atlantic trade, but ships were lost. Storms, fires, collisions, and groundings took their toll. The men who shipped out knew the risks. They also knew the rewards.

A fast passage meant partly monetary reward. The crew of a clipper that won the premium might receive a bonus from the owner or the captain. Some ships had a tradition of sharing the prize money. More importantly, speed meant a quick turnaround. A ship that arrived in London in September could load for China again and be back in the tea season the following year. A slow passage meant missing the season. Missing the season meant sitting in port for months, eating through the advance wages and losing the opportunity for another berth. The economics of the sailor’s life were tied to the economics of the ship.

The economics of the ship were tied to the economics of the tea. The tea in the hold was a collection of parcels, each bought by a different broker, each destined for a different buyer. The brokers advertised the arrival of their tea by naming the ship that carried it. “Ex Ariel” meant something to the wholesale market. It meant speed. It meant freshness. It meant that the tea had been picked in May and would be on the auction floor in September. The flavor of green tea faded with time. The first tea to arrive commanded the highest prices. The premium was a mechanism to align the incentives of the shipper and the shipowner. The shipper wanted speed. The owner wanted profit. The premium made them partners.

The partnership extended to the crew, though the terms were less formal. A captain who had a reputation for fast passages could attract the best men. The best men meant better work aloft, better handling in storms, and better chances of winning. The captain’s reputation was his capital. John Keay of Ariel had a reputation. So did Donald MacKinnon of Taeping and George Innes of Serica. They were known as men who could drive a ship hard without breaking her or her crew. The sailors who signed with them knew what to expect. Hard work lay ahead. So did regular food and a fair chance of arriving.

The arrival was months away. In the meantime, the crew lived in a world of ropes, sails, and wood. The deck of a clipper was a machine for harvesting wind. Every rope had a name and a function. The sails were arranged in a hierarchy from the courses to the royals. The handling of the sails required coordination. An order from the captain passed through the mates to the boatswain to the men at the halyards and sheets. The evolution of tacking or wearing ship was a dance that had to be practiced until it was automatic. The crew of a tea clipper was a team. They had to work together because the wind did not wait.

The wind in the China Sea in May and June was the southwest monsoon. It blew from the southwest, which meant that ships leaving Foochow had to beat against it to reach the Sunda Strait. The strait was the gateway to the Indian Ocean. Once through, the wind shifted to the southeast, and the ships could run before it to the Cape. The monsoon was predictable in its general direction but variable in its strength. A strong monsoon meant hard beating and heavy seas. A weak monsoon meant calms and light airs. The captains watched the barometer and the sky. They adjusted their sails and their courses. They raced.

The race was against each other and against the clock. The Ninety-Day Clock started when the first chest was loaded. Every day in port was a day lost. Every day at sea was a day gained or lost depending on the wind. The captains knew the math. A passage of ninety days meant arriving in early September. A passage of a hundred days meant arriving in late September. The market opened in September. The first tea to arrive set the price. The later tea followed the price. The difference could be significant. The premium was only part of the equation. The real money was in the tea itself.

The tea itself was in the hold, packed in chests, surrounded by ballast and provisions. The men who had loaded it were now the men who would sail it. They ate their salt pork and biscuit, drank their tea and coffee, and stood their watches. The watches were four hours each, with two hours of dog watch in the evening to rotate the schedule. The men slept in hammocks slung from the deck beams. They washed in buckets of salt water. They worked in all weather. The work was hard. The discipline was strict. The pay was low. But the food was regular, the berth was secure, and the passage had a definite end.

The end was London. The ship would dock at the East India Docks or the London Docks, and the tea would be unloaded into warehouses. The crew would be paid off, and the men would look for their next berth. Some would sign on for the return voyage to China. Others would go to the coal trade or the Australian run. A few would end up in the hospital or the poorhouse. The tea trade was one part of a larger system. The system moved goods and people around the world. The wind provided the power. The ships provided the platform. The men provided the labor. The capital provided the incentive.

The incentive for speed shaped every aspect of the shipboard system. The loading patterns favored stability over capacity. The crew rotations favored experience over numbers. The provisions favored durability over variety. The design of the ship itself favored speed over safety. A clipper was long and narrow, with a sharp bow and a fine stern. She carried a vast spread of canvas. She was built to sail fast in a following wind. She was not built to survive a hurricane or a grounding. The owners accepted the risk because the rewards were high. The captains accepted the risk because their reputations depended on it. The crew accepted the risk because they had little choice.

The acceptance of risk was a condition, not a contract. The men who shipped on a tea clipper knew that they might die. They also knew that they might make money. The calculation was not explicit. It was embedded in the practice of the trade. A sailor did not sit down and weigh the odds. He signed the articles, went aboard, and did his work. What mattered was the work. What he was paid for was the work. What kept the ship moving was the work.

The ship moved because the wind blew. The wind blew because the earth turned and the sun heated the oceans. The patterns of the wind were known. The monsoons, the trades, the westerlies—they were predictable enough to plan a voyage around. The captains studied the wind. They knew when to expect a shift and where to position their ships to take advantage of it. The race was partly a test of sailing skill and partly a test of weather judgment. A captain who read the sky correctly could gain days. A captain who misread it could lose the race.

The judgment of the captain was constrained by the judgment of the owner. The owner had invested in the ship and the cargo. He had paid for the provisions and the crew. He expected a return. The captain was his agent. The captain had discretion in how he sailed the ship, but he had to justify his decisions. A captain who pushed too hard and lost his spars would answer for it. A captain who was too cautious and lost the race would also answer for it. The balance was delicate. The successful captains were the ones who found the edge and stayed on it.

The edge was different for each ship. Ariel was an extreme clipper, built for speed. Taeping was similar. Serica was slightly smaller but equally fast. Fiery Cross was an older ship, but she had won the premium twice before. Taitsing was new and untested. The ships were matched, but they were not identical. The differences in design, condition, and crew could make a difference in a close race. The captains knew each other’s vessels. They had raced against each other before. They had opinions about which ship was faster in which conditions. The opinions were part of the calculation.

The calculation extended to the crew. A captain who knew his men could push them harder. A captain who did not trust his crew had to be more careful. The relationship between the captain and the crew was not democratic. The captain gave orders. The crew obeyed. But the crew was not passive. Good days and bad days came to every ship. The men could respond to encouragement or coercion. They could desert if they had the chance. The captain had to manage them as well as the ship. The best captains were the ones who could get the most out of their men without breaking them.

The breaking point was real. A crew that was pushed too hard would make mistakes. A mistake aloft could cost a man his life. A mistake on deck could cost a sail or a spar. The captain had to know when to push and when to ease off. The race was long. There would be calms and storms. There would be days when nothing happened and days when everything happened. The captain had to pace his ship and his crew for the distance. The sprint at the beginning was only the first phase.

The first phase was the run down the China Sea. The ships had to beat against the monsoon, tacking back and forth to make progress to the south. The work was hard. The crews were on deck day and night, handling the sails as the wind shifted. The heat was intense. The decks were wet with spray. The motion was violent. The ships pitched and rolled. The men slept when they could, in snatches between watches. They ate what the cook gave them. They drank tea and coffee. They endured.

The endurance was part of the job. The sailors of the China trade were accustomed to the conditions. They had made the run before. Experience had taught them what to expect. The apprentices were learning. They watched the able seamen and tried to copy them. Names of ropes and handling of sails came gradually. Climbing the rigging and working on the yards came with practice. Steering and keeping a lookout were skills acquired over weeks. The discipline of the watch system became second nature. The sea’s indifference to their comfort became a fact of life.

The indifference of the sea was a fact. The wind did not care who won the premium. The waves did not care who reached London first. The ships were small and the ocean was large. A storm could sink any of them. A calm could stop them all. The race was a human invention. The sea was not part of it. The sea was the medium in which the race took place. The conditions of the sea shaped the race, but they did not determine it. The determination came from the captains and the crews.

The crews were now committed. They had signed the articles. They had loaded the tea. They had stowed the provisions. They had adjusted the trim. They had gone to sea. There was no turning back. The race was on. The premium was waiting. The clock was ticking. The wind was blowing. The ships were sailing.

The sailing was a continuous process. The ships did not move in straight lines. They tacked and wore. They adjusted their courses. They shifted their sails. The captains watched each other. They knew where their rivals were, or they guessed. They knew that Fiery Cross had left first. They knew that Ariel, Taeping, and Serica had left together. They knew that Taitsing had followed. They knew that the race would be close. The close races were the ones that mattered. The close races were the ones that tested the captains and the crews.

The test was physical and mental. The captain had to make decisions with imperfect information. He had to guess what the weather would do. He had to guess what his rivals would do. He had to balance the risk of pushing hard against the risk of falling behind. He had to manage his own fatigue and the fatigue of his crew. He had to maintain his authority without provoking resistance. He had to keep his ship and his men in condition to finish the race.

The finish was months away. In the meantime, the ships sailed. They sailed through the China Sea, through the Sunda Strait, through the Indian Ocean, around the Cape of Good Hope, and up the Atlantic. They sailed through calms and storms. They sailed through days of boredom and nights of danger. They sailed with a million pounds of tea in their holds and a crew of men on their decks. They sailed for the premium. They sailed for the reputation. They sailed for the money. They sailed because that was what they did.

The system that sent them sailing was larger than any one ship or captain. Capital came from London. Wind came from the planet. The ships were the interface. The men were the mechanism. The tea was the payload. The premium was the incentive. The race was the result.

The result was uncertain. The ships were matched. The captains were experienced. The crews were capable. The weather was unpredictable. Anything could happen. A dismasting, a grounding, a collision, a fire—any of these could end the race for one ship or more. The owners knew the risks. The captains knew the risks. The crews knew the risks. They sailed anyway.

They sailed because the rewards outweighed the risks. They sailed because the trade demanded it. They sailed because they had no better options. They sailed because they were sailors. The reasons were multiple. The result was the same. The ships were at sea, and the race was on.

The tea in the hold was silent. It did not know that it was the object of the race. It did not know that its value depended on when it arrived. It did not know that men had worked to load it and would work to sail it. Tea—leaves dried and rolled and packed in chests. A commodity. Money.

The men on deck were not silent. They sang as they hauled. They shouted as they worked. They talked in their watches below. They were men. They were individuals. They were the crew. They were the ones who would make the ship sail fast or slow. They were the ones who would win or lose the race.

The race was a contest of wind and will. The wind was the same for all the ships. The will was different. Each captain had his own approach. Each crew had its own character. The ships were machines, but they were operated by men. The men made the difference.

The difference would show in the daily runs. The logbooks recorded the distance sailed each day. The captains compared their runs. They knew that a few miles a day could add up to days over the course of the voyage. They pushed for every mile. They pushed their ships and their crews. They pushed themselves.

The pushing had limits. A ship pushed too hard would break. A crew pushed too hard would fail. The captain had to find the optimum. The optimum was not fixed. It changed with the conditions. The captain had to adjust continuously. He had to read the wind, the sea, and his ship. He had to read his men. He had to make decisions in real time, with no second chances.

The decisions started with the loading. The mate who supervised the stowage of the last chest was making a decision that would affect the ship’s handling for the entire voyage. The captain who ordered the trim adjusted was making a decision that would affect the ship’s speed. The owner who hired the crew was making a decision that would affect the ship’s performance. The system was a chain of decisions. Each link mattered.

The chain stretched from London to Foochow and back. The owners in London provided the capital. The brokers in Foochow provided the tea. The captains provided the leadership. The crews provided the labor. The ships provided the platform. The wind provided the power. The premium provided the incentive. The race was the outcome.

The outcome would be decided in September. The ships would arrive, and the tea would be unloaded. The premium would be paid. The crews would be paid off. The ships would be refitted. The cycle would begin again. But for now, the ships were at sea. The race was on. The clock was ticking.

The clippers were now fully loaded systems—tea, salt, men—committed to their sprint, with every ounce of cargo and every man’s effort now leveraged against wind and time.
