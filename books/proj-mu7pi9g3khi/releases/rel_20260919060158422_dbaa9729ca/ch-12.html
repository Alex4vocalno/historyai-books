Chapter 12

The Committee’s Ledger and the Potter’s Field

The pledge was being kept. Somewhere in the city a man was dying alone in a room above a shuttered shop, and a Black nurse sent by the Free African Society was sitting with him, wiping the black vomit from his lips. Somewhere else a cart was rolling through empty streets, driven by a Black carter who had been hired that morning for two dollars, carrying a pine box toward the northwest. The pledge was being kept, and it was being recorded.

On a single day in late September, the Dunlap and Claypoole’s American Daily Advertiser carried a notice that might have been any notice: a woman dead after a short illness, the consort of a Philadelphia merchant. The language was restrained, almost formulaic, the kind of announcement that had filled the city’s newspapers for years. But by the last week of September 1793, such notices had thinned to almost nothing. The papers themselves had shrunk. Printers were dead or fled, and the type was set by skeletal crews. What remained of the press was mostly silence.

And across town, in the ledger book of the Committee of Twenty, the scale of what was happening appeared in a different register entirely. The entry for that same week recorded the burial count at the city’s new Potter’s Field: over one hundred interred. The number was written in a clear hand, in ink, between ruled columns. No names. No consort. No short illness. Just the count.

The contrast was the epidemic’s defining tension. A death notice was a person. A burial return was a number. And the committee, which had taken over the governance of Philadelphia from a collapsed municipal authority, was rapidly becoming an institution that could process the second but had nearly lost the capacity to register the first. The ledger was doing what ledgers do: reducing chaos to entries, converting catastrophe into line items. But the reduction was not neutral. It changed what the crisis meant, even as the crisis was being lived. The committee’s minutes and financial accounts together formed a kind of civic machinery for the management of mass death, and by late September the machinery was running at a tempo no one had anticipated and no one could slow.

On September 12, Mayor Matthew Clarkson summoned fellow citizens interested in helping the Guardians of the Poor. The Guardians, the city’s standing poor-relief body, had been overwhelmed almost from the first weeks of the epidemic. Their almshouse was full, their staff was dead or fled, and their funds were exhausted. Clarkson’s summons drew a group of men willing to constitute themselves as a committee, and within days they had formed the Committee of Twenty, an ad hoc governing body that would manage the crisis for the duration.

On September 14, Clarkson was joined by 26 men who formed committees to reorganize the fever hospital, arrange visits to the sick, feed those unable to care for themselves, and arrange for wagons to carry the sick to the hospital and the dead to Potter’s Field. The committee met daily. It appointed subcommittees. It issued orders. It spent money it did not yet have, on the strength of pledges from merchants who had not yet fled. In every functional sense, it was the government of Philadelphia.

The committee’s first and most urgent problem was the dead. They were accumulating faster than the living could manage. The city’s churchyards were full. The Lutheran minister J. Henry C. Helmuth, among others, kept records of burials that climbed week by week into territory no parish had seen. The dead could not remain in houses, where they threatened the living. They could not remain in the streets, where they lay in some neighborhoods for days. They had to be moved, and they had to be put somewhere.

The committee chose a tract of ground northwest of the city, on what was then open land beyond the built streets. The site was designated Potter’s Field. The name itself carried a specific meaning: a burial ground for the poor, for strangers, for those whose families could not afford a churchyard plot or who had no one left to claim them. In the crisis, it became the city’s principal cemetery. The committee ordered coffins. It hired carters. It directed the digging of graves. And it recorded everything.

The records were meticulous. The committee’s minutes tracked the procurement of coffins in batches, sometimes by the dozen, sometimes by the hundred. Pine boards, nails, linen shrouds. The materials were listed and priced. The carters were hired at fixed rates, and their names, where they survived, were entered into the payment rolls. Many of the carters were Black men. Many of the grave diggers were Black men. The labor was among the most dangerous in the city. Yellow fever was not understood to be transmitted by mosquitoes, a fact that would not be established for nearly a century. But it was understood to be contagious in some manner, and the handling of the dead, the proximity to the sick and the dying, was understood to carry risk. The men who drove the carts and dug the graves were working at the edge of the epidemic’s reach.

Richard Allen and Absalom Jones had organized the Black workers. They had answered the call from Benjamin Rush and the committee, and they had mobilized the Free African Society’s members to serve as nurses, carters, and burial crews. The labor was relentless. Jones, in the pamphlet he and Allen would publish the following year, described the conditions: the sick were often found alone, abandoned by families who had fled. The nurses entered houses where the stench was overpowering, where the dead lay beside the living, where the black vomit had soaked through bedding. They washed, they fed, they carried. And when the patients died, the burial crews came.

The committee paid for the labor. The financial accounts show disbursements for nursing, for carting, for digging. The amounts were modest. A nurse might receive a few shillings a day. A carter might earn two dollars for a day’s work. A grave digger might be paid by the grave. The money came from a relief fund that the committee managed, sustained by donations from merchants and citizens who had remained, and by pledges from those who had fled but agreed to contribute. The fund was always insufficient. The committee borrowed against promises. It spent ahead of receipts. It managed the gap between what was needed and what was available, and the gap was always widening.

What the ledger captured, and what the death notices could not, was the scale. The weekly burial returns from Potter’s Field climbed through September. The numbers were not abstractions. Each entry represented a person who had been carried from a house, placed in a coffin, driven through the streets, and lowered into a grave. But the entries did not record names. They recorded counts. The committee’s minutes noted the procurement of lime, used to cover the graves and suppress the odor of decomposition. They noted the need for additional grave diggers when the existing crews could not keep pace. They noted the expansion of the burial ground when the first trenches were full.

The numbers told a story that no individual account could tell. A death notice in a newspaper was a singular event, a moment of private grief made public. But the burial returns were cumulative. They added up. And in the adding, they transformed the epidemic from a series of individual tragedies into something that could only be grasped as a collective statistic. The committee did not intend this transformation. It was a byproduct of the administrative necessity of managing a crisis that had exceeded the capacity of every existing institution in the city. But the effect was profound. The dead were being quantified, and the quantification was changing the nature of the loss.

The committee’s minutes also recorded the procurement of food, medicine, and supplies for the sick poor. Linen for bandages. Cordials and stimulants. Bedding, which was consumed at an alarming rate because it could not be cleaned or reused after a patient died. The financial accounts show payments to apothecaries, to grocers, to lumberyards. They show the cost of maintaining the Bush Hill hospital, which the committee had commandeered as the city’s principal fever institution. The carts, the horses, the harnesses appear as line items. So do the coffins, over and over, in batch after batch.

The cumulative figure was staggering. By the time the epidemic subsided in late October, the committee had spent tens of thousands of dollars, an enormous sum for a city of fifty thousand people whose commercial life had been suspended for two months. The money had been spent on the basic materials of crisis management: coffins, carts, nurses, food, lime, boards, nails. The ledger was a catalog of necessity stripped to its most elemental form. No policy debate appears in it. No constitutional question. Only the procurement of the means to care for the sick and bury the dead.

But the ledger was also a record of what was being lost. The names that did not appear in its columns were the names of people who had been reduced to entries in a count. The committee knew this. The minutes reflect occasional moments of recognition, brief acknowledgments that the numbers represented human beings. But the recognition could not be sustained. The pace was too fast, the volume too great. The committee met daily, sometimes for hours, and the business before it was always the same: more sick, more dead, more coffins, more graves. The repetition was its own kind of horror. The entries blurred together. A hundred coffins on Monday. A hundred and twenty on Wednesday. The numbers climbed, and the individual stories vanished into the columns.

The carters drove the dead through streets that were empty. Philadelphia in late September was a city that had lost its population. The federal government had fled. President Washington had left for Mount Vernon, and the secretaries of state, treasury, and war had scattered to their own homes. The state government had withdrawn. The municipal government had collapsed. What remained was the committee, the nurses, the carters, the grave diggers, and the sick. The city’s commercial life had stopped. The counting houses were shut. The wharves were quiet. The market stalls were bare. The only traffic was the carts.

The carts moved in a steady procession toward the northwest. They passed the shut churches, the shuttered shops, the abandoned houses. They carried their loads in plain pine boxes, sometimes covered with a cloth, sometimes not. The drivers were mostly Black men, and they worked without relief. The graves were dug in advance, in long trenches, and the coffins were lowered in rows. Lime was thrown over them. Earth was shoveled in. The next row was begun. The process was industrial in its efficiency, and it had to be. There was no other way to manage the volume.

The committee’s management of Potter’s Field was an act of improvisation. The city had no established protocol for mass burial. The churchyards had always been sufficient. The Guardians of the Poor had managed occasional pauper burials, but nothing on this scale. The committee had to invent the process from nothing. It had to find the land, secure the labor, procure the materials, and establish the procedures. It had to do this while also managing the hospital, the nurses, the food supply, the relief funds, and the coordination of a volunteer workforce that was itself dying.

The Black workers were dying. The belief that Black people were immune to yellow fever, which Rush had advanced and which the committee had accepted, was false. Allen and Jones, in their pamphlet, recorded the deaths of Black nurses and carters. The toll among the Free African Society’s members was significant. They had volunteered on the understanding that they were protected, and they were not. The committee’s ledger recorded their payments, but it did not always record their deaths. They were workers first, and if they died, they became entries in the same burial returns they had been filling.

The irony was structural. The committee relied on Black labor because it had no other labor. The white workforce had fled. The Black community had stayed, had answered the call, had taken on the most dangerous work in the city. And the committee paid them for it, in modest sums, and recorded the payments in neat columns. The financial relationship was transactional. The labor was voluntary, in the sense that the workers had agreed to it, but it was also coerced by circumstance. There was no one else. The city had emptied, and the people who remained were the people who could not leave, or who had chosen not to, or who had been told they were immune and had believed it.

The ledger was the city’s true account. What the epidemic cost, not in lives, which were beyond accounting, but in materials and labor, appeared in its columns. The price of a coffin, the wage of a carter, the cost of a pound of lime. The daily expenditure of a city that had been reduced to its most basic functions: feeding the sick, nursing the dying, burying the dead. And the scale, in numbers that could not be argued with, that could not be explained away, that could not be softened by rhetoric or minimized by official optimism.

The committee’s minutes also recorded its own internal strain. Members fell ill. Members died. The committee shrank as the epidemic progressed, and replacements had to be found. The men who served were not soldiers or officials. They were merchants, clergymen, attorneys, and tradesmen who had stayed behind and taken on the burden of governing a city in extremis. They met in rooms that were sometimes thick with the smell of sickness. They debated procurement orders and burial logistics and relief disbursements with the gravity of men who knew that every decision affected lives. And they kept records, because record-keeping was what they knew how to do. It was the one administrative instinct that survived the collapse of everything else.

The burial returns from Potter’s Field were the starkest document the committee produced. They listed, by week, the number of interments. The numbers rose through September, peaked in early October, and then began a slow decline as the weather cooled. The peak weeks recorded hundreds of burials. The cumulative total, by the epidemic’s end, would approach five thousand dead. The number was so large that it defied individual comprehension. It could only be understood as a figure, a column total, a sum. And that was the ledger’s final function: to make the incomprehensible legible, to render mass death as a number that could be written, read, and entered into the record.

The making of the record was itself a civic act. In March 1794, the Mayor’s Committee published its minutes. The rapid succession of other yellow fever epidemics in Philadelphia and elsewhere in the northeastern United States inspired many accounts of the efforts to contain, control, and cope with the disease. The most notable was by the Lutheran minister J. Henry C. Helmuth. Rush wrote accounts of his own experiences and theories. Mathew Carey published his narrative of the crisis, which went through multiple editions. And Allen and Jones published their refutation, answering the censures that had been cast on Black workers in some of those accounts. The publication of the minutes was part of this rush to record, to make the epidemic legible in retrospect. But the minutes themselves had been kept in real time, under pressure, by men who were writing while the carts rolled past their windows.

The published minutes revealed the machinery of crisis governance. Procurement orders, payment rolls, burial counts, correspondence with donors and contractors, all appeared in the printed pages. The institutional skeleton of a city that had been stripped to its frame stood exposed. And with unintentional clarity, the record showed how the epidemic had been managed as a quantified crisis, how individual death had been subsumed into collective statistic, and how the machinery of order had been built on the labor of men who were themselves dying.

The financial accounts were perhaps the most revealing document. Every disbursement, every payment, every purchase appeared in the columns. What the committee valued, in the most literal sense, was what it was willing to pay for. Coffins generated the most numerous entries. Nursing payments were the most consistent. Food and medicine fluctuated with availability. Lime disbursements climbed steadily through September and October. The accounts were a portrait of a city in extremis, drawn in dollars and shillings.

The accounts also revealed the dependency. The committee had no independent revenue. It relied entirely on donations and pledges. The merchants who had remained, or who had fled but maintained their financial commitments, sustained the operation. Stephen Girard, the French-born merchant who would later take command of the Bush Hill hospital, was among the donors. The committee’s survival depended on the willingness of wealthy men to part with money they could easily have withheld, and the ledger recorded their contributions with the same precision it recorded the cost of coffins.

The precision was the point. The committee governed by record. In the absence of legal authority, of police power, of the ordinary apparatus of the state, it maintained its legitimacy through documentation. Every decision was minuted. Every payment was recorded. Every burial was counted. The records were the committee’s proof that it had acted, that it had managed, that it had not abandoned the city. They were also the evidence of what the management had cost, in money and in lives, and they would be scrutinized in the months that followed.

The scrutiny had already begun. Carey’s narrative, published before the epidemic was over, had cast aspersions on the Black workers, suggesting that some had exploited the crisis for personal gain. Allen and Jones, in their refutation, answered these charges directly. The conditions under which Black nurses and carters had worked were described in detail. The deaths were recorded. The labor was documented. And the workers had been paid, but modestly, and the payment was not exploitation but compensation for service rendered under conditions of extreme danger. The dispute would continue, but the ledger was unambiguous. The payments were recorded. The labor was documented. The deaths were counted. The numbers were there, in the committee’s own hand, and they told a story that the polemics could not contradict.

The ledger’s story was this: a city had collapsed, and the people who remained had built, from nothing, a system for managing the consequences. The system was crude, efficient, and dehumanizing, not by design but by necessity. It processed the dead in batches. It paid the living in shillings. It recorded the catastrophe in columns. And it held, in its neat entries, the measure of what the epidemic had cost.

By late September, the committee was meeting daily. The burial count was still climbing. The fund was still insufficient. The nurses were still working. The carts were still rolling. And the ledger was still being kept, its columns filling with numbers that no one had wanted to write and that no one could stop writing, because the writing was the only form of order that remained.

The epidemic was now being managed as a quantified crisis of mass death. The Potter’s Field trenches were full. The committee had ordered another expansion. A fresh consignment of coffins had arrived from a supplier in Germantown, paid for from the relief fund. The numbers in the burial returns for the last week of September were the highest yet recorded. And somewhere in the committee’s rooms, a clerk was entering the figures, his hand steady, the ink still wet, the page still turning.
