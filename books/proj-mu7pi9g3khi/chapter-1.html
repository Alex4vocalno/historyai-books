Chapter 1

The Nation’s New Seat of Government

The brigantine cleared the Delaware’s channel bars on the morning tide, August 2, 1793, and made for the Arch Street wharf under a sky the color of hammered lead. Her hold was tight with coffee from Jamaica and sugar from Saint-Domingue, cargo for the merchants who had built their brick counting houses within sight of the river. On deck, passengers who had endured six weeks at sea squinted toward the shoreline. They saw the masts of a hundred vessels stacked against the sky, the steeples of a dozen churches, and behind them, rising above the rooftops, the bell tower of the State House where the republic had declared its independence and now conducted its business. Philadelphia, the temporary capital of the United States, received them with noise: the cries of stevedores, the creak of tackle, the rumble of cart wheels on cobblestone.

The pilot who guided the vessel to her berth had worked the Delaware for nineteen years. He knew the rhythms of a port city in high summer—the smell of tar and hemp, the stench of bilge water pumped into the dock, the sweeter rot of fruit left too long in the sun. He had watched the city double in size since the war ended, watched the federal government arrive with its clerks and congressmen and secretaries, watched the streets fill with strangers looking for work or influence or simply a place to sleep. The pilot did not know that he was guiding the ship into a city on the edge of catastrophe. He knew only that the harbor was crowded and the warehouses full.

Philadelphia in the summer of 1793 was a living, precarious political experiment. The Residence Act of 1790 had designated the city the temporary capital for a decade, after which the seat of government would move to a permanent site on the Potomac River.

The arrangement was a compromise born of political necessity, a concession to Southern legislators who feared a northern capital and northern bankers who feared a southern one. Philadelphia had won the prize by default: it was the largest city in the nation, with a population approaching fifty thousand, and it possessed the infrastructure—roads, printing presses, meeting halls—to host a government still learning its own dimensions.

But the victory was temporary by design. The federal presence was a tenant, not an owner, and everyone in the city knew that the lease would expire. In 1793, the largest yellow fever epidemic in U.S. history would kill about ten percent of the city’s population.

The influx began almost immediately after the Residence Act passed. Members of Congress, Supreme Court justices, and the officers of the executive departments arrived in waves, seeking quarters near the State House. George Washington, re-elected to a second term in March 1793, occupied a house on Market Street, a block from the seat of the legislature. His cabinet secretaries found lodging in the same neighborhood, close enough to walk to their offices and to each other’s doors.

Foreign diplomats joined the procession: Edmond-Charles Genêt, the newly appointed minister from France, had landed in Charleston in April and was making his way north, preceded by reports of his inflammatory rhetoric and his insistence that the United States honor its revolutionary alliance against Britain. The city’s innkeepers and landlords raised their rates. Property owners subdivided houses and rented rooms by the week. The scramble for shelter pushed the boundaries of the settled city outward, filling the alleys and yards that threaded between the main streets.

The federal government’s presence transformed Philadelphia’s daily rhythms. Clerks copied letters and filed reports in rented rooms. Messengers hurried between departments carrying sealed instructions. The newspapers—the Pennsylvania Gazette, the Federal Gazette, the National Gazette—printed proceedings of Congress, extracts of debates, and advertisements for goods and services aimed at the new political class. Coffeehouses like the City Tavern became the unofficial headquarters of factions and parties, where rumors circulated and deals were struck over punch and madeira. The State House yard, a public green behind the building where the Declaration had been signed, served as a promenade for citizens eager to see and be seen. The capital was not merely a location but a performance, a demonstration that the republican experiment could function.

Yet the performance depended on a fragile foundation. The government rented its meeting spaces because it owned no buildings of its own. Congress assembled in the county courthouse, a structure built for local courts and adapted for national use. The Supreme Court shared space with the mayor’s court. The executive departments operated from leased offices scattered through the commercial district. Washington’s house on Market Street belonged to Robert Morris, the financier of the Revolution, who had moved to the suburbs to accommodate the president. The arrangement was practical—Philadelphia had ready-made facilities that a purpose-built capital would lack for years—but it reinforced the sense that the federal government was passing through. The capital was a guest, and guests, no matter how powerful, do not rearrange the furniture.

Stephen Girard understood this better than most. A French-born trader who had arrived in Philadelphia during the Revolution, Girard had built a fortune through shipping and speculation. His counting house on Water Street overlooked the wharves where his vessels unloaded their cargoes. He knew the tides of trade and the tides of politics, and he knew that both were subject to forces beyond any individual’s control. The outbreak of war between Britain and France in 1793 had thrown Atlantic commerce into chaos. American ships risked seizure by both navies. Insurance rates climbed. Merchants who had grown rich on the carrying trade now faced ruin. Girard, like his competitors, calculated risks daily: which cargoes to insure, which routes to avoid, which captains to trust. The port that had made Philadelphia wealthy was also its point of greatest vulnerability.

The waterfront was the city’s beating heart and its most dangerous organ. The wharves stretched along the Delaware from Vine Street south to South Street, a continuous line of wooden pilings and stone quays where vessels from Europe, the West Indies, and the coastal trade jostled for position.

Steam did not yet power ships; every vessel that arrived had ridden the wind and the current, and every vessel brought with it the contents of its hold and the condition of its passage. Some carried coffee and sugar. Others carried passengers fleeing the slave uprising in Saint-Domingue, refugees with stories of fire and bloodshed who likely carried the disease from the island where it was endemic. Still others carried sailors who had been at sea for months, men whose health had been tested by bad water, spoiled provisions, and contagious shipmates. The port was a gateway for goods, for people, and for whatever invisible burdens they carried.

The city’s infrastructure strained to absorb the flow. Philadelphia had paved streets and a rudimentary system of water pipes, but it relied on wells and cisterns for drinking water, and its drainage ran through open gutters into the Delaware and the Schuylkill. The alleys behind the main thoroughfares—narrow passages where the laboring poor rented rooms by the month or the week—collected the runoff of daily life: kitchen slops, chamber pots, animal waste, the detritus of workshops and stables. The smell was strongest near the docks, where the tide deposited refuse in the slips and the sun baked the mud at low water. Residents with the means to leave the city in summer did so, retreating to country houses or boarding houses in the countryside. Those who remained lived with the heat and the smell and the crowds.

Dr. Benjamin Rush walked the streets of Philadelphia with a physician’s eye and a reformer’s conviction. A signer of the Declaration of Independence, Rush had served as surgeon general of the Continental Army and now operated a private practice while teaching at the University of Pennsylvania. He was fifty-one years old in 1793, with a reputation that extended beyond medicine into moral philosophy, abolitionism, and political controversy. Rush believed that disease was not a random affliction but a consequence of environmental and moral conditions. He had argued for years that Philadelphia’s streets, its water supply, its drainage, and its public markets required systematic improvement. The city’s elected officials, he thought, were too often content to let private interests dictate public policy. The result was a metropolis that grew faster than its capacity to manage growth.

Rush’s medical theories placed him at the center of ongoing debates among Philadelphia’s physicians. He had trained in Edinburgh, where he absorbed the system of medicine that classified diseases by their causes and prescribed treatments based on the body’s response to stimuli. Rush adapted these ideas to his own observations, developing a practice that emphasized the body’s need to eliminate morbid matter through bleeding, purging, and induced sweating. His critics argued that his remedies were more dangerous than the diseases they treated. Rush dismissed them as timid or ignorant. He had treated patients through the Revolutionary War, through the privations of Valley Forge, through earlier outbreaks of fever in Philadelphia. His confidence was absolute. His methods were not.

The medical establishment in Philadelphia was a small, fractious community. The College of Physicians, founded in 1787, provided a forum for discussion but no mechanism for enforcing standards. The Pennsylvania Hospital, established in 1751, offered care to the poor and served as a training ground for younger doctors. The University of Pennsylvania’s medical school competed with rival institutions for students and prestige. When a patient fell ill, the choice of physician depended on geography, money, and reputation. There was no public health department, no centralized authority for tracking disease, no requirement that deaths be reported or their causes recorded. The city’s Board of Health existed primarily to impose quarantines on arriving ships—a power it exercised sporadically and with limited effectiveness.

The Free African Society, a mutual aid organization founded in 1787 by Absalom Jones and Richard Allen, operated in a different world entirely. Jones, a former slave who had purchased his freedom, served as a lay preacher at St. George’s Methodist Church. Allen, also formerly enslaved, had founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church after experiencing discrimination in white-led congregations. Together, they had built the Free African Society into an institution that provided burial insurance, sickness benefits, and community support to Philadelphia’s growing population of free Black residents. The society was not a medical organization, but its members cared for one another when illness struck. They visited the sick, prepared bodies for burial, and collected funds to support widows and orphans. They did so without recognition from the white citizens who passed them on the street, and without expectation that the city’s institutions would offer them anything in return.

The society’s members lived in the neighborhoods that white Philadelphia preferred not to see. North of Vine Street and south of South Street, beyond the boundaries of the orderly grid that William Penn had laid out a century before, the city’s Black population rented rooms in crowded houses and worked at the margins of the economy. Some were skilled artisans—carpenters, coopers, seamstresses—but most found employment as laborers, porters, and domestic servants. They were essential to the city’s daily functioning, carrying goods, cleaning houses, cooking meals, and performing the work that white citizens considered beneath them. Yet they were excluded from the formal structures of civic life. They could not vote, could not hold public office, and could not expect protection from the constables who patrolled the streets. When Rush and other white physicians debated medical theory in the College of Physicians, Jones and Allen tended to their own.

The political class that summer was preoccupied with matters that seemed, at the time, more pressing than public health. Washington’s second term had begun in March with an inauguration at Congress Hall. The president, now sixty-one years old, was weary of office and eager to retire to his plantation. His cabinet was divided. The secretary of state and the secretary of the treasury had been feuding for years, their disagreements over fiscal policy, foreign relations, and the nature of federal authority spilling into the newspapers through surrogate writers. The secretary of war, caught between them, tended to side with the treasury. The president, who had once presided over a harmonious council, now found himself mediating disputes that threatened to fracture his administration.

The news from France made matters worse. Genêt’s arrival had galvanized Democratic-Republican societies, groups of citizens who celebrated the French Revolution and demanded that the United States honor its 1778 treaty with France. Washington, advised by his cabinet, had issued a proclamation of neutrality in April, declaring that the United States would not take sides in the European war. Genêt ignored it. He commissioned privateers to seize British ships and recruited American sailors to serve on French vessels. The president demanded his recall. The French minister became a diplomatic crisis that would not end until Genêt’s replacement arrived.

Through it all, the business of government continued. The Treasury Department collected duties on imports, the largest source of federal revenue. The State Department corresponded with ministers abroad and received petitions from citizens at home. The War Department maintained a small standing army and negotiated with Native American nations on the frontier. Congress had adjourned in March, but its members remained in the city or traveled the roads between Philadelphia and their home districts. The Supreme Court had concluded its session. The machinery of the republic turned slowly, but it turned. Citizens who wished to petition their government could walk to the State House and hand their papers to a clerk. Those who wished to observe the proceedings could enter the galleries. The capital was open, accessible, and, for the moment, functioning.

The merchant class that supplied the government and the port operated on a different calendar. For them, August was the height of the shipping season, when vessels arrived from the Caribbean laden with sugar, coffee, rum, and molasses, and departed with flour, timber, and manufactured goods. The newspapers printed shipping lists: arrivals, departures, cargoes, destinations. The coffeehouses filled with captains and supercargoes exchanging news of markets, prices, and hazards. The insurance brokers on Front Street assessed risks and set premiums. The auctioneers at the vendue houses sold goods to the highest bidder. Money moved through the city in a continuous stream, lubricating transactions, paying wages, settling debts. Philadelphia’s economy was a living system, and in the summer of 1793, it was running at full capacity.

The laborers who loaded and unloaded the ships lived in a different Philadelphia altogether. They rented rooms in the alleys and courts that branched off the main streets, crowded spaces where families shared quarters with boarders and strangers. They worked for wages that barely covered expenses, and they depended on the flow of trade for their livelihoods. When ships arrived, work was plentiful. When the port was quiet, they scrambled for odd jobs. They had no savings, no insurance, no safety net. If they fell ill, they relied on charity or the modest support of mutual aid societies like the Free African Society. If they died, their families depended on the same institutions to bury them.

The city’s churches, synagogues, and meeting houses provided spiritual sustenance and social organization. The Anglicans had Christ Church and St. Peter’s; the Presbyterians had their meeting on Third Street; the Quakers gathered at the Great Meeting House on Second Street; the Catholics attended St. Mary’s on Fourth Street; the Jews worshipped at Mikveh Israel in a building they shared with a Lutheran congregation. Each congregation maintained its own burial ground, its own charity fund, its own network of support. The city’s religious diversity was a source of pride and a source of division. Sects that had persecuted each other in Europe now coexisted, uneasily, in a nation that had no established church and guaranteed free exercise of religion. But cooperation across sectarian lines was limited. When the city faced a crisis, each congregation tended to its own.

The summer of 1793 was hot, even by Philadelphia standards. The temperature rose in June and stayed high through July and into August. Rain fell in sudden storms that turned the streets to rivers and then left them steaming when the sun returned. The air was thick with humidity, the kind of heat that saps energy and shortens tempers. Citizens complained about the weather and sought relief in the public squares, the gardens, and the riverside promenades. Those who could afford it left the city entirely, retreating to the countryside or the springs at Bristol, twenty miles up the Delaware. Those who remained endured.

The city’s markets operated on fixed days, and the streets around Market Street filled with farmers selling produce, butchers selling meat, and hucksters selling whatever they could carry. The noise was prodigious. The smell, in the heat, was worse. The city had no systematic method for removing waste. Householders were responsible for cleaning the gutters in front of their properties, but enforcement was inconsistent. Dead animals, spoiled food, and human waste accumulated in the alleys and along the waterfront. The Board of Health had no authority to compel cleaning and no budget to hire laborers. The result was a city that smelled, in summer, like a city that had outgrown its capacity to manage itself.

The physicians who observed the conditions had different theories about what they meant. Rush believed that the miasmas rising from rotting organic matter could generate fevers. His colleagues in the College of Physicians were not so sure. Some attributed fever to contagion, to something passed from person to person. Others thought it arose from a combination of factors: heat, moisture, overcrowding, bad diet, intemperance. The debates were theoretical, conducted in Latin and in English, with references to Hippocrates and Sydenham and the Edinburgh school. They had no practical consequence. No one was draining the marshes or cleaning the alleys or improving the water supply. The city grew, and the conditions that Rush decried grew worse.

The refugees from Saint-Domingue added a new element to the city’s population. The slave uprising that had begun in 1791 had turned the Caribbean’s richest colony into a zone of chaos and bloodshed. White planters, free people of color, and enslaved people who had escaped or been caught in the violence fled by any means available. Ships arrived in Philadelphia carrying passengers who had lost everything: property, family members, their place in the world. Some were wealthy enough to rent houses and establish themselves in the city’s French-speaking community. Others arrived destitute, dependent on charity. They brought stories of atrocities, of fire and massacre, of a world turned upside down. Philadelphia listened, and wondered if the violence would spread. These refugees represented five percent of the city’s total population.

The federal government’s response to the refugee crisis was limited. The State Department had no mechanism for processing asylum claims. The Treasury Department had no funds allocated for relief. The president expressed sympathy but took no action. The city’s charitable institutions, already stretched thin, did what they could. The French community organized relief committees. Churches took up collections. Individual citizens offered shelter. The refugees were a humanitarian problem, and in the absence of a federal response, they became a local one.

The port continued to receive ships. Each vessel brought cargo, passengers, and crew. Each vessel tied up at the wharves and unloaded its contents into the crowded, noisy, smelly space between the river and the city. The stevedores who worked the docks handled bales and barrels and boxes without asking what was inside. The carters who transported goods to warehouses and markets did not inquire about origins. The merchants who bought and sold cargo depended on the flow of trade for their livelihoods. The system worked because it had to work. Philadelphia was a commercial city, and commerce required movement.

The pilot who had guided the brigantine to her berth continued his work. He brought in brigs and schooners, sloops and snows, vessels from Boston and Baltimore, from Kingston and Port-au-Prince, from London and Liverpool and Le Havre. He knew the captains and the crews, the quirks of each ship, the depths of each channel. He knew the river as a living thing, with moods and currents and hidden dangers. He did not know what the ships carried besides cargo. He did not know that some of the vessels arriving in the summer of 1793 carried something invisible, something that would test the city’s institutions, its medical establishment, its social fabric, and its claim to be a functioning capital of a functioning republic.

The complaints began in early August. Residents near the wharves petitioned the city council about the smell. The gutters, they said, were clogged with filth. The low tide exposed mud that had not been cleaned in months. The heat made the stench unbearable. The council referred the petition to a committee. The committee requested a report from the Board of Health. The Board had no inspectors and no budget. The matter was deferred. The smell remained.

A clerk in the Treasury Department, walking to work on a morning in early August, noticed a man lying on the ground near the Arch Street wharf. The man was a rigger, a worker who handled ropes and rigging on the ships that docked along the waterfront. He had collapsed, suddenly, without warning. The clerk summoned help. Passersby carried the man to a nearby boarding house. By evening, his skin had turned yellow. By morning, he was dead. The clerk thought nothing of it. People died in cities all the time, especially in summer, especially among the poor. He went back to work.

The pilot, the merchant, the physician, the minister, the president—none of them knew what was coming. They lived in a city that had grown too fast, that had outpaced its infrastructure, that had welcomed the world through its port without asking what the world might bring. They lived in a capital that was temporary by design, a tenant in a house it did not own, a government that had not yet tested its capacity to manage a crisis. They lived in a republic that had declared its independence and survived its first decade, but that had not yet faced the question of what a government owes its citizens when the citizens begin to die.

A complaint about offensive odors from the Arch Street wharf landed on a desk in the city controller’s office on August 5, 1793. The controller noted the date and the substance of the grievance in a ledger he kept for such matters. The complaint would be referred to the Board of Health. He did not follow up. The Board had no inspectors to send. The smell, like the heat, like the crowds, like the ships that arrived daily with their cargoes and their passengers, was part of the city’s summer routine. The controller filed the complaint and turned his attention to accounts that actually required his oversight.

The ledger entry was the only official acknowledgment of a problem that would, within weeks, empty the capital, kill thousands, and expose the fragility of a government that had not yet learned whether it bore responsibility for the welfare of the people who lived under its authority.
