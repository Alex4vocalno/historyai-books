Chapter 27

The New Calculus of City and Capital

Seen from above, two distinct construction sites answered the same question with different tools. Along the Potomac, surveyors drove stakes and hacked through growth toward a line on paper. Along the Delaware, stevedores hauled silt while laborers widened a street, their shovels striking old cobbles. One was building a capital; the other was trying to prove it still deserved one.

The question had been theoretical before August 1793, resolved on paper and deferred in practice. The Residence Act of 1790 had fixed the permanent seat of government on the Potomac after a bargain among Hamilton, Jefferson, and Madison, trading the assumption of state debts for a southern capital. Philadelphia was the temporary capital, its ten-year lease running to 1800. The arrangement satisfied no one fully, which was precisely why it had passed. No one expected the lease to be broken early.

The epidemic broke it. Not in a single stroke, but in the way that trauma works on political architecture, slowly and then with accumulating force. The image of a president fleeing his own capital hardened into a permanent feature of the national memory. Congress had been unable to convene. An abandoned executive mansion stood vacant on Market Street while the government scattered across Pennsylvania. That image did not fade when the frost came. It became an argument available to anyone who wanted to claim that Philadelphia was unfit to hold the republic’s business.

By 1796, the argument was being made openly. Southern legislators who had always resented the northern capital now had something stronger than regional jealousy. They had the autumn of 1793, and they invoked it with the precision of lawyers citing precedent. Why should Congress return to a city that had proved it could not protect the government during the season when the government was required to sit? Why should senators and representatives subject themselves to a place whose air turned lethal between August and October? The questions carried the force of experience. Several members had been in Philadelphia during the epidemic, or had arrived shortly after, and their letters home confirmed what the southern caucus argued in debate.

The recurrences made the argument stronger. Yellow fever returned to Philadelphia in the summer of 1797, and again in 1798 it was epidemic. Each outbreak was smaller than 1793. The city had learned something about quarantine, about cleaning the docks, about the discipline of isolation. But each confirmed that the underlying vulnerability had not been removed. The disease had struck other ports too. Other major ports also had epidemics, beginning with Baltimore in 1794, New York in 1795 and 1798, and Wilmington and Boston in 1798, making yellow fever a national crisis. The fact that other cities were also afflicted did not comfort Philadelphia’s defenders; it merely suggested that no American port was safe, and that a purpose-built capital, away from the wharves and the shipping channels, might serve as a refuge.

Yellow fever had visited North America before. English colonists in Philadelphia and the French in the Mississippi River Valley recorded major outbreaks in the late seventeenth century. Additional epidemics struck Philadelphia, Baltimore, and New York throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The disease traveled with the ships that carried sugar, coffee, and enslaved people from the Caribbean. What changed in the 1790s was not the pathogen but the political cost of its arrival. In a city that was also a capital, an epidemic became a failure of governance, a breach in the basic contract between a government and the people it was supposed to protect.

Washington watched the Potomac construction with the proprietary attention of a man who had chosen the site himself. He had ridden the ground in 1791, selecting the exact bend in the river where the new city would rise. He had appointed Peter Charles L’Enfant to lay out the streets and avenues. He had endured the slow pace of building through the mid-1790s, the shortage of funds, the quarrels between commissioners, the reluctance of workers to labor in malarial lowlands. By 1796, the foundation of the Capitol was rising, and the executive mansion had walls but no roof. The progress was slow. But it was progress in the direction Washington wanted, and each report from the commissioners confirmed that the permanent capital was moving toward completion.

The letters Washington received from Philadelphia told a different story. The city was attempting sanitary reform. Streets were being widened where the old lanes had been too narrow for carts to pass without brushing against the walls of houses. The docks were being cleared of the accumulated filth that Benjamin Rush and others had identified as a probable source of the miasma. A committee of the city corporation had recommended a new sewer system, and some sections were already under construction. The improvements were real. They were also, inescapably, defensive measures, the actions of a city trying to prove that 1793 would not happen again.

The problem for Philadelphia was that it had to prove a negative. No amount of street-widening could guarantee that a ship from the West Indies would not bring the fever back. No dock-clearing could promise that the next August would be safe. The city was asking Congress to trust its improvements, to return each summer on faith. And the memory of 1793 did not reward faith. The civic evaporation of that September, when the president departed, the departments shuttered, and the streets fell silent except for the carts carrying the dead, had shown what happened when a capital city failed under pressure. The lesson Congress drew was not that Philadelphia would do better next time. The lesson was that any city could fail.

John Adams felt the lesson personally. He had been in Philadelphia during parts of the epidemic’s aftermath, and his letters during the late 1790s reflect a growing dread of the sickly season that settled over the city each August. Adams was not a man given to dramatic language about disease. But the prospect of returning to Philadelphia for the congressional session, knowing that the fever might return, weighed on him. He wrote to Abigail about the risk. He consulted colleagues about whether the session could be shortened or moved. The vice president of the United States was reluctant to set foot in the capital.

The reluctance was shared. Members of Congress from New England and the middle states had less cause to fear than southern members, whose constitutions were said to be unaccustomed to the Philadelphia climate. But the fear cut across regional lines. No congressman wanted to explain to his family that he had died of a fever in a city that everyone knew was dangerous. The 1793 mortality, roughly five thousand dead, more than nine percent of the population, was no abstraction. Members had seen the number confirmed in the newspapers, in the burial returns, in the accounts of survivors. It made the annual trip to Philadelphia feel like a gamble.

The newspapers tracked the shift. Philadelphia’s editors defended the city with the fervor of civic boosters, publishing accounts of the sanitary works, the new sewers, the cleaned docks. They argued that the fever was imported, not native, that no city with clean streets and pure air could generate the pestilence from its own soil. The standard argument had the merit of being partly true. But it also conceded the essential point: Philadelphia could be safe only if the conditions were right, and the conditions depended on factors the city could not fully control. A ship could arrive. A mosquito could survive the journey. The fever could return.

By 1797, the defensive boosterism had begun to curdle. The recurrence of fever that summer stripped away the claim that the sanitary reforms had solved the problem. The editors grew quieter. The tone in the newspapers shifted from insistence to resignation, from declarations of safety to comparisons with other cities. The argument could no longer hold the capital.

Congress debated the question directly. Southern members rose to argue that the temporary residence in Philadelphia should be ended before the 1800 deadline, that the government should move to the Potomac as soon as the buildings were ready, even if that meant moving before the ten-year term expired. They invoked the flight of 1793. They described the empty streets, the shuttered offices, the president’s carriage leaving the city. They asked whether the republic could afford to have its government dissolve every time a fever season arrived. The answer, they suggested, was self-evident.

The northern members fought back. They argued that the Potomac site was unfinished, that the buildings were not ready, that moving the government to a construction site in a swamp would be worse than keeping it in a city that had suffered an epidemic. They pointed to the progress of the sanitary works. They produced testimony from physicians. They made the case that Philadelphia was recovering, that the improvements were working, that 1793 was an anomaly.

The case was weaker than it looked. The fever had returned. The improvements had not prevented the return. And the image of 1793, the empty capital, the abandoned government, was more powerful than any statistical argument about mortality rates or sewer construction. The southern caucus had the better story. A city that had emptied once could empty again. The logic was not airtight, but it did not need to be. It needed only to be persuasive enough to overcome the inertia of a temporary arrangement that no one had expected to break.

Washington’s own position was clear. He had never intended Philadelphia to be permanent. The Residence Act had been his compromise, and the Potomac had been his choice. The epidemic had not changed his mind about where the capital should be. It had changed the political conditions under which the move would occur. Before 1793, the move was a constitutional obligation, scheduled for 1800, proceeding at the pace of construction. After 1793, the move was an urgent necessity, driven by the memory of a government that had fled its own seat. The epidemic provided the argument that the Potomac’s advocates had lacked: not that the south deserved the capital, but that the nation required a capital that would not vanish.

The pace of construction on the Potomac accelerated. Washington directed the commissioners to push forward with the Capitol and the executive mansion, pressing for completion before the 1800 deadline. Funds were scarce, and the commissioners wrote to the president regularly, describing the difficulty of finding workers, the cost of materials, the slow pace of masonry in a city that was still mostly forest and swamp. But the political pressure to finish was now compounded by the political pressure to leave Philadelphia. Each summer that the fever threatened to return was a summer in which Congress might have to flee again, and each flight would confirm the argument that the temporary capital was untenable.

Philadelphia’s civic leaders understood what was happening. The city had invested heavily in its identity as the federal capital, and the removal of the government would mean the loss of the revenue, the prestige, and the population that the capital attracted. The sanitary reforms were, in part, an effort to hold the government by proving the city was safe. But the reforms were also an effort to hold the city’s own self-respect, to demonstrate that Philadelphia could govern itself, could protect its people, could maintain the standards of a civilized metropolis. The two motives were tangled. A city that cleaned its docks to prevent fever was also a city that cleaned its docks to prevent Congress from leaving. The line between public health and civic image was never clean.

The Free African Society’s work during the epidemic haunted this reckoning in ways that the city’s leaders did not fully acknowledge. During the 1793 yellow fever epidemic, Richard Allen and Absalom Jones helped to organize free blacks as essential workers to care for the sick and deal with the dead. They were appealed to by respected physician Benjamin Rush. The labor of Black Philadelphians, the nursing, the carting, the burying, had kept the city functioning when its white elite had fled. The city’s gratitude had been partial and contested.

Allen and Jones had published their narrative to correct the record, to refute the charges that Black nurses had exploited the sick, to establish the fact of their service and their sacrifice. The city had not fully reckoned with what it owed.

The sanitary reforms of the late 1790s were framed as the work of the corporation, the physicians, the civic committees. The Black workers who had held the line in 1793 were not named in the appropriations for street-widening or dock-clearing. Their labor had been essential. Their compensation remained unsettled.

The irony was structural. The city was spending money to prove it could survive the next epidemic, but the people who had made survival possible in the last one were still waiting for payment. The debt was not only financial. It was the debt of acknowledgment, of recognition, of the simple admission that the civic order had been maintained by the people the civic order had been designed to exclude. Philadelphia was cleaning its streets and widening its lanes, but it was not settling its accounts.

The national government was conducting its own accounting. The District of Columbia was rising on the Potomac, its streets laid out in the grand geometry that L’Enfant had designed, its public buildings advancing toward completion. The cost was enormous, and Congress appropriated funds in increments, debating each allocation. But the appropriations passed. The political will to finish the permanent capital was firm, and it was reinforced each summer by the threat of fever in Philadelphia. The memory of 1793 was doing the work that constitutional theory alone could not do. It was turning a compromise into a conviction, a schedule into a deadline, a plan into a necessity.

Adams, who succeeded Washington as president in 1797, inherited the arrangement. He was not as personally invested in the Potomac site as Washington had been, but he understood the political logic. The government could not remain in a city that frightened its own officers. The fear was not irrational. The fever had returned in 1797, and Adams had seen the city empty again, not as completely as in 1793, but enough to confirm that the pattern was established. Philadelphia’s summers were a risk. The Potomac, for all its unfinished state, was not yet a city. It did not have wharves crowded with ships from the Caribbean. It did not have narrow streets where miasma could accumulate. It was, in the logic of the time, a place where a government could sit without fear of dissolution.

The logic was imperfect. The Potomac lowlands had their own fevers, their own miasmas, their own reputation for unhealthiness. But the Potomac did not have the specific memory that Philadelphia carried. It had not yet emptied. It had not yet shown that it could fail. In the calculus of political risk, the unknown was preferable to the known catastrophe. A capital that might fail was better than a capital that had failed.

The double construction site was resolving itself. One site was building toward the future. The other was repairing the past. The political logic favored the future. The nation wanted a capital that had not yet failed, a city that had not yet emptied, a seat of government that had not yet demonstrated its own fragility. Philadelphia had demonstrated its fragility. The president had fled. The government had dissolved. The city had been kept alive by the labor of people the city had not fully thanked or paid.

The fever did not kill the capital. The fever provided the argument that killed the capital. The distinction mattered.

Philadelphia’s civic leaders could point to the sanitary reforms and argue that the city had responded to the crisis, that the medical catastrophe had been addressed, that the next epidemic would be met with better preparation. They were not wrong. But they were arguing about medicine when the decision was being made on the basis of politics.

The medical response was real. The political consequence was structural. The epidemic had shown that the capital could evaporate, that the government, the commerce, the population could vanish in a matter of days, leaving behind only the people who could not afford to leave. That demonstration of fragility was more decisive than any mortality count.

The nation did not move its capital because five thousand people had died. The nation moved its capital because the government had fled, and the fleeing had revealed that the capital was not permanent. It was conditional. It was temporary in a way that the Residence Act had not intended and could not remedy.

By 1798, the decision was effectively made. The buildings on the Potomac were approaching completion. Congress had appropriated the funds to finish them. The president had directed the commissioners to press forward. The southern caucus had won the argument, not because they had the better constitutional case, since the Residence Act was clear that Philadelphia was the capital until 1800, but because they had the better evidence. The evidence was the memory of 1793, reinforced by the recurrences of 1797 and 1798, confirmed by the letters of reluctant congressmen and the editorials of resigned newspaper writers. The evidence was the empty capital.

In June 1800, the federal government completed its removal to the District of Columbia. President Adams issued the order. The departments packed their records and loaded them onto wagons. The clerks closed their offices in Philadelphia and prepared to open them in a city that was still more construction site than metropolis.

The wagons rolled south on a road that was long and rough, carrying the papers of state toward a capital whose streets were still unpaved and whose buildings still smelled of fresh stone and wet plaster.

The government left behind the State House where independence had been declared, the Congress Hall where the Bill of Rights had been debated, the executive mansion on Market Street where Washington had conducted the nation’s business. The move was not a triumph. It was a retreat to a place that had not yet been tested, a wager that a city built from scratch on a river bend would prove more durable than a city that had already failed.
