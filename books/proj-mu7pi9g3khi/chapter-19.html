Chapter 19

The Mayor’s Last Appeal

Seen from above, the city lay under a single sheet of cold that had arrived on the night of October 16, ending weeks of stagnant heat that had settled over it like a held breath. Zoom in and the puddles along Dock Street wore a skin of ice; the awnings over the market stalls on High Street hung stiff and crackling. The air tasted different. It carried no sweetness.

Across the city, in the printing house that still operated on a skeleton crew, a compositor’s fingers found the type for a different kind of document. The forme on the bed of the press held a proclamation: Mayor Matthew Clarkson’s direct appeal to the citizens who remained. The type was set in the usual way, letter by reversed letter, locked into the iron frame with wooden quoins. The words it spelled out were a plea. They asked men who could still stand to serve as constables, to guard the property of those who had fled, to help carry the dead from houses where they lay. The press came down. The sheet pulled free. The ink was still wet.

The contrast was the chapter’s central irony, though no one in the printing house would have used that word. Nature had turned. The frost was breaking the fever’s back. And yet the city’s own mayor was setting type for a document that admitted, in its careful, official phrasing, that the mechanisms of civic order had failed. The proclamation was a request made by a man who had no power to enforce a command.

The vacuum at the center of Philadelphia’s governance had been widening since August. First the federal government had dissolved. President Washington left on September 10, taking with him the secretaries of state, treasury, and war, the clerks, the correspondence, the weight of federal authority that gave the city its reason to exist as a capital. The state government followed. The legislature adjourned. The governor departed. What remained was the municipal shell: a mayor, a city council reduced to a standing committee, and a handful of constables who had not fled or died.

The Committee of Twenty, formed in mid-September to manage the crisis, had been the improvised bridge between absent authority and the city’s survival. Its members had taken on the work of government: coordinating nurse visits, managing Bush Hill hospital, overseeing the burial of the dead, distributing food and medicine to the poor who could not flee. But the committee was a volunteer body with no statutory power. It could request. It could organize. It could not compel. And by mid-October, its ranks had thinned. Some members had died. Others had succumbed to exhaustion or had fled to the countryside after weeks of exposure to the sick. The committee met daily, but its minutes from this period are terse, almost telegraphic: reports of deaths, requests for nurses, notes on supplies running low.

The volunteer corps that the committee had organized had withered in parallel. The men who had initially stepped forward to serve as nurses, corpse-carriers, and guards had been depleted by the same forces that emptied every other institution in the city. Some were dead. Some were sick. Some had simply walked away, unable to face another house where the smell of black vomit hung in the closed air. The Free African Society, whose members had answered the initial call to serve as nurses and burial workers under the belief that Black people were immune to the fever, had suffered its own losses. Richard Allen and Absalom Jones had organized the effort, and their people had carried the city’s dying and its dead through September and into October. But immunity had proved a fiction. Black nurses had fallen ill and died alongside white ones. The labor force was broken.

Into this vacuum, Clarkson moved. He had been mayor since 1792, a merchant and Revolutionary War veteran who had taken the office as a civic duty rather than a political ambition. He had stayed in the city throughout the epidemic, working from City Hall with skeletal clerical support, signing orders, receiving reports from the committee, and watching the administrative machinery of a city of fifty thousand people grind down to a mechanism operated by a few dozen exhausted hands. His proclamation was the formal acknowledgment that the crisis had outpaced every structure designed to contain it.

The document itself was a study in the language of depleted authority. It did not invoke emergency powers, because no such powers existed. It did not threaten penalties, because the jail was nearly empty and the sheriff had no deputies to enforce warrants. It asked. The language was direct, even plain, stripped of the ornate phrasing that characterized official proclamations of the period. Clarkson appealed to the citizens who remained to step forward as constables, to protect property from the looting that had begun in abandoned neighborhoods. He asked for men to assist in the removal of the dead, whose bodies still lay in houses and yards across the city, uncollected by a burial system that had collapsed under the weight of numbers. He asked for a final collective effort.

The proclamation was posted on the walls of City Hall, on the doors of churches that still stood open, on the lampposts along High and Chestnut streets. It was printed in the newspapers that still managed to produce issues, their pages thinned by the absence of advertising and the flight of their compositors. It was read aloud in the streets where people still gathered: at the water pump on Arch Street, at the market stalls that had reopened with a trickle of produce from the surrounding farms, at the Bush Hill hospital where Stephen Girard and Jean Devèze still worked.

The reception was uneven. Some men came forward. A handful of constables answered the call, enough to provide a minimal patrol in the most vulnerable wards. A few volunteers offered to help with the dead, though the burial returns from this period suggest that the numbers of bodies being interred had already begun to decline. The frost was working. The death rate, which had peaked in late September and early October at over a hundred per day, was falling. But the city was not yet safe, and the proclamation’s reception made clear that safety and order were not the same thing.

The medical catastrophe was real. The era’s scientific ignorance about disease transmission meant that the fever had been able to spread unchecked through a population that did not understand what carried it or how to stop it. Benjamin Rush’s aggressive bleeding and purging, administered to hundreds of patients, had been contested by French physicians like Devèze, who favored milder treatments. The dispute had been fought in print and in the wards at Bush Hill, with Rush’s methods drawing both fervent adherents and bitter critics. The medical debate was genuine, and its consequences were measured in lives. But the medical catastrophe alone did not explain the civic collapse. The fever had exposed a structural fragility that was institutional, not primarily medical.

The question was not simply why the fever killed so many. It was why the city’s response had been so improvised, so contingent, so dependent on the voluntary labor of a few dozen men and the coerced service of a Black community that had been recruited under a false premise. The answer lay in the sequential dissolution of the city’s formal layers. The federal government left because it could. The state government left because it could. The wealthy left because they could. Each departure stripped away a layer of authority, resources, and personnel, until what remained was the bare substrate: a mayor, a committee, and the people who had no choice but to stay.

Clarkson’s proclamation was the ghost of evaporated authority. It was a document that admitted, through the very fact of its existence, that the formal structures of governance had been hollowed out. A mayor with real power does not need to plead with his citizens to perform the most basic functions of civic order. A mayor with real power commands, and the machinery of enforcement carries out the command. Clarkson had no machinery. He had a printing press and the hope that enough men would read his words and feel the pull of obligation.

The reception of the proclamation laid bare the limits of that hope. Some who read it responded with exhausted compliance. They had been in the city for weeks, nursing the sick, burying the dead, guarding property. They were tired beyond measure. They came forward because they could not imagine doing otherwise, not because the proclamation had stirred them to action. Their compliance was a kind of inertia: the motion of bodies that had been moving in the same direction for so long that they could not stop.

Others responded with bitter resignation. They had stayed because they could not afford to leave, or because they had nowhere to go, or because they had been caring for family members who were sick and could not be moved. The proclamation asked them to serve not just their own households but the city at large. Some did. Others read it and turned away. They had given enough. The city had taken their families, their neighbors, their health. It could take no more.

Still others responded with outright defiance. The proclamation asked men to guard property. But whose property? The houses of the wealthy who had fled to Germantown and the countryside, leaving their homes to the care of strangers or to no one. Some of those who remained saw the proclamation as an invitation to protect the interests of people who had abandoned the city and its people. They refused. The social contract, already frayed by the flight of the elite, snapped for them. They would not serve.

Mathew Carey, in his 1793 account of the epidemic, captured the moral landscape that the proclamation revealed. He set the dedication of men like Joseph Inskeep, a Quaker who served on the Mayor’s Committee and also visited the sick, against the selfishness of others. When Inskeep contracted the fever, he asked for help. Carey’s account made the comparison pointed: some men gave everything, including their lives, while others gave nothing and fled. The proclamation was addressed to both groups. It asked the Inskeeps to give more and the others to give something. The imbalance was the document’s implicit subject, though Clarkson could not say so directly.

The committee’s own records from this period reflect the strain. The minutes note the difficulty of finding men willing to serve as nurses or corpse-carriers. They record the deaths of volunteers. They track the declining supplies at Bush Hill, where Girard and Devèze continued to manage the hospital with a fraction of the staff they needed. The committee had been the city’s improvisation, its substitute for the government that had left. But improvisation has limits. The committee could not replace the federal treasury, the state militia, the federal marshals, the customs service, the postal system, or any of the other mechanisms that made a city of fifty thousand people function. It could only fill gaps, and the gaps were growing.

The frost changed the equation, but not in the way that the proclamation’s reception suggested. The cold air killed the mosquitoes that carried the fever, though no one in Philadelphia in 1793 knew that mosquitoes were the vector. The connection between cold weather and the disease’s decline was empirical, observed but not understood. The death rate fell. The hospitals began to empty. The streets, silent for weeks, saw the first tentative movement of people who had been hiding indoors. But the frost did not restore the institutions that had collapsed. It killed the fever. It did not rebuild the social contract.

Clarkson’s proclamation was the last official act of a city that had ceased to function as a political community. It was a document from a mayor to a remnant. The people who read it were not the citizens who had elected him or the city that had appointed him. They were the survivors, the trapped, the exhausted, and the defiant. The proclamation asked them to hold the line. Some did. Some did not. The line held, barely, not because of the proclamation but because the fever was dying on its own, killed by weather that no one had summoned and no one understood.

The dissolution of the city’s formal layers, which had begun when the federal government departed in September, had reached its terminal phase. What remained was not a city in any functional sense. It was a collection of survivors in a geographic space that had once been a capital. The mayor’s office existed. The committee existed. The proclamation existed. But the authority they represented had drained away, and the document on the printing press was evidence of the draining, not a remedy for it.

The frost settled over the city on the night of October 16 and held through the following days. The death toll, which had been falling, continued to fall. By the end of the month, the daily count of burials had dropped to a fraction of its September peak. The committee’s minutes from this period record the change in a single, repeated notation: fewer deaths. Fewer admissions to Bush Hill. Fewer requests for nurses. The crisis was ending. But the city that the crisis had revealed was not the city that had entered it.

The proclamation had asked for constables and corpse-carriers. It had asked for men to guard property and remove the dead. It had asked for the basic maintenance of civic order. The response it received was enough to prevent total collapse, but only barely. The looting that had begun in abandoned neighborhoods slowed but did not stop. The bodies that had accumulated in houses and yards were removed, but some had lain for weeks. The patrols that walked the streets at night were a token force, not a police presence. The city’s order was a thin veneer over the chaos that the epidemic had exposed.

Clarkson’s appeal was the last gasp of a hollow shell. The formal structures of governance had evaporated, leaving behind a document that could request but not require, that could plead but not command. The mayor who signed it was a man of genuine commitment, who had stayed when others left and who had worked through the crisis with a dedication that the committee minutes and the newspaper accounts recorded without embellishment. But his dedication could not replace the institutions that had departed. His proclamation was evidence of the absence, not a solution to it.

The frost held. The fever died. The proclamation was posted and read and answered by some and ignored by others. The city that emerged from the epidemic was not the city that had entered it. The capital that had emptied was not the capital that would refill. The social contract that had frayed would not be mended by a frost or a proclamation. It would require the return of the people and the institutions that had left, and the return would be slow, tentative, and marked by the same divisions that the epidemic had exposed.

The mayor’s unheeded plea left the city as a hollow shell, its order a thin veneer over chaos, awaiting tangible relief from outside.
