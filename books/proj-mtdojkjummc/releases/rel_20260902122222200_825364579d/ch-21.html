Chapter 21

The Atlantic’s Silent Passage (Spring–Summer 1817)

Three years earlier, the logbook of the Mary-Ann, out of Liverpool, offered two entries that spring which, read together, formed a perfect diptych of dashed hope. The first, dated the ninth of April, 1817, was all efficient promise. “Wind W by N, moderate. Made all sail. Passengers embarked.”

The ship cleared the Mersey with a hold packed with flour barrels and decks crowded with hopefuls, her routine departure one of dozens that season. The people on her rails were refugees from a broken season, having sold meager possessions for berths in steerage, fleeing the famine and social unrest of 1816. Their destination was Baltimore.

The sky above Liverpool was pale but clear, the wind steady. It was a beginning.

The second entry, fourteen days later on the twenty-third of April, was shorter, etched with a new kind of frustration. “Unseasonable calm. Thick haze obscures sun. No progress.” The Mary-Ann was adrift in a silent ocean, her sails hanging limp. The wind had not merely died; it had been replaced by a pervasive, windless stillness and a dimming of the light itself—the same “dry fog” that had reddened and dimmed sunlight in the eastern United States the previous year.

The passengers who had boarded to escape a crisis now found themselves immobilized within it, on a vast blue plain under a sun bleached of its power. The two entries, separated by a fortnight and several hundred miles of ocean, captured the essential discovery of the 1817 crossing: the voyage of escape had become an extension of the ordeal.

The anomaly was not waiting for them in America. It was traveling with them. This chapter charts the westward migration of the climatic crisis across the Atlantic Ocean, focusing on the experience of European emigrants who, fleeing the famine and social unrest of 1816, embarked for North America only to encounter its lingering effects in a new theater. The outcome of that season’s crossings was a catalog of perilous, prolonged journeys marked by shortages, disease, and despair even before landfall. To understand that outcome requires unpacking the inner works of the crossing itself—the physical mechanism that transformed a passage of hope into a vector of suffering.

The same stratospheric veil of volcanic sulfate aerosols from Mount Tambora, which had chilled continents and killed crops for two years, did not dissipate neatly with the calendar. It persisted into 1817, and over the open ocean it enacted a different kind of violence.

It disrupted the fundamental mechanics of sailing: wind and visibility. The “dry fog” noted by mariners—a persistent, sun-diming haze—obscured the celestial bodies navigators used to fix their position. More critically, it appears to have suppressed or altered the typical wind patterns of the North Atlantic, the trade winds and westerlies upon which captains relied for predictable passage.

Ships expecting a six-week crossing found themselves becalmed for days or weeks on end, their carefully calculated stores of food and water bleeding away with each stationary day. The crisis, therefore, was not a static event confined to continental pockets of famine.

It was mobile. It flowed along the very routes of commerce and migration, and the voyage itself became a causal mechanism that compounded the human cost and geographically extended the disaster’s reach.

The push from Europe in the spring of 1817 was powerful and specific. It was the culmination of pressures built over two terrible years. The aftermath of the Napoleonic Wars—a continent littered with debt, disrupted markets, and demobilized soldiers—had been brutally compounded by the volcanic winter.

Europe, still recuperating from the wars, suffered its worst famine of the century in 1816. Low temperatures and heavy rains resulted in failed harvests in Great Britain and Ireland. In Ireland and Switzerland, potato and grain harvests had failed utterly under relentless rain and frost. In Germany and Britain, soaring grain prices had triggered the most widespread and violent period of food riots since the French Revolution. While mortality rates varied, the economic despair was universal.

In German-speaking lands, historian John D. Post identified a crucial pattern: the wave of emigration spurred by this despair caused a greater net population loss than excess mortality itself. People were not just dying; they were voting with their feet, or what remained of their assets, for a way out.

This was the human cargo that filled the westbound ships—not adventurers, but refugees from a climatic shock they could not name.

They sought a future unmediated by ash. What they did not know was that the ash was still there, suspended miles above them, waiting to meet them on the open sea.

The mechanism operated invisibly but with concrete effects recorded in ship logs and passenger diaries from that season. Mariners repeatedly noted the “unseasonable calm” and the “thick haze.” This was not a common fog, wet and cold, but the dry, high-altitude volcanic aerosol veil that reddened and dimmed the sun.

Its presence meant more than just gloomy days. For navigation, it was a profound handicap. Celestial navigation required clear sightings of the sun at noon and stars at night to calculate latitude and longitude. The persistent haze made these sightings difficult or impossible for long stretches. Captains were forced to rely on dead reckoning—estimating position based on speed and direction—a method fraught with error over thousands of miles, especially when the wind itself could not be trusted. The typical wind patterns were disrupted.

Ships reported encountering zones of eerie stillness where the winds should have been steady, or finding light, variable breezes that offered no sustained propulsion. The Atlantic, usually a highway driven by reliable forces, became a place of uncertainty and delay.

The consequences of this disruption stratified sharply by party, turning the ship into a microcosm of the crisis.

For the captains and navigators, the challenge was professional and existential. Their authority and profit depended on a timely passage. A prolonged voyage consumed extra stores, risking the health of their human cargo and their own crew. More dangerously, miscalculations in position could lead to tragedy—sailing past destinations, hitting unseen hazards, or adding even more weeks to the journey as they corrected course. The logs show a growing anxiety, a shift from brisk notation to terse recordings of “no observation” and “wind failed.” Their expertise, built on a lifetime of understanding the ocean’s rhythms, was rendered partially obsolete by an atmospheric condition none of their charts explained. For the emigrant passengers, the ordeal was one of escalating physical and psychological torment.

They had embarked with finite hope and even more finite resources. The cost of passage often consumed their last capital. They packed what food they could afford for a voyage they were told would last a certain number of weeks. When those weeks stretched into months due to the calms, the arithmetic of survival turned against them.

Ship provisions, often meager and of poor quality to begin with, were rationed ever more tightly. Water casks turned foul. The biscuit weeviled. Hunger became a constant passenger. The nutritional deficits, combined with the cramped, unsanitary conditions inherent in steerage—where hundreds might be packed into a dark, low-ceilinged hold with minimal ventilation—created a perfect incubator for disease.

While specific mortality figures for individual 1817 crossings are elusive, the broader pattern is stark. Between 1816 and 1819, major typhus epidemics occurred in parts of Europe, including Ireland, Italy, Switzerland, and Scotland, precipitated by the famine. More than 65, 000 people died as the disease spread out of Ireland. Typhus, spread by body lice in crowded, unclean conditions, thrived in precisely the environment these prolonged voyages created. The disease did not respect the shoreline. It traveled with its hosts.

A ship becalmed for extra weeks was not just a hungry ship; it became a floating reservoir of infection.

Despair followed sickness. Having sold everything to buy this chance, passengers now watched their family members sicken and die in a wooden prison on a motionless sea, their promised land an invisible horizon away. The crisis they had sought to leave behind—the famine, the disease, the economic desperation—was being replicated in microcosm around them. They were not escaping it. They were carrying it.

For the climatic crisis itself, the voyage acted as a geographic amplifier and a human disseminator. The suffering did not end at sea; it was imported. Survivors who finally stumbled ashore in Baltimore, New York, or Quebec were often physically depleted and financially ruined, needing immediate aid and adding to the strain on New World communities. Furthermore, they brought with them the pathogens nurtured during passage. The Atlantic crossing became a bridge for epidemic disease, linking the famine-weakened populations of Europe to the port cities of North America.

The “Year Without a Summer” thus demonstrated its reach not only through failed harvests in New England or Canada in 1816, but through the fevered arrivals of 1817. The world was coupled not just by shared weather but by shared vectors of human movement.

Some might argue that the crises of 1816-1818 were primarily the result of pre-existing political, economic, and social frailties—that the volcanic winter was merely a minor trigger that would have been absorbed without lasting consequence in a more robust or differently structured world. There is truth in the premise but failure in the conclusion. The pre-existing frailties were real: the postwar economic dislocation, the rigidities of grain markets, the vulnerability of monoculture crops like the potato. These conditions defined the form and severity of the crisis. But the trigger was not minor. The Tamboran eruption provided a discrete, exogenous shock of unprecedented scale—a global temperature drop driven by physics, not politics. In a more robust world, the shock might have been buffered. But in this world, the existing frailties acted as force multipliers.

The key causal sequence is clear: the volcanic aerosol veil directly caused the crop failures (the cold, wet summers). Those failures, hitting specific socio-economic structures, produced famine and riot. That famine and economic despair, in turn, drove the emigration.

And then, crucially, the same aerosol veil directly intervened again by disrupting the transatlantic voyages of that emigration, compounding the suffering and spreading its consequences. The volcano did not create Europe’s social structures. But it did test them to breaking point, and then it reached out to sabotage the primary escape route from the chaos it had helped unleash. The pre-industrial world was fragile enough for the trigger to matter catastrophically.

The Mary-Ann’s story was one of thousands. Other vessels from Le Havre, Bremen, and Glasgow reported similar fates in the spring and summer of 1817. The ocean, which had always been a barrier and a conduit, temporarily became a trap. The silence of the calm was not peaceful; it was oppressive, a physical manifestation of the stalled hope of tens of thousands.

On some ships, discipline broke down as rations neared exhaustion. On others, a grim communal resolve held, forged in shared suffering. The diaries that survive speak less of the sky’s strange color—by 1817, many had lived under the dry fog for two years and accepted it as a strange new normal—and more of the empty horizon, the still sails, the taste of spoiled water, and the sound of coughing in the dark hold. The passage did end, of course. Land was finally sighted.

But the arrival was rarely triumphant. It was often a relief shaded with grief and debilitation. Port authorities in American cities sometimes faced dismaying scenes: ships arriving with a significant fraction of their passengers dead or dying, the survivors emaciated and penniless, requiring immediate charity or hospitalization. These arrivals were the human precipitate of the climatic shock—its final, weary testament washed up on a new shore. They had traversed an ocean only to deliver proof of the world’s tightened coupling.

The concrete toll of death and delay at sea created a pressure point of human displacement and suffering that arrived on North American shores not as an abstract statistic, but as shiploads of hungry, sick, and desperate people. They presented colonial and municipal authorities with an immediate and tangible problem: how to manage this unforeseen influx of distress. The crisis had traveled west, and now its human cost demanded to be paid in new coin—in almshouse budgets, in quarantine protocols, in the political tensions between established residents and destitute newcomers. The ocean had been crossed, but the consequences of the broken summer were just beginning their new chapter on land, where systems of relief and rule would now be tested in turn.
