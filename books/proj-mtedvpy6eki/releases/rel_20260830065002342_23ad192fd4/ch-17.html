Chapter 17

The First Aerial Assault

At 10: 00 hours on April 30, 1986, the Government Commission issued Operational Directive No. 2. The order was technical, precise, and devoid of metaphor.

It commanded the helicopter units of the Kyiv Military District to commence an aerial bombardment of Unit Four’s reactor crater. The objective was not destruction but suffocation. The payloads were to be sand, for smothering the graphite fire; boron carbide, for absorbing neutrons and preventing any new chain reaction; and lead, to melt and seal fissures. The flight path was a straight line from the staging area at Chernobyl’s auxiliary airfield to a hover position directly over the glowing maw. The required altitude for release was between 150 and 200 meters. The directive contained no data on the radiation levels at that altitude, because the instruments that had tried to measure them had maxed out.

At that same hour, Senior Lieutenant Alexander Sorokin of the 325th Independent Helicopter Regiment was being briefed for the first sortie. He was twenty-eight years old.

The known radiation level in the airspace above the reactor, extrapolated from the shredded remains of the earlier dosimeter charts and the physical symptoms of pilots who had flown reconnaissance, was estimated at between 1, 000 and 2, 000 roentgen per hour. A dose of 500 roentgen over five hours is considered lethal.

Sorokin’s Mi-8 helicopter, like all the others, had no specialized shielding. His mission was to fly into that field, maintain a stable hover against the violent thermal updrafts from a fire burning at over 2, 000 degrees Celsius, and release his load of sand from an external sling. He and his crew were given additional lead plates to sit on, thick paper filters for their respirators, and a briefing that lasted less than fifteen minutes.

These two facts—the crisp institutional order and the individual man in an unshielded cockpit—defined the opening of the most desperate phase of the response. The operation was commanded by the Air Forces of the Kiev Military District, under the direct instructions of Deputy Prime Minister Boris Shcherbina.

The briefing for Senior Lieutenant Sorokin and his crew was a study in stark contrasts. Held in a makeshift operations room at the auxiliary airfield—a space that still smelled of fresh paint and dust—it presented a mission of terrifying simplicity against a backdrop of incomprehensible physics. The officers from the Government Commission pointed to large-scale photographs of the reactor building, the roof blown off and the central hall a gaping pit. The objective was clear: fly there, hover, and dump. Yet the parameters of the enemy were unknown. The thermal plume rising from the crater could shear a rotor blade; the radiation field would penetrate aluminum and flesh alike; the sheer heat might ignite their fuel or warp their controls.

Sorokin listened, his training wrestling with the surreal. He was a military pilot, accustomed to orders and maneuvers, but this was not a combat run against a ground target. This was an attempt to feed a monster, to placate an invisible fury with bags of sand. The lead plates they were given were laughably inadequate, more psychological armor than physical protection.

As they walked to their aircraft in the late morning light, the normal pre-flight checks took on a funereal gravity. Each tap of a gauge, each strap of a helmet, was an act of commitment to an outcome they could not predict.

The flight path was brutally direct. Lifting off from Pripyat, Sorokin could see the empty streets of the model city below, laundry still hanging on some balconies. Then, almost immediately, the silhouette of the power plant dominated the horizon, its fourth block crowned not by a roof but by a shimmering distortion of air—the visible signature of immense heat.

As they crossed the perimeter fence, the first wave hit them not as radiation but as noise. The roar of the open fire was a deep, constant thunder felt through the airframe as much as heard. Then came the taste: a sharp, acrid metallic flavor that seeped through their paper filters, a sensory confirmation of ionization in the air they breathed. Within seconds, the cockpit dosimeters clicked frantically before their needles pinned themselves against their maximum scales, useless.

Navigation shifted from instruments to instinct. Sorokin focused on maintaining visual alignment with other landmarks—the skeletal remains of the ventilation stack, the intact third unit—as thermal currents buffeted the Mi-8 like turbulence over a volcano. The helicopter shuddered, wanting to rise on the hot column; holding it at 150 meters required constant, muscle-burning counter-pressure on the controls.

Reaching the hover point over Unit Four was to stare into chaos made visible. Through the swirling smoke and steam, Sorokin could glimpse colors no fire should have: a sinister cherry-red glow from deep within the crater, punctuated by flashes of incandescent white where graphite blocks burned with nuclear fury. The heat radiating upwards was palpable even through the cockpit glass; it felt like opening a colossal furnace door.

The mission’s technical precision met reality here. The external sling, carrying its net of sandbags, had to be released manually by a crewman using a cable cut-off switch. As Sorokin fought to hold position, his flight engineer counted down and released. The bags tumbled away, vanishing into the smoke below. There was no satisfying thud of impact, no visible change in the fire’s behavior. The sand seemed to be swallowed whole by the inferno, its tons of material as insignificant as a handful of gravel thrown into a blast furnace.

The immediate effect was actually counterproductive: the downdraft from their rotors whipped smoke and radioactive debris back up around them in a choking cloud before they could turn away.

The return flight was silent save for the engine whine. The adrenaline that had sustained them during the approach ebbed, leaving behind a hollow feeling and an acute awareness of their own contamination. Back at the auxiliary airfield, ground crews in protective suits approached with trepidation. The helicopters were not just vehicles; they were now major sources of radiation themselves. Using long-handled brushes and hoses, decontamination teams washed down the exteriors with a foaming solution, but everyone knew this was superficial.

The interiors, where Sorokin and his crew had sat absorbing gamma rays through their seats and neutron flux through their bodies, were already hot zones. The pilots emerged stiffly, their flight suits dusted with radioactive ash. They underwent a rudimentary check at a field dosimetry station; the numbers were often guessed or capped due to instrument failure. Their personal sacrifice was logged not in roentgens accurately measured, but in shifts assigned and sorties flown.

Sorokin’s first flight was not an isolated act but the opening note in a grim symphony that would play out over hundreds of sorties in the coming days. He was soon joined by pilots from across the Soviet Union’s military and civilian aviation branches: men like Major Nikolai Antoshkin, commander of an elite helicopter regiment brought in from Uzbekistan; civilian Mi-26 pilots from Aeroflot; even young lieutenants fresh from flight school. Each brought his own skills and fears to this unprecedented battle. They developed tactics on the fly—literally. They learned that approaching from downwind offered marginally less smoke inhalation but worse radiation exposure from drifting plumes; that flying at dusk made targeting slightly easier as the crater’s glow intensified against gathering dark; that releasing payloads from slightly higher altitudes gave more time for escape but reduced accuracy catastrophically.

The institutional machinery behind these individual acts of bravery was itself an experiment in crisis logistics. The Government Commission’s directive had issued an order but could not instantly conjure up materials or perfect delivery systems. The supply chain for sand became a critical bottleneck initially; trucks dug it from riverbanks and quarries miles away, creating clouds of radioactive dust at loading sites before it even reached Pripyat. Boron carbide pellets were scarce and had to be rushed from specialized institutes; lead came in ingots meant for industrial use and had to be broken or melted down for aerial delivery—sometimes simply thrown out of helicopter doors in lump form in desperate improvisation. This frantic procurement underscored the reactive, improvisational nature of the entire campaign, a stark contrast to the precise, technical language of the operational directive.

As April 30th wore into May 1st—the national holiday of International Workers’ Day normally celebrated with parades—the operation scaled up relentlessly. Helicopters now flew in continuous relays from dawn until nightfall when visibility finally forced them to stop.
The staging area became a chaotic ballet of rotor wash and shouting ground controllers.

The institutional response also adapted in the face of the failure of conventional tactics. Scientists like Valery Legasov, a member of the Government Commission, argued forcefully for a change in payload composition. He proposed adding clay and dolomite, materials that might create a crust over the fire, smothering it more effectively than sand alone. This led to a frantic new search for supply lines even as sorties continued.

Meanwhile, military commanders, grappling with the human cost, instituted a rotation system, pulling pilots after a “certain number” of flights. But the “certain number” was an arbitrary guess, since accurate dosimetry was impossible. Some men vomited after landing; others developed early radiation burns on hands and necks where protective gear failed.

Yet the queue of aircraft waiting to takeoff never diminished. New crews arrived to replace those too ill to continue, embodying the vast, impersonal machinery of the Soviet state, which could expend individuals but could not admit defeat.

The psychological toll of this asymmetric battle manifested subtly but profoundly. Pilots trained for combat where the enemy could be shot at and seen understood they here faced an indifferent physical force. There was no reciprocity, no satisfaction in hitting a target, only a repeated immersion in a lethal environment. Many reported a surreal detachment during the approach, a mental narrowing of focus solely on holding the hover and hitting the release point. This hyperfocus was a survival skill, and also a defense mechanism against contemplating the absurdity of the task.

Post-mission, though, thoughts crowded in. They thought about families unaware of what they were doing, about holiday celebrations elsewhere in the country, and about the strange taste of metal that would linger in their mouths for days.

By the evening of May 1st, after nearly forty hours of continuous operations, the first aerial assault had achieved a paradoxical status:
it was both a monumental feat of human endurance and technological improvisation, and an undeniable strategic failure.
Thousands of tons of material had been dropped into the reactor crater, yet the core still burned fiercely, and radiation readings around the plant remained catastrophic.

The helicopter crews had sacrificed their health, and possibly their lives, to buy not a solution but time—precious hours and days during which alternative plans could be formulated.
They had also provided the world something perhaps equally important in the moment: a visible symbol of the struggle against chaos.
Footage of helicopters flying over the shattered plant, broadcast internationally, served as proof that Soviet authorities were “dealing” with the situation, however inadequately.
This theater of control, while real courage was involved, helped shape the initial global perception of the crisis.

Within the inner circles, however, the truth was clear. Reports filtering up the command chain spoke of exhaustion, diminishing returns, and mounting casualties among aviators and ground crews. At a meeting late that night, commission members confronted data showing the fire intensity undiminished despite herculean efforts. The argument shifted from “how continue bombing” to “what do next when bombing fails.” The aerial campaign would continue for weeks, becoming a permanent feature of the response, but its role was redefined from an offensive designed to extinguish the fire to a defensive holding action to limit the release until more drastic measures were ready. This marked the transition from a heroic assault to the grinding, logistical holding action that would characterize the next phase.

The first aerial assault was not an act of war, but it shared war’s calculus: the expenditure of limited resources and human risk against a strategic objective that was, by any conventional measure, impossible.

For seventy-two hours, the catastrophe had been a secret negotiated between disbelief and data. Now, with the evacuation of Pripyat complete and the world beginning to ask questions triggered by radioactive clouds drifting across borders, the response escalated from diagnosis to direct intervention. The open core was a geopolitical as well as a physical threat; it had to be seen to be acted upon, and the action had to be seen to be taken.

The helicopters were both a tool of crisis management and a symbol of control. Their success or failure would determine whether the secret could be contained or whether it would blow wide open. The initial sorties were acts of extraordinary airmanship, performed in conditions for which no manual existed. Taking off from the dusty field near the Jupiter plant in Pripyat, Sorokin’s Mi-8 and five others formed a loose queue.

The approach was a short flight of less than four kilometers, but it crossed an invisible frontier. Within a minute, the radiation dosimeters inside the cockpits, crude devices meant for routine monitoring, would peg at their maximum readings.
