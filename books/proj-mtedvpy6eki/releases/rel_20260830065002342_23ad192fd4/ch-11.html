Chapter 11

The Commission Assembles

The evacuation buses were still loading in Pripyat when the telephone rang in Boris Shcherbina’s Moscow apartment. It was Sunday morning, April 27. The order from the Central Committee did not mention ghost towns or abandoned pets. It spoke of an accident at the Chernobyl Atomic Energy Station.

Shcherbina, a Deputy Prime Minister and the head of the state’s Bureau for Fuel and Energy, was to form a government commission, proceed immediately to the site, and take charge. His understanding of the task was procedural: he was to impose order. The system had dispatched its senior fixer to locate a problem and solve it. The time was approximately 10: 00 AM Moscow Time.

In Pripyat, the first buses were pulling away from the curb, carrying families who believed they would return in three days. The commission was flying toward a reality that would dismantle the premise of a temporary solution. The assembly in Moscow possessed a brutal, paperwork efficiency. Alongside Shcherbina, the state attached the expertise deemed necessary for mastery.

Valery Legasov, First Deputy Director of the Kurchatov Institute of Atomic Energy, represented the nuclear establishment. Evgeny Velikhov, a leading physicist and vice-president of the Academy of Sciences, joined him. The roster included hydrometeorologists like Yuri Izrael, radiologists like Leonid Ilyin, medical officers, and representatives from the Ministry of Energy and the KGB.

This was not an investigative panel. It was a crisis command, a physical extension of the Cabinet of Ministers designed to assess, direct, contain, and report back with solutions that would restore normalcy. Their purpose was control.

Their IL-86 aircraft departed Moscow in the early afternoon. On board, the initial briefings were fragmentary and ominously contradictory. They knew of a fire and an explosion. They knew the reactor was damaged. They did not know—could not yet conceive—that the reactor core no longer existed as a coherent structure. The paradigm for every prior incident in the nuclear industry was one of contained technological failure. A problem to be solved. Legasov recalled the atmosphere as one of grave concern, but not panic.

They were preparing to manage a severe accident, not to witness a singularity. The visual evidence began during descent. As the plane approached Kiev’s military airport near Zhulyany that evening, twilight gathering over the Dnieper, they saw it on the northern horizon: a faint, sullen glow.

It was not the bright flame of a burning building. It was a dull, persistent luminescence, like a banked furnace seen through haze. It marked the Chernobyl plant, over sixty miles away. No one commented on it aloud. For men accustomed to reading industrial landscapes, the sight was deeply abnormal. A fire visible from that distance, after more than thirty hours, was not a simple electrical blaze.

They landed and transferred immediately to a convoy of black Volga sedans and minibuses for the drive north. The road to Pripyat had become a major artery for the reverse traffic of evacuation; their headlights passed long lines of empty buses returning from Kiev after depositing their loads of residents. The evacuation Shcherbina’s commission had been briefed on was already a fait accompli.

They were arriving to govern a ghost town and a dying reactor. The silence on the road was oppressive.

Pripyat, April 27, Evening
The commission entered Pripyat between 8: 00 and 9: 00 PM. The city was eerily still. Lights burned in apartment windows left on in the haste of departure. The streets were empty save for militia patrols. The air carried a strange, metallic tang that every scientist noted but none could immediately place.

They were taken to the Pripyat City Executive Committee building, now the ad-hoc emergency headquarters. In a second-floor office, Shcherbina convened his first council of war. The local officials and plant managers who met them were men hollowed out by thirty-six hours of sleepless trauma. Their reports were chaotic, layered with desperation and lingering disbelief. Viktor Bryukhanov, the plant director, was reportedly in a state of shock. Firefighters were still battling blazes on the roof of Unit Three and in the turbine hall. The most urgent need, everyone agreed, was to stop the fire in the reactor itself and prevent a second explosion.

Here, the commission’s mandate of control met its first irreducible obstacle: data. Or rather, the absence of reliable data. Shcherbina demanded facts. What was burning? How much radiation was being released? What were the levels in Pripyat? The answers were either unknown or unbelievable.

The standard dosimeters available on site had maxed out almost immediately after the explosion. Their scales ended at 3.6 roentgen per hour—a reading that had become a bitter joke among those first on the scene. When asked for readings, operators would simply say “three point six,” because it was the only number their instruments could show, whether the true level was four times or four hundred times higher.

More sensitive equipment had been rushed from Moscow and Kiev, but its deployment and interpretation took time. The initial maps of radiation spread, hastily drawn by teams from the Hydrometeorological Service, showed fantastical numbers: hundreds, then thousands of roentgens per hour near the wreckage. These figures invited instinctive rejection. They could not be correct.

If they were correct, then the situation was not an accident but an open-ended radiological event. Shcherbina, the industrial manager, grew frustrated. He needed actionable information, not theoretical physics. He turned to Legasov and Velikhov. What was to be done? The scientists could not give him a quick answer.

The reactor was an unknown. They hypothesized that the glow was from burning graphite moderator blocks. If true, and if the fire continued, it would loft radioactivity higher into the atmosphere for hundreds of miles. They needed helicopters, boron, dolomite, sand, lead—anything to smother and neutralize the burning core. But how much? How to deliver it? No one had ever tried to bomb a reactor from the air before.

That night, the commission made its first consequential decision from the collision of political imperative and scientific uncertainty. The immediate priority was to attack the fire from above. A fleet of helicopters would be assembled. They would begin dumping materials into the gaping maw of the reactor building at first light.

It was a brute-force solution, a gesture of control when understanding was absent. The order was given. The Ministry of Defense and the Aviation Ministry lurched into motion.

The Zone, April 28, Early Morning
Before dawn on April 28, the commission members dispersed to confront different facets of the crisis. Shcherbina remained at headquarters, establishing communications with Moscow and coordinating the growing military operation. Legasov and Velikhov prepared to go to the plant itself. Their journey from Pripyat to the plant was a descent into an alien landscape. Radiation levels spiked wildly from one sector to another. Hot particles of nuclear fuel and graphite dust littered the ground. The air near the reactor crackled with a static charge that made hair stand on end. When they arrived at the administrative block adjacent to Unit Four, they were met with surreal devastation. The northern wall of the reactor building was gone. Where there should have been solid concrete and steel, there was a jagged silhouette against the sky. A steady plume of greyish smoke rose from within.

Inside that opening, in the darkness, they could see the faint cherry-red glow of burning materials.

It was now clear beyond any doubt. This was not a contained fire. The reactor vessel had been breached. The core was exposed. Legasov stood there and made a mental calculation that severed the last link to the paradigm of a manageable accident. The graphite moderator was burning. Graphite burns at extremely high temperatures, around 700 degrees Celsius. To extinguish it, you must either smother it completely or cool it rapidly. Water would dissociate into hydrogen and oxygen at such heat, risking a hydrogen explosion. Sand and clay might simply bake into a crust, sealing in heat and pressure below. They were looking at a fiendish problem: a fire that could not be put out with conventional means, sitting atop a mass of molten nuclear fuel that was continuing to fission.

Back at headquarters, another front of the crisis was opening. Medical data began to arrive in more systematic form. Doctors from Moscow’s Hospital No. 6, specialists in radiation sickness, had set up a triage station.

The numbers of seriously ill were climbing—not just firefighters now, but plant operators and technicians who had been on duty or had returned to help. The symptoms were textbook and terrifying: violent vomiting, intense headaches, a metallic taste in the mouth. In the most severe cases, skin reddened and ulcerated—the erythema that signaled massive dose exposure. These were not the vague ailments of shock. They were the specific biomarkers of acute radiation syndrome. The medical evidence was becoming a data set that pointed to one conclusion: an enormous release of radioactivity had occurred at the moment of explosion.

By mid-morning on April 28, the Government Commission was straddling two realities. In one reality, it was fulfilling its function. It issued orders. It mobilized resources. It compiled reports for Moscow that framed the response as vigorous and effective. Helicopters were already making their first runs over the reactor, crews braving thermal updrafts and radiation to dump sacks of sand and boron. In the other reality, it was becoming a captive audience to a verdict delivered by physics and biology.

The scientific data—the impossible dosimeter readings, the maps of fallout, the condition of the patients—coalesced into a picture of such scale that it threatened to overwhelm the political framework of “containment.” The secret was still held within Soviet borders, but it was growing too large for the bureaucracy to hold. The commission’s very presence, intended to certify control, was instead authenticating catastrophe.

The pressure focused on a single, unavoidable question Shcherbina could not answer for his superiors: what was the total release? How much radioactivity had escaped, and where was it going? The hydrometeorologists offered grim forecasts. Winds had shifted. The plume was heading north-northwest.

It was then that Valery Legasov took a step that would formally break the seal of domestic secrecy. Following protocols for international nuclear accidents, and under pressure from scientists who understood the implications of the wind direction, he made a recommendation. The Soviet government must notify the International Atomic Energy Agency that an accident had taken place at Chernobyl. The first notification was minimal, a masterpiece of bureaucratic understatement.

Shcherbina’s operational instincts, forged in the vast industrial projects of the Soviet state, demanded a chain of command, clear metrics, and a definitive end point. The reports that filtered into the Pripyat headquarters offered none of these. The “three point six” roentgen refrain was not merely a technical failure; it was a profound epistemological rupture. For the managers and engineers present, a dosimeter was a tool of governance, translating invisible danger into manageable numbers. When every device in the city reported the same, useless maximum, it severed the link between observation and understanding.

The higher readings from specialized equipment, when they finally arrived, were so astronomically outside the bounds of any civil nuclear accident protocol that they were instinctively processed as error. To accept them as truth was to accept that the rulebooks were obsolete, and that they were now operating in a realm beyond any precedent. Shcherbina’s frustration with the scientists was not merely personal impatience; it was the collision of a bureaucratic worldview with a problem that defied bureaucratic solution.

The order to attack the reactor with helicopters, given that first night, was less a tactical plan than an assertion of sovereignty. It was a declaration that the state would not be passive before the phenomenon. The logistical challenge was immense and improvised. The materials themselves—sand, clay, boron, lead—were chosen from a hastily compiled list of substances that might either smother fire or absorb neutrons.

There was no manual for creating a five-thousand-ton aerial blanket over a radioactive inferno. The helicopters, primarily heavy-lift Mi-8s and Mi-26s from nearby military districts, were not designed for precision bombing runs over a thermal plume laced with gamma radiation and beta-emitting dust. Crews, many of whom were young conscripts, received minimal briefing on the radiological hazard; the primary concern was the mechanical danger of the updrafts and the treacherous geometry of the wreckage. This operation, which would become the iconic image of the early response, was born not from confidence but from a desperate need to do something that looked like control, a performative mastery for the benefit of Moscow and the commission’s own sense of purpose.

For Valery Legasov and Evgeny Velikhov, the dawn journey to the plant on April 28th was a passage from theoretical concern into visceral, physical shock. The spike in their dosimeters’ chattering as they neared the complex provided a grim audio confirmation of the escalating hazard. But it was the visual evidence that proved definitive.

Standing before the skeletal remains of Unit Four, they were not just scientists analyzing data points; they were men confronting the unthinkable. The open core was a topological impossibility in their world, a negative space where solidity should have been. The cherry-red glow within was a beacon of continuous energy release, indicating that the fission process, while perhaps slowed, had not ceased.

Legasov’s rapid mental calculations about graphite combustion and the perils of water were the frantic application of textbook physics to a scenario those textbooks never envisioned. In that moment, he understood what the commission was truly facing: not a repair job, but a phenomenon without precedent.

The authorities conceded only that a minor accident had occurred. But the act of notification itself was a crack in the facade. It was an admission, however guarded, to an external body. Once you begin evacuating more than 100, 000 people, the scale of your actions begins to betray the smallness of your official story.

For Boris Shcherbina, standing in that quiet office in an abandoned city, the weight of command had transformed. He had been sent to fix a broken machine. He was now realizing he presided over an open-ended disaster.

The helicopters circling overhead were not extinguishing a fire; they were performing a ritual of control over a process science did not yet know how to stop. Every sack of sand that vanished into the smoke was a temporary measure, a placeholder for knowledge that did not exist.

The commission had assembled to impose order. But the data flooding in spoke a language of absolute magnitude. It did not negotiate. The secret was no longer a piece of state information to be doled out or withheld.

It was becoming a physical fact, moving on the wind toward borders that no committee could command. The pressure now was no longer about managing the accident. It was about managing the admission that the accident was unmanageable. And the wind was already changing.
