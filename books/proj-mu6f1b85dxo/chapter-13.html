Chapter 13

The Boat on the Stony Beach

The Victory Point note had fixed the chronology: April 1848, ships abandoned, 105 souls departing south. Yet a chronology without physical form remained an abstraction, a document that could be filed and forgotten. What the Arctic had not yet delivered was a scene that could not be abstracted—a tableau of objects and remains so particular that no reader in London could mistake it for anything other than human extremity. Lieutenant William Hobson found such a scene on the morning of 30 May 1859, twenty-five days after he had pried open the cairn and copied its water-stained message into his journal.

He was traveling with a small party along the western coast of King William Island, moving south from Victory Point toward Erebus Bay. The terrain offered no shelter: gravel ridges, snow patches, the constant wind that scoured the island’s interior and carried the smell of the sea when the direction shifted. At approximately five miles south of the cairn site, Hobson crested a ridge and saw, below him on the stony beach, a ship’s boat. Twenty-eight feet in length. Hauled clear of the tide line. Pointing toward the water as if ready for launching.

The position arrested him. This was not driftwood, not debris from a ship crushed in the ice. The boat lay on its keel, deliberately placed, its bow aimed at a sea two miles distant across terrain that would have defeated any attempt at floating. Hobson approached with the caution his service had taught him, the same methodical advance he had used at the cairn. What he found inside the vessel stopped his progress more completely than any physical obstacle.

Two human skeletons occupied the boat. One lay beneath the after-thwart, partially covered by fragments of a boat-cloak that had rotted to shreds. The other was forward, near the bow, its bones more scattered, its posture suggesting collapse rather than arrangement. Both wore the remains of naval greatcoats, woolen trousers, stockings still clinging to the leg bones. The skeletons had not been jumbled together in panic. They had died in place, within the architecture of the vessel that had carried them, surrounded by objects that testified to lives maintained until the final hour. The Victory Point note had recorded the death of Sir John Franklin on 11 June 1847 and the subsequent command of Captain Francis Crozier; here was the physical consequence of that leadership transfer.

Hobson’s eye moved from the bones to the cargo, and the scene’s strangeness multiplied. This was not the minimal equipment of desperate flight. Officer’s silver plate—monogrammed, crested—lay in a chest near the stern. Silk handkerchiefs, still folded. A dressing case with ivory fittings. Shoes, multiple pairs, some apparently unworn. Combs, toothbrushes, a sponge. Books: The Vicar of Wakefield, volumes of religious poetry. And weapons: two double-barreled guns, loaded, hammers at half-cock, ready for use. Powder horns full. Shot pouches heavy with their contents. The inventory mirrored relics John Rae had obtained from Inuit in 1854—silver spoons, a plate engraved ‘Sir John Franklin, K.C.H.’—but here they lay in their original, agonizing context.

The juxtaposition was unbearable. The skeletons spoke of extremity, of bodies finally defeated by cold and starvation. The cargo spoke of distinction, of identities preserved, of a relationship to material civilization that had outlasted its utility. These men had hauled silver plate across fifty miles of ice and gravel. They had carried books they could not read in the dark, shoes they could not wear on frozen feet, guns they had not fired at game that may or may not have existed. They had maintained, until the moment of death, the equipment of the lives they had led in England.

Hobson recorded what he could in the journal he carried, the same book that held his transcription of the Victory Point note. The two discoveries now faced each other across its pages: the document that described a departure, and the scene that showed what became of at least two of those who departed. The note had spoken of ships and dates and latitudes, of Crozier commanding 105 souls toward the Great Fish River. The boat spoke of something the note could not contain: the particular horror of a death that preserved its circumstances in such detail that the living could reconstruct not only what happened but what it had felt like to believe, until the final hour, that rescue remained possible. The note, in two parts on a pre-printed Admiralty form, was written by Captain James Fitzjames; the boat was his command’s physical epitaph.

Francis Leopold McClintock reached the site on 2 June 1859, traveling with the main sledge party from the Fox, which lay ice-bound far to the east. He had examined Hobson’s preliminary notes. What he found exceeded them in specificity and in the demands they made on his capacity for description. The boat was one of the expedition’s pinnaces—heavy, clinker-built, designed for work rather than speed. Its draught, when fully loaded, would have exceeded three feet. The men who had hauled it to this ridge had dragged more than a ton of vessel and cargo across terrain that McClintock, tracing their route, estimated at nearly fifty miles from the nearest possible landing point.

The skeletons required closer attention than Hobson’s circumstances had permitted. McClintock approached them with the method that had served him in mapping coastlines and measuring magnetic variation: positions noted, distances measured, relationships between bones and objects recorded with precision. The skeleton beneath the after-thwart lay on its back, the skull separated from the spine and resting near the right shoulder. The arms were extended, the hands near the hips. The posture suggested sleep or exhaustion—the body laid down for rest that had become permanent. The second skeleton, forward, was more disturbed. McClintock believed this resulted from the boat’s own movement over years of frost and thaw rather than deliberate interference. Both showed evidence of naval uniform: buttons, belt clasps, the remains of wool fabric that had outlasted the flesh it covered.

The cargo demanded its own inventory, and McClintock understood that this inventory would become evidence in a proceeding whose nature he could not yet fully foresee. The silver plate bore the Franklin family crest and the initials of officers known to have sailed with the expedition. The books were standard naval issue, distributed for moral improvement. The chocolate and tea, still sealed in their tins, represented the expedition’s preserved provisions—concentrated calories that had apparently been hoarded rather than consumed. Most disturbing were the guns. Both were loaded with shot and powder, caps on the nipples, ready to fire. Yet no game had been taken. The powder horns remained full. The shot pouches held their contents. The men had carried the apparatus of survival to their deaths without using it.

McClintock’s examination extended to the boat itself. The hull showed damage consistent with hauling over ice: garboard strakes sprung, keel scarred by gravel. Someone had attempted repairs—leather patches, tar, the improvisation of men who understood wooden vessels. The oars were worn at the handles, evidence of prolonged use. The mast had been unstepped and lashed along the thwarts, the sail folded beneath it. This was not a boat abandoned in panic but one prepared for a journey that had been interrupted, or completed in a sense its builders had not intended.

The position of the skeletons offered the most troubling evidence. McClintock concluded that the men had died in the boat rather than beside it. The after-thwart skeleton’s posture suggested a deliberate arrangement—the arms placed, the body extended as if for sleep. The forward skeleton’s scattered bones might indicate a more violent end, or simply longer exposure to weather and animals. What McClintock could not determine, and what would trouble subsequent investigators for more than a century, was whether these two had died together or separately, whether one had survived to arrange the other, whether they had been alone or part of a larger party that had continued south.

The boat’s location mattered as much as its contents. McClintock measured its position with precision: approximately two miles from the nearest water deep enough to float it, on a gravel ridge that offered no shelter from wind or snow. The men who had hauled it here had chosen this spot deliberately, or had been forced to stop by exhaustion that could not be overcome. The ridge commanded no particular view, protected from no particular direction. It was simply where they had stopped.

This was the second definitive physical discovery of the McClintock expedition. Where the Victory Point note had established a chronology, the boat established a condition. The note described decisions made by officers in command; the boat revealed the consequences of those decisions for ordinary seamen—or perhaps for officers themselves, for the skeletons’ clothing and belongings suggested status that could not be definitively assigned without closer examination than McClintock’s circumstances permitted. The note had spoken of 105 souls departing in April 1848. The boat showed what remained of at least two of them eleven years later: bones, belongings, the material residue of lives that had ended in a vessel designed for other purposes. It materialized the horror Inuit had described to Rae in 1854: white men starving on the land.

The inventory continued. McClintock found a mahogany writing desk, its contents sodden but legible: paper, pens, ink, the apparatus of correspondence that no one had used to write a final message. A medicine chest with bottles still sealed. A clasp knife, open, its blade rusted to the handle. Coins: sovereigns, shillings, the currency of an economy that had no purchase here. And everywhere, the evidence of clothing maintained beyond utility: spare shirts, spare stockings, a greatcoat with the buttons still fastened as if ready for wear.

The most intimate objects were the most disturbing. A hairbrush with strands still caught in its bristles. A razor, its edge dulled by eleven years of weather. A ring, its inscription illegible. These were not the tools of Arctic survival but the equipment of daily life, carried across the ice because their owners could not imagine themselves without them. The silk handkerchiefs, monogrammed, folded with care. The dressing case with its silver fittings. The very particularity of these objects—their resistance to the general category of “relic”—made them unbearable. A man had owned this brush, had brushed his hair with it, had packed it for a journey he believed would end in rescue. A man had folded these handkerchiefs, had touched this silver, had loaded these guns that he never fired.

McClintock’s journal, written that evening in the sledge tent, struggled to contain the scene’s implications. He had expected to find evidence of the Franklin expedition’s fate. He had not expected to find it so particular, so material, so resistant to the categories of naval reportage. The Victory Point note could be copied, transmitted, filed. The boat required description, measurement, the translation of physical fact into language that could travel. Every item he listed—the silver plate, the silk handkerchiefs, the loaded guns—carried a weight of implication that exceeded its material presence. The expedition had not simply failed; it had failed while maintaining, until the final hour, the distinctions and equipment of its origin.

The skeletons posed questions that McClintock could not answer. He was not a medical man; he could not determine age, cause of death, or identity from bones exposed to eleven years of Arctic weather. He noted what he observed: the positions, the clothing remnants, the absence of obvious trauma. He did not speculate about cannibalism, though the possibility would occur to him later, when John Rae’s reports from 1854 were recalled in light of this new evidence. For now, he recorded the fact of death without its manner.

Two men had died in this boat. Others had hauled it here, for the weight of cargo and vessel exceeded what two men could have moved.

What had become of the rest? The Victory Point note spoke of 105 souls. The boat contained two skeletons. The arithmetic of absence was itself a kind of evidence. Future DNA analysis would later identify remains from this area as those of men like James Fitzjames, whose addendum to the note had explained their circumstances.

The boat’s orientation seemed significant. It pointed toward the sea, as if its final occupants had maintained, even in death, the intention of reaching water. Yet the sea was two miles distant, across terrain that McClintock himself found exhausting to traverse with a lightly loaded sledge. The men who had hauled this boat had done so with a persistence that suggested either extraordinary hope or the inability to abandon an investment already made. The cargo they carried—silver plate, books, multiple pairs of shoes—made no sense as the equipment of survival. It made perfect sense as the equipment of identity, the objects through which these men understood who they were.

McClintock’s examination extended into a second day. He directed his men to draw what they could, to measure with precision, to preserve the scene’s arrangement before the necessary work of salvage began. For salvage was necessary. The Fox lay far to the east, supplied for a single season of search. Every object taken from the boat would have to be carried back across the ice, loaded onto the ship, transported to England. The evidence of the Franklin expedition’s end would become cargo, subject to the same logistical constraints that had governed its original departure.

The transformation began with the skeletons. McClintock directed that they be carefully collected, the bones separated from the surrounding debris, the positions recorded before disturbance. This was not desecration but documentation—the only method available for bringing this scene within reach of those who had not seen it. The skull beneath the after-thwart was lifted first, then the vertebrae, then the long bones of the arms and legs. The clothing remnants were preserved with them, the buttons and clasps that might someday identify these men. The second skeleton, more scattered, required more reconstruction. McClintock himself supervised, his hands in the remains of someone else’s disaster.

The cargo followed. The silver plate was counted, its crests recorded, its condition assessed. The books were examined for inscriptions, annotations, any personal mark that might attach them to known individuals. The Vicar of Wakefield had no owner’s name. The volumes of religious poetry contained no marginalia. The chocolate and tea were sampled—still edible, McClintock noted, though he did not record whether any of his men consumed them. The guns were unloaded, their charges drawn, the powder tested. Both had been loaded for eleven years without firing.

The most personal items received particular attention. The hairbrush with its strands of hair. The ring with its illegible inscription. The dressing case with its ivory fittings, water-stained but intact. McClintock understood that these objects would become relics in a sense the word had not previously possessed in this search. Not evidence of the expedition’s general fate, but tokens of particular lives—lives that could be named, perhaps, if the right connections were made. The silver plate could be traced to officers’ messes. The books might be matched to ships’ libraries. The brush, the ring, the handkerchiefs might someday find their way to hands that recognized them.

The boat itself presented the greatest difficulty. Twenty-eight feet of clinker-built hull, too heavy to transport entire, too significant to abandon. McClintock decided on disassembly. The thwarts were removed first, then the plank ends, then the stem and stern posts. Each piece was measured, its condition noted, its position in the original structure recorded. The keel, scarred by its hauling over ice and gravel, was marked for particular attention. Someday, naval architects might reconstruct from these fragments the vessel that had carried two men to their deaths.

The work took three days. On the fourth, McClintock directed his party to begin the return journey. They traveled now with additional weight: the bones of two men, the fragments of their boat, the objects they had carried across the ice in the belief that civilization could be transported, that its tokens would preserve them, that rescue would come before the final extremity. The loaded sledges moved slowly across King William Island’s gravel ridges and snow fields, retracing a route that Franklin’s men had taken in the opposite direction eleven years before.

McClintock, walking beside the sledge that carried the boat’s fragments, considered what this discovery added to the Victory Point note’s chronology. The note had established that the ships were abandoned in April 1848, that Franklin was dead, that Crozier commanded a reduced party attempting to reach the Great Fish River. The boat showed what that attempt had required: the hauling of vessels across terrain they were not designed for, the maintenance of naval distinctions in circumstances that rendered them absurd, the accumulation of objects that testified to an identity their owners could not abandon even when it could not save them. Crozier, a veteran of William Parry’s Arctic expeditions, had led this march; the boat was a fragment of his final command.

The loaded guns were the detail that would not release him. McClintock had unloaded them himself, drawing the charges, finding both powder and shot intact. The men in the boat had carried weapons ready to fire, yet they had not fired them. Either there had been no game, or they had lacked the strength to hunt, or the hope that had sustained their journey had failed at the last. The guns represented possibility maintained until impossibility. They were, in their way, the most terrible objects in that terrible cargo.

The journey back to the Fox would take weeks. McClintock’s party traveled with the evidence of the Franklin expedition’s end distributed among their sledges: bones, boat fragments, silver plate, silk handkerchiefs, loaded guns now unloaded. Each night they camped, and each morning they loaded again, and each day they measured their progress against the ice and the distance. The objects they carried did not speak, but they testified. The expedition that had left England in 1845 with steam engines and screw propellers and libraries of twelve hundred volumes had ended here: two skeletons in a boat, pointing toward a sea they could not reach, surrounded by the equipment of lives they could not save.

The boat’s relics were now cargo.
