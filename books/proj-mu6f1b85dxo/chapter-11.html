Chapter 11

The Seal-Skin Boots

The precise, hard-won understanding of the Arctic’s logistical barriers now sat with him, pressuring him to devise a new approach. Francis Leopold McClintock, commanding the Fox from winter quarters at Port Kennedy, had watched his first sledging party return from King William Island with frostbitten hands and nothing found. The ice had beaten them. The distance had beaten them. The theoretical possibility of finding traces remained just that—theoretical—while the actual cost of looking, measured in frozen flesh and wasted calories, mounted with each failed sortie.

McClintock did not call a council. He studied his charts, consulted his logs, and made a decision that would alter the geometry of the search. He would send a second party westward, not toward King William Island directly, but along the western coast of the Boothia Peninsula—a route that might intersect with Inuit trails and, through them, with intelligence no British expedition had yet gathered firsthand.

Lieutenant William Hobson received his orders in the dim light of the Fox’s cabin on 17 May 1858. He was twenty-six years old, had served in the Baltic and the Pacific, and had never commanded men in conditions like these. McClintock gave him six men, two sledges, forty-two days of provisions, and instructions to proceed to Cape Walker, then to coast southward and westward along Boothia’s shore, probing for signs of Franklin’s party or of natives who might have encountered them. The latitude of Cape Walker—77°30’N—placed it at the theoretical exit of the Northwest Passage, the point where Franklin, had he succeeded, would have emerged into open water. Hobson understood the logic. He also understood that logic had guided searchers to disappointment for thirteen years.

They left before dawn on 18 May, hauling 680 pounds per sledge across sea ice still uncertain with the season. The temperature stood at minus 23 Fahrenheit. Within three days they had covered forty miles, reaching Cape Walker on the 21st, where Hobson planted a cairn with a record of their passage. The coast beyond ran southwest, offering a choice: continue overland across Boothia’s interior, or follow the shoreline with its promise of game and possible native contact. Hobson chose the shore. The choice would prove decisive, though not in any way his instructions had anticipated.

The Boothia Peninsula in late May presents a landscape of absolute refusal. Granite ridges rise from sea ice still locked to the land, their faces stripped of soil by glaciation and wind. The only movement comes from the parties of eider ducks moving north, and from the sledges of men who have no business being there. Hobson’s party made twelve to fifteen miles daily when the surface held, less when pressure ridges forced detours inland.

They cached provisions at regular intervals, marking each deposit with a cairn and a note, calculating the mathematics of return against the mathematics of advance. By 25 May they had reached a latitude of 70°20’N, having traced the western shore of Boothia to a point where the coast turns decisively westward toward the mouth of the Great Fish River. Here, if anywhere, they might encounter the Inuit who ranged this coast in pursuit of seal and caribou.

The encounter came on 28 May, though “encounter” misrepresents the quality of the meeting. Hobson’s journal records the sequence with the flat precision of a man trained to observe without interpreting prematurely. At approximately 3 p. m., while the party was encamped on the sea ice near a small island, two figures appeared on the shore to the south. Through his glass, Hobson distinguished them as Inuit—an adult male and a boy—walking toward the camp with the unhurried pace of people who have spotted strangers before the strangers spotted them. Hobson ordered his men to remain with the sledges and advanced alone, carrying no weapon, offering in his outstretched hands a strip of tobacco and a knife.

The distance closed to twenty yards, then ten. The Inuit man stopped, assessed, and waited. Hobson placed the tobacco on a rock and stepped back. The man approached, examined the offering, and produced from his own pouch a strip of dried meat, which he laid beside the tobacco. The exchange had begun.

What followed over the next forty-eight hours was a transaction in which nothing was given freely and everything was measured. The Inuit man—Hobson never learned his name, recording him only as “the first native”—was joined by four others from a camp of three families concealed behind a low ridge. They had been watching the British party for two days, debating whether these qallunaat represented threat or opportunity. Their decision to reveal themselves was pragmatic: the strangers carried metal, and metal had become scarce in this country since the disappearance of the ships that had once brought it.

Hobson had brought an interpreter, a man named Peterson who had learned some Inuktitut during previous Arctic service. The interpretation was fragmentary, dependent on gestures, repetition, and the slow building of mutual vocabulary. But certain words emerged with clarity. Ships. White men. Dead. South. West. The Inuit had seen ships—two ships—frozen in ice to the south and west of where they now stood. They had seen white men walking away from those ships, dragging boats across the ice. They had seen the white men die, one by one, until none remained to be seen.

This was not new information in its general outline. John Rae had brought similar testimony back to London in 1854, and had been denounced for his pains. What was new was the specificity of place and the material evidence that accompanied it. The Inuit produced objects: a silver spoon bearing the crest of an English family, a button from a naval uniform, a knife with a bone handle, and—most striking to Hobson’s eye—a pair of boots made from seal skin, lined with fur, and cut to a shape that suggested they had been fashioned for European feet.

The boots became the center of the transaction. Hobson wanted them. The Inuit wanted metal—knives, needles, the small tools that made life possible in this economy. The negotiation extended through an afternoon and into the following morning, with Peterson struggling to convey that the British sought information, not merely goods, and the Inuit understanding perfectly that information was itself a form of goods, to be traded or withheld according to advantage. By the morning of 29 May, Hobson had acquired the boots, two silver spoons, a knife, and several smaller items. In exchange he had given two knives, a hand saw, two files, two gimlets, a quantity of nails, and strips of iron that could be worked into tools. The imbalance of the exchange did not concern him. What mattered was the documentary record he could now assemble.

He examined the boots in the light of his tent that evening. They were constructed with the skill of people who had made thousands of such garments: seal skin for the outer, caribou fur for the lining, sinew thread drawn tight in waterproof seams. But the dimensions were wrong for Inuit feet—too long in the sole, too narrow in the heel. They had been made, the Inuit indicated through gesture, for a white man who had traded for them or received them as gift during the years when contact was still possible. The man was dead now. Many white men were dead. The boots had passed through several hands before reaching this camp.

Hobson’s journal entry for 29 May runs to four pages, the longest continuous narrative he would write during the expedition. He recorded the location precisely—latitude 69°42’N, longitude 92°15’W—along with a description of the Inuit camp, the number of inhabitants, their apparent health and resources, and the full inventory of traded items. He noted the Inuit’s repeated gestures toward the southwest, toward a land they called “King William,” where the ships had been seen and where the white men had walked until they could walk no further. The geographical intelligence was crude—no Inuit had traveled to the ships and returned to tell of it, their knowledge coming secondhand from other bands who had ranged farther—but it converged on a single point. King William Island. The place McClintock’s first party had failed to reach.

The party remained with the Inuit through 30 May, trading for additional information and observing the routines of a life adapted to conditions that would kill unprotected Europeans in hours. Hobson watched a woman prepare seal meat, her knife—obtained, perhaps, from some previous contact with Franklin’s men—moving with economical precision. He noted the construction of the snow houses, the storage of meat in ice caches, the constant attention to clothing that meant the difference between survival and death. The Inuit, for their part, studied the British with equal attention, particularly their sledges and the dogs that pulled them. The British dogs were starving, their rations insufficient for the labor demanded. The Inuit dogs were fat on seal meat. The contrast was not lost on either party.

On 31 May, Hobson made the decision to turn back. His provisions were half consumed, the distance to the Fox still substantial, and the information he carried—geographical, material, testimonial—more valuable than any additional days of exploration could yield. He cached a record of his findings at the Inuit camp, marked with a cairn and a note for any subsequent party, and began the return journey. The Inuit watched them go with the same neutrality they had displayed on arrival. They had traded. They had gained metal. They had given away objects that held no practical value for them, only narrative weight—the story of the ships, the story of the dead men, the story that would now travel eastward to other qallunaat who would pay other prices to hear it.

The return journey occupied eleven days. Hobson drove his men hard, knowing that delay meant consumption of provisions that could not be replaced, that every hour on the ice increased the risk of breakup or storm. They reached the Fox on 11 June, having traveled approximately 450 miles and been absent twenty-four days. McClintock met them at the sledge berth, his inspection of the relics immediate and silent. He handled the seal-skin boots with particular attention, turning them in his hands, measuring the sole against his own foot, noting the construction and the wear patterns. Then he asked Hobson to repeat, word for word, everything the Inuit had communicated about location.

The interview extended late into the evening. McClintock’s journal records his own process of assessment: the reliability of Inuit testimony, previously established by Rae but disputed in London; the material corroboration offered by the traded objects; the geographical convergence on King William Island as the site of the disaster. He faced a decision that would shape the remainder of the expedition. The Fox could remain at Port Kennedy through another winter, sending out additional sledging parties in the spring of 1859. Or he could risk an early departure, attempting to reach King William Island by water before the ice closed in, establishing a base from which to search the area the Inuit had identified. The first option was safe, conventional, consistent with Admiralty instructions. The second was desperate, dependent on ice conditions that could destroy the ship, and potentially decisive.

Hobson’s testimony tipped the balance. Not the testimony alone—the objects mattered, the boots and spoons that could be displayed in London, that could survive the testimony funnel where raw narrative might not. McClintock understood the evidentiary economy. Rae had brought back stories and had been accused of credulity or worse. McClintock would bring back things—things that could be photographed, catalogued, exhibited, believed. The seal-skin boots, sized for a European foot and made by Inuit hands for a man now dead, embodied the convergence of cultures and catastrophes that had consumed Franklin’s expedition. They were the material proof that the testimony funnel required.

The redirection of search effort followed with institutional speed. McClintock’s report to the Admiralty, dispatched by the first available whaler in August 1858, emphasized the geographical intelligence: the natives had all indicated King William’s Land as the scene of the disaster. His private journal, written for eventual publication, dwelt longer on the encounter itself—the caution of the initial contact, the slow building of trade relations, the remarkable intelligence of the Inuit in conveying information without shared language. Both accounts centered on Hobson’s achievement, the lieutenant who had gone where McClintock’s first party had failed, who had established the human connection that charts and instruments could not.

Lady Jane Franklin, reading McClintock’s dispatch in London during the autumn of 1858, seized on its implications with the energy that had sustained her through thirteen years of advocacy. She had funded the Fox expedition privately, raised £2, 000 by subscription, and retained theoretical control of its operations despite McClintock’s naval command. Now she pressed the Admiralty for additional support, for the dispatch of supply ships to Baffin Bay, for official recognition of the breakthrough her money had purchased. The seal-skin boots became, in her correspondence, emblematic of the larger truth: that Franklin’s men had not simply vanished but had survived, had traded, had walked, had died in a specific place that could now be found and honored. The boots were humble objects, made of animal skin and sinew, yet they carried the weight of national narrative that Lady Jane had spent her life constructing.

The Admiralty’s response was measured, constrained by the fiscal conservatism of a government recovering from the Crimean War’s expenses and the Indian Mutiny’s demands. They would not send additional ships. They would extend McClintock’s authorization to continue operations through 1859. They would await further evidence before committing to the full-scale expedition that Lady Jane requested. The seal-skin boots, displayed in the Admiralty’s chart room alongside Hobson’s journal and the silver spoons, persuaded officials that something had been found. They did not yet persuade them that everything could be known.

The Fox remained at Port Kennedy through the winter of 1858–59, its crew reduced by scurvy and cold to the essential minimum required for survival. McClintock used the months to plan the next phase of operations, studying the ice charts, calculating the probabilities of an early breakup, preparing the sledges and dogs for the decisive push. Hobson, recovered from the physical exhaustion of his journey, worked with him, translating his field experience into operational plans. The seal-skin boots hung in McClintock’s cabin, a visible reminder of what waited to be found, of the men who had worn them and the men who had made them and the distance—cultural, geographical, temporal—that separated the living from the dead.

In April 1859, sled parties set out from Fox to search on King William Island. On 5 May, the party led by Lieutenant William Hobson discovered the Victory Point Note, which detailed the abandonment of Erebus and Terror, death of Franklin and other crew members, and the decision by the survivors to march south. The document was dated 25 April 1848. Hobson found it on 5 May 1859. The interval—eleven years and ten days—measured the distance between the event and its discovery, between the last official act of Franklin’s expedition and its first recovery by those sent to find it.

The seal-skin boots had led here. The Inuit testimony, materialized in objects and confirmed by documents, had provided the geographical key that thirteen years of searching had failed to locate.

Hobson copied the record, replaced it in the cairn with his own note of discovery, and began the journey back to report what he had found. The boots remained in his pack, still humble, still made of seal skin and sinew, now carrying an additional layer of meaning. They were the link between the written record and the human experience it summarized, between the official narrative of exploration and the actual conditions of survival and death in the Arctic.

A man had worn them, had traded for them, had perhaps died in them. His name was not recorded in Hobson’s journal, nor in the Victory Point document, nor in any subsequent account. The boots stood for him, as they stood for all the unnamed who had made and traded and worn them, whose lives had intersected with the grand designs of empire at the moment of empire’s failure.

The material relics and the specific geographical intelligence they carry now direct all subsequent efforts toward King William Island.
