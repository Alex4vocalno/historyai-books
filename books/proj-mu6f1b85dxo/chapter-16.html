Chapter 16

The Captain’s Private Papers

The Admiralty’s official verdict appeared in April 1859, closing the books on active search while sealing away the physical evidence in vaults and storehouses. Yet that same month, in a London drawing room, Lady Jane Franklin held a letter her husband had written fourteen years before, its folds worn soft by her own hands. The document had never been published. She had quoted from it in parliamentary lobbies and fundraising appeals, but always selectively, and always with an eye to the effect produced. Now, with the Admiralty’s communiqué establishing the authorized version of events, she faced a new calculation: what to do with the private voice that contradicted, or at least complicated, the public icon.

The letter from Whalefish Islands, dated 12 July 1845, sat in her archive alongside others from the same final voyage. Franklin had written it during the last stop before entering Baffin Bay, when the expedition was still intact, still hopeful, still proceeding according to plan. The handwriting was his own, the prose measured and warm, the references to domestic matters—gardens, acquaintances, the price of coal—interspersed with observations about ice conditions and the behavior of the steam engines. Nothing in it suggested panic or premonition. But neither did it display the serene certainty that the official narrative would attribute to him. The letter spoke of success as conditional, dependent on factors beyond his control: “if the ice permits,” “should the season prove favorable,” “barring unforeseen obstacles.” These were the qualifications of a man who had been defeated by the Arctic before and understood that it could happen again.

Lady Franklin had known this voice. She had lived with it for thirty years of marriage, through his earlier expeditions and their aftermaths, through the hunger and the honors, through the transformation of a naval officer into a national figure. The public knew a different man: “the man who ate his boots,” the hero of the Coppermine disaster, the steady commander who had brought his men through starvation by force of character. Her husband’s private letters from the 1820s and 1830s, which she had begun to release in the 1850s, supported this image in their essentials—showing struggle, perseverance, faith in divine providence—while adding a dimension of human frailty that made the heroism more affecting. The 1845 correspondence was different. It contained no disaster to overcome, no trial to demonstrate character. It showed a commander embarking on an enterprise whose risks he understood better than his sponsors did.

She did not destroy the letters. She filed them, annotated them, and in some cases copied extracts for circulation to trusted correspondents. The mechanism of this circulation was personal and political: a letter shown to an MP who might support a new search, a passage quoted to a journalist who could be relied upon to use it appropriately, a summary sent to a naval officer whose influence she needed. The full texts remained hers, their existence known to a widening circle but their contents controlled. This was not secrecy in the service of deception, or not only that. It was the management of grief and reputation in a culture that demanded both emotional truth and public propriety, and that offered no clear guidance when the two conflicted.

The contrast between this private archive and the official record sharpened through the late 1850s. When McClintock returned from the Fox expedition in September 1859, he brought the Victory Point record: two documents written on the standard Admiralty form, their text brief and their implications devastating. “Sir John Franklin died on the 11th June 1847,” the first statement read, in the handwriting of Captain Fitzjames. The second, added by Crozier, reported the deaths of nine officers and fifteen men and announced the survivors’ intention to abandon the ships and march south. These were official communications, composed according to naval protocol even in extremity, and they entered the record as such. The Admiralty printed them in the London Gazette, reproduced them in parliamentary papers, and incorporated them into the narrative that would be taught in schools and inscribed on memorials.

What they did not contain was as significant as what they did. No description of Franklin’s death—cause, circumstances, last words. No explanation of why the ships had been abandoned, or why the retreat had been directed toward the Back River rather than east toward known whaling grounds. No acknowledgment of the conditions that had reduced 129 men to 105, and 105 to the unknown number who set out on foot. The Victory Point record was a chronology, not an account. It established facts without interpreting them, and in so doing it invited interpretation that would serve institutional interests. Franklin died in command, having led his expedition to within sight of its goal. His officers carried forward his mission until forced by circumstances to retreat. The disaster was environmental, not human; ice and fate, not error or misjudgment, had determined the outcome.

This official Franklin required suppression of alternative testimony. The Inuit accounts that McClintock had gathered, describing starving men, possible cannibalism, and the abandonment of boats and equipment in states of disorder, were acknowledged in the expedition report but dismissed as unreliable. The physical evidence—the boat with its two skeletons on King William Island, the scattered relics suggesting desperate improvisation rather than organized retreat—was catalogued without narrative commentary. The Victory Point record stood alone as the authorized voice from the scene, its naval form lending it authority that oral testimony and material fragments could not challenge.

But the private papers continued to circulate. James Fitzjames’s letters to his sisters, preserved against his fabricated gentry background and his illegitimate birth, reached the public through a family memoir published in 1860. The memoir was protective, emphasizing Fitzjames’s dedication and charm, but it could not entirely conceal the anxiety that had accompanied his departure. He had recruited the crew personally, selecting men whose fitness he vouched for, and this responsibility weighed on him. His letters spoke of the Northwest Passage as “the last great problem,” a phrase that combined ambition with finality. He had encountered ice conditions in the Canadian Arctic in 1842 that suggested the difficulty of Franklin’s proposed route, and he had communicated these concerns to his commander.

The memoir did not quote directly from these warnings. It paraphrased, contextualized, softened. But its existence created a space of doubt around the official narrative that had not existed before. Readers who encountered both the Admiralty’s communiqué and the Fitzjames family’s account faced a choice between two versions of the same events, neither fully documented, each carrying different kinds of authority. The official version had institutional weight; the private version had the persuasion of intimate detail.

More significant still were the traces of Francis Crozier. In 1845, Crozier had joined Captain Sir John Franklin as captain of the Terror on the Franklin expedition to traverse the last unnavigated section of the Northwest Passage. Crozier himself had been offered the command of the expedition but, with what contemporaries called “characteristic modesty,” he had deferred to Franklin. This deference, praised in the official record as a model of naval subordination, took on darker coloring as more became known.

Crozier had served in the Arctic before, under James Clark Ross in 1839–1843, and had formed judgments about ice conditions, magnetic variation, and the reliability of existing charts that differed from Franklin’s. He had accepted second position rather than insist on leadership he may have believed himself better qualified to exercise. And he had inherited command of a disaster that consumed him along with the men he led.

After Franklin’s death on 11 June 1847, Crozier took over as expedition leader, a fact confirmed by the Victory Point Note.

Crozier’s private papers, such as survived, were fragmentary. A journal from the Antarctic voyage, carried by one of the Terror’s officers and recovered from the King William Island boat, contained marginal annotations in his hand. These were technical notes for the most part: comparisons of ice conditions, observations about compass variation, corrections to published positions.

But one passage, flagged by a clerk in the Admiralty’s hydrographic department during the 1860 review of recovered materials, suggested a more fundamental skepticism. Crozier had questioned the accuracy of William Parry’s 1829 survey of the Boothia Peninsula—the survey on which Franklin’s route depended. The maps showed open water where Crozier’s experience suggested ice, favorable currents where he had found obstruction.

This was not the marking of a man who accepted authority uncritically. It was the trace of an officer who had learned to verify, to doubt, to build his own picture of the Arctic environment against the official maps that had sent previous expeditions to destruction.

The significance of this annotation was not immediately apparent. McClintock, focused on the Victory Point record and the physical relics, had noted the voyage book’s presence without analyzing its contents in detail. The hydrographic clerk who flagged it in 1860 lacked the standing to force reconsideration of the official narrative. The annotation entered the archive as a curiosity, a technical footnote to a settled verdict. But its existence was known, and its implications spread through the network of naval officers and Arctic veterans who maintained their own informal channels of information.

Lady Franklin found herself managing a double inheritance. She possessed her husband’s last letters, with their conditional hopes and their unspoken awareness of risk. She received, through her network of contacts, reports of other officers’ papers as families chose to share them. Her position was impossible in strict logic: she needed to maintain pressure for continued search, which required emphasizing the expedition’s importance and the possibility of survivors; she needed to defend her husband’s reputation, which required controlling information that might suggest poor judgment; and she claimed to honor the truth, even as that truth became increasingly complex.

In 1860, she made a calculated decision. Through a sympathetic press, she published a selection of Franklin’s letters from the 1820s and 1830s—the period of his earlier Arctic expeditions, when he had established his reputation through demonstrated perseverance. These letters showed Franklin as human, struggling, sometimes despairing, always enduring. They did not include the 1845 correspondence. That remained private, its contents known only to Lady Franklin and a few confidants. The published selection thus created a partial intimacy: readers could feel they knew Franklin’s inner life while remaining ignorant of his final, crucial uncertainties.

This partial release established a pattern. Private papers circulated in fragments, their full contents withheld by families who feared for reputations or by institutions who judged certain information inappropriate for public consumption. The result was a stratified archive: the official record, complete and authorized; the semi-official memoir, selective and protective; and the withheld document, whose existence was sometimes acknowledged but whose contents remained speculative.

The pressure of this stratification fell unevenly. For the Admiralty, it was manageable. The official narrative had institutional weight; private papers could be acknowledged as personal impressions without threatening the verdict of 1859. For the families, it was anguishing. They possessed documents that complicated the public story, yet lacked standing to force reconsideration. For the historical record, it was distorting. The evidence for a complete picture remained dispersed, its assembly requiring access and persistence that few possessed.

The mechanism by which private knowledge entered public consciousness was neither straightforward nor innocent. Lady Franklin interviewed veterans of the expedition’s preparation, recording their testimony in notes that she preserved but did not publish. One such interview, conducted in 1856 with a seaman who had visited the Erebus during fitting-out, preserved Fitzjames’s private assessment of his commander’s enthusiasm.

The seaman reported Fitzjames speaking of Franklin’s determination to push through the ice pack by August, despite warnings based on his own 1842 experience.

Lady Franklin never published this testimony. It remained in her papers, available to authorized biographers but excluded from public campaign materials. The reason is discernible: it attributed to her husband a willful disregard of expert advice, a determination to proceed according to his own timeline. This was not the Franklin she needed to present.

Yet she preserved the record, perhaps unable to destroy evidence she considered genuine, perhaps anticipating fuller disclosure when the political moment allowed.

By 1865, the accumulation of such withheld documents had created a distinctive condition in the historiography of the Franklin expedition. Lady Franklin, now seventy-three and in failing health, authorized publication of a selection of her husband’s 1845 correspondence. The timing reflected her awareness that her archives would pass to heirs who might not share her protective instincts. The selection was careful—omitting passages that suggested doubt or error—but it included the Whalefish Islands letter with its conditional clause: “I have every hope of success, if the ice permits.” The accompanying editorial emphasized serene faith in providence. The letter itself spoke of practical contingency.

This was the limit of what private papers could achieve in their controlled circulation. They could complicate, suggest, intimate. They could create space for doubt without requiring its full expression. They could preserve evidence that institutions found inconvenient. What they could not do, given their fragmentation and the interests that governed their release, was overturn the official narrative or force systematic reconsideration.

Yet their influence was cumulative. Each document that entered the public sphere added weight to the scale against official certainty. Each family memoir that quoted from withheld letters created readers who suspected more remained unsaid. Each naval officer’s hint of reservations entertained by experienced officers maintained a constituency for revision.

The documents that survived the ice did so imperfectly, bearing material witness to conditions that official reports smoothed away. Fitzjames’s letters, preserved in a waterproof tin discovered beneath a cairn on King William Island, bore stains of salt and time, their ink feathered at edges where moisture had seeped through waxed seals despite careful preparation.

These physical traces spoke a language of contingency that standardized forms could not capture—the handwriting growing tighter in later correspondence, the pressure of the pen visibly heavier, as if composition required greater physical effort against cold fingers or mounting psychological resistance.

When Lady Franklin held these pages in her Portland Place study, she handled not merely testimony but artifacts of deterioration, objects that had survived where their author had perished. Unlike the printed Admiralty forms used for the Victory Point record, these private documents lacked standardized margins and naval protocol that conferred bureaucratic legitimacy; they were messy, human, susceptible to water damage and fading memory, resistant to administrative ordering that turned tragedy into policy lessons.

Between Crozier and Fitzjames existed a correspondence that never entered official logbooks but circulated in fragments among surviving relatives, revealing tensions invisible in command hierarchies. As captain of the Terror and second-in-command of the expedition, Crozier maintained parallel observations distinct from Franklin’s optimistic dispatches forwarded to Whalefish Islands. His informal memoranda to Fitzjames contained technical anxieties that could not be voiced directly to Franklin without risking insubordination charges under Articles of War.

Crozier questioned specifically the placement of magnetic observatories on both vessels, noting in a pencil annotation carried by Lieutenant Irving’s recovered body that proximity of iron steam engines would corrupt compass readings precisely where navigation required greatest precision for threading uncharted channels.

This was accumulated wisdom from his Antarctic service under Ross in 1839–1843, where he had watched similar instrumentation fail under comparable magnetic conditions; the memorandum’s existence suggested senior officers had identified critical vulnerabilities before entering pack ice, yet lacked hierarchical standing—or perhaps institutional courage—to force alterations to Franklin’s predetermined route. Fitzjames, as captain of Erebus, had been responsible for recruiting expedition personnel and had selected many men he knew personally, including George Henry Hodgson.

Lady Franklin’s editorial hand fell heaviest upon those passages revealing her husband’s dependence upon subordinates’ expertise rather than his own judgment. In preparing materials for sympathetic publication in 1860, she excised a paragraph from Franklin’s final letter describing his consultation with Crozier regarding ice conditions in Baffin Bay—a consultation resulting in the decision to hug the western shore rather than seek open water eastward as originally planned. The omission was strategic: acknowledging Franklin had altered course based on advice would suggest uncertainty in his original navigation plan, whereas public narrative required a commander whose decisions flowed from unerring instinct confirmed by experience. She retained his meticulous provisioning calculations while removing his admission that tinned meat from Goldner’s factory had arrived with visible solder defects even before departure; these calculated silences created a composite portrait of preparedness without vulnerability, ensuring Franklin appeared as architect rather than adapter of strategy.

The Admiralty processed these private intrusions with bureaucratic efficiency designed specifically to neutralize their interpretive impact. When families submitted letters for potential inclusion in official records, the hydrographic office extracted only navigational coordinates and meteorological observations, filing personal observations under “miscellaneous” headings guaranteeing archival obscurity alongside obsolete charts. Captain Washington’s compilation of Arctic papers included Franklin’s official instructions but relegated Fitzjames’s warnings about crew inexperience with steam machinery to an appendix marked “private correspondence—not for general circulation.” This segregation ensured future historians encountered expedition paperwork first as administrative triumph, only later—if diligent—discovering human reservations accompanying its launch; physical documents migrated from family drawing rooms to Admiralty chart rooms where temperature controls designed to preserve leather-bound maps accelerated decay of personal letters written on inexpensive rag paper unsuited to long storage.

By 1865 these accumulated withholdings created a phantom archive haunting authorized versions without displacing them.

Sherard Osborn, who had commanded one of the 1850s search expeditions, built his post-naval career on this constituency. In lectures and articles through the 1860s, he developed a critique that stopped just short of accusing the dead. He spoke of mismanagement in the search, of evidence misread, of Inuit testimony unjustly dismissed. He mentioned, without naming sources, private journals that recorded mounting concern during the voyage north. His audience understood that he spoke from access they lacked. The private papers, circulating in this half-visible manner, created an expectation of future revelation that kept the Franklin case alive beyond what the official record could sustain.

The intimate voices from private papers had entered the record, creating a new pressure for professional circles to reconcile or suppress them.
