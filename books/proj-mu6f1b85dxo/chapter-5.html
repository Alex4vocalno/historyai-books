Chapter 5

The First Mark on the Map

Four naval squadrons pushed into Lancaster Sound under the metallic glare of the August sun, their crews scanning the shoreline for any irregularity in the rocks or the snow. The machinery of the search was in motion, a routine refined over five years—examine, record, report, proceed—as timetables from distant London offices dispatched vessels north with the regularity of a seasonal migration. The ice had not yielded Franklin. But on a shingle beach at the edge of Wellington Channel, where Captain Erasmus Ommanney of HMS Assistance led a sledging party across the frost-shattered gravel, it began to yield the first physical mark that would convert rumor into history.

Captain Erasmus Ommanney of HMS Assistance led a sledging party across the shingle beach of Beechey Island on August 23, 1850. The island sat at the eastern entrance to Wellington Channel, a low crescent of gravel and frost-shattered rock that offered one of the few anchorages on a coast otherwise walled by ice. Ommanney had dispatched his boats according to the standard pattern—close inspection of every inlet, every beach, every elevation that might conceal cairn or signal or shelter. The party moved in the diffused light of the Arctic summer, when the sun circled the horizon without setting and shadows lost their definition. They were looking for anything that broke the monotony of stone and snow: a scrap of canvas, a vertical line where nothing vertical should exist, a human arrangement in a landscape that had never known it.

What they found were wooden markers. Three of them, aligned on a slight rise above the beach, each bearing a name and date carved into the headboards. The wood was weathered but intact, preserved by cold and aridity against the rot that would have claimed them in any temperate climate. The inscriptions read: John Torrington, January 1, 1846; John Hartnell, January 4, 1846; William Braine, April 3, 1846. Torrington had been a petty officer, twenty years old. Hartnell was an able seaman, twenty-five. Braine was a Royal Marine, thirty-two. All three had died during the winter of 1845–1846, the first winter of Franklin’s expedition.

The discovery did not arrive alone. Within days, other search vessels converging on the same waters found corroborating evidence. Captain William Penny of HMS Lady Franklin landed parties on the same beach. Robert Goodsir, surgeon on Penny’s brig, examined the graves and recorded their particulars. The masters of other vessels, operating independently in adjacent sectors, received reports of the find and redirected their search patterns toward Beechey Island. The Admiralty’s systematic sweep, designed to cover maximum coastline with minimum duplication, had produced an unplanned concentration: multiple expeditions, multiple command structures, multiple sets of sailing orders, all arriving at the same small island within weeks of one another.

The convergence was not coincidence but the mathematical outcome of the search protocol itself. The Admiralty had divided Lancaster Sound and its approaches into sectors, assigned each sector to specific vessels, and instructed all captains to examine any site showing evidence of human habitation. Beechey Island sat at the junction of multiple sectors, visible from several approaches, offering the combination of accessible beach and elevated ground that the instructions specifically prioritized. The graves were not hidden. They had been placed where they could be found—by Franklin’s own men in 1845, establishing a depot for a return journey that never happened. The searchers in 1850 were reading a message composed for readers who were not them, discovering a burial ground that had been intended as a waypoint rather than a terminus.

Ommanney’s party examined the campsite that extended behind the graves. They found the remains of a winter establishment: stone walls erected as windbreaks, the foundations of a smithy, scattered tools and equipment, vast quantities of preserved meat in lead-soldered tins. The tins represented the industrial solution to the ancient problem of Arctic sustenance. Goldner’s Patent Preserved Provisions, manufactured by a London contractor, had filled the holds of Erebus and Terror with what the Admiralty believed to be a three-year supply of nutrition. On Beechey Island, the searchers found stacks of these tins—some opened and consumed, others still sealed, others apparently discarded. The quantity suggested both abundance and failure: enough food to indicate that the expedition had not starved immediately, enough waste to suggest that something had gone wrong with the provisions themselves.

The bodies presented a different kind of evidence. Torrington, Hartnell, and Braine had been buried with care, in wooden coffins sunk into the permafrost, the graves marked and recorded. This was not the haste of disaster but the procedure of a functioning naval establishment, following regulations for burial at sea or on land, maintaining the forms of military order in circumstances of extremity. The headboards gave dates and names; the alignment of the graves suggested an established cemetery rather than emergency interment; the depth and construction indicated time and labor that could have been devoted to other purposes. Franklin’s expedition, in this first winter, had still possessed the capacity for ritual, for record-keeping, for the long view.

Yet the men were young. Torrington at twenty, Hartnell at twenty-five, Braine at thirty-two—none had reached the age where Arctic service typically extracted its toll. Naval expeditions expected losses: scurvy, exposure, the cumulative damage of cold and damp and inadequate diet. But these deaths, clustered in the first months of the expedition, suggested a different pattern. Something had weakened these men before the ice had time to do its ordinary work. The searchers noted this without yet understanding it, recorded the ages without yet drawing conclusions, sent their reports southward without yet knowing what questions to ask.

The exhumation and examination of the bodies became a matter of professional obligation. Goodsir, as surgeon, conducted the autopsies that naval protocol required. The cold had preserved what temperate climates would have destroyed: soft tissue, organs, the physical evidence of disease. The bodies were not skeletons. They were cadavers, frozen in the condition of death, offering information that bones alone could not provide. Goodsir recorded his findings with the precision that medical science demanded, noting discolorations, organ conditions, the state of the digestive tract. The lead-soldered tins, he observed, might have contributed to a condition that was not yet fully understood: chronic lead poisoning, accumulating slowly through contaminated food, producing symptoms that mimicked other diseases and resisted easy diagnosis.

Forensic examination in circumstances without precedent: Arctic exploration had produced dead before, but never in such preservation, never with such documentary apparatus, never with such immediate scientific attention. The graves of Beechey Island became a laboratory as well as a cemetery, a site where the methods of naval medicine confronted the specific conditions of polar death. What Goodsir learned, what Ommanney recorded, what the converging captains agreed upon, would establish the template for all subsequent investigation of the Franklin disaster. The search had found its first physical evidence, and that evidence demanded interpretation.

The interpretations multiplied as the information traveled. In London, the Admiralty received Ommanney’s dispatch through the chain of command that the search protocol established: ship to depot ship, depot ship to Greenland, Greenland to London, each transfer adding delay to the already substantial lag of Arctic communication. The news arrived in October 1850, two months after the discovery, filtered through the handwriting of clerks who had not seen the graves and the summary judgments of captains who had. The Board of Admiralty read that Franklin’s expedition had wintered on Beechey Island in 1845–1846, had buried three men, had left extensive provisions and equipment, and had proceeded westward into unknown waters. The official narrative adjusted to accommodate this information: the expedition had survived its first winter, had maintained order and supply, had continued its mission. The graves were regrettable but not alarming, consistent with the expected hazards of service in extreme climates.

Lady Jane Franklin received the news through private channels as well as public. Her husband’s expedition had been absent for five years. She had spent those years transforming private grief into public pressure, cultivating the network of influence that connected her to the Admiralty, the press, the scientific societies, the political establishment. The Beechey Island discovery gave her something concrete to work with: not the absence she had been fighting against, but a presence that required explanation. She studied the reports, questioned the captains on their return, commissioned her own analysis of what the graves implied. The three dead men became, in her hands, evidence of both survival and risk—proof that the expedition had functioned through its first winter, warning that it had not functioned perfectly, argument for the intensification of search rather than its conclusion.

The search vessels themselves did not linger at Beechey Island. The Admiralty’s instructions prioritized mobility over investigation: find traces, record them, proceed to the next sector, maintain the sweep. Ommanney left parties to complete the documentation while he pushed westward with Assistance, following the coastline of Devon Island toward the unexplored channels that Franklin might have attempted. Penny continued his independent operations, claiming priority for his own discoveries against the Admiralty’s official expedition. Other captains, arriving late, found the graves already examined and turned their attention to the broader question of where Franklin had gone after leaving Beechey Island. The concentration dispersed, following the logic of the search pattern, each captain competing for the discovery that would resolve the mystery.

What they left behind was a fixed point. Beechey Island, with its three graves and its abandoned tins and its stone windbreaks, became the first coordinate in the geography of Franklin’s disaster. Every subsequent search expedition would visit it, measure against it, use it as the starting point for projections westward. The winter of 1845–1846, recorded in wood and stone and frozen flesh, established the timeline that all later evidence would extend or complicate. The expedition had been there. It had buried its dead. It had consumed some of its provisions and discarded others. It had built structures for permanence and then abandoned them. These facts were not in doubt. What they meant, how they connected to the larger absence, remained the subject of investigation.

The forensic phase of the search had begun. Not the rescue phase, which had ended with the first sighting of the graves—no living men could have survived five years in the Arctic, and the searchers knew it. Not the recovery phase, which would require finding what remained of the ships and their crews. The forensic phase: the systematic examination of physical evidence, the construction of narrative from material remains, the translation of Arctic objects into London documents. The Admiralty’s hydrographers would chart the campsite with precision. Its medical officers would analyze Goodsir’s autopsy reports. Its clerks would file the accounts of tin consumption, of grave construction, of the distribution of equipment across the beach. Each piece of evidence would be checked, cross-referenced, debated in the journals and the committee rooms.

The lead in the tins became a particular focus. Goldner’s Patent Preserved Provisions had been controversial from the start, the contractor’s methods questioned by competitors and by some naval officers. The Beechey Island discovery gave substance to these suspicions: tins that had swollen, tins that had been discarded uneaten, tins that might have contaminated their contents with the solder that sealed them. The connection between lead and the three deaths could not be proven from the evidence available in 1850, but it could be suspected, discussed, added to the accumulating weight of concern. The expedition had carried the best technology of its age, and that technology might have been its undoing.

The youth of the dead men raised another question. Naval medicine understood scurvy, understood exposure, understood the gradual deterioration that Arctic service inflicted. It did not understand why healthy young men would die in the first winter of a well-supplied expedition, in circumstances that their officers described as orderly and successful. Goodsir’s autopsies suggested multiple causes: tuberculosis in Torrington’s case, possible heart disease in Hartnell’s, undetermined factors in Braine’s. The specific diagnoses mattered less than the pattern they suggested: vulnerability where resilience was expected, individual failure where collective endurance was required. The Franklin expedition had not been defeated by a single catastrophe. It had been eroded by accumulated damage, some of it perhaps self-inflicted, some of it perhaps unavoidable.

The searchers in 1850 could not pursue these implications. They were bound by their instructions, by the limited season of navigable water, by the competition among expeditions that the Admiralty had set in motion. They recorded what they found, sent their reports southward, and continued the sweep that the protocol demanded. The graves of Beechey Island became a reference point in their logs, a confirmation that the search was not futile, a reminder of what they were looking for. Each subsequent discovery would be measured against this first one: more recent or more ancient, more hopeful or more despairing, closer to or farther from the resolution that the Admiralty and the public demanded.

In London, the winter of 1850–1851 became a season of reassessment. The Beechey Island evidence forced acknowledgment that Franklin’s expedition had encountered serious difficulty—three dead in the first winter, provisions possibly compromised, the ships pushed westward into waters where no search had yet penetrated. But it also permitted continued hope: the expedition had functioned through that winter, had maintained the capacity for organized movement, had left records that suggested intention rather than desperation. The Admiralty authorized additional expeditions, additional funding, additional instructions to search the coastlines of King William Island and the channels beyond. Lady Jane Franklin intensified her campaign, using the concrete evidence of the graves to argue for more concrete action.

“The first traces of the missing ships,” ran one official summary—“a few scattered articles,” besides three graves—were discovered at Franklin’s winter quarters (1845–1846) on Beechey Island by Captain (afterwards Sir) Erasmus Ommanney of the “Assistance” in August 1851 and brought home to form “the basis” of a new phase of speculation and search. This was “the authorized version,” “the reduced account” that would enter “the official record.” It compressed months of investigation, multiple vessels, competing claims, and scientific uncertainty into “a single sentence” “of achievement.” The testimony funnel had done its work: “the raw experience” “of Arctic discovery,” with all its ambiguity and human cost—“had been shaped into admissible form.”

Yet the fixed point of Beechey Island and its incomplete evidence created an imperative that no official summary could satisfy. Three graves, dated, named, measured, and left behind; a winter camp abandoned to the ice; a trail leading westward into territory that remained blank on the Admiralty’s charts. The search had found its first mark on the map, but the map itself had grown larger with the finding, its empty spaces more demanding, its silence more articulate.

The institutions that had generated the search would now be required to sustain it indefinitely, their timetables and protocols adapted to a task that had no visible terminus. The ice had yielded a beginning, not an ending, and every subsequent expedition would set out from this beach knowing that the Arctic kept its secrets not by hiding them but by revealing them one at a time, each discovery opening into further mystery, each answer becoming a question that London would have to learn to keep asking.
