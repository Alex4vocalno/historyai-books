Chapter 28

The Letter in the Glass Case

Seen from above, the history of Arctic exploration is preserved in a climate-controlled vault beneath a museum in Ottawa, where temperature and humidity are fixed to halt the decay of paper and ink. Within this space, documents from different eras—a transcription of Inuit speech filtered through nineteenth-century categories, the Victory Point record extracted from a cairn, a brass cylinder, and the last known words of expedition leaders—now coexist. They have not ceased to generate interpretation but have entered a phase where preservation and access govern their existence, as the institutions once divided by their meaning now unite to ensure their physical survival.

From above, Ottawa appears as a grid of government buildings and frozen rivers, the parliamentary precinct at its center, the museum rising on the eastern edge where the city yields to parkland. The climate-controlled transport van that moved between the conservation laboratory and this building in the winter of 2025–2026 followed a route determined by traffic patterns and security protocols, its cargo invisible to the drivers of ordinary vehicles who shared the road. The van’s interior maintained the same parameters as the vault: temperature, humidity, vibration dampening, all monitored by sensors that transmitted data to a control center. This was the final stage of a journey that had begun on the coast of King William Island, when Hobson placed the cylinder in his sledge bag and began the haul back to the ship.

The route between vault and gallery followed the logic of modern museum design. A freight elevator capable of bearing seismic loads. A corridor with positive air pressure to exclude contaminants. A staging area where conservators in cotton lab coats inspected each object before transfer. In the winter of 2025–2026, this route was traversed repeatedly as curators prepared the permanent installation of what the museum designated the Franklin Collection.

The Victory Point note occupied the center of that installation, not because it offered the most information—McClintock’s published account had made its contents public since 1859—but because it had become, through successive acts of institutional attention, the primary coordinate against which all other evidence was measured. The graves on Beechey Island held bodies without names. The boat found on King William Island contained skeletons without context.

Rae had purchased relics from Inuit intermediaries, but their stories traveled through translation and transcription. The ships themselves, resting on the seabed, had yielded their location but not their final hours. Each discovery had been integrated into a narrative whose spine was the timeline that Crozier had established, the dates of wintering and departure and death that filled the margins of the standard form: the ships wintered at Beechey Island in latitude 74° 43′ 28″ N., longitude 91° 39′ 15″ W. after ascending Wellington Channel to latitude 77°, then proceeded south-west to winter in the ice at latitude 70°5’N, longitude 98°23’W in 1847.

The installation opened to the public on a morning in early 2026. Visitors entered through a corridor lined with reproductions of Admiralty charts, the successive search routes of 1848–1859 traced in faded ink, each expedition’s failure to find the ships marked by the dates of its return. The corridor turned, and the lighting changed, and the Victory Point note appeared behind glass, the two sheets mounted at a slight angle so that the handwriting could be read from either side of the case. The museum had chosen not to reproduce the text at full size on an adjacent panel, nor to offer a simplified narrative of what the note contained.

Instead, a smaller display case held the brass cylinder, its surface still bearing the scratches that Hobson’s knife had made when he forced it open in the presence of McClintock and the assembled officers of the Fox. The juxtaposition was deliberate: the container and its contents, the act of opening and the state of preservation, the nineteenth-century urgency of information extraction and the twenty-first-century commitment to material survival.

The note itself was fragile. The paper had darkened where moisture had reached it, and the ink had feathered in places where the surface sizing had degraded. The standard form that the Admiralty provided for such records—printed in London, distributed to every ship engaged in polar exploration—had never been designed for the conditions in which it was finally used. Crozier’s hand was visible in the first entry, the careful formation of letters that recorded the ships’ position on 28 May 1847, the latitude and longitude measured with instruments now themselves preserved in the museum’s collection. The entry stated: ‘H.M.S. hips Erebus and Terror Wintered in the Ice in Lat. 70°5’N Long. 98°23’W Having wintered in 1846-7 at Beechey Island in Lat 74°43’28”N Long 91°39’15”W After having ascended Wellington Channel to Lat 77° and returned by the West side of Cornwallis Island. Sir John Franklin commanding the Expedition. All well.’

Fitzjames’s hand appeared in the second entry, added in the margins after the first form had been filled, the writing compressed and hurried, the date of 25 April 1848 placed above the signature of the man who had by then assumed command. The museum’s label noted these facts without elaboration. It named the hands. It gave the dates. It did not explain what had happened between the two entries, nor what had happened after the second. That work was left to the visitor, or to the audio guide, or to the scholarly apparatus that the museum made available through its website, where high-resolution scans permitted examination of the paper’s fiber structure and the chemical composition of the ink.

This mode of presentation represented a decisive transformation in the status of the Franklin evidence.

For the first century after McClintock’s return, the relics had been dispersed: the Royal Naval Museum at Greenwich held some items, the Royal Geographical Society others, private collectors acquired what they could through auction or gift. The Victory Point note itself had passed through multiple hands, its provenance occasionally disputed, its physical condition subject to the varying competence of nineteenth-century preservation.

The museum installation of 2026 consolidated what had been scattered, and in doing so it enacted a claim about the proper location of historical memory. The note did not belong to the Admiralty that had printed the form, nor to the family that had inherited McClintock’s papers, nor to the scholars who had transcribed and interpreted its contents. It belonged to a public that was now understood to have a right of access not merely to information about the Franklin expedition, but to the material objects through which that information had been transmitted.

The glass case that held the note was engineered to specifications developed for the Declaration of Independence and the Magna Carta. The glazing consisted of laminated sheets with interlayers that filtered ultraviolet radiation, the frame incorporated sensors that would trigger alarms if temperature or humidity deviated from established parameters, the cavity behind the display surface was filled with an inert gas that would not react with the paper or support combustion. These were not visible features. What visitors saw was the note itself, the handwriting, the stains that marked its passage through ice and water and the hands of searchers. The case was designed to disappear, to present itself as pure transparency, and in this transparency it enacted a final transformation of the evidentiary process that had begun with Rae’s interviews in 1854 and McClintock’s sledging journeys in 1858–1859.

The search that had started with the breaking of seals—the brass cylinder forced open on King William Island, the graves opened on Beechey Island, the ships located by sonar and dive teams—now concluded with the making of a seal. The note was sealed against the future, its physical survival guaranteed by technologies that the men who had written it could not have imagined, its interpretation opened to a public that they had not anticipated. The museum’s director, in remarks delivered at the installation’s opening, described this as the completion of a national obligation.

The government of Canada, which had funded the searches that located Erebus in 2014 and Terror in 2016, which had supported the archaeological work that followed, which had negotiated the agreements that placed the wrecks under joint guardianship with the Inuit Heritage Trust, now assumed responsibility for the permanent preservation of what had been found. The wrecks were now protected as a combined National Historic Site called the Wrecks of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror National Historic Site.

The obligation was not merely to memory, the director suggested, but to the specific form of memory that material objects made possible. The note in its case was not a substitute for the expedition it recorded. It was a witness to that expedition’s material reality, a proof that the events of 1845–1848 had occurred in a world of paper and ink and brass, of measured positions and signed names, of institutional procedures that continued to function even as the institutions themselves failed.

This claim about material witness had been tested throughout the preparation of the installation. Conservators had debated whether to display the note with the same orientation it had held in the cylinder, or whether to flatten it for better visibility. They had considered whether to supplement the original with a facsimile that would permit closer approach, and had decided against this on the grounds that it would diminish the authority of the artifact itself. They had discussed lighting levels, display angles, the placement of the label, the design of the audio guide. Each decision involved a calculation about the proper balance between access and preservation, between the public’s right to see and the object’s need for protection. These calculations were not new.

They had been made, with different technologies and different institutional frameworks, by the Admiralty clerks who had filed McClintock’s report in 1859, by the Royal Geographical Society curators who had catalogued the relics in the 1870s, by the auction house employees who had handled the dispersal of private collections in the 1920s. What was new in 2026 was the scale of the commitment: the permanent installation, the climate-controlled case, the digitization of the entire archive, the legal designation of the wrecks as a National Historic Site.

The designation had been finalized in 2024, following negotiations between the government of Canada, the government of the United Kingdom, and the Inuit Heritage Trust. The agreement established a management plan for the two wreck sites, specifying how they would be monitored, what archaeological work would be permitted, how artifacts would be conserved and stored. It recognized Inuit traditional knowledge as a form of evidence equivalent to the documentary and archaeological record, and it established procedures for incorporating that knowledge into future management decisions.

This was the institutional culmination of a process that had begun with Rae’s interviews in 1854, continued through the scholarly work of David C. Woodman in the 1990s—whose books Unravelling the Franklin Mystery (1992) and Strangers Among Us (1995) reconstructed the final months from Inuit accounts—and gained new urgency with the discoveries of 2014 and 2016. The Inuit testimony that Victorian Britain had rejected, that Charles Dickens had denounced as the fabrication of savages, was now enshrined in international agreement as a legitimate source of historical understanding.

The museum installation acknowledged this transformation without dramatizing it. A separate gallery, adjacent to the Franklin Collection, presented the Inuit perspective through contemporary artwork and oral history recordings, through maps that showed the search routes as they had been experienced by the people who watched the ships pass and the men struggle ashore. The division between these galleries—documentary evidence in one, oral testimony in another—preserved a certain institutional separation that the management agreement had theoretically dissolved. But the physical proximity suggested a different relationship than the nineteenth-century archive had permitted.

The visitor who examined the Victory Point note, who studied the precise formation of Crozier’s letters and the compression of Fitzjames’s final entry, could walk twenty meters and hear an Inuit elder describe what her grandparents had seen on the coast of King William Island. The two forms of evidence remained distinct in their materiality: paper and ink on one side, voice and memory on the other. But they shared a space, and a temperature, and a regime of preservation that implied their equal claim to historical attention.

This sharing had been hard-won. The negotiations over the management plan had extended across years, involving disputes over sovereignty, over the ownership of artifacts, over the proper balance between scientific research and public access. The United Kingdom had maintained a residual claim based on the original Admiralty sponsorship of the expedition, a claim that Canadian negotiators had resisted on the grounds that the wrecks lay in Canadian waters and that the search effort had been predominantly Canadian-funded. The Inuit Heritage Trust had argued for a primary role in management, citing the continuous Inuit presence in the region and the specific traditional knowledge that had guided the searches that finally located the ships.

The agreement that emerged was a compromise, as such agreements always are, but it represented a decisive shift from the unilateral Admiralty control that had governed the search in the 1850s. The Franklin expedition was no longer a British naval matter. It was an international heritage site, jointly managed, permanently accessible, its interpretation open to multiple frameworks.

The digitization of the archive, completed in the same period, extended this principle of access to the documentary record. The museum’s website offered high-resolution images of every known Franklin relic, from the Victory Point note to the brass buttons recovered from the seabed, accompanied by full provenance information and scholarly commentary. The Admiralty records of the 1845–1859 searches, previously available only to researchers who could visit the National Archives at Kew, were now searchable from any location. Rae’s letters, McClintock’s journals, the Inuit testimony as recorded by Hall and Schwatka and later researchers: all of this material was presented without editorial framing, permitted to contradict and complicate itself as the original archive had done. The website’s architecture made no distinction between official and unofficial sources, between metropolitan and Indigenous perspectives, between the documents that had shaped Victorian understanding and the ones that had been ignored or suppressed.

This democratization of access represented the final working-out of a tension that had structured the entire Franklin search. From the beginning, information about the expedition had been subject to delay and filtration.

The Admiralty learned of the 1845 departure through official dispatches, but learned of the 1847 wintering only when a whaler spoke the expedition in Baffin Bay, and learned nothing at all of the catastrophe until Rae’s report in 1854. McClintock’s discovery of the Victory Point note in 1859 had arrived in London months after the fact, mediated by the limited bandwidth of Arctic communication. Even then, the note’s contents had been subject to interpretation and debate, its lacunae filled by speculation that the Admiralty could not control.

The digitized archive of 2026 eliminated these delays. The note was visible to anyone with an internet connection, its physical presence in Ottawa no longer a barrier to examination, its interpretation subject to the collective attention of a global public rather than the professional judgment of a small group of officials and scholars.

But the elimination of delay did not produce the elimination of uncertainty. The note remained as it had been: a partial record, written in extremis, containing dates and positions that could be checked against other sources but also containing silences that no amount of scrutiny could fill. The digitization made these silences more visible, not less. High-resolution imaging revealed the texture of the paper, the pressure of the pen, the places where the ink had dried before the writer could complete a stroke.

These material details generated new questions. Had Fitzjames paused before writing the final date? Had Crozier’s hand trembled when he recorded the latitude? The questions were unanswerable, but their unanswerability was itself a form of knowledge. The note testified to the conditions of its own production: the cold that made fingers stiff, the urgency that compressed thought, the institutional habit that persisted even when the institution itself had dissolved.

The museum’s installation made space for this uncertainty. The label beside the note did not summarize what it said; it quoted the text in full, including the standard-form language that Crozier had not needed to alter, the marginal additions that Fitzjames had crowded into available space, the signature that confirmed the transfer of command.

Visitors who expected a clear narrative of what had happened to the expedition found instead a document that recorded only positions and dates, that mentioned “Sir John Franklin” without stating that he was dead, that referred to the abandonment of the ships without explaining why.

The audio guide, developed in consultation with historians and archaeologists, offered possible interpretations without endorsing any of them. It described the controversy over Rae’s report, the Victorian refusal to accept Inuit testimony, the gradual scholarly reassessment that had culminated in Woodman’s work. It noted the archaeological evidence that had confirmed some details of the Inuit accounts and complicated others. It concluded with the observation that the note’s placement in the glass case represented not the solution of a mystery but the stabilization of a question.

This stabilization was the specific achievement of the 2026 installation. The Franklin search had always oscillated between two impulses: the desire for definitive knowledge, for a final answer to what had happened, and the recognition that such knowledge was perpetually deferred by the nature of the evidence. The Victorian Admiralty had resolved this oscillation by declaring the search closed after McClintock’s return, accepting the Victory Point note as sufficient explanation and relegating Inuit testimony to the margins of official history. The twentieth-century revival of interest, driven partly by amateur enthusiasts and partly by academic historians, had reopened questions without achieving consensus. The discoveries of 2014 and 2016 had provided new material for interpretation, but had also demonstrated how much remained unknown: the precise circumstances of the ships’ sinking, the distribution of survivors, the sequence of deaths on the retreat south. The museum installation did not resolve these uncertainties. It institutionalized them.

The glass case was the instrument of this institutionalization. Its transparency was not merely physical but epistemological: it displayed the note without interpreting it, made it visible without making it speak, preserved it for future questioning rather than closing the question with present authority. The inert gas that filled the cavity, the sensors that monitored the environment, the alarms that would trigger if conditions deviated: these technologies served a single purpose, to maintain the note in a state of permanent potential, available for interpretation that the museum could not predict and did not control. The case was a time machine in reverse, not transporting the visitor to 1848 but preserving 1848 for the visitor, holding open a connection to a moment that would otherwise have been lost.

This preservation had a cost, measured in the resources devoted to climate control and security, in the curatorial labor of monitoring and maintenance, in the opportunity cost of devoting gallery space to a single document when other stories might be told. The museum’s annual report acknowledged these costs without apology, presenting them as the necessary price of public access to foundational historical evidence. The report noted that the Franklin Collection had attracted visitation numbers exceeding projections, that the website had recorded downloads from countries that had no direct connection to the expedition, that scholarly requests for high-resolution images had come from disciplines ranging from forensic anthropology to the history of paper manufacture. These metrics of use validated the installation’s design, confirming that the note’s value lay not in its capacity to answer questions but in its capacity to generate them.

The final act of the installation’s preparation had been the sealing of the case. This occurred in January 2026, before the public opening, in a ceremony that the museum did not publicize.

The conservator who performed the operation had trained for years in the handling of fragile documents, had worked on manuscripts from the medieval period to the contemporary, had developed specific protocols for the Victory Point note based on its unique history of preservation and exposure. She wore cotton gloves that had been washed in deionized water and dried in a contaminant-free environment. She lifted the note from its temporary housing with tools designed to minimize physical contact, placed it on the custom-fitted support that would hold it in the display position, closed the case, and activated the sealing mechanism.

The click of the latch was audible in the silent gallery. The inert gas began to flow. The sensors registered the change in atmospheric composition. The note was now in its permanent state, visible but untouched, present but protected, available for interpretation but immune from alteration.

This act of sealing completed a cycle that had begun with the breaking of seals: the brass cylinder forced open by Hobson in 1859, the cairn dismantled to extract its contents, the archive opened to extract its testimony. Each of these acts had been destructive as well as revelatory, damaging the physical container in order to access the information it held. The museum’s seal reversed this logic, protecting the physical object at the cost of removing it from direct contact. The note could no longer be handled, examined under microscope, subjected to chemical analysis. These forms of investigation would proceed on the digitized images, on samples taken before the sealing, on the accumulating body of scholarly interpretation. The note itself was now fixed, its physical state stabilized, its contribution to knowledge shifted from the generation of new information to the maintenance of a material connection to the past.

The glass case under museum lights, the note visible within: this was the endpoint of the evidentiary process that Rae had initiated in 1854, when he wrote down what Inuit witnesses told him and transmitted their words to an institution that did not know how to receive them. The search that began with the breaking of seals ends with the making of a transparent one, permanently anchoring the story.
