Chapter 19

The Unbroken Circle of Testimony

The Victory Point note, found in a cairn of stone by Francis Leopold McClintock on 25 May 1859, measured eight inches by six, folded into a waterproof cylinder, its ink faded by three Arctic winters. The handwriting compressed by cold fingers, the message dated with precision: “28 of May 1847.” Latitudes and longitudes, deaths recorded, a decision to abandon ship. The language was naval, the format prescribed by Admiralty instruction.

It named Sir John Franklin as commanding, noted that all was well, then appended in a different hand and different ink the news that Franklin had died on 11 June 1847. The ships had been beset since 1846. The survivors would start tomorrow, 26 April 1848, for Back’s Fish River. This was the last written communication from the expedition. McClintock copied it, folded the original into his dispatch case, sailed for England, and deposited it in the official archive as definitive evidence.

Yet as the 19th century closed, this singular written record was not the only archive. A parallel, diffuse corpus of Inuit testimony, gathered over three decades by explorers like John Rae, Charles Francis Hall, and Frederick Schwatka, had been accumulating—a composite oral record waiting for the instruments that could verify its claims, instruments that would not arrive until the acoustic surveys of a later century.

Decades before those instruments, however, the question John Washington had penciled in 1875 had not been answered. But in 1880, as Washington sat in his Admiralty office collating hydrographic surveys, a different inquiry was already underway on the ice—one that would accumulate testimony the Navy lacked instruments to record. Lieutenant Frederick Schwatka of the United States Army had departed for Hudson Bay in the spring of 1878, traveling aboard the schooner Eothen, accompanied by a party that included Inuit guides and interpreters. Their destination was King William Island, and their method was not reconnaissance from shipboard but immersion: living among the Inuit, learning their place names, tracing their memories across terrain where no European had wintered on foot.

Between that frozen record and Schwatka’s expedition lay twenty-one years of accumulating, dismissed, and rediscovered testimony. The Inuit had been speaking of white men on the ice since at least 1850, when searchers first reached Beechey Island and found the graves of three sailors, their headboards dated 1846. Sir John Richardson, searching by sledge in 1848, had encountered no Inuit; James Clark Ross, commanding the naval expedition that same year, had found no living witnesses.

But John Rae, traveling for the Hudson’s Bay Company in 1854, had met an Inuk near Pelly Bay on 21 April who told of thirty-five to forty white men who had died of starvation on an island west of the Boothia Peninsula. The Inuk possessed a silver fork and spoon, a medal, and other articles that Rae recognized as European. The story included details that would haunt Victorian England: that the men had fallen upon each other, that their bones showed knife marks, that some had been eaten.

Rae arrived in London on 22 October 1854. His party was entitled to £10, 000 reward. But the Admiralty, which had offered that sum for information establishing the fate of the expedition, had not anticipated the form that information would take. Rae’s report, released to the press, described what he called the last dread alternative—cannibalism—as established fact through indigenous testimony. The outcry was immediate. Lady Jane Franklin enlisted Charles Dickens, who wrote in Household Words that Rae had gathered what he characterized as the wild tales of a herd of savages. The Inuit were unreliable, Dickens argued; the knife marks could indicate the preparation of bodies for burial, not consumption. The Admiralty accepted Rae’s geographical conclusions but not his interpretation of the final days. The reward was paid. The testimony was filed.

This was the inheritance Schwatka carried with him in 1878. He was not the first to attempt systematic collection.

Charles Francis Hall, the Cincinnati engraver turned Arctic enthusiast, had lived among Inuit communities between 1860 and 1869—first near Frobisher Bay on Baffin Island, then at Repulse Bay on the Canadian mainland. Hall spoke no Inuktitut when he arrived; he learned by pointing, gesturing, building vocabulary through repetition. He found camps, graves, and relics on the southern coast of King William Island—material confirmation that matched no European map. He concluded that some of the Franklin survivors had reached the Great Fish River, that bodies had been found in a boat, that an Inuk named Sŭ-pung-er had possessed a gold cap-band from a naval officer’s uniform.

Hall’s methods were erratic, his documentation sometimes contradictory, his personal obsession with the expedition’s fate shading his translations. But he established a precedent that Schwatka would extend: the investigator must become, temporarily, a member of the community that held the knowledge.

The Eothen reached Hudson Bay in August 1878. Schwatka’s party wintered at Depot Island, then traveled overland to King William Island, arriving in March 1879. They would remain through the following year, covering 2, 751 miles by sledge, living on the ice when necessary, returning repeatedly to Inuit settlements to verify, cross-reference, and map testimony against terrain. William Henry Gilder, a newspaper correspondent, kept the expedition journal; Schwatka directed the questioning. Their interpreters—one named Heinrich Klutschak, another Joe Ebierbing, who had accompanied Hall—translated not only words but concepts: the Inuit description of a ship’s location required understanding of ice movement, of how a vessel locked in floes might drift, sink, or be broken up across decades.

The testimony accumulated in fragments. An Inuk named Tookoolito, who had traveled with Hall, described seeing a very large ship with three masts, frozen in ice off the west coast of King William Island, full of books. The date was uncertain—sometime after 1850, before the ice claimed the hull. Another account, from a hunter named Poo-yet-ta, placed a party of white men dragging a boat across the ice south of where the ship was, their bodies later found in a tent.

The descriptions of the men themselves were consistent across multiple witnesses: thin, dark-faced, moving slowly, some falling and not rising, others continuing toward a river they never reached. The Inuit had not approached them at first, fearing the kabloonat and their unknown intentions. When they did approach, the men were dead or dying. The material they left behind—silverware, telescopes, guns without powder—circulated through Inuit trade networks for years.

Schwatka mapped these accounts against McClintock’s findings. The Victory Point note had established a departure point: the ships abandoned in 1848, the survivors heading south. The Inuit testimony suggested a more complex dispersal. Some men had remained with the ships longer than the note indicated. A group had attempted to return to the ships after the initial abandonment. The boat found by Hobson in 1859, containing two skeletons and a bizarre cargo of silk handkerchiefs, button polish, and a copy of Samuel Johnson’s The Vicar of Wakefield, represented not the main party but a splinter group, perhaps seeking the cache of food known to have been left at Cape Herschel. The Inuit had observed these movements from a distance, their own survival depending on precisely the kind of environmental knowledge that had failed the Europeans.

The physical evidence Schwatka collected corroborated specific details. At Starvation Cove on the Adelaide Peninsula, he found bones identified as human, scattered in patterns consistent with Inuit descriptions of where men had fallen. The location was seventy miles from the nearest point where McClintock had found relics—beyond the range that weakened survivors could reasonably have traveled in a single season, suggesting multiple waves of attempted escape or extended encampment. Schwatka measured, photographed, and left the remains in place; his expedition lacked the means for transport or formal identification. The bones would not be revisited for over a century.

What emerged from this accumulation was not a single narrative but a composite, one that required accepting the validity of oral transmission across thirty years. The Inuit did not date events by calendar; they located them by ice conditions, by the presence of particular animals, by the life stages of informants who had been children, then adults, then elders. Schwatka’s achievement was not translation alone but correlation: matching when the ice was bad to known weather patterns, when I was first married to demographic records, near the place where the ship was seen to McClintock’s charted locations. The result was a geography of disaster that the Admiralty’s archives could not contain—a map of decisions, reversals, and slow death traced through observation rather than documentation. It was an alternative narrative of the expedition’s final years, detailing the movement of starving men across a landscape they could not master.

The divergence between systems of knowledge became explicit in Schwatka’s handling of the cannibalism question. Where Rae had reported it directly, drawing the fire of Dickens and Lady Franklin, Schwatka encountered the same evidence embedded in longer narratives. Inuit witnesses described finding bodies with flesh removed from the bones, but emphasized that they had not observed the act itself, that the condition of the corpses suggested starvation rather than violence between the men. Schwatka, writing for American audiences less invested in Franklin’s heroic reputation, presented this testimony without the defensive framing that had characterized British responses. The physical evidence—the knife marks, the distribution of bones—supported multiple interpretations. The Inuit testimony offered context that written records could not: the specific desperation of men who had consumed their leather boots, their gun wadding, their remaining hope.

Hall’s earlier work, for all its irregularities, had established crucial place names that entered the standard nomenclature. Ogzeukejuak, the Inuit name for the western shore of King William Island, became the reference point for multiple accounts of ship sightings.

Keeuna, a hunter who had visited a wrecked vessel as a young man, provided descriptions of internal layout—cabins, stores, a dead man in a bunk—that suggested the Erebus or Terror had remained intact and accessible for years after abandonment. The temporal uncertainty was itself informative: Keeuna could not say whether his visit occurred in the 1850s or 1860s, indicating that the ships had persisted as landmarks, sources of timber and metal, across the period when European searchers were looking elsewhere. With the assistance of his guides Ipirvik and Taqulittuq, Hall had gathered hundreds of pages of Inuit testimony, including accounts of visits to Franklin’s ships and encounters with white men near Washington Bay.

The systematic quality of Schwatka’s collection exposed the limitations of earlier, more episodic approaches. McClintock had found the Victory Point note because he knew to look for a cairn; he found the boat with the skeletons because Inuit guides led him to it.

But he had not remained to learn the network of stories that connected these points, to understand that the boat represented one decision among many, that other parties had gone other directions, that the final catastrophe had been distributed across space and time rather than concentrated in a single desperate march. The Admiralty’s search protocol, with its emphasis on coastal navigation and visible wreckage, had been designed for ships that might still contain survivors, then for bodies that might still be identified. It had not been designed for the gradual dissolution of evidence that Schwatka documented—the scattering of relics through trade, the weathering of graves, the transformation of ships into sources of raw material for Inuit tools and shelter.

By 1880, when Schwatka’s expedition returned to New York, the accumulated testimony had reached a critical mass that could no longer be dismissed as wild tales. The published account, The Search for Franklin, included maps correlating Inuit place names with European coordinates, appendices of verbatim testimony, and Gilder’s photographs of terrain and relics. The response in England was muted; the Admiralty had officially closed the search, and Schwatka was an American officer pursuing a British disaster. But the evidence entered the literature, available to subsequent investigators who would approach it with different assumptions. In a speech at a dinner given in his honour by the American Geographical Society in 1880, Schwatka noted his expedition had made “the longest sledge journey ever made both in regard to time and distance” of eleven months and four days and 4, 360 km (2, 710 mi).

The contrast between official and unofficial knowledge systems persisted in institutional memory. The Hydrographic Office maintained its charts; the Royal Geographical Society published Schwatka’s findings without fully integrating them. What the accumulation demonstrates is the operation of a filter that persisted across decades: raw testimony, collected at cost and risk by individuals who accepted Inuit expertise as expertise, was gradually processed into forms that institutions could accommodate. The Victory Point note remained the primary document, its precise dates and naval format lending it authority that oral accounts, however detailed, could not match. The ships themselves remained unlocated, their positions described by Inuit witnesses but not verified by European sighting.

John Rae died from an aneurysm in Kensington, west London, on 22 July 1893. A week later his body arrived in Orkney. He was buried at St Magnus Cathedral in Kirkwall, Orkney. A memorial to Rae, lying as in sleep upon the ground, is inside the cathedral. The memorial by North Ronaldsay sculptor Ian Scott includes a brass plaque that reads: Celebrated Arctic Explorer. His contribution to Franklin scholarship remained contested; his acceptance of Inuit testimony, vindicated by Schwatka’s more systematic work, had never been fully rehabilitated in official circles.

The £10, 000 reward had been paid, but the recognition that might have accompanied it—appointment to naval rank, publication in Admiralty archives, inclusion in the heroic narrative of Arctic exploration—had been withheld. The outcome of Lady Franklin’s efforts to glorify the dead of the Franklin expedition meant that Rae, who had discovered evidence suggesting a much less noble fate, was shunned somewhat by the British establishment.

The testimony funnel, examined across these three decades, reveals a consistent pattern. Information that arrived through established channels—naval officers, written dispatches, physical documents—was processed immediately, debated publicly, and incorporated into official record. Information that arrived through Inuit mediation was delayed, disputed, and required repeated corroboration before acceptance. The delay was not merely institutional conservatism; it reflected genuine epistemological difficulty. The Inuit described events they had not witnessed directly, transmitted through chains of narration whose reliability European investigators could not independently verify. Their geography was precise but differently organized, their chronology anchored to ecological rather than calendrical cycles. The investigators who succeeded—Hall erratically, Schwatka systematically—were those who accepted these differences as features to be mapped rather than deficiencies to be corrected.

What accumulated between 1850 and 1880 was therefore not simply more evidence but a different kind of evidence, one that required different methods of authentication. Schwatka’s 2, 751 miles of sledge travel, his winter encampments, his patient return to the same informants with refined questions—this was the labor necessary to render oral testimony admissible. The cost was measured in years and in the gradual erosion of informants themselves: by 1880, many of those who had seen the ships or the men were dead, their knowledge preserved only in the written records of Hall, Schwatka, and their interpreters. The unbroken circle of testimony was, in fact, already breaking; what the expeditions captured was the final generation of direct transmission.

The alternative narrative that emerged from this collection was coherent in outline if not in every detail. The ships had been abandoned in 1848, but not simultaneously or completely. Some men had returned to them. A large party had attempted the overland route to the Great Fish River, failing at various points along the Adelaide Peninsula. Smaller groups had dispersed, some reaching as far as the mainland, others dying within sight of their starting point. The ships had remained visible, frozen in ice or sunk in shallow water, into the 1850s and possibly later—sources of material that Inuit communities utilized as the European search continued elsewhere. The final deaths had occurred not in a single catastrophe but in a distributed collapse, men falling at intervals across terrain they had not understood and could not survive.

This narrative contradicted no physical evidence found by McClintock or others, but it significantly complicated the heroic frame that had dominated British interpretation. The Victory Point note, read in isolation, suggested decisive leadership and coordinated action: Franklin dead, Crozier in command, a clear plan to reach safety. The Inuit testimony, mapped across the same geography, suggested fragmentation, reversals, and the gradual dissolution of command structures in conditions that no leadership could overcome. Both records were partial. The note was written by men who did not know they were leaving the final record; the testimony was collected from observers who did not understand what they were observing. The integration of the two—the chapter’s central achievement—required accepting that official and unofficial knowledge systems could illuminate different aspects of the same events.

By 1880, the search for Franklin had produced two complete archives: one in the Admiralty’s files, centered on the Victory Point note and McClintock’s dispatches; another in the published accounts of Hall and Schwatka, in the maps and testimonies that correlated Inuit geography with European coordinates. The first archive was closed, its conclusions accepted as definitive. The second remained open, its implications not fully pursued. The ships that the Inuit had described—one sunk in shallow water off King William Island, another further south near O’Reilly Island—had not been found. The precise locations, transmitted through place names and oral tradition, lacked the instrumental confirmation that would make them actionable for naval search.

The pressure that Schwatka’s expedition established, without resolving, was this: a coherent alternative narrative existed, validated by methodical collection and geographical correlation, yet its central claims remained untested by the technology of discovery. The Inuit had said where the ships were. No diver had looked. The testimony had been filtered through translation, publication, and professional debate, emerging as admissible evidence in a form that pointed toward specific locations in specific waters. But the Admiralty’s interest had waned; private expeditions lacked the resources for submarine investigation; the ice that preserved the testimony also prevented its verification. The search had arrived at an impasse where knowledge was sufficient to guide action, yet action was not taken.

What remained was the record itself: the accumulated testimony, mapped and published, waiting for instruments that could test it. The Victory Point note had been found because someone knew to look. The ships, if they existed where the Inuit had placed them, would be found only when someone knew to look in those specific coordinates with means to search below the surface.

The circle of testimony, unbroken through three decades of collection, had delivered its final message. The evidence lag that had characterized the search from its beginning—the delay between Arctic event and London recognition—now took a different form: not the time required for news to travel, but the time required for technology to catch up with testimony that had already been received, translated, and filed. The testimony had been waiting for a century and a half, filed in Admiralty archives, published in expedition narratives, translated from Inuktitut and entered in leather-bound journals. The technology was new. The gap between them defined the work of the next two decades.
