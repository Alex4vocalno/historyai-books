Chapter 25

The Report on the Table

Seen from above, the entire enterprise resolved into a converging network of channels—laboratories, offices, trusts, and panels—all feeding a single authoritative stream. The core tension was no longer about discovery, but about control: what story would be permitted, and who would hold the pen. Years of pressure, built through dives and recoveries, through discoveries and negotiated protocols, now demanded a final, formal shape. The abstract conflict between knowing and letting rest had become a daily practice, where every choice in a lab or meeting room carried weight for the silent and their kin.

Descend to the concrete: a Tuesday in November 2019, when a Parks Canada conservator lifted a lieutenant’s epaulette from its desalination bath in a climate-controlled room on the outskirts of Ottawa. The object had traveled three thousand miles from the seabed of Terror Bay, where HMS Terror lay in thirty-three meters of water, to this basin of precisely monitored saline solution. The gold thread had corroded to a dull bronze, the fabric beneath it preserved by the cold and dark of the Arctic deep.

The conservator made no notes on first impression, no speculation about identity. The protocol forbade it. The object would speak only when subjected to the full sequence of analysis: X-ray fluorescence to map elemental composition, scanning electron microscopy to examine fiber structure, comparison against the Admiralty’s 1843 uniform regulations. Each step generated data that would be entered into a database shared with the Inuit Heritage Trust, each interpretation subject to review.

The epaulette represented one thread in a larger convergence. In a laboratory at the University of Alberta, a fragment of wood from Erebus underwent dendrochronological analysis, its growth rings matched against the master chronologies of European oak and Baltic pine to determine not only age but origin—whether the timber had come from the Royal Dockyards at Deptford or from commercial suppliers in the Baltic trade. The answer would bear on questions of construction quality, of Admiralty procurement practices, of whether the ships had been sound when they sailed or compromised from the first.

In a forensic facility in Toronto, bone fragments recovered from the Terror site underwent isotopic analysis, the ratios of carbon, nitrogen, and strontium in the collagen offering signatures of diet and geographic origin. The goal was identification: to match these remains against the expedition’s muster rolls, to give names to the anonymous dead, to transform “skeleton” into a specific officer or one of the hundred seamen whose fates had been inferred but never proven.

The work proceeded under conditions of strict collaboration. The 2018 gift of the wrecks to Canada and the Inuit, with the Inuit Heritage Trust holding care of the sites, had established a governance structure that made every analytical decision subject to joint review. This was the testimony funnel transformed: not the Victorian pattern of Inuit witnesses deposed by naval officers, their statements filtered through translation and prejudice, but a formal partnership in which Inuit knowledge holders sat on review panels, in which community protocols governed the treatment of human remains, in which the question of what constituted legitimate inquiry was itself negotiable. The conservator in Ottawa could not publish findings without clearance from this joint structure. The university researchers could not access samples without demonstrating that their questions served interests recognized by both partners.

The epaulette emerged from its bath in December 2019, transferred to a drying chamber where temperature and humidity declined in controlled stages over six weeks. By February 2020, it was stable enough for detailed examination. The X-ray fluorescence revealed copper and zinc in proportions consistent with Admiralty-issue gold lace, the alloy standard established in 1833. The fabric beneath was wool, not the cotton that would have indicated a later replacement. The rank markings—two stars and a crown—identified it as belonging to a lieutenant, matching four names on the Erebus muster: Gore, Le Vesconte, Fairholme, and Hodgson. Which one had worn it could not be determined from the object alone. The conservator filed this finding without speculation, awaiting the bone analysis that might, if remains could be matched to names, allow a provisional attribution.

The dendrochronology results arrived in March 2020, just as pandemic restrictions began to disrupt laboratory access. The Erebus timbers showed a consistent pattern: Baltic oak, felled in the winter of 1835–1836, seasoned for seven years before installation in the hull reconstruction of 1843–1844. This was standard Admiralty practice, the wood sound and properly cured. The analysis offered no support for theories that the ships had failed structurally, that inferior materials had doomed the expedition. The timbers had been good. Whatever killed the men and trapped the vessels in ice had not been faulty construction.

The bone analysis proved more complex. In 1993, three bodies had been found at site NgLj-3 near Erebus Bay, remains originally discovered by McClintock’s expedition in 1859, rediscovered and reburied by Schwatka two decades later, then located again by a scientific team in the early 1990s. The initial forensic examination had proved ambiguous, and the examiner concluded the initial findings were “almost certainly incorrect.” The 2019–2020 analysis of new remains from the wreck sites employed methods unavailable three decades earlier: ancient DNA extraction to establish genetic profiles, stable isotope analysis to reconstruct diet and mobility, radiocarbon dating refined to within a decade by accelerator mass spectrometry. The goal was to resolve the uncertainty that had plagued earlier efforts, to achieve the identification that would transform archaeological data into personal narrative.

The genetic analysis required comparison against reference samples. Descendants of the expedition’s officers had been located through genealogical research, their DNA solicited through ethical protocols that explained how their contributions would be used and protected. The match, if it came, would be announced jointly by Parks Canada and the Inuit Heritage Trust, with family members consulted before any public statement. This was the evidence lag reversed: not the Victorian pattern of Inuit testimony discounted until physical proof compelled belief, but a deliberate architecture of verification in which multiple stakeholders held veto power over interpretation.

The first matches came in June 2020, though the announcement was delayed until September to allow full peer review. The remains from NgLj-3, the site McClintock had found in 1859, were identified through genetic analysis. The profile matched samples from living descendants of a Terror officer. The isotopic signature indicated a diet consistent with British naval rations, then a shift toward whatever protein sources the expedition had been able to obtain—seal, possibly caribou, possibly the contents of the tins that had been found nearby. The dating placed death in the winter of 1847–1848, consistent with the Victory Point record’s statement that two dozen men, including Franklin, had died by April 1848.

This identification carried specific historical weight. The officer whose remains were identified had been a favorite of Franklin’s, a young man whose career the expedition commander had advanced. His presence at NgLj-3, far south of the ships’ last known position, confirmed that the retreating party had traveled that route, that McClintock’s 1859 discovery had been precisely located, that the Victorian searchers had been right in their reconstruction even when they lacked proof. The physical corroboration arrived 161 years after the initial finding, closing a loop that had remained open through generations of speculation.

The joint assessment report took shape through the winter of 2020–2021, compiled by a working group that met by video conference when travel was impossible, in person when restrictions eased. The document was not merely a scientific synthesis but a negotiated text, its conclusions requiring consensus between federal officials, academic researchers, and Inuit representatives. The format itself represented a departure from precedent: not the Admiralty’s confidential memoranda to the Board, not the explorer’s published narrative with its single authoritative voice, but a multi-authored statement in which different perspectives were flagged where agreement could not be reached.

The report’s central finding concerned the timeline of abandonment. After the death of Sir John Franklin on 11 June 1847, Captain Francis Crozier of HMS Terror became the expedition leader. Fitzjames became second in command of the expedition, as well as command of Erebus. Fitzjames wrote an addendum to the Victory Point record, the document that would become the foundation of all subsequent reconstruction. The record stated that the ships had been deserted on 22 April 1848, by which point two dozen men, including Franklin, had died. The survivors, now led by Crozier and Fitzjames, set out for the Canadian mainland and disappeared.

But physical evidence from Terror complicated this narrative. The dendrochronology of wood samples from the wreck, combined with the stratigraphy of debris fields on the seabed, indicated that the ship had remained intact and possibly inhabited longer than previous assumptions allowed. The arrangement of artifacts, the condition of fittings, the absence of certain expected debris suggested that some party or parties had returned to the vessel after April 1848, that the ship had not been finally abandoned when the record claimed. This did not contradict the written document so much as complicate it: the record was true as far as it went, but incomplete, a snapshot of a decision rather than a final account.

The isotopic analysis of bone fragments supported a related conclusion. Multiple individuals showed dietary signatures inconsistent with shipboard rations alone, indicating extended survival on hunting and foraging. The nitrogen isotope ratios suggested consumption of marine mammals, the carbon ratios a mixed diet of terrestrial and aquatic resources. These findings aligned with specific elements of Inuit testimony collected in the 1860s and 1870s, accounts that described encountering men who had lived among the ice for multiple seasons, who had built shelters, who had traded or stolen from Inuit camps. The testimony that Victorian officials had found implausible—too chaotic, too contradictory, too incompatible with naval discipline—now received physical corroboration. The men had survived longer than the Admiralty had believed possible, had adapted in ways that the searchers of the 1850s could not imagine.

The report addressed the question of lead poisoning, the theory that had dominated Franklin scholarship since the 1980s. Analysis of bone samples showed elevated lead levels, consistent with previous findings from Beechey Island burials, but the isotopic signature of the lead matched that of the expedition’s food tins rather than the ship’s water systems. This refined rather than overturned the poisoning hypothesis: the source was confirmed, the mechanism remained unclear. Whether lead had contributed significantly to mortality, or whether the levels represented a chronic condition that the men had tolerated, could not be determined from skeletal evidence alone. The report noted this uncertainty explicitly, refusing the temptation to resolve what the data could not support.

The collaborative review process produced its own tensions. Inuit knowledge holders on the working group emphasized the importance of site integrity, of limiting further disturbance even when scientific questions remained unanswered. Federal officials pressed for maximum analytical access, citing the public interest in definitive resolution. Academic researchers occupied an intermediate position, their professional commitment to complete knowledge tempered by the ethical frameworks that now governed their work. The final report represented a series of compromises: certain proposed excavations were deferred, certain analytical methods were ruled out as too destructive, certain questions were left open rather than subjected to invasive investigation.

The report was released in June 2021, simultaneously in English, French, and Inuktitut, with a public presentation that included Inuit elders, government ministers, and family representatives of the identified remains. The event was staged not as a triumph of science but as an act of stewardship: the knowledge gained was framed as obligation rather than achievement, a burden of memory that now fell on the living. The descendants spoke of their ancestor not as a mystery solved but as a presence acknowledged, a name restored to a body that had lain anonymous for generations.

The scientific verdict was in. The epaulette remained in storage, its owner unidentified, its evidence partial. The dendrochronology data had been deposited in an open-access repository, available for reanalysis by future researchers with better methods or different questions. The genetic profiles of the identified remains had been sequestered under protocols that restricted their use, protecting descendant privacy even as they enabled historical knowledge. The testimony funnel had produced its modern form: not a single authoritative narrative but a managed plurality, in which different kinds of evidence—physical, documentary, oral, genetic—were assigned different weights by different communities.

The report on the table was not the end of inquiry but its transformation. The questions that remained—whether other remains could be identified, whether the ships’ final seasons could be reconstructed in detail, what had become of Franklin’s body—were now explicitly framed by the ethical commitments established in 2018. The desire to know continued, but it operated within constraints that the Victorian searchers would not have recognized. The obligation to let rest had been given institutional form, not as prohibition but as negotiation, a standing requirement that each new question be justified against its cost in disturbance and displacement.

The conservator’s silence in those first moments of examination was not merely procedural but historical, an inheritance of disciplinary caution shaped by generations of premature conclusions. When Charles Francis Hall had interviewed Inuit witnesses in the 1860s, he had pressed them for names, dates, and locations that would fit his predetermined theories of Franklin’s fate, recording their answers through interpreters whose grasp of nuance was uncertain. When Frederick Schwatka had led his overland expedition in 1879, his party had collected physical evidence—skeletal remains, expedition artifacts, Inuit accounts—without systematic protocols for chain of custody or collaborative interpretation. The objects they retrieved had entered museum collections with fragmentary documentation, their provenance degraded by each transfer between institutions and each subsequent scholar’s selective attention. The 2019 protocols represented an institutional memory of these failures, a determination that the final phase of Franklin inquiry would not replicate the interpretive violence of earlier stages.

The contrast between laboratory conditions and the circumstances of original use became itself a subject of analysis. The lieutenant’s epaulette, examined under controlled humidity and spectral illumination, had been manufactured in conditions of industrial haste that the analysis partially reconstructed.

The Admiralty’s 1843 uniform regulations, consulted by the conservator in digitized form from the National Archives at Kew, specified gold thread of particular weight and composition; the actual object showed economies that the regulations had not authorized, the alloy slightly base, the weaving slightly irregular. These were not defects but signatures of wartime procurement, of the Crimean War’s drain on naval stores, of the pressure to outfit five ships for Arctic service while maintaining fleets in the Baltic and Black Seas. The epaulette thus carried two histories: its official identity as naval insignia, and its material reality as a product of strained supply chains, made by hands whose names had not been recorded for the Admiralty’s purposes but whose labor was now partially visible in microscopic variation.

The collaborative structure of the joint assessment introduced temporal complications that the Victorian searchers had not faced.

When McClintock had compiled his narrative of the 1857–1859 expedition, he had written with the confidence of immediate return, his account published while memories were fresh and controversies active. The modern report, by contrast, accumulated through layers of delay: the seasonal restriction of Arctic access, the years required for conservation stabilization, the months of peer review, the additional months of intergovernmental and intercultural consultation.

Each delay carried risk that preliminary findings would leak, that unauthorized interpretations would circulate, that public expectation would crystallize around hypotheses that the final report might not support. The working group’s video conferences during the pandemic winter of 2020–2021 addressed not only substantive questions but procedural ones: how to maintain security of findings when participants worked from home, how to ensure that Inuit representatives with limited bandwidth could participate fully in discussions where technical vocabulary predominated, how to document decisions that had been reached through compromise rather than consensus.

The bone identification that matched remains to a Terror officer’s descendants required navigation of ethical territory that had no Victorian precedent. The genealogical research that located living relatives had itself been conducted under protocols established for forensic identification of war dead, adapted for this historical application. When contact was made, the researchers could not simply request DNA samples; they had to explain how genetic information would be stored, who would have access, what would happen if unexpected findings emerged—kinship relationships not recorded in family memory, genetic markers indicating susceptibility to conditions with medical implications. Some descendants declined to participate, their refusal respected without pressure. Others contributed samples with explicit restrictions on use, their conditions entered into binding agreements that would govern all subsequent research.

The Arctic itself remained the final arbiter. The wrecks of Erebus and Terror, now documented and protected, continued their slow decay in waters that would eventually claim whatever the conservators had not removed. The ice that had trapped them in 1846 still formed each winter across the routes they had sought to navigate. The Inuit communities whose ancestors had witnessed the expedition’s end still hunted and traveled across the same landscape, their knowledge of it continuous where the naval record was fragmentary. The scientific verdict had established facts that could not be disputed, but these facts opened onto questions that science alone could not answer—about the value of complete knowledge, about the claims of the dead upon the living, about how a nineteenth-century imperial disaster should be remembered in a twenty-first-century circumpolar world.

The report sat on the table, its pages marked with the signatures of multiple authorities, its findings already being cited in scholarly articles and contested in online forums. The physical objects that had generated it—the epaulette, the bone fragments, the timber samples—were dispersed across conservation facilities, their individual stories preserved in databases and display cases. The landscape that had yielded them remained, its place names still bearing the marks of the search: Franklin Strait, McClintock Channel, Rae Isthmus, the memorials and cairns that marked where men had died looking for other men. The inquiry had reached its formal conclusion, but the ground itself retained claims that no document could discharge.
