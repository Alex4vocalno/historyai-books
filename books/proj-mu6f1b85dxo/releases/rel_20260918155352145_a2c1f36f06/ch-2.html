Chapter 2

A Distant and Frozen Sea

A captain raised his glass toward an unbroken field of white, six weeks after two ships had slid away from England. On the morning of 19 May 1845, the Erebus and Terror cast off from Greenhithe in Kent with twenty-four officers and one hundred ten men, their departure staged as public ceremony and national investment. The ships had emerged from Deptford Dockyard bearing technologies that the Admiralty considered decisive: steam engines from the foundries of London and Greenwich, screw propellers designed to hoist clear of ice, heating ducts that would carry warmth to berths below the waterline, and libraries of twelve hundred volumes to sustain intellect through the polar night. The Erebus alone carried 1, 200 imperial gallons of liquor. The provisions, calculated for three years, assumed a single-season transit of the Northwest Passage if ice permitted—the same ice that would receive Dannett’s unremarkable observation on 26 July.

Sir John Franklin, fifty-nine years old, commanded from the Erebus. His second, Captain Francis Crozier, held the Terror. Between them lay four previous Arctic expeditions, two overland journeys that had mapped vast stretches of North America’s northern coast. Franklin’s reputation rested on those maps, on the Narratives he had published, and on his survival of the 1819–1822 expedition—an ordeal that had cost eleven of twenty men to starvation and murder. The Admiralty had selected him despite, or perhaps because of, this history. He was known to return.

The ships stopped at Stromness in the Orkney Islands for fresh water, then shaped course for Greenland in company with HMS Rattler and the transport Barretto Junior. The passage to Disko Bay took thirty days. There, on Greenland’s west coast at the Whalefish Islands, the expedition conducted its final provisioning. Ten oxen carried aboard the Barretto Junior were slaughtered for fresh meat and transferred to the discovery ships. Crew members wrote their last letters home, which recorded that Franklin had banned swearing and drunkenness. This was the last fresh food the crews would taste until they emerged, successful, on the Pacific coast—or so the plan assumed.

At the Whalefish Islands, five men were discharged due to sickness and sent home on Rattler and Barretto Junior. Their departure reduced the final crew to one hundred twenty-nine. The Barretto Junior and Rattler turned back for England, carrying the expedition’s last direct communication. The letters composed at Disko Bay were uniformly optimistic. James Fitzjames, captain of the Erebus, sketched his fellow officers in good-natured caricature—masterly drawings that showed men at ease with their circumstances. Fitzjames had been five times gazetted for gallantry in the China War. His presence represented the Admiralty’s confidence that capable officers would overcome the deficiencies that had plagued earlier ventures. His sketches captured this confidence: individual faces, relaxed poses, an implicit narrative of competence and camaraderie.

The letters spoke of good health, good spirits, good prospects. No one expressed doubt about the steam engines, though installed in haste and untested under polar conditions. No one questioned Franklin’s age, or his years away from sea command. The Admiralty’s selection of him over James Clark Ross—younger, more recently Arctic-experienced—went unmentioned, though it had been controversial in the small world of naval exploration. The letters performed their function: they reassured families, satisfied sponsors, and created a record of professional assurance that would be read and reread in the years of waiting.

The Erebus and Terror, now alone, continued north toward the ice-choked entrance to Lancaster Sound. The whaling fleet was working the same waters—not naval vessels but commercial ships from Aberdeen and Hull, their masters experienced in the seasonal rhythms of ice and open water. Bound for Pond’s Bay and the whaling grounds beyond, their routes converged with the expedition’s at the threshold of the archipelago. For a period in late July, discovery ships and whalers strove together to find passages through loose ice, their different purposes temporarily aligned in the common necessity of reading the ice’s movements.

Captain Dannett of the Prince of Wales, a Hull whaler, made the entry on 26 July 1845. His glass showed two naval ships, three-masted, steam-assisted, moored to an iceberg in Baffin Bay, waiting for good conditions to cross to Lancaster Sound. They were waiting for the ice to open. Their crews appeared healthy. Their rigging was in good order. The language of the log was the language of whalers: positions, conditions, the disposition of other vessels. The Erebus and Terror were simply there, present, unremarkable. Dannett recorded no observation about their commander, their destination, their prospects. He recorded what he saw and turned his own ship south, toward the Davis Strait fishery and home.

The Enterprise, Captain Robert Martin, also sighted the naval ships in Baffin Bay. The two accounts agree in essentials: the Erebus and Terror in good order, crews visible and active, waiting for an opening that would allow them to enter Lancaster Sound and begin their westward passage. The whalers, having no business in those waters once ice closed, continued south. Their logs note the parting as routine. On 26 July, the last of them turned away. The Erebus and Terror pursued their solitary course alone.

This was the last documented sighting by European eyes.

The entry in the Prince of Wales’s log, made in the ordinary course of commercial voyage, would become within a few years a coordinate of immense anxiety. But in the moment of its making, it carried no weight beyond itself. The ships were visible, then they were not. The ice that held them was, for Dannett, an obstacle to his own passage, not a threshold into silence.

The precision of these sightings matters. The whalers’ logs give dates, positions, physical descriptions. They record the state of the ice, the direction of wind, the presence of other vessels. They do not speculate about the future or interpret beyond immediate navigation. This is the documentary style of commercial shipping: factual, reticent, practical. It is also, for the historian, a kind of negative space. The whalers saw nothing alarming because there was nothing alarming to see. The expedition was proceeding according to plan. The ice was behaving as ice behaved in late July. The ships were where they were supposed to be.

This absence of alarm is the chapter’s central fact. The Erebus and Terror sent no distress signals because they were not in distress. They did not turn back because their orders directed them forward. They left no trail of abandoned equipment or sick men because their equipment was functioning and their men were healthy. The last European witnesses saw a competent naval operation executing its instructions with the smoothness the Admiralty had anticipated. The testimony they carried south was, in its way, more destabilizing than any report of disaster could have been. It confirmed that the expedition had entered the Arctic in strength and good order. It offered no basis for anxiety, no grounds for early search, no warning that the silence about to descend was anything other than temporary.

The silence did descend, and it was total. The Erebus and Terror passed into Lancaster Sound and beyond European observation. Their subsequent movements must be reconstructed from fragmentary evidence discovered years later: a note at Victory Point, graves on Beechey Island, scattered relics, Inuit testimony. But in the summer of 1845, none of this existed as knowledge. The expedition simply continued, its progress unwitnessed, its fate unmade.

The Admiralty’s instructions to Franklin, delivered in the final meeting of 19 April 1845, had prescribed a timeline. He was to penetrate the Northwest Passage, or prove its impossibility, and return to England within three years. The three-year provision was standard for Arctic expeditions, recognition that ice and weather could delay even the best-planned voyage. What the instructions did not anticipate—what they could not anticipate, given the state of knowledge—was the possibility that the expedition might succeed in its outward passage and fail in its return, trapped in ice far from any witness, its fate unknown until physical evidence could reach London.

This gap between event and knowledge, between what happened in the Arctic and what could be known in the capital, is the structural condition of the Franklin search. It begins here, in the summer of 1845, with the whalers’ unremarkable sightings and the expedition’s unremarked disappearance. The Admiralty would not begin to worry for three years, and would not organize systematic search expeditions until 1848. This delay was not negligence. It was the rational response to a record that showed everything proceeding normally. The Erebus and Terror had vanished into a space that British imagination had not yet learned to read as dangerous. The ice that held them was, on the charts, a blank to be filled. The passage they sought was, in prevailing scientific opinion, open to a determined navigator. The men who commanded them were, by every visible measure, capable of their task.

The winter of 1845–1846 passed without word. This was expected. Even if Franklin had transited the passage in a single season, his return would require at least the following summer. The Admiralty’s assumption, shared by the expedition’s families and the British public, was that silence meant progress. The ships were either completing their mission or wintering on Beechey Island, their crews occupied with scientific observations and routine maintenance that polar winters demanded. This assumption was reinforced by the absence of contrary evidence. No wreckage washed ashore in Greenland. No Inuit traders appeared at Hudson’s Bay Company posts with stories of white men in distress. The Arctic remained, from London’s perspective, an empty stage on which a successful drama was being enacted out of sight.

The whalers who had seen the Erebus and Terror returned to their home ports and ordinary lives. Their logs were deposited with shipowners, examined by insurers, perhaps consulted by naval officers planning future operations. The entries for late July 1845 became, in the immediate aftermath, mere historical data—one season’s observations among many. It was only later, when the expedition’s fate became national preoccupation, that Dannett’s and Martin’s sightings were extracted from the mass of whaling records and assigned their retrospective significance. In 1845, they were simply the last entries in a sequence that had no need of an ending.

The expedition’s own records from this period—whatever deck logs, journals, and sketchbooks the officers maintained—have not survived. We know that Fitzjames drew his companions, that Franklin maintained his methodical notes, that Crozier kept the astronomical observations that were his particular specialty. But the ice that would preserve the ships’ location for a century and a half did not preserve these documents. The testimony of the expedition’s initial success exists only in the whalers’ external observations and in the letters carried home by the Barretto Junior.

Those letters, read and reread in England through 1845 and 1846, performed a double function. They reassured their immediate recipients—wives, parents, siblings—that their men were well and professionally engaged. And they circulated more broadly as evidence of the expedition’s sound management. Lady Jane Franklin, the commander’s wife and most effective advocate, used them to counter any suggestion that her husband’s age or the haste of preparations might have compromised the venture. The Admiralty, in its internal deliberations, cited them as confirmation that the expedition had entered the Arctic with every advantage modern science and naval organization could provide.

The letters could not, of course, predict what would happen after their composition. They recorded a moment—Disko Bay, June 1845—when possibility was still intact. The optimism they expressed was, in context, warranted. The ships were sound, the crews healthy, the supplies adequate, the leadership experienced. Nothing in the visible world suggested that this foundation would prove insufficient. The Arctic, in the summer of 1845, was not yet the graveyard it would become in retrospect. It was simply the next field for British exploration, awaiting the systematic application of methods that had succeeded elsewhere.

The whalers’ final sightings, made a month after the letters were written, extended this record of normality. The Erebus and Terror were still in good order, still proceeding according to plan. The ice they waited upon was, for Dannett and Martin, an obstacle to be avoided, not a trap to be feared. Their logs contain no presentiment of disaster, no special attention to the naval ships beyond professional courtesy of noting their presence. The encounter was, for the whalers, unremarkable. For the expedition, it was the last contact with the world that had sent them forth.

The ships passed into Lancaster Sound. The ice closed behind them, or it did not; the distinction mattered only to those who would come looking, and they would not come for years. The Admiralty’s files continued to accumulate the ordinary business of naval administration: promotions, supply requisitions, disciplinary hearings, the thousand documents of a fleet at peace. Franklin’s name appeared in them occasionally, in reference to the three-year provision, in acknowledgment that no report had yet been received. These notations were routine. The absence of news was, in 1845 and 1846, indistinguishable from the presence of success.

The Admiralty’s confidence in Franklin’s expedition rested upon more than the reputation of its commander or the specifications of its equipment. It rested upon a particular reading of Arctic history that the 1845 voyage seemed destined to confirm. Since Parry’s successful wintering in Melville Island in 1819–1820, British naval science had treated the Canadian Arctic archipelago as a soluble problem—a matter of sufficient preparation, adequate provisions, and determined leadership. The ice was an obstacle, certainly, but an obstacle that methodical observation and technological innovation could overcome. The Erebus and Terror embodied this conviction in their very construction: steam engines that could extricate them from floes, screw propellers that could be lifted clear of ice damage, heating systems that would preserve crew health through the polar night. These were not experimental features but proven technologies, adapted from other naval contexts and now assembled in unprecedented combination.

The selection of Franklin himself reflected this institutional optimism. His age—fifty-nine—was noted by some observers, as was his absence from sea command since 1825. But the Admiralty’s deliberations, preserved in the correspondence of the Second Secretary, reveal a preference for proven character over recent experience. Franklin had returned from his 1819–1822 overland expedition when others would have perished. He had survived the wreck of the HMS Porpoise in 1803. His reputation was not merely for exploration but for endurance, for the capacity to sustain morale and discipline through conditions that destroyed lesser expeditions. The Admiralty’s choice suggested a belief that Arctic survival was less a matter of technical skill than of moral constitution—that the right commander could impose order upon an environment that had defeated others.

This belief was not unfounded in the evidence available to 1845. The disaster of Sir John Franklin’s earlier expedition had been an overland journey, dependent upon indigenous assistance and improvised transport. The naval Arctic expeditions of the 1820s and 1830s, by contrast, had demonstrated that ships properly prepared could winter in the ice and emerge with crews intact. Parry’s four voyages had established protocols for ventilation, diet, and occupation that seemed to solve the problems of scurvy and depression that had plagued earlier ventures. The Erebus and Terror carried these protocols forward: lemon juice in quantity, preserved meats selected by nutritional theory, libraries and theatricals for mental stimulation. The expedition represented not a gamble but a culmination—the application of accumulated wisdom to a problem that had been reduced to manageable dimensions.

The letters carried home by the Barretto Junior must be read in this context of institutional and intellectual confidence. When Fitzjames sketched his fellow officers, when Franklin noted the good spirits of the crew, they were not merely reassuring their correspondents. They were confirming a narrative that their sponsors expected to hear—that the methods were sound, the preparations adequate, the outcome predictable. The absence of doubt in these letters is striking not because doubt would have been more realistic but because doubt would have been unprofessional. Naval exploration operated within a culture of confident reporting, in which expressions of anxiety were understood as admissions of inadequate preparation. The letters from Disko Bay performed their function within this culture, establishing the expedition’s credentials for success in the very terms that would make its subsequent silence so difficult to interpret.

The whaling captains who sighted the Erebus and Terror in late July operated within a different commercial culture but one equally committed to the language of practical observation. Dannett and Martin had no investment in Franklin’s success or failure; their logs recorded the naval ships as they would record any other vessels encountered in Baffin Bay.

This commercial detachment lends their testimony a particular authority. They had no reason to minimize difficulties or exaggerate competence. Their observation that the crews appeared healthy, the rigging in good order, the ships properly positioned for their intended passage represents an uninterested confirmation of the expedition’s apparent condition.

The whalers’ subsequent departure—turning south toward their own destinations, leaving the naval ships to their western course—was equally unremarkable. The encounter had lasted only hours, perhaps a day. The ice conditions that held both sets of vessels would soon separate them, the whalers seeking open water, the expedition awaiting its passage into Lancaster Sound.

This final separation, witnessed by no one after the whalers’ departure, acquires its significance only in retrospect. At the moment of its occurrence, it was simply the ordinary divergence of vessels with different purposes.

The whalers’ logs gathered dust in Hull and Aberdeen. The letters from Disko Bay were folded into family archives, their optimism gradually tempered by the passage of time. The Erebus and Terror sailed west, or they did not; the record cannot say. What it can say, what it must say, is that the last European eyes to see them saw nothing worth remarking beyond the fact of their presence. The expedition had entered the Arctic with every appearance of competence. It had vanished with every appearance of intention. The silence that followed was not yet a mystery. It was simply the ordinary condition of ships beyond the reach of observation, doing what they had been sent to do.

The ships vanish into the archipelago, beginning a long, unremarkable silence that has not yet been interpreted as alarm.
