Chapter 8

The Disgrace of the Mess

The report reached London on the autumn mail from Hudson Bay, and on the morning of 23 October 1854 a clerk carried it to the offices of The Times. The newspaper printed the full text that Tuesday without editorial comment, embedding John Rae’s findings among the shipping news and parliamentary summaries that formed the paper’s routine fare. The document described silver spoons, a star-shaped medal, a piece of plate engraved with Sir John Franklin’s crest. It described ships crushed in ice. It described, in the same uninflected prose, what Inuit witnesses had communicated through gestures and fragmented English: that some of Franklin’s men, in their final extremity, had eaten the bodies of their dead companions.

The morning edition moved through London before most readers had finished breakfast. By noon it had entered the reading rooms of Pall Mall and the committee rooms of Westminster. By evening it had reached the house on Spring Gardens where Lady Jane Franklin received visitors.

—-

Rae himself was still at sea, homeward bound aboard a Hudson’s Bay Company supply ship, when his words began their independent life in the capital. He would not arrive until 22 October, stepping onto the London docks with his collections of Franklin relics carefully packed, his reward of £10, 000 already approved by the Admiralty for whoever first ascertained the expedition’s fate. The money was his by right of discovery. The controversy, he would find, came attached without surcharge.

The immediate response accumulated in layers through the following days. The Admiralty, which had solicited and received Rae’s report through official channels, found itself in possession of intelligence it had not fully prepared to manage. Sir James Graham, First Lord of the Admiralty, had authorized the publication; the document was, after all, a fulfillment of the search mandate his department had issued. Yet the particular fulfillment—cannibalism among naval officers, attested by Inuit hunters, transmitted through a Hudson’s Bay Company employee rather than a commissioned officer—introduced a category error into Victorian public life. The navy could acknowledge shipwreck. It could acknowledge starvation. It had no ceremonial provision for this. The Admiralty had urged the Hudson’s Bay Company to mount a follow-up expedition as early as October 1854, but this administrative action could not contain the public storm.

Within the Athenaeum Club and the United Service Institution, where Arctic veterans gathered to read the newspapers over coffee, the report generated a specific quality of discomfort. These were men who had themselves wintered in ice, who understood the mathematics of scurvy and the mechanics of frostbite. They knew what the Arctic did to human bodies. What they could not immediately accommodate was the social geometry of Rae’s testimony: that the truth about Franklin’s men had been held, for years, by people the British classified as savages, and that this truth had now arrived through a commercial channel rather than a naval one. The Hudson’s Bay Company was respectable, certainly. It was not the Royal Navy.

—-

Lady Jane Franklin received the news in her drawing room, where she had maintained for nearly a decade the infrastructure of a continuing search. Her husband’s fate had become, through her efforts, a permanent feature of British public life—petitions to Parliament, subscriptions for search vessels, correspondence with hydrographers and astronomers who might suggest where ice patterns would carry a disabled ship. She had shaped the narrative of Franklin as hero, as scientific pioneer, as the man who would complete the Northwest Passage where others had failed. Rae’s report did not contradict this narrative directly; it ended it. The expedition had not died in the act of discovery. It had died in retreat, in disintegration, in a manner that the Times had now placed beyond her editorial control.

Her response was immediate and strategic. Within days she had enlisted Charles Dickens, the most widely read author in England, to address the public damage. Dickens was not a random choice. He had published in Household Words a series of articles celebrating Arctic exploration as a moral theater, a demonstration of British character under extreme conditions. He had met Lady Franklin, corresponded with her, understood the symbolic weight of her widowhood. In the issue dated 2 December 1854, Household Words carried his direct rebuttal to Rae’s findings, an essay titled “The Lost Arctic Voyagers” that would set the terms of controversy for months to come.

Dickens’s argument was not with the physical evidence. He acknowledged the silverware, the medal, the location of the relics near King William Island. His objection was epistemological and racial: the Inuit could not be trusted as witnesses, their testimony was inherently unreliable, and the specific charge of cannibalism was a projection of their own moral condition onto men of superior civilization.

He asserted that there was no reason whatever to believe that any of Franklin’s members prolonged their existence by the dreadful expedient of eating the bodies of their dead companions. The sentence carried the weight of his authority as a novelist who had trained the British public to recognize moral truth through narrative.

He proposed an alternative scenario: that Franklin’s men had been murdered by the Inuit themselves, that the natives had fabricated the cannibalism story to cover their crime, that the relics had been stolen from corpses rather than received as gifts or payment.

This was not merely a defense of Franklin’s honor. It was a defense of the evidentiary system through which British knowledge was produced. Dickens understood, with the instinct of a professional storyteller, that Rae’s report threatened more than one expedition’s reputation. It threatened the hierarchy of who could speak with authority about remote places. A naval officer, reporting through official channels, could be believed. A Hudson’s Bay Company surgeon, reporting Inuit testimony, could not—regardless of what objects he placed on the table.

—-

The objects themselves, meanwhile, had entered a different circuit of meaning. Rae’s collection—spoons, forks, a small star-shaped piece of silver, the plate with Franklin’s crest—was displayed at the Admiralty and later at the Royal Geographical Society. Crowds came to see them. They were photographed, described in the illustrated press, incorporated into the material culture of Victorian mourning. The things were accepted as authentic, even sacred. The story that explained their condition and location was rejected as contamination.

This dissonance became the structural fact of the controversy. The relics proved that Rae had found something real. The relics could not, by themselves, explain what had happened to the men who owned them. For that explanation, the public had only Rae’s report, which carried the double disability of its source (Inuit witnesses) and its medium (a commercial employee rather than a naval officer). The result was a peculiar suspension: the material evidence was admitted, the narrative evidence was expelled, and the gap between them was filled by speculation organized around racial and class prejudice.

Dickens was not alone in this work. The Illustrated London News and the Athenaeum published correspondence questioning Rae’s methods, his haste, his reliance on “savage” testimony. The Morning Post defended the navy’s honor against implicit slur. The pattern was consistent: the attack concentrated on Rae’s credibility as a witness, not on the physical objects he had brought back. No one suggested that he had forged the silverware. Many suggested that he had misunderstood, or been deliberately misled, or had himself succumbed to the moral contagion of prolonged association with native peoples.

—-

Rae, arriving in London on 22 October, found himself celebrated and attacked in the same breath. The Admiralty paid his reward. The Royal Geographical Society invited him to present his findings. The Times published his additional letters clarifying details of the Inuit testimony. Simultaneously, he was made to understand that his report had caused a kind of social damage that money could not repair. He had violated protocol by publishing directly, by including the cannibalism detail without preparing the ground, by treating Inuit witnesses as equivalent to European informants.

His response was characteristic: precise, defensive, grounded in the practical ethics of Arctic travel. In a letter to the Times dated 28 October, he defended his methods and his witnesses. The Inuit, he noted, had no motive to deceive him. They had brought him objects of value, had described locations he could verify, had behaved with consistent honesty in matters where their truthfulness could be tested. The charge that they had murdered Franklin’s men was, he pointed out, contradicted by their own account of finding the bodies already dead, and by the absence of any marks of violence on the relics they had recovered.

This defense had limited effect. The controversy had moved beyond the specific claims of Rae’s report into a general argument about the reliability of non-European testimony and the moral impossibility of cannibalism among British naval officers. Dickens continued to prosecute this argument through December, publishing a second article in Household Words that developed his theory of Inuit murder and European innocence. The magazine’s circulation—forty thousand copies weekly—ensured that his version reached households that would never see Rae’s original report.

—-

The Admiralty, caught between its official recognition of Rae’s achievement and the public storm his report had generated, adopted a posture of administrative neutrality. Sir James Graham declined to endorse either Rae’s narrative or Dickens’s alternative. The department’s formal position was that the fate of Franklin’s expedition remained, in certain particulars, undetermined. This was technically accurate—Rae had not found the ships, had not located Franklin’s own body, had not established the exact sequence of deaths. It was also politically necessary. To accept Rae’s full account would be to acknowledge that the navy’s greatest Arctic disaster had ended in moral horror. To reject it entirely would be to explain away evidence that had already entered the public record.

The result was a peculiar form of institutional paralysis. The Admiralty continued to authorize searches—vessels would sail for the Arctic in 1855, 1856, 1857—while officially treating the central question as still open. This was the evidence lag in its most acute form: physical proof had arrived, had been verified, had been rewarded, but could not be metabolized into official history because its narrative content violated the culture’s self-image. The lag was not measured in miles or months now, but in the distance between what could be seen and what could be said.

Lady Jane Franklin understood this structure with the clarity of someone who had spent years manipulating it. Her campaign for continued search was no longer, after October 1854, a campaign to find her husband alive. It was a campaign to find an alternative ending—to discover, through some new expedition, a narrative that would displace Rae’s. She needed a death scene that could be staged with appropriate dignity: officers dying in rank order, the commander preserving discipline to the last, perhaps even a final document in Franklin’s own hand that would explain and redeem the disaster. Rae’s Inuit witnesses offered none of this. They offered only fragmentation, confusion, the suggestion that the expedition’s final phase had been without order or meaning.

—-

The winter of 1854–55 thus established a pattern that would persist for years. The relics remained on display, accumulating the aura of authenticated objects. The cannibalism accusation remained in circulation, periodically denied, periodically revived, never fully resolved. Rae returned to his surveying work, his reputation secure in geographical circles and damaged in the wider public sphere. Dickens moved on to other subjects, his defense of naval honor having served its immediate purpose. The Admiralty continued to process search proposals with the bureaucratic regularity of a department that had learned to manage permanent uncertainty.

What had changed was the terms of engagement. Before Rae’s report, the search for Franklin had been organized around the possibility of rescue—of finding men alive, or at least of determining their fate with the completeness that would allow proper mourning. After October 1854, the search became a forensic competition between incompatible narratives. Every subsequent expedition would carry not only the practical equipment of Arctic travel but the burden of disproving or confirming what Rae had relayed. The physical objects he had recovered—spoons, medal, plate—would remain the fixed points around which interpretation circled. The human voices he had transmitted would remain excluded from official credit, their testimony filed under the category of unreliable report.

This was the disgrace of the mess: not that cannibalism had occurred—this remained, for many, unproven and unprovable—but that the knowledge of it had arrived through the wrong channels, carried by the wrong people, entering public discourse without the mediating structures that made information respectable. The scandal was formal before it was moral. Rae had broken the protocol of the testimony funnel, allowing raw experience to reach the breakfast tables of London without having been filtered through naval command, scientific committee, or the editorial judgment of authorized persons. The result was not simply disagreement about facts. It was a demonstration that the system for producing official knowledge could reject information it could not accommodate, even when that information came with physical proof attached.

—-

Through December 1854, the controversy maintained its intensity without reaching resolution. Dickens’s articles continued to appear. Rae’s letters to the press continued to defend his methods. The Admiralty maintained its silence on the substantive questions while processing the administrative consequences of Rae’s reward. Lady Jane Franklin, having mobilized her literary ally, began to consider more direct action—action that would not depend on the Admiralty’s bureaucratic rhythms or the Times editorial schedule.

The display of Rae’s relics at the Admiralty and the Royal Geographical Society occasioned a particular species of Victorian spectatorship that illuminated the cultural mechanics of the controversy. Visitors approached the glass cases with the reverence due to sacred objects, yet their attention fixed upon the silver spoons and Franklin’s crested plate with a selective intensity that bracketed away the circumstances of their recovery. The Illustrated London News described the collection as “mute witnesses” to the expedition’s fate, a formulation that preserved their evidentiary value while rendering them literally silent about the human context that had produced them.

This was the material culture of empire at its most contradictory: objects could be extracted from indigenous hands, transported to metropolitan centers, and invested with national significance, yet the knowledge systems of those indigenous intermediaries remained categorically excluded from the hermeneutic circle.

The spoons were photographed, catalogued, discussed in learned papers. The Inuit witnesses who had located them, who had described the condition of the bodies nearby, who had demonstrated through gesture the act of cutting flesh from bone—these figures remained photographic negatives, present as absence, necessary to the production of meaning yet denied standing as producers of meaning themselves.

The racial architecture of Dickens’s argument in “The Lost Arctic Voyagers” drew upon a well-established colonial epistemology that assigned differential credibility to European and non-European observers. His assertion that the Inuit were “a people so low in the scale of humanity” that their testimony required automatic discounting was not original to him; it reproduced the hierarchies of the Report of the Select Committee on Aborigines (1837) and the emergent anthropology of Robert Knox and others. What Dickens added was the narrative sophistication of a novelist who understood that the public’s relationship to distant events was mediated through character and plot.

By constructing the Inuit as potential murderers and fabricators, he supplied a story that competed with Rae’s on formal grounds: it had villains, it had innocent victims, it preserved the moral arc that Victorian readers had been trained to expect from tales of British enterprise.

The cannibalism narrative, by contrast, offered no such satisfaction. It described men of rank and education reduced to the same desperate measures that had characterized the Donner Party or the crew of the Medusa, collapsing the distance between civilization and savagery that colonial discourse required. Dickens’s alternative theory was not merely a defense of Franklin’s men; it was a restoration of narrative order, a reassertion of the boundary that Rae’s report had threatened to dissolve.

The institutional pressures upon Rae himself revealed the asymmetries of status that structured Victorian knowledge production. As a Hudson’s Bay Company employee, he occupied a position that was professionally respectable yet socially ambiguous—neither the commissioned officer whose word carried automatic weight, nor the independent gentleman scholar whose leisure permitted disinterested inquiry. His medical training gave him scientific credentials, but his long residence among indigenous peoples, his adoption of Arctic travel techniques that European explorers considered beneath their dignity, his willingness to learn from native survival strategies—all of this marked him as contaminated by proximity to the colonial frontier.

The attacks upon his character concentrated with peculiar intensity on his method of travel: that he had moved through the Arctic not with the heavy equipment and large parties of naval expeditions, but with small groups of indigenous guides, living as they lived, eating as they ate. This was presented not as evidence of practical intelligence but as moral compromise, a surrender of British standards that cast doubt upon his capacity to judge the behavior of others.

The Athenaeum suggested that his “intimacy with savage life” had rendered him “unfit to discriminate between the probable and the monstrous,” a charge that conflated epistemological reliability with social distance from the objects of knowledge.

The Admiralty’s administrative paralysis had structural roots that extended beyond the immediate scandal.

The pressure generated by this unresolved scandal would not dissipate. It would be converted, by those with resources and determination, into a new form of search—privately financed, privately directed, operating outside the official channels that had failed to produce an acceptable ending. The evidence lag had become intolerable. The testimony funnel had demonstrated its capacity for blockage. What remained was the possibility of direct action: a vessel purchased, equipped, and sent to the Arctic by those who would not accept the incomplete account that official institutions had settled for.

The scandal left behind it a specific, material consequence. In the offices of the Royal Geographical Society, in the drawing rooms where Lady Jane Franklin received her visitors, in the dockyards where commercial vessels were inspected for possible conversion to Arctic service, the unresolved state of the Franklin question was generating the conditions for a final, private expedition. The controversy had demonstrated that the Admiralty would not, or could not, pursue the matter to a conclusion that satisfied public honor. Someone else would have to do it.
