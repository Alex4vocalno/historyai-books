Chapter 7

The Twelve Coats on the Rocks

In the spring of 1853, while Lady Jane Franklin’s petition still gathered signatures in London drawing rooms, John Rae stood on the deck of the Prince Albert as she cleared Stromness Harbour in the Orkney Islands on 5 July. He was forty years old, a Hudson’s Bay Company surgeon who had already completed two Arctic surveys, and he carried orders that made no mention of Franklin. The company wanted the final gap in the northern coast of North America surveyed: the unknown shore between Boothia Peninsula and the mouth of the Back River.

Rae had designed his own method for such work. Travel light, move fast, live off the land through Inuit trade and hunting, cover ground that would swallow conventional expeditions whole.

He had no ship to winter in, no naval supply chain, no Admiralty reports to file. What he had was a proven capacity to extract precise geographical information from Inuit hunters and to survive where organized parties starved.

The Prince Albert deposited him and his party at Repulse Bay, on the Arctic Circle, on 18 August. From there Rae pushed west through the autumn, establishing his base on the Boothia Peninsula and beginning the survey work that would occupy him through the winter of 1853 and the spring of 1854. He traveled with twelve men—Orkney boatmen and Cree hunters—moving on snowshoes and dog sleds, sleeping in skin tents, eating what they could kill or trade. The company had given him two years. He intended to use them efficiently.

The first encounter came on 21 April 1854, near Pelly Bay—now Kugaaruk—when Rae met an Inuk who carried news of white men. The man told of a party of thirty-five to forty Europeans who had been seen four years earlier, in the spring of 1850, dragging a boat and sledges southward along the western coast of King William Island. They were thin. They were starving. They had not been seen since. The Inuk did not know what became of them.

Rae recorded this with his characteristic precision, noting the location, the date of the original sighting, the number of men, their direction of travel. He made no immediate conclusions. But he continued west, and as he moved he collected more fragments—stories from other hunters, details that accumulated like sediment around a submerged wreck.

By early May, Rae had reached a point on the coast where the evidence became material. An Inuit party brought him objects they had found on the northwest coast of King William Island, near a place the charts did not name with precision. Rae examined them in the light of his tent, and what he saw shifted the ground beneath his feet.

Silver forks. Silver spoons. A plate from a ship’s chronometer. These objects were not random debris scattered by ice and time. They were personal possessions, marked with ownership, carried by men who had expected to use them at table. Rae turned the spoons in his hands and found the crests engraved there: the arms of Franklin’s officers, the heraldic devices that identified men who had dined in the great cabin of HMS Erebus or Terror. He found a silver fork with the initials “F.R.M.C.” (Francis Rawdon Moira Crozier, captain of the Terror, second in command of the expedition). He found spoons bearing the Franklin family arms.

The objects were cold in his hands. They had passed through other hands first—Inuit hands, who had found them on dead men or among abandoned gear—and now they rested with him as evidence that could not be argued away. Rae understood immediately what he held. These relics did not come from some peripheral encounter, some hunting party or survey detachment. They were the possessions of the expedition’s senior officers, carried from the ships into the ice, dragged across the snow, and left finally on the shore of an unnamed island where no European had yet set foot.

He bought them. The Hudson’s Bay Company would reimburse him. He wrapped them carefully and continued his survey, but now with a different attention. Every Inuit encounter became an interrogation, every story a potential thread leading back to that party of thirty-five to forty men seen dragging their boat in 1850.

What Rae learned in the weeks that followed would reshape everything.

The Inuit had not merely seen the white men from a distance. They had approached them, traded with them, watched them die. They described a large party—Rae estimated forty men from the consistency of the accounts—who had been encountered on the west coast of King William Island in the spring or early summer of 1850. The men were dragging a boat mounted on a sledge, heading south toward the mainland coast and the Back River. They were in desperate condition. Some could barely walk. They offered the Inuit silver spoons, forks, and other items in exchange for seal meat, but the Inuit had little to spare and could not save them.

This intelligence was already more specific than any previous report. The Admiralty searches of 1848–1850 had found the winter quarters at Beechey Island, the three graves from 1846, and abandoned equipment alongside a message cairn that promised further information if the ships should be forced to abandon them. But that message, dated April 1848, had placed the expedition intact at a location from which escape seemed possible. The ships were beset, but the officers were alive, the stores were sufficient, and a retreat southward was planned. The Admiralty had clung to this document as evidence that the expedition had survived its first crisis and might yet be found.

Rae’s informants described something else entirely. The party of forty had no ships. They had no organized camp. They were moving, slowly, toward a destination they would never reach. And they were not alone in their final days.

The Inuit described other parties of white men, smaller groups, scattered along the coast. They described a place where many had died—a large party, one hunter called it, using gestures to indicate numbers Rae calculated at perhaps twenty. They described skeletons. They described graves that were not graves, bodies left where they fell, bones picked clean by foxes and weather.

And they described what the white men had done to survive.

Rae recorded this with the same precision he applied to latitude and longitude, but the words carried a weight that geographical coordinates did not. The Inuit told him that some of the dead had been eaten by the living. They described the discovery of human remains in cooking pots. They described the cutting of flesh from corpses, the distribution of this flesh among survivors too weak to hunt. They were specific about the locations—near a large river, on the western shore of the island—and consistent in their accounts across multiple informants who had not spoken with one another.

Rae had heard such stories before. In his 1846–1847 expedition, Inuit hunters had told him of white men seen far to the west, of possible shipwrecks, of mysteries that might or might not relate to the Franklin expedition. He had learned to distinguish between the solid information that Inuit observation could provide and the elaborations that grew around any narrative of the unknown. He cross-checked. He asked the same questions of different informants, separated by distance and time, and compared their answers. He tested their reliability by asking about geography he already knew, about weather patterns, about animal behavior. The hunters who spoke to him in April and May 1854 passed these tests. Their descriptions of the coast matched his own surveys. Their accounts of the white men’s condition were consistent in their essentials even when they differed in detail.

What they told him about cannibalism was not isolated gossip but repeated testimony accompanied by physical evidence that Rae himself had collected. The silver spoons and forks were not items that starving men would trade lightly; they were among their last valuables offered for food that could not save them. The condition of those who carried these objects—thin, dragging themselves across snow—explained why they died and why some chose to eat rather than join them.

Rae made a decision that would define his reputation and the reception of his findings. He recorded everything. He did not filter the cannibalism reports through Victorian sensibility, did not soften them into euphemism, did not suggest that the Inuit might be mistaken or malicious. He noted that the practice was described as occurring in the last resort, that the men who resorted to it had been driven to the last dread alternative, but he did not doubt that it had occurred. The physical evidence supported the testimony. The testimony was consistent and independently corroborated. The only honest course was to report what he had found.

He completed his survey work through summer 1854 mapping coastline connecting Boothia Peninsula to previously explored regions west of Back River—a significant geographical achievement in its own right: final link in Northwest Passage though not one any ship could use—but Rae knew his survey would be overshadowed by what he carried in packs.

On 31 August 1854, he arrived at the Hudson’s Bay Company post on Hudson Bay with his collections and his report. He had been in the Arctic thirteen months. He had traveled more than six thousand miles, most of it on foot or by sledge. He had completed the survey the company had commissioned. And he had solved, to the extent that solution was possible without visiting the sites himself, the mystery that had occupied the British Empire for nine years.

The relics were laid out for examination: the silverware with its identifiable crests, the chronometer plate, the other metal objects that had survived Arctic corrosion. The company’s chief factor wrote to London with preliminary news even before Rae’s formal report could be prepared. The objects were undoubtedly portions of the equipment of Sir John Franklin’s Expedition, he reported. The implications were clear.

Rae spent September and October at the post, writing. His report to the company was detailed, methodical, restrained. He described his survey work first, as duty required, then appended the intelligence he had gathered regarding the Franklin expedition. He named his informants where he could—Inuit hunters whose names he had recorded phonetically, whose territories he had mapped, whose reliability he had tested. He located their stories in specific places: the western coast of King William Island, the vicinity of the Back River, the unnamed shore where the final party had been seen dragging their boat toward a salvation they would not find.

He described the cannibalism with the same factual directness he applied to ice conditions and tidal observations. From the mutilated state of many of the corpses and the contents of the kettles, he wrote, it was evident that the wretched countrymen had been driven to the last dread alternative as a means of prolonging existence. He did not elaborate. He did not need to. The words carried their own weight.

The report was sealed and dispatched to London in October 1854, carried by the annual company ship that would reach England before winter closed the Atlantic. Rae remained at the post, then traveled to the Red River settlement, then began the long journey east that would bring him to London in the new year. He did not hurry. He had done his work. What happened to that work once it reached the Admiralty and the press was beyond his control.

But he knew what he had found. The silver spoons told him. The Inuit accounts told him. The geography told him—King William Island, the western shore, the desperate movement south toward the Back River and the settled country beyond. The expedition had not survived. The ships had been abandoned, probably in 1848, probably in the ice somewhere north of the island. The men had marched south, probably more than one hundred of them at first, probably hauling boats and supplies that grew heavier as their strength failed. They had died in groups, at different times, in different places. Some had reached the western shore of King William Island. A smaller party, perhaps the last survivors, had tried to cross to the mainland and had failed. The final survivors, reduced to the last dread alternative, had eaten their dead and then died themselves.

“This was not” appears twice; rewrite as plain statement: “The Admiralty had prepared for a different story.” “Lady Jane Franklin had spent nine years building public support for a different story.” “It was not” appears once; rewrite as plain statement: “The story was one of heroic survival…”

Rae had seen enough of Arctic travel to understand how this had happened. He had traveled light, lived off the land, moved fast. The Franklin expedition had done none of these things. They had brought ships that could not be dragged across ice, supplies that could not be replenished, equipment that assumed a support network that did not exist. When the ships failed them, they had tried to carry their world with them—boats, sledges, the heavy apparatus of naval civilization—until carrying it became impossible and abandoning it became fatal. The Inuit, who had watched them pass, had understood what the white men did not: that survival in this country required traveling as the country permitted, not as European doctrine prescribed.

The twelve coats on the rocks. Rae recorded this detail too, though its meaning was unclear—a pile of garments, perhaps discarded as men weakened past the need for warmth, perhaps left as markers for a return that never came. The image stayed with him. Twelve coats, carefully folded or simply dropped, on the stones of an Arctic shore where no European would walk for five more years. The coats outlasted the men who had worn them. The silver spoons outlasted the hands that had held them. The story outlasted the tellers, passing from Inuit memory to Rae’s report to the permanent record of what had been found.

At the Hudson’s Bay Company post, in the autumn of 1854, Rae prepared his collections for shipment. The silverware was wrapped, the report copied, the evidence chain complete from Inuit testimony to material object to written document. He had done what no naval expedition had managed: he had traced the expedition’s final movements, identified its catastrophic end, and brought back proof that could not be disputed.

The sealed report, carrying Rae’s findings, was now in transit to London, where its contents would ignite a scandal.
