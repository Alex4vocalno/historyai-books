Chapter 23

The Bell from the Wreck

Seen from above, the journey was a series of transfers across a landscape indifferent to the object’s age. It began in the hold of a vessel, secured to a pallet, then moved through modern warehouses and loading bays. The route carried it south by truck, traversing a valley beyond the timber line and past oilfields, through territories once marked as blank spaces on maps. Arriving at its destination, the crate bore the marks of its passage, having been opened and resealed, its contents photographed while still bearing the residue of a distant bay.

The conservators at the Canadian Conservation Institute worked in a laboratory that smelled of deionized water and polyethylene glycol. They had been told what to expect. The Parks Canada dive team had already seen the bell on the seabed, had already read the letters through the murk of a camera feed, and had already transmitted the identification to Ottawa before the artifact was lifted. But seeing it in a tank was different from seeing it on a screen. The bell sat in a bath of filtered water, its surface furred with corrosion products, and the conservators waited for the sediment to loosen before they could read what was underneath.

When the letters emerged, they were not elegant. They had been cast into the bronze at a foundry in London before the ship ever left the Thames, and they read: HMS Erebus 1845. The date was the year of departure. The name was the name of the ship. There was no ambiguity left in the object. It had been made for that vessel, had hung in that vessel, had gone down with that vessel, and had now come back up. The conservators recorded its dimensions and its weight and the composition of its alloy, and they began the slow process of removing the chloride that would otherwise eat the metal from within. The bell would take years to stabilize. It would sit in successive baths, each one weaker in salt than the last, while the chemistry of the sea was drawn out of it one immersion at a time.

The announcement came in stages. First there was the identification, released by Parks Canada with a photograph of the bell still in its tank. Then there were the ceremonies. In Ottawa, the bell was displayed at a press conference where officials from Parks Canada and the Government of Canada stood in front of it and spoke about national heritage and Arctic sovereignty. In London, a smaller event was held, where the bell was loaned for exhibition and where the British press treated it as a relic of imperial tragedy. The two ceremonies told different stories about the same object. In Canada, the bell was evidence of a national claim to the Arctic, a physical proof that Canadian institutions had found what Canadian institutions had been looking for. In Britain, it was a memorial, a fragment of a lost age, a thing to be mourned rather than possessed.

Neither ceremony mentioned the Inuit who had seen the ship before the searchers did. Neither ceremony mentioned the fact that the wreck had been found in a place where Inuit oral testimony had long placed it, in Wilmot and Crampton Bay, near the Adelaide Peninsula, where the ice had taken the ship and where the stories had kept it. The bell was presented as a discovery, not as a confirmation. It was framed as the end of a search, not as the validation of a knowledge system that had been dismissed for a century and a half.

The bell itself said nothing about any of this. It sat in its tank and gave up its chloride ions and waited for the next bath.

The search for Terror did not wait for the bell to stabilize. It accelerated. Parks Canada had been running annual surveys since 2008, when the government announced a six-week search deploying the icebreaker CCGS Sir Wilfrid Laurier with a budget of seventy-five thousand dollars and a dual mandate: find the ships, and reinforce Canada’s claims regarding sovereignty over the Northwest Passage. The mandate had always been double. The search was science and it was also policy, and the two had never been fully separable. But after 2014, when Erebus was found in Wilmot and Crampton Bay near the Adelaide Peninsula, the pressure changed. There was now a known wreck to protect, a known site to study, and a known success to repeat. The following seasons brought more sonar lines, more autonomous underwater vehicles, more side-scan data processed in real time aboard survey vessels holding position against the wind.

The high-tech approach had limits. The seabed around King William Island was shallow and littered with ice-scoured rock, and the sonar returns were cluttered with false positives. The searchers were looking for a wooden ship in a landscape of stone and sediment, and the instruments could not always tell the difference. They needed a better starting point.

They got one from a memory.

Sammy Kogvik was a Canadian Ranger and an Inuit crewmember on the Arctic Research Foundation’s vessel Martin Bergmann when he told a story to the expedition leader. Years earlier, he said, he had been travelling in Terror Bay when he saw something strange: a long wooden pole sticking out of the sea ice. He had thought it might be a mast. He had taken a photograph, but the photograph was lost. The story was not a chart coordinate or a sonar contact. It was a recollection, passed from one person to another, of a thing seen once and not understood at the time.

The Arctic Research Foundation acted on it. In September 2016, the Martin Bergmann entered Terror Bay and ran a sonar survey along the line Kogvik described. The wreck was there. It was seated upright on the seabed at a depth of sixty-nine to seventy-nine feet, largely intact, its hull still coherent after more than a century and a half in cold water. The announcement came on 12 September 2016. The wreck of HMS Terror had been found in Terror Bay, off the southwest coast of King William Island, at 68°54′13″N 98°56′18″W, in what was described as “pristine” condition.

The discovery was ninety-two kilometres south of the location where the ship had been reported abandoned. It was some fifty kilometres from the wreck of Erebus. It was nearly one hundred kilometres south of where historians had long assumed it must lie.

The gap between assumption and fact was not a small error of navigation. It was a structural failure of interpretation. The Victorian search narratives had placed Terror where the written record said it should be, near Victory Point, where the ships had been abandoned according to the note found by McClintock’s sledge party in 1859. The Inuit testimony had placed it elsewhere, in Terror Bay, where men had seen a ship with a mast above the ice. The written record had been preferred. The testimony had been treated as imprecise, confused, or unreliable.

The wreck was found where the testimony said it would be.

The Arctic Research Foundation’s expedition was not the only one searching that season. Parks Canada had its own survey underway, with its own sonar data and its own priorities. The two efforts were parallel and not always coordinated. The foundation’s discovery was announced before Parks Canada had confirmed it independently, and there were questions afterward about who deserved credit and who had jurisdiction over the site. The wreck lay in Nunavut, within Canadian waters, but the foundation was a private organization and Parks Canada was a government agency, and the lines between them were not clearly drawn.

The site itself was pristine. That was the word used in the announcement, and it was accurate. The cold water and the lack of light had preserved the ship to a degree that surprised even the archaeologists who had studied Erebus. The hull was intact. The decks were intact. The cabin windows were intact. There was a wide exhaust pipe from the locomotive engine still installed in its original location, which meant that Terror had not been stripped or salvaged before it sank. It had gone down as it was, with its equipment and its stores and whatever else remained aboard.

The bell from Erebus and the wreck of Terror entered public memory together, as two halves of a single story. The bell was the symbol: small, portable, inscribed with a name and a date, easy to photograph and easy to display. The wreck was the substance: large, immovable, inaccessible except through cameras and sonar images, too deep for divers without specialized equipment and too fragile for casual visitation. The bell could travel. The wreck could not.

The ceremonies that followed reflected this difference. The bell was loaned to museums and shown in exhibitions. The wreck was monitored by Parks Canada and protected under legislation that governed access to historic sites. The bell became a relic that Canadians could see in person. The wreck became a site that Canadians could only see on screens.

Both were presented as evidence of national achievement. Both were framed as answers to questions that had been asked for generations. But neither answered the question that mattered most to the descendants of the men who had sailed on those ships.

What happened to the crews?

The ships had been found. Their positions were known. Their condition was documented. But the men who had sailed them were still missing. The wrecks were not graves in any formal sense; they were structures that might contain human remains or might not. The cabinet in Erebus’s quarter gallery, still unopened when the first season ended, might contain documents or instruments or personal effects that would tell what happened in the final months. The wreck of Terror might contain similar evidence, preserved by the same cold water that had preserved the ship itself.

The discovery of both wrecks provided conclusive geographical answers but created a new pressure: the ships were now known to guard the most human evidence of the disaster.

The evidence lag that had defined the search from its beginning did not end with the discovery of Erebus or Terror. It changed form. The old interval—the years that once stretched between an event in the Arctic and its slow acceptance in London—had been measured in ships that sailed north and returned with nothing or with relics that took months to reach the Admiralty. Now it was measured in seasons and in data streams, in sonar files transmitted by satellite and posted online within hours of collection. The public could follow the search in real time. The wreck could be seen on YouTube before it was seen by archaeologists.

But the fundamental problem remained. The ships were found. The men were not explained.

The Inuit testimony that had guided the search for Terror had also described what happened to the crews: men who walked south, men who fell, men who were seen by hunters in the years after the ships were abandoned. That testimony had been collected by John Rae in 1854 and by McClintock’s interpreters in 1859 and by generations of Inuit who had passed the stories down through families and communities. It had been written down by outsiders who did not always understand what they were hearing or trust what they were told.

Now it had been confirmed by sonar.

The confirmation did not resolve the contradictions in the written record. It added to them. If Terror was found in Terror Bay, then the note at Victory Point did not tell the whole story of where the ships went or when they went there. If Erebus was found in Wilmot and Crampton Bay, then the drift patterns reconstructed by historians were wrong or incomplete. The physical evidence did not replace the written evidence; it complicated it.

The bell sat in its tank in Ottawa and gave up its chloride ions one bath at a time.

The wreck of Terror sat upright on the seabed in Terror Bay and waited for the next season of study.

The men who had sailed on those ships waited for someone to find them.

The search for Franklin had begun as a rescue mission and become a forensic inquiry. It had passed through phases: the Admiralty searches under James Clark Ross, the private expeditions funded by Lady Jane Franklin’s campaign, the overland journeys of John Rae and Thomas Simpson, the sledge parties of McClintock and Hobson, the graves on Beechey Island, the boat with two skeletons on King William Island, the note at Victory Point that told of Franklin’s death and the abandonment of the ships. Each phase had added evidence and each phase had raised new questions.

The discovery of Erebus in 2014 and Terror in 2016 added two more pieces to a puzzle that had never been completed. They confirmed what Inuit testimony had long asserted: that one ship had been seen in Wilmot and Crampton Bay and another in Terror Bay, that both had survived longer than the written record suggested, and that their final positions were not where historians had placed them. The wrecks were designated as the Wrecks of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror National Historic Site, with their precise locations undisclosed.

They also confirmed that the ships themselves were now known sites with known coordinates, protected by law and monitored by institutions, accessible only through permits and cameras and carefully managed research programs.

The next phase would require opening what had been closed for more than a century and a half.

The cabinet in Erebus’s quarter gallery was still sealed when the first season ended. It would be opened in a later season, under controlled conditions, with conservators standing by to stabilize whatever was inside before it deteriorated in air after so long underwater. The contents might be documents or instruments or nothing at all. They might answer questions or raise new ones.

The wreck of Terror would be surveyed more thoroughly in subsequent seasons. Its interior would be mapped by remotely operated vehicles small enough to enter through openings in the hull. Its cabins would be photographed and its artifacts catalogued before any decision was made about recovery.

The men who had sailed on those ships would remain missing until someone found their bones.

The bell from Erebus hung in its tank in Ottawa and waited for its next bath.

The wreck of Terror sat upright on the seabed in Terror Bay and waited for its next visitor.

The Arctic Research Foundation’s decision to act on Kogvik’s recollection was not a rejection of the technology that had driven the search for a decade; it was an admission that the technology needed a place to begin. Sonar could map a seabed with astonishing resolution, but it could not choose which patch of seabed deserved the closest look. A remembered mast above the ice, offered by a man who had grown up travelling the same waters his ancestors had travelled, supplied precisely what the instruments lacked: a hypothesis grounded in experience rather than in the written record’s assumptions.

The convergence was uneasy. Kogvik’s story had no coordinates attached to it, no depth soundings, no corroborating chart. It had only the authority of a person who had been there and remembered what he saw. For much of the previous century and a half, that kind of authority had counted for little against Admiralty documents and published narratives. In 2016, it directed a vessel to a specific bay and told the crew where along the shoreline to run their lines.

The institutional friction that followed the discovery was a measure of how much had changed since the Admiralty sent its first relief expeditions into Lancaster Sound. The wreck lay in Nunavut, in waters subject to Canadian sovereignty and Inuit land claims, and its fate was now entangled with questions that had nothing to do with Victorian exploration: who spoke for the site, who controlled access, who benefited from the story it would generate.

Parks Canada held a statutory mandate over historic wrecks. The Arctic Research Foundation held the credit for finding it. Inuit organizations, whose testimony had guided the search and whose homeland contained the wreck, held a claim that was both older and more immediate than either. The three parties did not always agree on procedure, but they agreed on one principle that would have been unthinkable to the men who sailed with Franklin: that the knowledge of the people who lived in the Arctic was evidence, not folklore, and that any future work on the wrecks would have to be conducted with their consent and participation. This principle was formalized in a memorandum of understanding governing access to both wrecks.

That principle had been building for years before the bell came up. The 1993 agreement that created Nunavut, and the land-claim structures that accompanied it, had begun to formalize Inuit authority over heritage within the territory. The discovery of Erebus in 2014 had accelerated the process, because the wreck lay in an area subject to Inuit use and occupancy, and because the Inuit oral record had placed it there long before any sonar array confirmed it. By the time Terror was found, the framework had shifted enough that Parks Canada and Inuit organizations could negotiate a memorandum of understanding governing access to both wrecks. The memorandum acknowledged something the Victorian searchers never had: that the Inuit were not bystanders to the Franklin story but participants in it, witnesses whose accounts of ships and starving men had been preserved across generations while the Admiralty’s own searches failed.

The wrecks themselves imposed their own terms on this new arrangement. Erebus lay in shallow water, broken and scattered, its hull collapsed and its contents dispersed across the seabed. Terror lay deeper but intact, seated upright, its decks still coherent and its cabins still sealed. The difference shaped what each site could yield and what each would demand. Erebus would require excavation, the careful recovery of artifacts from a debris field that had been disturbed by ice and current for more than a century and a half.

The Arctic held what it held and gave it up slowly, one season at a time, one artifact at a time, one answer at a time.

The search would continue because it could not stop.
