Chapter 22

The Cabinet in the Quarter Gallery

The sonar image had shown only mass and depth: a green pixelated shadow eleven meters long, resting in thirty meters of water, registered at 68°14′44.8″N 98°52′22.3″W. The coordinates were precise. The object was not. Between that electronic signal and any claim of identity stretched a gap that technology alone could not close.

Six days later, on September 7, 2014, the first remote-operated vehicle descended through the same hole in the ice that had admitted the side-scan sonar. The ROV was a tethered machine, camera-mounted, built for inspection rather than retrieval. Its operators worked from a control room aboard the Canadian Coast Guard vessel, screens glowing in the perpetual twilight of an Arctic autumn. The vessel held position in Wilmot and Crampton Bay, engines cycling against wind and current, while the cable paid out through freezing water. The descent took forty minutes. The image that reached the surface first was not a hull or a mast but a curve of metal against sediment, filmed in the flat illumination of the ROV’s lights. The operators adjusted altitude and angle. The curve resolved into a bell: a ship’s bell, lying on its side in the silt, the crown slightly buried, the mouth open toward the camera.

The bell was the first concrete object. It was also the first evidence that could be compared against a known inventory. HMS Erebus had carried a ship’s bell cast in 1826, the year of her construction at Pembroke Dockyard. The Admiralty’s specifications for bomb vessels of the Hecla class required a bell of particular dimensions, bronze, marked with the ship’s name and the date of her launching. The ROV’s camera could not yet read any inscription through the sediment that half-obscured the surface. But the shape was unmistakable: not a modern fishing vessel’s signal bell, not industrial debris, but the ceremonial instrument of a Royal Navy ship of the early nineteenth century. The operators held position and recorded. Above them, on the surface, archaeologists compared the video feed against deck plans preserved in the National Maritime Museum at Greenwich, plans drawn in 1826 and modified in 1845.

The identification did not proceed from the general to the specific. It moved in the opposite direction: from this object, this bell, to the ship that had carried it, to the expedition that had sailed with it, to the questions that had accumulated since 1848. The bell was small enough to be lifted by two men. It had once hung in a belfry or on a deck bracket, sounded to mark the watches, to signal fog, to announce death. Now it lay in thirty meters of Arctic water, its clapper perhaps still attached, its tone forever silenced. The ROV recorded it from three angles before moving on.

The second object appeared in the same dive sequence: a cannon, mounted on a carriage, resting upright against what seemed to be deck planking. The barrel was bronze, identified by its color even through the filtering effect of water and artificial light. The carriage had collapsed partially, the wheels buried, the trail sunk into sediment. But the mounting was clearly deck-mounted, not a loose gun but a fixture of the vessel’s armament. HMS Erebus had carried six cannon of various calibers, plus mortars, plus small arms: the equipment of a ship built for bombardment, repurposed for polar exploration. The presence of a deck-mounted cannon confirmed that the ROV was filming an intact structure, not scattered debris. The gun had not rolled from a capsized hull. It stood where it had been secured, in a ship that had settled rather than shattered.

The archaeologists aboard the support vessel worked in real time. They had access to the 1845 fitting-out records, to the modifications made at Deptford Dockyard, to the inventories compiled before departure from Greenhithe. Each object the ROV transmitted was checked against these documents. The bell matched the specifications. The cannon matched the armament list. The deck planking visible around the gun matched the dimensions of Erebus’s upper deck, rebuilt in 1845 to accommodate the steam engine and its exhaust funnel. The evidence was not yet conclusive. But it was directional: each match narrowed the possibilities, each confirmation built a chain that led toward certainty. This forensic process mirrored the earlier search for the Victory Point Note, but now the evidence was not a single paper but a constellation of objects, each requiring verification against the known record.

The ROV completed its first survey dive on September 7 and surfaced for battery exchange and data download. The archaeological team, led by Ryan Harris and Marc-André Bernier of Parks Canada, reviewed the footage through the night. They were working against time. The ice that had held open their access hole was reforming. The season for Arctic navigation was closing. They had perhaps two weeks before weather and ice conditions would force withdrawal until the following year. The discovery of a probable wreck was not, in itself, sufficient. They needed objects that could be identified with precision, that could survive the transition from underwater image to public announcement, that could withstand the scrutiny of historians and the expectations raised by seventeen decades of search. This pressure to produce definitive proof echoed the Admiralty’s demand for ‘decisive information’ a century earlier, when John Rae’s report arrived in 1854.

On September 9, the ROV descended again. This time the operators followed the line of the deck they had identified, moving sternward from the cannon. The Erebus had been a small ship: 372 tons, 104 feet in length, crowded with equipment and men. Her stern section contained the officers’ quarters, the captain’s cabin, the spaces where decisions had been made and records kept. The ROV’s lights swept across what appeared to be a collapsed structure, timbers fallen inward, the geometry of the hull distorted by weight and time. Within this collapse, the camera found objects that had settled into the angles formed by fallen beams.

The first was a ceramic plate, intact, lying face-down in sediment. The ROV could not turn it without risk of damage. But the visible surface showed the pattern of Royal Navy issue: the blue transfer-printed design used for officers’ mess equipment in the 1840s. The plate was not unique to Erebus. It was consistent with Erebus. The operators recorded its position and moved on.

The second object was more specific: a writing desk, or what remained of one. The wood had rotted to a dark pulp, but the brass fittings survived, green with corrosion but still recognizable: hinges, a lock mechanism, the frame of a drawer. Desks of this pattern had been issued to naval officers for shipboard use. The presence of such an object in the stern quarter suggested that the ROV was filming the remains of an officer’s cabin, perhaps the captain’s own quarters. Sir John Franklin had occupied a stern cabin on the lower deck, a space that combined sleeping quarters with administrative function. The desk might have been his. The desk might have contained his papers. The desk was inaccessible, buried under fallen timbers, its drawers presumably filled with water and sediment. But it existed. It was there.

The third object, filmed on September 10, completed the sequence that would allow public identification. The ROV had moved to the extreme stern, where the hull curved inward to form the transom. Here the structure was more intact, protected perhaps by the angle of the ship’s settlement. The camera found a cabinet: a wooden cupboard, doors closed, mounted against what had been the inner wall of the quarter gallery. The quarter gallery was a small extension of the stern, projecting over the water, containing toilet facilities and sometimes additional storage. On Erebus, the quarter gallery had been modified during the 1845 refit, its interior reconfigured to accommodate the new heating system. The cabinet had survived where larger structures had failed. Its doors were swollen shut, the wood dark with preservation, the brass handles still in place.

The ROV held position for eleven minutes, recording the cabinet from multiple angles. The archaeologists on the surface studied the footage in conjunction with the deck plans. The cabinet’s location matched the known position of storage compartments in the modified quarter gallery. Its construction matched the joinery specified in the 1845 work orders. Its survival suggested an anaerobic environment within the collapsed hull, a space where oxygen had been excluded and organic material preserved. The cabinet might contain anything. It might contain nothing. Its presence was itself the evidence: a human-scale object, a fixture of daily life, intact enough to be recognized, specific enough to be matched against the historical record.

On October 1, 2014, Parks Canada announced that the wreck was HMS Erebus. The identification rested not on a single object but on the cumulative weight of the evidence chain: the bell, the cannon, the deck structure, the ceramic plate, the writing desk, the cabinet in the quarter gallery. Each object had been compared against the 1845 inventories and the archaeological expectations derived from them. Each comparison had returned a match. The announcement was made in Ottawa, in London, in the media markets that had sustained public interest in the Franklin search for a century and a half. The coordinates remained unpublished, protected under the terms of the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement and the Heritage Wreck Site designation that followed identification. The wreckage had been found on September 2, but the formal identification came a month later, a final, official lag between discovery and recognition.

The forensic aftermath extended through October and into November. The ROV completed seventeen dives before ice closed the site. Each dive added objects to the inventory, each object added precision to the archaeological understanding. The bell was filmed again, closer, the sediment brushed away by the ROV’s manipulator arm. The inscription became partially visible: the date 1826, the name partially obscured but consistent with “Erebus.” The cannon was identified more precisely: a 12-pounder brass gun, one of two recorded in the 1845 armament list as mounted on the upper deck. The writing desk was surveyed but not disturbed; its drawers remained unopened, their contents preserved for future recovery under more controlled conditions.

The cabinet in the quarter gallery became the focal point of archaeological speculation. Its doors could not be forced without risk of destroying what lay within. The ROV’s manipulator arms were not delicate enough for such work. The cabinet would wait for the following season, for specialized equipment, for the establishment of a permanent monitoring station at the site. But its presence shaped the interpretation of everything else. A cabinet in a quarter gallery was storage for personal effects, for medical supplies, for the small necessities of shipboard life. If the cabinet had survived, other containers might have survived. If the contents had been preserved by anaerobic conditions, they might include paper, leather, materials that could survive underwater if protected from oxygen and disturbance.

The archaeological method applied to the Erebus site was distinct from the search techniques that had preceded it. The side-scan sonar had been a survey tool, covering ground, identifying anomalies. The ROV was a forensic instrument, working in place, building evidence through accumulation rather than through elimination. Each object was recorded in situ, its position mapped against the hull structure, its condition assessed for preservation potential. The archaeologists were not searching for a ship. They were reconstructing a ship, piece by piece, from the material that remained. The process was slow, expensive, dependent on weather and ice and the logistical capacity of Parks Canada and its Coast Guard partners. It was also irreversible. Each dive disturbed the site slightly, each measurement removed some uncertainty and created new obligations for protection.

The contrast between the sonar image and the ROV footage marked a transition in the nature of the Franklin inquiry. The sonar had offered abstraction: mass, depth, location. The ROV offered specificity: this bell, this gun, this cabinet. The abstraction had generated hope but no certainty. The specificity generated certainty but also new questions. The Erebus had been found where it was not expected, in Wilmot and Crampton Bay rather than in the waters north of King William Island where the 1859 searches had concentrated. Its condition was better than the scattered debris and abandoned boats that McClintock had found. Its contents were accessible in ways that the Victory Point record, written in haste and left in a cairn, had never been. The ship was a time capsule. The time capsule required interpretation.

The interpretation proceeded on multiple tracks. The archaeological team focused on preservation and documentation, building the case for site protection and future recovery. The historians focused on reconciliation: how did the location of the wreck correspond to the Inuit testimony collected in the 1850s, to the relics found by Rae and McClintock, to the Victory Point record’s account of the ships’ abandonment in 1848? The location in Wilmot and Crampton Bay suggested that Erebus had traveled farther south than the 1859 searchers had believed, that the ship had remained navigable longer than the Victory Point record implied, that the final abandonment had been more complex than a single desperate march. The cabinet in the quarter gallery, intact, suggested that some part of the ship’s interior had remained habitable, or at least accessible, after the date of the last known written record.

The Inuit testimony, recorded by John Rae in 1854 and by others in subsequent decades, described a ship that had sunk in shallow water, visible from the surface, visited by hunters who recovered materials from its decks. The Erebus site matched this description in depth but not precisely in location. The testimony had placed the sunken ship in the vicinity of O’Reilly Island, farther north. The archaeological discovery required a re-reading of the testimony, a recognition that Inuit geographical knowledge, transmitted across generations and filtered through interpreters, might not align with European cartography. The testimony funnel that had shaped the nineteenth-century search—raw experience reduced to admissible form, Inuit observation translated into naval coordinates—was now operating in reverse: the physical evidence forcing a reconsideration of the testimony that had preceded it.

The ROV dives of September and October 2014 established the methodology that would govern subsequent work. No object was recovered. The bell remained on the seabed, filmed but not lifted. The cabinet remained closed. The writing desk remained in place. The archaeological ethic of in situ preservation, developed for terrestrial sites and adapted for underwater contexts, was applied with strictness born of the site’s significance. The Erebus was not a shipwreck to be salvaged. It was a heritage site to be monitored, studied, protected against the natural processes of decay and the human processes of interference. The discovery had transformed the Franklin search from an operation of finding to an operation of stewardship.

The stewardship carried costs that became apparent as the season ended. The support vessel withdrew in early November, leaving a temporary marker buoy that would not survive the winter ice. The site would be unmonitored for eight months, its coordinates known only to the archaeological team and the government agencies responsible for protection. The following season would require new icebreaking, new positioning, new dives. The expense was substantial: the 2014 search had been funded through a combination of government allocation and private partnership, the latter organized by the Victoria Strait Expedition and its corporate sponsors. The discovery had generated public interest that would sustain political support for continued funding. But the scale of the work required—multiple seasons, specialized equipment, international collaboration—exceeded the resources that had found the ship. This logistical reality echoed the challenges faced by Lady Jane Franklin in funding the Fox a century and a half before.

The cabinet in the quarter gallery became, in the months that followed, a symbol of what remained to be done. It was photographed, analyzed, discussed in conference presentations and journal articles. Its probable contents were speculated upon: medicines from the ship’s dispensary, perhaps, or personal papers from an officer’s effects, or the small instruments of navigation and timekeeping that had equipped the expedition. Each speculation generated new research into the 1845 inventories, new comparisons with other naval wrecks of the period, new protocols for the handling of waterlogged organic materials. The cabinet was not yet evidence. It was the promise of evidence, deferred until the technology and the funding and the permissions aligned.

The discovery of Erebus had closed one phase of the Franklin inquiry and opened another. The phase that closed was the search for location: the 170-year effort to fix the ships’ positions on a chart, to transform Inuit testimony and scattered relics into coordinates that could be visited and verified. The phase that opened was the interpretation of presence: the work of understanding what the ships contained, how they had been used, what they could reveal about the final years of the expedition. The cabinet in the quarter gallery stood at the threshold between these phases. It was a physical object, located, filmed, measured. It was also a container of unknowns, its contents protected by swollen wood and freezing water and the deliberate patience of archaeological method.

The wreck’s pristine and interpretable state—now physically accessible—created a new pressure: what did this time capsule mean, and what must be done with it? This was not an academic question. The Erebus lay in waters claimed by Canada and inhabited by Inuit communities with their own relationship to the Franklin story; it was subject also to international maritime law and domestic heritage legislation. Every decision about access, recovery or publication of findings required negotiation among these interests.

The ship lost in 1848 had become, by 2014, a site of competing obligations: to science and history; to descendants of those who had sailed; to descendants of those who had watched them sink. In the quarter gallery the cabinet remained unopened; whatever answers it contained stayed suspended through another season and another chapter as another generation of searchers stood once more at screens aboard vessels holding position against Arctic winds—yet whether any generation would agree on what those answers meant remained an open question.
