Chapter 3

The Machinery of Unease

A clerk in the Hydrographic Office dipped his pen into the inkwell as another inquiry was laid open before him. The letter was from Captain William Parry, retired in Bath, with memories of three Arctic expeditions that had taught him what ice could do to men who underestimated it. He wanted to know: had any news arrived from the whalers in Davis Strait? In the London winter of 1846–47, filled with coal smoke and yellow fog, James Washington opened the daily correspondence ledger to an entry dated 12 December 1846 and recorded the query in his neat hand. He noted that no intelligence had been received, filed the letter in a bundle that would grow thick with such inquiries, and did not flag it for urgent attention. The expedition had sailed with provisions for three years, and eighteen months remained before any reasonable anxiety could begin.

The machinery of empire was not indifference, exactly, but a system calibrated to manage risk through scheduled intervals rather than imaginative dread. The Admiralty had sent Sir John Franklin north with no requirement to report his position, no rendezvous point, no contingency for silence. The assumption embedded in the sailing orders was that competence would announce itself through success, and that failure, if it came, would arrive with physical evidence: wreckage, survivors, a message in a cairn. Until such evidence appeared, the absence of news was simply the ordinary condition of ships beyond reach, doing what they had been sent to do. The silence that followed their departure had not yet been interpreted as alarm. It was merely the expected state of vessels operating where no telegraph could follow, where the only messengers were the whalers who chanced upon them in open water.

Yet the silence accumulated weight in private. In that same winter of 1846–47, the Hydrographer of the Navy received a letter from his friend John Barrow, the senior Second Secretary at the Admiralty who had done more than any man to promote Arctic exploration. Barrow’s son George had sailed as a midshipman in the Terror. The elder Barrow made inquiries through official channels and was reassured that no grounds for anxiety existed.

But he wrote again in February 1847, and the tone had shifted from inquiry to something closer to insistence. The Hydrographer replied with the standard formulation: the ships were provisioned for three years, the whalers had reported them well in the summer of 1845, the ice conditions in Lancaster Sound were known to be variable. His confidence was professional, not personal. He had spent his career translating the Arctic into charts and sailing directions, and he understood better than most how little those documents revealed about what actually happened to men who disappeared into the ice.

The difference between Barrow’s private unease and the Hydrographer’s professional restraint measured the distance between love and bureaucracy. George Barrow was twenty years old. The Admiralty’s files contained no provision for the anxiety of fathers.

In March 1847, the whaling fleet prepared to sail for Davis Strait. The Admiralty sent no special instructions to the masters beyond the standing request to report any unusual sightings. The whalers were commercial vessels, not naval auxiliaries; their cooperation was voluntary, their observations unreliable. They had their own hunting grounds to reach, their own profits to secure. The clerk who compiled their reports that autumn noted the usual litany of ice conditions, whale sightings, weather. No ship had encountered the Erebus or Terror. No wreckage had been seen. The clerk wrote “nothing” in the margin and turned to the next document.

This was not yet a search. It was the collection of negative data, the gradual confirmation that a vast area contained no trace of what it was supposed to contain. The Arctic swallowed information as efficiently as it swallowed ships. Each whaler’s report that found nothing added to a pattern that had no name, only absence.

Lady Jane Franklin did not wait for official concern to crystallize. In April 1847, she wrote to the Hydrographer proposing that a small steamer be sent to Lancaster Sound to establish a depot of provisions and a message post where her husband’s ships might find news and supplies if they emerged from the west. The proposal was sensible, even prescient: it recognized that Franklin might be blocked by ice but still alive, that a single point of contact might bridge the gap between expedition and rescue. The Hydrographer forwarded the letter to the Admiralty with a noncommittal endorsement. The Lords of the Admiralty declined. The expedition was not overdue. The expense was not justified. The precedent—sending relief before distress was confirmed—would create an expectation that the Navy could not sustain.

Jane Franklin kept the correspondence. She would return to it, and to the men who had rejected it, with a persistence that would outlast careers and governments. For now, she accepted the refusal with the surface compliance expected of naval wives, and turned to her own networks of influence. She wrote to Arctic veterans, to scientific societies, to the editors of newspapers. She collected information about ice conditions, about the experiences of previous expeditions, about the limits of the Admiralty’s commitment. She was building a case before the case was acknowledged to exist.

The summer of 1847 brought no news. The whalers returned in September with their holds full of oil and their logs empty of sightings. The Admiralty’s public posture remained unchanged: confidence in Franklin’s competence, confidence in the provisions, confidence in the plan. But behind this confidence, the machinery of unease began to turn. In October 1847, the Admiralty’s Arctic Committee—an informal body of officers with polar experience—met to discuss what might be done if the expedition failed to return in 1848. The minutes of this meeting have not survived, but its existence is recorded in a memorandum from the First Lord to the First Sea Lord, dated 23 October 1847. The memorandum proposes that preparations be made for the possible necessity of sending relief, and asks for estimates of the ships and stores that would be required.

The first official acknowledgment that Franklin might need help arrived tentative and conditional, hedged with subjunctives. The preparations were to be made, not executed. The necessity was possible, not probable. The relief was contingent on failure, and failure had not been admitted. The machinery moved, but it moved slowly, constrained by its own design.

The plan that emerged from these discussions revealed the Admiralty’s assumptions about what had gone wrong. Franklin, they believed, had pushed too far west, become trapped in the ice off the Alaskan coast, and would need rescue from the Pacific side. The search would therefore proceed through the Bering Strait, not Lancaster Sound. This was the logical extension of Franklin’s original instructions, which had directed him to exit the Arctic via the Bering Strait after completing the Northwest Passage. It was also, as later events would prove, exactly wrong. The Erebus and Terror were not in the Pacific. They were in the central Arctic Archipelago, roughly halfway between the Atlantic and Pacific search zones, in a region that no rescue plan had considered.

The choice of the Pacific route reflected more than misjudgment. It reflected the Admiralty’s institutional memory of previous Arctic disasters. In 1819–22, William Parry’s expeditions had demonstrated that Lancaster Sound offered a viable entrance to the Northwest Passage, but the final exit remained uncertain. The Franklin expedition had been designed to solve this problem by completing the passage from the Atlantic side and emerging in the Pacific. If Franklin had followed his instructions, he would now be in the Pacific—or dead in the attempt. The Admiralty could not imagine that he had stopped halfway, that he had abandoned the goal for survival, that he had become trapped in a region that his own orders had treated as transitional rather than terminal. The machinery of planning could only process the information it had been designed to receive.

In November 1847, the Admiralty approached James Clark Ross. The approach was indirect, almost embarrassed. Ross was the most experienced Arctic navigator alive, the discoverer of the North Magnetic Pole, the veteran of four expeditions that had pushed deeper into the ice than any man living. He was also, by his own account, finished with the Arctic. He had married late, at forty-five, to a woman half his age, and he had promised her that he would not return to the ice. The Admiralty’s invitation put this promise to the test.

Ross’s hesitation is documented in a letter to his friend and former shipmate, Commander Edward Belcher, dated 3 December 1847. Ross wrote that he had been asked to take into consideration a relief expedition, that he had initially refused, that he had been pressed again with assurances that no other man could do the work. The Admiralty’s need and Ross’s reluctance circled each other through the winter. The First Lord wrote personally. The Duke of Wellington, Master-General of the Ordnance, added his voice. Ross’s wife, Ann, watched her husband’s resistance erode under the weight of obligation and, perhaps, the secret attraction of one more adventure.

He accepted on 3 March 1848. The commission appointed him to command two ships, the Enterprise and the Investigator, with orders to proceed to the Bering Strait and search for Franklin along the Alaskan coast. The ships were not ready. The stores were not assembled. The season was already advanced. Ross would not sail until June, by which time the Arctic navigation window was narrowing. The machinery of preparation, so slow to start, now raced against constraints it had created.

The choice of Ross revealed the Admiralty’s preference for prestige over innovation. Ross was a known quantity, a safe pair of hands, a man who had succeeded before. He was also a man of fixed methods, committed to the same techniques—sledge travel, depot laying, systematic coastal survey—that had served him in the 1830s. The Admiralty did not ask whether these methods were suited to a search rather than an exploration, whether the scale of the unknown area demanded new approaches, whether the time lost to preparation might be recovered through boldness. They asked only for Ross, and Ross they received.

His instructions, dated 15 March 1848, directed him to establish himself on the Alaskan coast, to send out sledge parties in all directions, to remain in the Arctic for two years if necessary. The Admiralty provided two ships where four might have been justified, a two-year commitment where three or four might have been required. The scale of the search was calibrated to the scale of the anxiety, and the anxiety was still, officially, provisional. Franklin was merely delayed. Ross was merely precautionary. The expedition was relief, not rescue.

This calibration would prove fatal. The Erebus and Terror had already been abandoned when Ross received his commission. The note that would later be found at Victory Point, the definitive record of the expedition’s collapse, was already written, already cached, already waiting for eyes that would not arrive for eleven years. The date on that note—25 April 1848—fell between Ross’s acceptance and his sailing, a gap of six weeks in which the last survivors of Franklin’s command were leaving their ships for a death march that no search plan had anticipated.

The Victory Point record, when finally discovered, would contain two messages. The first, dated 28 May 1847 and signed by Lieutenant Graham Gore and Mate Charles Frederick Des Voeux after being filled out by James Fitzjames on board ship, reported all well: ‘H.M.Ships Erebus and Terror Wintered in the Ice in Lat. 70°5’N Long. 98°23’W Having wintered in 1846-7 at Beechey Island’—a clerical error for 1845–46—and noted a party had left the ships on 24 May 1847. The second message, added in April 1848 and signed by Captain Francis Crozier after Fitzjames wrote an addendum explaining their circumstances, recorded catastrophe: ‘HMShips Terror and Erebus were deserted on the 22nd April… having been beset since 12th Sept 1846’, with crews reduced to 105 souls under Crozier’s command after Sir John Franklin died on 11 June 1847.

Between these two messages, a year of decisions had passed that no document recorded. Why had Franklin pushed west in 1846 when the ice was closing? Why had he not retreated to Beechey Island, where supplies might have been cached? Why had Crozier waited until April 1848 to abandon ships that had been trapped since September 1846? The questions would accumulate faster than answers, each one exposing the gap between what London assumed and what the Arctic demanded.

In the spring of 1848, these questions did not yet exist. Ross prepared his expedition with the methodical care that had always characterized his work. He selected officers who had served with him before, men whose loyalty and competence were proven. He inspected the ships, the stores, the equipment. He wrote to his wife with reassurances that he would return, that this was the last time, that the Arctic had no more hold on him. The letters crossed with her replies, which begged him to reconsider, to remember his promise, to think of the life they were building together.

The personal cost of Ross’s acceptance would be measured in these letters, in the months of separation, in the child born while he was beyond communication. The institutional cost would be measured in time. Every week of preparation, every delay in sailing, every cautious choice of route and method, carried the search further from the moment when it might have mattered. The persistent delay between event and acknowledgment—what the book will later call the Evidence Lag—was not a failure of individual judgment but a feature of the system. Information could only travel as fast as ships could sail, and ships could only sail when the ice permitted. The Arctic imposed its own calendar, and London’s machinery of response could not accelerate it.

Yet even within these constraints, choices were made that narrowed the possibility of success. The Admiralty’s fixation on the Pacific route, Ross’s commitment to established methods, the scale of the expedition relative to the scale of the search area—these were decisions that reflected institutional preference rather than Arctic reality. The machinery of unease had produced a plan that addressed the wrong ocean, assumed the wrong problem, and deployed the wrong resources. Not negligence exactly: competent procedure applied to an unprecedented situation by men confident that what had worked before would work again.

In April 1848, as Crozier’s party dragged their boats across the ice of King William Island, Ross was still in England. His ships would not reach the Bering Strait until August. By then, the survivors of Franklin’s expedition were already dead or dying, scattered across a landscape that no search plan had included. The two expeditions—Franklin’s and Ross’s—moved in parallel through the same months, separated by thousands of miles and by the failure of imagination that had placed them in different worlds.

The public announcement of Ross’s mission came on 15 March 1848, the same day as his formal instructions. The Times reported it briefly, without editorial comment. The Arctic was still distant, still frozen, still beyond the horizon of immediate concern. Lady Jane Franklin wrote to thank the First Lord for his attention to her husband’s welfare. Her letter was civil, grateful, carefully calibrated to maintain the access she would need in the months to come. She did not mention that she had proposed a different search, in a different direction, a year before. She did not need to. The record was in her files, and the files were growing.

The machinery of unease had produced its first product: a rescue mission launched too late, in the wrong direction, under constraints that guaranteed its inadequacy. The Admiralty could claim that it had responded to private anxiety with public action, that no effort had been spared, that the most experienced Arctic commander had been entrusted with the task. These claims were true, as far as they went. They did not go far enough. The gap between institutional response and Arctic reality was already visible to those who knew how to look, and it would widen with every season that passed without news.

James Clark Ross’s search expedition was authorized in 1848, a decision already years behind the unfolding catastrophe. The ships that would carry him west had not yet left their moorings when the last of Franklin’s men were leaving theirs, when the Victory Point note was being folded into its tin cylinder, when the silence that would last more than a century was beginning to settle over the ice.
