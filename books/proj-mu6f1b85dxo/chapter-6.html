Chapter 6

The Lady’s Memorandum

In January 1851, Lady Jane Franklin sat at her desk in London and began drafting a memorandum to Sir Francis Baring, First Lord of the Admiralty. The document accumulated pages: dense with hydrographic argument, citing currents through Wellington Channel, the probable drift of pack ice, the testimony of whaling masters who had observed unusual conditions in specific years. She proposed that the search redirect toward the western coast of Boothia Peninsula, toward the unexplored shores of King William Island, toward the regions where her husband’s written instructions would have directed him if ice proved impassable to the east. The memorandum was copied in her own clear hand, then dispatched through formal channels. It was a strategic brief, written by someone who had studied the problem with professional thoroughness.

The Admiralty’s response came through the usual machinery: polite acknowledgment, restatement of existing plans, assurance that Captain William Penny’s expedition in vessels she had helped fund would investigate the areas she had specified. This fell short of what she sought. She had learned, during her years in the colonies, how administrative reassurance could function as containment. She began to operate outside official channels.

The ice had yielded a beginning, not an ending. The three graves found on Beechey Island in August 1850—John Torrington, John Hartnell, and William Braine, their names recorded by Robert Goodsir, surgeon on the brig Lady Franklin—proved that Erebus and Terror had survived at least one winter. The remnants of that winter camp from 1845 to 1846, the organized nature of the burial ground, the absence of any message indicating subsequent disaster: these fragments spoke to her of continued mission, of ships pressing westward, of her husband alive or having left record of his fate. The silence itself argued for continued search.

By October 1850, the Admiralty’s clerks had filed their reports. The ships that had converged off Beechey Island’s desolate coast had done their work and dispersed. HMS Resolute and Intrepid sailed for home. Captain Erasmus Ommanney turned Assistance toward the east, hunting for open water that might carry word of Franklin’s progress. The search, in the ordinary course of naval business, would have begun its contraction toward conclusion.

Lady Jane Franklin refused this ordinary course.

She was fifty-four years old, childless, possessed of private income and a reputation for intellectual seriousness that had always set her apart from ornamental conventions of her rank. She had accompanied her husband to Van Diemen’s Land during his governorship, survived the political storms of that posting, returned to England to find him appointed to a final, glorious command. The Admiralty’s three-year provision estimate had expired in May 1848. The graves on Beechey Island demonstrated survival. The logical structure of her position was vulnerable—silence can indicate many things—but its emotional force was considerable. She constructed a narrative of continued existence that the Admiralty could not directly refute without appearing to abandon its own officers.

Her method was systematic. She maintained correspondence with every naval officer who had served in the Arctic, soliciting private opinions on where the search should concentrate. She cultivated editors at The Times and the Illustrated London News, providing information that shaped coverage without appearing to dictate it. She addressed meetings of the Royal Geographical Society, where her presence as a woman in that masculine company drew attention she converted to argumentative weight. She raised money for private expeditions that would supplement Admiralty efforts, ensuring her name became attached to vessels and search parties that government could not directly control.

A schooner purchased with subscription funds sailed in 1851 under a naval officer with instructions to probe the region south of Beechey Island. When this expedition returned with negative results, she was already organizing its replacement.

The Admiralty found itself in a position no institutional manual addressed. The widow of a missing officer was not, by established protocol, a stakeholder in operational decisions. Yet Lady Franklin could not be dismissed as hysteric or amateur. She knew the geography. She knew the officers. She controlled independent resources. Most critically, she had mastered Victorian machinery of public opinion, converting private grief into sustained claim on national attention. Each Admiralty meeting that discussed the search now had to account for what she would say, what she would publish, whom she would influence. The institutional caution that might otherwise have recommended winding down encountered counter-pressure that made such caution politically costly.

In April 1851, she published a pamphlet that distributed her arguments to wider audience than private correspondence could reach. The text reviewed the history of the search, criticized Admiralty reliance on single-season expeditions, proposed systematic program of wintering ships that could examine coastlines methodically. The pamphlet was reviewed, discussed, quoted in parliamentary debate. She had transformed the search from naval administrative matter into public cause, with herself as its most visible advocate.

The mechanism she employed was not unique to her situation. Victorian Britain had developed elaborate channels through which informed private opinion could pressure government institutions: the periodical press, the learned society, the subscription committee, the personal introduction to the influential. Lady Franklin operated all these channels simultaneously, with persistence that wore down distinction between official and unofficial action. When the Admiralty authorized additional search vessels in 1851—Assistance and Resolute returning north under Sir Edward Belcher, Pioneer and Intrepid under Sherard Osborn—it did so in atmosphere where failure to act would have drawn immediate public censure. The institutional decision and private campaign had become indistinguishable.

Her advocacy had specific, checkable effects. In November 1851, she wrote directly to senior Admiralty officials proposing that an officer whose sledging abilities she had come to admire through correspondence with Arctic veterans be given command of a new expedition. The Admiralty did not accept this suggestion immediately. But the name entered the file, the connection was established. In April 1857, at Lady Jane Franklin’s request, McClintock agreed to take command of the Fox and led a 25-man crew in a new search for Franklin’s lost expedition in the area west of Repulse Bay. The lag between her first proposal and its eventual implementation measured the distance she had to travel against institutional inertia. But she had set the mechanism in motion six years before it produced its result.

The winter of 1851–52 found her organizing on multiple fronts. She raised funds for a new private expedition to be commanded by a naval officer with experience in northern waters. She lobbied for appointment of a Hudson’s Bay Company officer with Arctic experience to lead another search. She maintained pressure on the Admiralty to extend operational season of its own ships, to authorize wintering parties that could continue the search when ice blocked channels. Her correspondence from this period, preserved in archives of the Royal Geographical Society and the Admiralty, shows methodical mind tracking multiple initiatives simultaneously: grants promised, subscriptions due, officers approached, politicians cultivated. The personal cost of this work—her health, her finances, her social position—she treated as irrelevant to calculation. The search was not simply a cause she supported; it had become the organizing principle of her existence.

The Admiralty’s response to this pressure was not uniform. Some officials admired her determination; others resented what they perceived as interference in professional matters. The family that had initiated the original Franklin search found itself in uncomfortable position of managing a widow whose energy exceeded their own. Sir Edward Belcher, given command of the 1852 expedition, regarded private involvement with aristocratic disdain, yet found his movements reported and second-guessed in ways that limited his operational independence. The hybrid nature of the search—part naval operation, part private venture, part public spectacle—created friction at every level of command. Instructions that would once have flowed through clear hierarchical channels now had to account for multiple sources of authority, multiple definitions of success.

The results of the 1851–52 search season illustrated both possibilities and limitations of her approach. One private expedition penetrated farther north than any previous, confirming existence of Smith Sound and opening theoretical routes to the polar sea. Another discovered a previously unknown strait that complicated all existing theories of Franklin’s probable route. Belcher’s squadron, operating with more substantial resources, achieved less: his flagship and consort were beset in ice, another vessel damaged, the season largely wasted in extrication rather than exploration. Lady Franklin received these reports as they arrived in London, extracting from each the elements that supported continued effort, minimizing or contextualizing setbacks. Her public statements emphasized discovery and progress. Her private correspondence pressed for more.

The strategic use of evidence became increasingly central to her method. The Beechey Island graves she treated not as terminus but as confirmation: proof that the expedition had survived its first winter, that the ships had been sound, that the men had been capable of organized activity. The absence of any message indicating subsequent disaster she interpreted as positive evidence, arguing that Franklin would certainly have left record of any catastrophic event. This construction required constant maintenance. Each expedition that returned without finding Franklin also returned with information that complicated the search: new coastlines that had not been examined, new channels that might have attracted a commander seeking open water, new testimony from Inuit hunters that required evaluation and integration.

Lady Franklin developed her own network for processing this information, corresponding directly with returning officers, obtaining copies of their charts and journals, commissioning independent analyzes from geographers and hydrographers. The Admiralty’s official archive of the search acquired parallel construction in her private papers, one that emphasized different possibilities, proposed different priorities, maintained pressure for different actions. The Testimony Funnel operated on discovered records, focusing on their literal text while ignoring context that might suggest darker interpretations.

The cost of this system fell unevenly. The Admiralty found its operational independence constrained by need to demonstrate activity to public audience whose attention Lady Franklin helped focus and sustain. Officers in the field found their decisions subject to retrospective evaluation by a private citizen whose geographical knowledge was genuine but whose stake in the outcome was personal and absolute. The search itself grew larger, more expensive, more institutionally complex, with each season adding new vessels, new routes, new theories to accumulated weight of effort. What had begun as specific rescue mission for 129 men in two ships had become permanent Arctic establishment, sustained by interaction of naval routine and private advocacy.

By spring of 1852, Lady Franklin had achieved position without precedent in British naval administration. She was not an officer, not a politician, not an official of any recognized kind. Yet her approval had become valuable, her criticism dangerous, her support essential to legitimacy of continued search operations. The expedition that would eventually solve the mystery of her husband’s fate was still five years in the future, its commander not yet selected, its funding not yet arranged. But the conditions that would make it possible—institutional commitment that could not be abandoned, public expectation that demanded resolution, network of experienced officers who had learned to respect her judgment—were already in place.

The Admiralty’s annual estimates for Arctic search operations, debated in Parliament during these years, show quantitative trace of her influence. The expenditure authorized for 1850, the year of the Beechey Island discovery, was substantial: £11, 000 for naval vessels, additional sums for supplies and equipment. The estimates for 1851 and 1852 maintained or increased this level, despite absence of any new information that would justify continued investment by ordinary bureaucratic standards. The institutional justification offered—that the honor of the service, the welfare of its officers, the national reputation for perseverance required continued effort—echoed the language of Lady Franklin’s own advocacy. The boundary between official policy and private pressure had become permeable to the point of disappearance.

Her campaign also established template for subsequent intervention in public affairs by women of her class. The combination of personal stake, intellectual preparation, and strategic use of social networks that she demonstrated would be adapted by others in different causes. But the specific achievement—the transformation of a missing persons case into permanent national obligation, the creation of a search apparatus that would operate for more than a decade beyond any reasonable estimate of survival—remained unique. She had found a way to make institutional time align with personal time, to prevent administrative closure that would have left her husband’s fate undetermined and her own grief without public expression.

The winter of 1852–53 brought new developments that would test this achievement. One of Belcher’s ships, abandoned in the ice, would be recovered by American whalers and returned to Britain as gesture of transatlantic solidarity. Another vessel, stationed at Bering Strait to intercept Franklin from the west, would report contact with Inuit who described ships crushed in ice far from any known location of the expedition. The geographical range of plausible search would expand to encompass entire Arctic archipelago, the temporal range to include possibility that survivors might still exist in some remote location. Lady Franklin would respond to each development with renewed organizational energy, raising funds for new expeditions, pressing the Admiralty for extended operations, maintaining the pressure that had become her life’s work.

The Evidence Lag persisted, narrowed by concrete proof of graves, yet continued in understanding their cause. The Admiralty possessed physical confirmation that Franklin’s expedition had reached Beechey Island, had buried its dead with proper ceremony, had maintained sufficient organization to construct memorials and record names. What happened after that winter remained inaccessible to institutional knowledge, filtered through delays of Arctic communication and the resistance of official imagination to possibilities that private advocacy kept pressing forward.

By March 1853, when Parliament again debated the estimates for Arctic search, the configuration of forces that Lady Franklin had established had become permanent feature of British public life. The Admiralty could not propose ending the search without preparing elaborate justification that would withstand her certain response. The press could not report returning expeditions without seeking her comment. The geographical societies could not meet on polar subjects without her presence as acknowledged authority. She had created a position from which she could not be removed by ordinary political means, embedding her personal quest in the institutional structure of the nation.

The consequence of this achievement extended beyond her own case. Lady Franklin’s successful campaign locked the Admiralty into prolonged, public search, creating conditions for the next, broader wave of voyages and the eventual involvement of John Rae. The hybrid model she established—naval resources supplemented by private funding, official operations validated by public advocacy, institutional decisions shaped by informed external pressure—would persist through the remaining years of the search. When Rae finally brought back physical evidence and Inuit testimony that resolved the fate of the expedition, he would find himself operating within a framework that Lady Franklin had constructed: a national expectation that demanded answers, an institutional commitment that could not be abandoned, a public audience whose attention she had trained upon the Arctic for more than a decade.

The ships would continue sailing north. The graves on Beechey Island would remain marked on charts as beginning, not ending. And the widow who had transformed grief into systematic advocacy would continue her work, each memorandum and subscription and public address extending the search beyond any point that bureaucratic caution would have maintained, until the Arctic finally yielded its full account of what happened to the men who had left England in May 1845 expecting to find the passage and return.
