Chapter 15

The Admiralty Weighs the Relics

The search had ended. The judgment had not yet begun.

In October 1859, the Admiralty’s boardroom at Whitehall received two deliveries on the same morning. The first was a copper cylinder no larger than a candle-holder, wrapped in waxed canvas and bearing the corrosion of twelve Arctic winters. Inside lay a sheet of paper photographed at the Royal Geographical Society the previous week, its edges crumbling, its text the last certain words from Sir John Franklin’s expedition. The second delivery was a memorandum from Sir Richard Saunders Dundas, Secretary of the Admiralty, proposing the composition of the official dispatch that would announce the expedition’s fate to Parliament. The physical evidence and its official interpretation would travel parallel paths through the autumn, converging in February 1860 upon a verdict that would close the active search without exhausting the mystery.

Francis Leopold McClintock had returned to England in September to public acclamation and a knighthood. The parliamentary grant of £5, 000, shared among the twenty-six officers and men of the Fox, recognized a discovery that the Admiralty had once declared impossible. McClintock did not attend the subsequent examinations. His absence allowed the institutional machinery to process his findings without the complications of firsthand testimony. The veterans of Arctic service who gathered in Whitehall—Sir James Clark Ross foremost among them, who had commanded Erebus and Terror through Antarctic blizzards and led the first search expedition in 1848—would determine what McClintock’s discoveries meant for the historical record. Their authority derived from suffering rather than evidence. This was the tradition that would frame the official verdict.

The Victory Point note presented immediate problems of reading. The standard account, reproduced in McClintock’s subsequent narrative and thereafter in every history of the expedition, presents two distinct messages: the first dated 28 May 1847, announcing “All well” and the wintering at Beechey Island; the second dated 25 April 1848, recording the abandonment of the ships, the death of Franklin on 11 June 1847, and the planned march toward the Back River.

This clarity was achieved through editorial selection. The original paper, photographed before further deterioration, showed erasures, overwriting, and the palimpsest of two hands working under conditions the Admiralty’s clerks could barely imagine.

Fitzjames had written the first message in the confidence of completed wintering. Crozier had added the second in the desperation of imminent evacuation, squeezing words into margins, crossing out the optimistic latitude that ice drift had rendered false.

The boardroom’s task was to extract from this damaged document a narrative that would satisfy parliamentary accounting and public mourning without admitting uncertainties that might reopen the search.

The chronology itself was disputed. The date of Franklin’s death placed it within the second winter, after the expedition had left Beechey Island and while the ships were still beset in Victoria Strait. This was later than the Admiralty had assumed in early planning, when officers calculated survival rations for three years and expected to find living men. The delay meant that the 1848 search expeditions, launched with such urgency, had been searching for corpses before leaving England. The boardroom did not circulate this calculation. To acknowledge it would be to acknowledge that the 1848 searches, the 1850–1851 fleet, the millions of pounds expended, had been exercises in retrieval rather than rescue.

The second message’s description of the ships’ position was precise enough to seem authoritative, vague enough to be wrong. McClintock had found no trace of Erebus and Terror at the coordinates given. The Inuit testimony Rae gathered, and which McClintock partially confirmed, placed the final sinking far to the south, in waters the Victory Point note did not mention. The Admiralty’s geographers debated whether the ships had drifted, whether Crozier’s position was estimated or observed, whether the longitude reading contained a copying error. The dispatch would publish the coordinates as given, with a footnote about subsequent information that directed the curious to Rae’s reports without endorsing their conclusions. This was the institutional method: to include without incorporating, to admit without admitting.

Meanwhile, at Somerset House, clerks inventoried the boat’s contents that Lieutenant William Hobson had discovered on 30 May 1859. The vessel lay six miles from King William Island’s shore, dragged across ice that had since melted, loaded with objects telling a story of systematic desperation.

Two skeletons occupied the boat. One lay in the bow, surrounded by boots and clothing; the other sprawled across blankets near the stern. Both had been stripped of flesh.

The medicine chest held bottles with labels still legible: Tincture of Opium, Spirits of Wine, Pills Antiscorbutic. The silverware bore crests of officers who had dined in Greenwich three years before. A copy of The Vicar of Wakefield lay water-warped but intact.

These objects were photographed, catalogued, and deposited in the Admiralty’s collection. The skeletons went to the Royal College of Surgeons for anatomical examination.

The connection between the two, the possibility that the men had died attempting to carry these very objects across the ice, was not drawn in the official minutes.

The forensic evidence that would eventually transform understanding of the expedition was not sought. Lead poisoning from tinned provisions, skeletal trauma indicating violence, isotopic signatures of desperate diets: these investigations lay more than a century in the future. The Admiralty’s concern was with identification and inventory, naming the dead when possible, accounting for property, closing the file. The anatomists who received the skeletons noted age and stature, compared them against the expedition’s muster rolls, pronounced them consistent with persons of the seafaring class. The possibility that they had eaten their companions was not mentioned in their report. Rae’s testimony on this point, gathered from Inuit witnesses in 1854 and confirmed by McClintock’s own interviews, remained in the file of native reports that the Admiralty did not circulate.

Lady Jane Franklin followed these proceedings from her house in Spring Gardens, maintaining the correspondence network that had sustained the search through fourteen years of official indifference. She had paid for McClintock’s expedition. She had specified the search area, supplied the Fox, directed investigation toward King William Island when Admiralty attention wandered. Now she awaited the official verdict that would determine whether her husband’s reputation would be secured or sacrificed to institutional convenience. Her letters to the Admiralty’s secretaries, preserved in the file, pressed for specific acknowledgments: that Franklin had discovered the Northwest Passage, that he had died in command of a successful expedition, that failure of survival was not failure of leadership. The boardroom’s dispatch would grant the first two. The third would be left to inference.

The drafting of the official announcement occupied November and December 1859. Dundas supervised the composition personally, revising successive drafts to achieve a tone of dignified regret that would satisfy the widow without encouraging further expenditure. The key passage declared that Sir John Franklin had completed the discovery of the Northwest Passage by reaching a point from which the known waters of America were accessible.

This was geographical casuistry. The Victory Point note recorded no such achievement. The ships had been abandoned thirty miles from the nearest open water, and the survivors had died attempting to reach a river that would not have carried them to safety.

But the claim served multiple purposes: it honored the dead, justified the search, and closed the account without admitting to the strategic irrelevance of the entire enterprise. On 26 October 1852, Franklin had been promoted to rear-admiral posthumously. The dispatch would treat this as recognition of accomplishment rather than consolation for failure.

The parliamentary return was presented in February 1860. Its publication coincided with the release of McClintock’s narrative, The Voyage of the ‘Fox’ in the Arctic Seas, which Lady Franklin had arranged to issue through a commercial publisher. The two documents offered complementary versions of the same events. The Admiralty dispatch emphasized discovery and duty. McClintock’s book, written under Lady Franklin’s editorial supervision, emphasized sacrifice and endurance. Neither addressed questions that the evidence had made unavoidable: why the expedition’s planning had assumed ice conditions that did not obtain, why rescue searches had been delayed until survival was impossible, why Inuit testimony that could have directed earlier efforts had been dismissed as unreliable. The official verdict had been constructed to make these questions unaskable.

The financial accounting accompanied the narrative closure. The parliamentary grant to McClintock’s expedition was contrasted in the press with the hundreds of thousands expended on official searches that had found nothing. Lady Franklin’s private expenditure, estimated at over £30, 000 across fourteen years, was not mentioned in the official papers. The Admiralty’s total outlay, which approached £675, 000 when naval vessels, salaries, and provisions were included, was buried in estimates of previous years. The comparison served to demonstrate the efficiency of private initiative over public bureaucracy, a conclusion that suited the political climate of the late 1850s without threatening Admiralty control of future policy. The Arctic would remain a naval preserve. The method of its exploration would not be reexamined.

The relics examined with such care in the autumn of 1859 were distributed to institutional homes that would preserve them without interpreting them. The Victory Point note went to the Royal Geographical Society, where it remains, the paper now too fragile for routine display. The boat’s contents were divided: silverware to the National Maritime Museum, medical supplies to the Royal Naval Hospital, the skeletons to a teaching collection that would eventually disperse them. The photographs taken in 1859, intended to preserve evidence against deterioration, became the primary objects of study for subsequent generations. The originals were consulted less frequently. The copies accumulated interpretive weight that the objects themselves had not possessed.

The Admiralty’s verdict achieved its intended effect. No further official searches were authorized. Occasional private expeditions, American, Scandinavian, British, would visit the Arctic in subsequent decades, but without the systematic commitment Lady Franklin had enforced. The mystery of Erebus and Terror’s final location, of the precise circumstances of their sinking, of the fate of survivors who had not been found, was left to accumulate the patina of speculation. The official narrative, with its confident assertion of completed discovery and dignified death, became the foundation of all subsequent history. Its gaps and silences were not noticed until new evidence, gathered by techniques unimagined in 1859, forced their recognition.

The institutional funnel had produced its verdict. The lag between Arctic event and London acknowledgment, which had measured years in the cases of Rae’s testimony and McClintock’s discoveries, was formally closed. The Admiralty’s machinery had transformed raw evidence into an authorized account that satisfied the requirements of state, public, and widow. That this account was necessarily incomplete, that it suppressed information that would have complicated its conclusions, that it established a pattern of selective reading that would persist for generations: these were not failures of the process but its defining characteristics. The judgment had been rendered. The evidence that might have overturned it was already being stored in archives where no one thought to look, awaiting the questions that no one thought to ask.

The Admiralty’s deliberations over the boat’s contents revealed a deeper institutional anxiety about what constituted acceptable knowledge.

Lieutenant Hobson’s discovery had included items that spoke to a collapse of military order: the boots arranged with evident care beside one skeleton, the blankets drawn up as if in sleep, the silver plate mixed indiscriminately with medical supplies. These were not the remains of a disciplined retreat but of something more desperate and more intimate.

The clerks at Somerset House, trained in naval inventory rather than forensic interpretation, recorded what they could quantify and passed over what they could not. The medicine chest’s contents were listed by bottle and label; the fact that opium and spirits of wine would have been precious commodities for men in extremis was noted without comment.

The presence of a novel among the survival gear—Goldsmith’s sentimental tale of a clergyman’s family persevering through misfortune—was catalogued as “book, one, water-damaged” without consideration of what comfort or delusion it might have offered.

Sir James Clark Ross’s presence in the boardroom carried particular weight. He had commanded the same vessels that now lay somewhere beneath the Arctic waters, had known Franklin as a colleague and rival, had himself returned from the 1848–1849 search with his health broken and his reputation for invincibility diminished. His testimony regarding the Victory Point note focused on what he would have done in Crozier’s position, a counterfactual exercise that allowed the board to avoid confronting what Crozier had actually done.

Ross emphasized the impossibility of wintering in the location where the ships were abandoned, the certainty that any competent commander would have attempted the southern march, the likelihood that the coordinates contained errors of transcription rather than observation. His authority as a survivor of similar circumstances made his interpretations difficult to challenge, even when they contradicted the physical evidence. The board preferred his experienced judgment to the uncertainties of the document itself.

The tension between Rae’s 1854 testimony and McClintock’s 1859 findings created a procedural difficulty that the Admiralty resolved through chronological stratification. Rae’s report, with its Inuit accounts of cannibalism and its location of the final sinking far from Victory Point, was acknowledged as prior information without being integrated into the official narrative. The dispatch would mention that “subsequent intelligence” had suggested other locations for the ships’ loss, directing readers to Rae’s published account without adopting its conclusions. This technique allowed the Admiralty to appear comprehensive while remaining selective. The cannibalism testimony, which Rae had reported and McClintock had partially confirmed through his own Inuit interviews, was excluded entirely from the official record. The dispatch’s silence on this point was not accidental oversight but deliberate construction. To admit that British naval officers had consumed their companions would have compromised the dignified sacrifice that the narrative required.

The photographic documentation of the relics represented a novel approach to evidence preservation that carried its own distortions.

The Royal Geographical Society’s photographer, working with equipment designed for architectural surveys and expedition panoramas, arranged the Victory Point note on a black cloth under natural light from a north window. The exposure time required several minutes, during which the paper had to remain absolutely still. The resulting image captured the text with clarity that the original no longer permitted, but it also imposed a false stability.

The photograph showed the note as a flat, complete document; the original was already curling at the edges, the ink spreading where moisture had penetrated, the overwritten passages creating visual confusion that the camera’s limited depth of field could not convey. The boardroom’s preference for the photograph over the object established a pattern of mediated evidence that would persist.

Future researchers would study the image rather than the artifact, interpreting the copy without confronting the physical deterioration that made the original increasingly inaccessible.

The anatomical examination of the skeletons at the Royal College of Surgeons proceeded according to protocols developed for identifying drowned sailors and executed criminals. The examiners measured cranial capacity, noted dental wear, compared femur length against the expedition’s recruitment records. They produced identifications that were probable rather than certain: one skeleton possibly belonging to Lieutenant Henry Thomas Dundas Le Vesconte, the other to an unidentified seaman. The possibility that these men had died in the boat, that they had been placed there by companions who continued the march, that they represented a deliberate sacrifice rather than accidental loss, was not within the scope of the examination. The surgeons reported what they could establish about identity and cause of death; they did not speculate about the circumstances of dying. Their silence on these points was incorporated into the official record as absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence.

Lady Franklin’s interventions during this period were characterized by strategic patience. She had learned through fourteen years of advocacy that direct confrontation with Admiralty officials produced resistance, while sustained pressure through multiple channels produced results. Her letters to Dundas and to the First Lord of the Admiralty, the Duke of Somerset, emphasized the honor due to her husband’s rank and service rather than the failures of planning and response that had doomed his expedition. She accepted the geographical casuistry that declared the Northwest Passage discovered because she understood that this claim would secure Franklin’s place in exploration history. She did not press for acknowledgment of the search’s delays, the dismissal of Inuit testimony, the inadequacy of the provisions and equipment, because she recognized that these admissions would require institutional self-criticism that the Admiralty would not perform. Her compromise preserved what could be preserved and abandoned what could not be saved.

The final drafting sessions in January 1860 brought together the various strands of evidence and interpretation into a document that would withstand parliamentary scrutiny and public memorialization. Dundas read successive versions to a small group of Arctic veterans, incorporating their suggestions for phrasing that would satisfy technical accuracy without admitting uncertainty.

In the spring of 1860, a clerk at the Royal Geographical Society filed the photograph of the Victory Point note in a portfolio marked “Franklin Papers,” noting the date of acquisition and the condition of the original.

The entry was routine. The portfolio was not consulted again for twenty years. When it was reopened, in 1881, the curator found that the paper had continued its slow decay despite the photographic preservation, the ink fading where Crozier’s hand had pressed hardest, the margins crumbling where Fitzjames had written his confident assurance that all was well. The photograph had become the document. The original had become a relic of a relic, its physical presence too fragile to bear the weight of interpretation that had been placed upon it. This transformation, unremarked at the time, established the pattern that would govern all subsequent engagement with the Franklin mystery: the copy displacing the original, the authorized version substituting for the inaccessible fact, the official narrative hardening into history while the evidence that might complicate it gathered dust in the archives of institutions that had moved on to other concerns.

The Admiralty’s official verdict was published, drawing a line under the active search but leaving unresolved questions that would fuel the mystery for generations.
