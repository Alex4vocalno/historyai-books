Chapter 5

Diesel Anomaly

────────────────────────────────────────

The generator’s deep thrum vibrated up through the soles of Ingrid’s boots as she stepped into the oily warmth of the machine shop, carrying with it the conclusion she’d reached alone in the archive an hour ago: the key to the edit was a station key. The truth wasn’t just outside in the storm — it was inside, at the controls. Someone had sat at the recording deck, lifted the cap from the erase head, and pressed a button. Someone with access. Someone with hands that didn’t shake.

The machine shop occupied the station’s east wing, a long room of bolted steel panels and cable trays that smelled of lubricant and cold metal. Pipes ran along the ceiling, sweating condensation in the heat. The diesel generator, a marine-grade V8, sat center floor on vibration mounts, its housing painted the same institutional gray as everything else at this latitude. Finn Agaard stood beside it with a spanner in one hand and a rag in the other, checking the fuel pressure gauge. He wore his usual canvas work jacket, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms smeared with black.

“Finn.”

“Finn.”

He didn’t look up. “Ingrid.”

“I need the diesel generator log for the week of June ninth.”

The spanner paused. He set it down on the tool board, wiped his hands slowly, and turned. His expression was the one she’d come to recognize over the past weeks: careful, neutral, the face of a man who believed that data should speak for itself and that people usually spoke too much.

“Which night?”

“June eleventh. The night shift.”

He pulled a clipboard from a hook on the wall. The logbook was bound in green vinyl, its pages ruled with columns for time, generator status, fuel level, and operator initials. He flipped to the relevant week and held it out.

“Standard night watch. Nothing unusual.”

She took it but didn’t open it yet. “I want to understand the station’s power systems. How the generator cycles, what the load looks like overnight. Solveig Solheim mentioned once that the diesel sometimes runs unevenly in cold weather, and I want to check the baseline readings against my hydrophone data. Noise floor, interference patterns.”

A plausible cover. Acoustic engineers routinely consulted generator logs to rule out mechanical interference in sensitive recordings. Finn would know that. He’d also know that asking for a specific night’s log, the night Solveig Solheim died, went beyond routine curiosity.

Finn studied her. “You’re looking for something specific.”

“I’m looking at the data. That’s my job.”

He gestured to the workbench against the far wall, a scarred steel slab bolted to the concrete floor. “Sit. I’ll walk you through it.”

She sat. The metal was cold even through her trousers. Finn pulled a second stool and positioned himself beside her, close enough to point at the page but far enough to maintain his careful distance. He opened the log to June eleventh and set it flat between them.

“Right. Twenty-hundred, shift starts. Operator on duty: Finn Agaard. You see the entry there? ‘20: 00, generator nominal, fuel 78%, FA.’ That’s standard. Every shift begins with a full status check. Pressure, temperature, fuel, output frequency. I write it down, initial it, move on.”

His finger moved down the column. “Twenty-two hundred. ‘Generator nominal, fuel 74%, FA.’ Routine. Two-hour intervals, that’s the protocol. The fuel drops four percent every two hours under standard winter load, which is consistent with the heating demand and the instrument load. Nothing deviates.”

Ingrid looked at the entries. The handwriting was compact, utilitarian. The letters leaned slightly left, the numbers small and precise. Finn’s hand. She’d seen his notation on equipment tags throughout the station, always the same: tight, efficient, no wasted strokes.

“Zero hundred,” Finn continued. “’Generator nominal, fuel 70%, FA.’ Then zero-two hundred, zero-four hundred. Same pattern. You see? Four percent drop, two-hour cycle. The generator ran clean all night.”

“And these are all your entries?”

“Every one. I had the full watch that night. Erik was supposed to split, but he said he was covering a different task. I didn’t ask.”

Ingrid let that pass. She looked at the column again, following the timestamps down the page. Twenty-hundred, twenty-two hundred, zero-hundred, zero-two, zero-four, zero-six, zero-eight. Seven entries, each at two-hour intervals. Each in the same hand.

Except one.

Her finger, cold from the metal tabletop, came to rest on the line between the zero-hundred and zero-two-hundred entries. A separate notation sat there, slotted between the standard two-hour checks. It read: “01: 00, generator check, nominal, EL.”

The ink was a different shade. Not by much. The other entries were written in the black of a standard issue ballpoint, the kind stocked in the station supply cabinet. This entry was bluer, the kind you’d get from a personal pen, something brought from the mainland. The pen pressure was different too. Finn’s strokes were quick and light, the marks of a man who wrote the same words dozens of times a week. This entry was pressed harder into the page, the letters slightly wider, the tail of the ‘L’ in ‘EL’ hooked at the bottom where Finn’s ‘A’ had no such flourish.

“Finn.” She kept her voice level. “This entry here. Zero-one hundred. ‘Generator check, nominal, EL.’ Who is EL?”

Finn leaned forward. His eyes tracked the line she indicated. He was quiet for a moment, reading it the way he read gauge dials, with the precise attention of a man who trusted instruments more than people.

“Erik Larsen,” he said. “Those are his initials.”

“You said he wasn’t on the generator watch that night.”

“He wasn’t. Not the official watch. But the protocol allows for supplementary checks. If someone is awake, if they pass through the machine shop, they can log a status reading. It’s encouraged. Redundancy.”

“So Erik came through at zero-one hundred and logged a check.”

“That’s what the entry says.”

Ingrid looked at the two entries side by side. Finn’s zero-hundred check and Finn’s zero-two-hundred check bracketed Erik’s zero-one hundred entry like bookends. Same column, same page, same protocol. But different ink, different pressure, different hand.

“Finn, look at the handwriting on this entry. The ‘nominal’ here. Compare it to your ‘nominal’ at zero-hundred.”

Finn looked. His hand, which had been resting on the edge of the logbook, went still. He didn’t speak. He didn’t look up at her. He just looked at the two words, side by side, the same word written by two different people in two different pens within an hour of each other.

Ingrid’s finger moved to the timestamp. “Zero-one hundred. June eleventh.”

She already knew what the tape counter log said. She’d memorized it in the archive. The hydrophone tape for that night showed continuous recording from twenty-hundred through zero-eight-hundred, with one exception. At zero-one hundred, the recording head signal dropped to silence. At zero-one-eleven, it resumed. Eleven minutes. Clean cut, not degradation. An edit.

The generator check was logged at zero-one hundred. The tape gap began at zero-one hundred.

“Finn,” she said. “I need to check something in the archive. Can I borrow this?”

He didn’t answer immediately. He was still looking at the entry. His thumb rested on the edge of the page, pressing a small crease into the paper.

“This could be a record-keeping error,” he said. “People use different pens. People write differently when they’re tired.”

“At zero-one hundred, the exact time someone logged a generator check that wasn’t part of the standard watch, the hydrophone tape goes silent for eleven minutes. That’s not a pen error.”

Finn looked up. The fluorescent light above the workbench caught the sheen of oil on his forehead. He closed the logbook and held it against his chest.

“You’re saying someone logged the generator running during the exact minutes the tape went blank.”

“I’m saying the timestamps match. Exactly. Not approximately. Exactly.”

He was quiet. The generator hummed behind them, steady and oblivious, the same machine that had been running that night, its output recorded by a man whose handwriting didn’t match the entry in question.

“Be careful who you ask about this,” Finn said. His voice had dropped, not to a whisper, but to the register of someone who had suddenly become aware of the walls and the pipes and the distance between the machine shop and the rest of the station. “Erik is particular about his logs. He considers the maintenance records his domain. The station runs on trust, Ingrid. And procedure. He’ll say that. He’ll say you’re overstepping.”

“I need the duty roster for that week. Not the summary. The actual log.”

“I’ll get you the roster.” He set the logbook on the workbench between them, then pulled his rag from his back pocket and wiped his hands again, slowly, as though the oil had become something he needed to remove. “But Ingrid. The generator was running. That’s what matters. Whoever wrote that entry, the generator was on. If the tape was blank and the generator was running, someone needed power for something during those eleven minutes. Something they didn’t want recorded.”

He picked up his spanner and turned back to the pressure gauge. The conversation was over. Ingrid took the logbook and left the machine shop, the door closing behind her with a heavy mechanical clunk that echoed down the corridor.

The archive was a converted storage room off the main hall, windowless and cold, lit by a single desk lamp that cast a yellow circle on the scarred wooden table. Ingrid sat down and opened both ledgers side by side. The diesel generator log lay open to June eleventh. Beside it, she placed the tape counter log she’d been maintaining in parallel, a notebook where she’d transcribed the hydrophone deck’s recording timestamps from the original machine logs.

Dust motes drifted in the lamplight. The pages rustled dryly as she aligned them. The diesel log showed zero-one hundred: generator check, nominal, EL. Blue ink, heavy pressure, hooked tail on the L.

The tape counter log showed zero-one hundred: recording head signal lost. Zero-one-eleven: signal resumed.

She turned back to the diesel log. The entry was there, physical, undeniable. A person had stood at the generator, or at least at the logbook, and written that the machine was running normally at zero-one hundred on June eleventh. A person using a pen that wasn’t station issue. A person whose handwriting pressed harder into the page, whose letters leaned differently, whose hand was not the hand of the technician who’d been on watch all night.

And at that exact minute, the hydrophone tape went silent.

Ingrid sat back. The two ledgers lay open before her, their aligned timestamps visible in the steady lamplight. Zero-one hundred in the diesel log, zero-one hundred in the tape counter log. The forged entry and the erased tape. The running generator and the silent hydrophone. A coordinated, physical act, performed by someone inside the station, someone who had needed both the power on and the record clean.

Erik Larsen’s initials were scrawled on the log entry. But the handwriting was all wrong—it was a clumsy imitation, the pressure and slant inconsistent with the real handwriting she’d seen in supply requisitions and weather notes. Someone had forged Erik’s initials. Someone who wanted it to look as though Erik had been in the machine shop at zero-one hundred.

Or, perhaps more chilling, Erik had written them himself in a disguised hand, to create a record of his presence at a time when he needed to be somewhere else. A record that pointed to the machine shop while he was, in fact, at the recording deck.

The question was no longer just who had erased the eleven minutes. The question was why someone had needed the generator running during that silence. What had been happening, in the dark, on the shore, while the tape spun blank and the diesel burned and the station slept around a lie.

Ingrid closed both ledgers and sat in the cold archive, listening to the hum of the generator through the wall, the same sound Solveig would have heard on the night she died, and the same sound that had been running, steady and nominal, while her sister drowned.

She didn’t hear Finn come in. He was just there, standing in the archive doorway with a mug of coffee he clearly wasn’t drinking, his face set in that expression she’d learned to read as fear compressed into stillness.

“You found something.”

She told him. All of it — the two logbooks, the mismatched handwriting, the generator running through eleven minutes of silence that should have been recorded. She told him the way she’d told no one else, plainly, without dramatizing, because Finn hated decoration almost as much as he hated lies.

He listened without moving. When she finished, he set the mug on the shelf beside the door and came to the table. He didn’t touch the ledgers. He looked at them the way you’d look at a weapon someone had left on a bench.

“Who else knows you have these?”

“No one.”

“Keep it that way.” He pulled out the chair across from her and sat, leaning forward with his forearms on his knees. “I mean it, Ingrid. Don’t ask Erik about the handwriting. Don’t ask anyone about the generator log. Not yet.”

“Why?”

“Because whoever wrote that entry wanted it found eventually — or wanted it to sit there, quiet, doing its job. Either way, they had a reason. And people who build records that point away from themselves tend to notice when someone starts pulling at the threads.” He glanced at the door, then back at her. “This station has six people on it right now. Six. And one of them is either protecting himself or protecting someone else. You won’t know which until you understand what happened during those eleven minutes. And you won’t understand that by asking questions.”

She studied his face. “You know something.”

“I know Erik. And I know that if he falsified a log, he had a reason he considered worth the risk.” Finn stood and picked up his mug again. “Be careful who you trust with this. Be careful even with me.”
