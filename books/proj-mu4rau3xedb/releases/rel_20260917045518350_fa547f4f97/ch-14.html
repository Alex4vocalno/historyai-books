Chapter 14

Ledger of Deceit

────────────────────────────────────────

Finn’s confession still burned behind Ingrid’s eyes when she pushed open the radio room door. The words he’d stammered through tears, the way his hand had pointed at Erik Larsen’s name on the ledger, the fund transfers disguised as equipment requisitions. She had a confession, yes. She had the shape of the theft. What she did not have was the truth of Solveig. The killer might still be out there, hidden behind a more sordid crime, and the ledger page in her hand was the only thread connecting the two.

Erik sat with his back to her, hunched over the frequency log, his pressed grey vest pulling tight across his shoulders. The shortwave crackled with a faint signal from some distant trawler reporting its position. Banks of equipment lining the walls cast a greenish glow across his windburned cheeks. His left hand rested beside a ceramic mug, fingers wrapped around a pencil stub. He was annotating the log in his careful, blocky print, the handwriting of a man who believed that neatness was a form of moral order.

She stepped inside and closed the door behind her. The latch clicked, and the sound was swallowed by the hum of transmitters and the faint static bleed from the speakers mounted above the console.

“You didn’t knock,” Erik said without turning. His voice carried the practiced patience of a man accustomed to interruptions, the station chief distributing his attention between bureaucracy and the sea.

“No.” She crossed the narrow room in four steps. The space between them shrank until she could smell the cold coffee on his desk and the wool of his vest, damp with the condensation that clung to everything in this station. “I’m not here for a social call.”

Erik set down the pencil. He turned slowly in the swivel chair, and his eyes found the ledger page she held extended between them. She watched his gaze travel from her fingers to the columns of figures, and the muscles around his jaw tightened. His nostrils flared once. He recognized it. Of course he recognized it. The page was from his own binder, kept in the supply closet three doors down the corridor, and she had walked out of that closet with it an hour ago.

“Where did you get that?” His voice had dropped half an octave. The paternal warmth drained from it, replaced by something cautious, measured.

“Finn gave me the numbers. The page is yours, though. Your handwriting on the requisition line. Your signature on the disbursement authorizations.” She held it closer, letting the green glow from the transmitter rack illuminate the columns. “Forty-three thousand kroner over eight months, Erik. Equipment that was never ordered. Repairs that were never made. The supply closet has the same number of cable spools it had in January. So where did the money go?”

His tongue pressed against the inside of his cheek. She could see the calculation behind his eyes, the rapid sorting of responses, the weighing of what she knew against what she might only suspect. His left hand lifted from the desk and adjusted the collar of his shirt beneath the grey vest. A habitual gesture, the kind of self-correction that buys seconds.

“Ingrid, you should not be going through supply records. That is station administration. It falls under my authority, not the engineering division.”

“I’m not here about station administration.” She lowered the page to the desk, placing it flat on top of the frequency log, directly between them. “I’m here about Solveig.”

The name hung between them. She watched for the flinch, the micro-expression, the involuntary tightening of the fingers or the throat. What she got was worse. Erik’s face went still. Not the composure of an innocent man, and not the panic of a guilty one. It was the blankness of someone who had rehearsed for this moment and was now discovering that the rehearsal had not prepared him.

“Solveig drowned. It was a tragedy. I reported it through every proper channel, and the coast guard closed the file.”

“The coast guard closed the file based on your report. Your timeline. Your account of the weather conditions and the tide.” Ingrid leaned forward, pressing her palms flat on the desk. “But you lied about the timeline, didn’t you? You lied because you were in the supply closet at the time you claimed to be on the observation deck. You lied because you couldn’t account for your movements without admitting where you’d been and what you’d been doing there.”

“That is a serious accusation.”

“It’s a question.” She straightened. “Did you kill my sister, Erik?”

His right hand found the ceramic mug. The fingers wrapped around it, tightened, released. A prop. Something to hold. “I did not kill Solveig. I would never have harmed her. She was a colleague and a friend, and I reported her death with the full weight of my responsibility to this station.”

“Responsibility.” She tasted the word and found it hollow. “You want to talk about responsibility? Let’s talk about forty-three thousand kroner. Let’s talk about the diesel fuel invoices you inflated for six consecutive months. Because Finn told me everything. He sat in that chair where you’re sitting now, and he cried while he explained how you had him process the duplicate requisitions. How you told him it was a budgeting workaround. How he found out it wasn’t.”

Erik’s face had gone the color of the grey vest. His lips pressed together until they formed a thin line, and the windburn on his cheeks stood out like two dark stains. He did not speak for a long moment. The shortwave crackled, and a voice in some distant language called out numbers into the static.

Then he said, “Finn has no idea what he’s talking about.”

“Finn has the duplicate invoices. He has the ledger entries in your handwriting. He has the bank transfer receipts.” She tapped the page between them. “And I have this. So I’m going to ask you one more time. Where did the money go?”

Silence. The transmitter hummed. Somewhere beneath the floor, the generator throbbed in its slow, mechanical heartbeat.

“I owe money,” Erik said. The words came out low, directed at the desk rather than at her. “From before. Debts I accumulated in Tromsø before I took this post. Gambling. Bad decisions. A man’s foolish pride.” His hand released the mug and opened, palm up, a gesture of surrender that looked practiced. “I borrowed from the station accounts. I intended to repay every krone. The transfer was going to happen in the next quarter, once my pension disbursement cleared.”

“You stole from the station.”

“I borrowed.” The correction came sharp, reflexive, the bureaucratic shield reasserting itself. Then his shoulders dropped, and the shield fell away. “Yes. I took the money. I took it because I was drowning in debt and I told myself I would return it before anyone noticed. That is the truth.”

Ingrid stared at him. She had built this moment in her mind across weeks of sleepless, methodical work, constructing the architecture of accusation. She had imagined the confrontation, the denial, the crack in his composure when she presented the evidence. What she had not imagined was the speed of the surrender. He gave up the theft too easily, like a man tossing cargo overboard to lighten a sinking ship.

“The theft explains the ledger,” she said. “It doesn’t explain the tape.”

Erik’s fingers went rigid around the mug. “What tape?”

“The hydrophone tape. The recording from the night Solveig died.” She watched his knuckles whiten. “The spectrogram shows a clean cut, not a degradation. That’s an edit. Eleven minutes removed with a razor and a splice block. Someone sat at the recording deck and cut those minutes out with precision. Someone who knew what was on the tape and what it would reveal.”

“I don’t know what you’re talking about.”

“You don’t know about the edit?”

“I don’t know what was on that tape. The hydrophone array is your division. The data is yours. Stay in your lane.”

“Someone edited that tape within hours of my sister’s death. The duty roster says you were the only person in the station with access to the recording deck between midnight and six a. m. The generator log shows power draw consistent with the deck equipment running at full capacity at two-fifteen in the morning. You were there, Erik.”

His jaw worked. The muscles beneath his beard bunched and released. He pulled the mug closer to his body, cradling it against his chest.

“I want the duty roster for that week,” Ingrid said. “Not your version. The actual log. The original, before you amended it.”

“It was amended for accuracy. The original had errors.”

“The original had the truth.”

Erik set the mug down. He placed it with deliberate care on a clear patch of desk surface, away from the frequency log, away from the ledger page, away from her hands. His fingers trembled as he withdrew them. Not the fine tremor of cold or age. This was something deeper, something that started in his wrists and traveled up his forearms.

“Someone left a note in my pocket,” he said.

Ingrid went still.

“It was folded. Tucked into the breast pocket of my coat, which hangs in the equipment room. I found it on the morning after.” His voice had flattened, the bureaucratic cadence gone, replaced by something careful and too even. “Typewritten. No signature. It said to edit the tape. It specified the time window. Eleven minutes, beginning at the timestamp marking twenty-three forty. It said if I did not comply, the ledger entries would be sent to the institute director in Bergen.”

“You’re telling me someone blackmailed you into editing the tape.”

“I’m telling you I had no choice.” His voice cracked on the last word, the careful evenness finally giving way. “The theft was real. The evidence was real. If that note had been sent, I would have lost my position, my pension, everything I have built in twenty-three years of service. I have a daughter in Tromsø. I have alimony payments. I cannot afford to lose this post.”

“So you cut the tape.”

“I cut the tape.” He said it to the frequency log, to the green light, to the static. Not to her. “I sat at the recording deck at two in the morning and I cut eleven minutes from my colleague’s hydrophone recording because a stranger told me to. Because I was afraid.”

Ingrid’s hands had gone cold. The certainty that had carried her into this room, the clean, burning righteousness of a sister with evidence and a target, was draining out of her. She had come here to force a confession to murder. She was getting a confession to cowardice, and the shape of the crime was changing under her hands.

“What was on the tape?” she asked. “In those eleven minutes. What were you told to remove?”

“I didn’t listen.” Erik’s eyes squeezed shut. “The note said not to listen. It said to remove the segment and destroy it. So I did.”

“You destroyed eleven minutes of evidence and you didn’t even listen to it?”

“If I had listened, I would know. If I had known, I would be complicit in something worse than theft. Ignorance was the only protection I had.” He opened his eyes, and Ingrid saw something in them that stopped her breath. Not guilt. Not shame. Something that looked out from behind his pupils with the flat, fixed attention of a man who has been watched by something he cannot identify.

“They knew about the money before Solveig died,” he said. “Whoever sent that note. They knew about the theft, they knew about the ledger, they knew I would comply. They had been watching me. They had been watching all of us.”

“They.”

Erik’s hand jerked. The motion was sudden, involuntary, and his fingers struck the ceramic mug. It tipped, rolled, and dark coffee spilled across the frequency log in a slow, spreading stain. The liquid crept over the inked columns, bleeding through the paper, warping the entries into illegible smudges. Neither of them moved to stop it. Erik stared at the spreading stain as though it were something he recognized, something he had been expecting.

“I don’t know who,” he said. “I don’t know how many. I don’t know what they wanted hidden on that tape. But whoever sent that note knew things about this station that no outsider should know. They knew the duty rotation. They knew where I kept the ledger. They knew the timestamp on the tape.” His voice dropped until it was barely audible beneath the transmitter hum. “I have not felt safe in this building since that morning. I lock my door at night. I check the locks. And I hear sounds in the corridor, footsteps that stop outside my room, and when I open the door there is no one there.”

The coffee had reached the edge of the desk and was dripping onto the floor in slow, rhythmic taps. The stain on the frequency log had spread until it covered the entries for the entire week of Solveig’s death. Erik sat in his grey vest with his ruined earlobe and his windburned cheeks and his hands that would not stop trembling, and Ingrid understood that she was not looking at the man who had killed her sister.

She was looking at a man who had been used.

The certainty she had carried into this room lay in pieces around her, and what remained was colder. Larger. She had accused him of murder, and he had handed her a confession to theft and coercion. The misdirection was not in his denial. It was in the architecture itself. Someone had built a structure of guilt around Erik, had known about the debts and the ledger, had waited until the night Solveig died to activate the trap. The edit was not Erik’s crime. It was someone else’s cover, and Erik was the instrument, disposable and terrified.

She picked up the ledger page from the desk. The coffee had not reached it. She folded it once, twice, and slid it into the back pocket of her trousers.

“I need that note,” she said. “The typewritten one. Do you still have it?”

Erik shook his head. “I burned it. The morning I received it. I burned it in the incinerator in the generator room because I was afraid of what it meant to have it.”

“You burned it.”

“I burned it because they told me to.” His voice was barely a whisper. “The note said to destroy the tape segment and the note. I did what I was told.”

Ingrid stepped back from the desk. The radio room felt smaller than it had when she entered, the walls closer, the green glow harsher. She looked at Erik Larsen, the man who had welcomed her to the station with a hand on her shoulder and condolences on his lips, and she saw him clearly for the first time. Not as her sister’s killer. Not as the bureaucrat who had lied about the timeline. As a frightened man who had been cornered by someone with more knowledge, more patience, and more ruthlessness than either of them possessed.

She left the radio room without another word. The corridor was cold, the metal walls sweating with condensation, the air carrying the taste of salt and the faint, metallic tang of the heating system. She leaned against the wall and pressed her palms flat against the steel, letting the cold travel up through her wrists and forearms until it reached her chest.

Her theory was broken. Not wrong, but incomplete. She had been so certain of the chain, the simple, legible chain from theft to blackmail to murder, that she had missed the hand that held the chain. Someone had known about the theft before Solveig died. Someone had known what the tape contained before the edit was ordered. Someone had been positioned to exploit both, to weaponize Erik’s guilt into a cover-up that protected not Erik, but themselves.

The pipe above her head groaned. Wind pressed against the station walls, and somewhere in the corridors, footsteps moved and then stopped. Ingrid stood in the cold passage with a damp ledger page in her pocket and a new question taking shape behind her ribs.

If Erik didn’t kill Solveig, who gave the order? And what were those eleven minutes hiding that was worth killing for?
