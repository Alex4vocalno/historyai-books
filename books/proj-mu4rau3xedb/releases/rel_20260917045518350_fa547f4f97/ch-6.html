Chapter 6

Frozen Alibis

────────────────────────────────────────

The mess hall still smelled of the chemical lemon cleaner someone had used to wipe down the tables before dawn, a sharp, antiseptic scent that sat on top of the stale coffee and the older, deeper smell of diesel fuel that lived in every surface of the station. The overhead strip light buzzed at a frequency Ingrid had already catalogued: 100 hertz, the same harmonic as the generator’s idle cycle, a sound that would become invisible within forty-eight hours as her auditory threshold adapted. Four mugs sat on the draining rack by the sink, all rinsed, all placed upside down in a neat row at identical intervals.

A fifth mug stood apart, still half-full of cold coffee, a film of oil shimmering on its surface. The duty roster was pinned to the corkboard with a single brass tack, its corners curling inward from the heat of the pipe that ran behind the wall. Someone had used a red ballpoint to circle a shift swap from two weeks ago, the ink pressed so hard it had embossed the sheet beneath.

A pen lay on the floor near the far chair, uncapped, as if dropped mid-sentence. The radiator beneath the window ticked and settled, expanding metal, the same rhythm as a clock but slower, arrhythmic, the tempo of something warming unevenly.

Erik’s hands were steady as he poured the coffee, but his eyes avoided the steam.

“Mind if I ask you a few things? Still getting oriented. The night Elin died — you were on generator duty?”

Ingrid kept her tone conversational, the notebook open on her knee, pen poised. She had learned, in the weeks since the spectrogram, to dress interrogation as orientation. People answered differently when they thought they were being helpful.

Erik set the pot back on the warmer. He didn’t look at the notebook. His gaze shifted to the steam rising from his cup, then back to her. “What’s this about, the logs again?”

“Standard shift. I ran the generator check at 2100, then the perimeter sweep at 2130. Came back to the mess hall, made coffee. That’s when the alarm went.”

Ingrid wrote the times in a column. Her handwriting was steady, her pulse was not. 2100.2130. Alarm. She didn’t look up.

“That’s a precise memory for a routine night.”

The corner of Erik’s mouth shifted, not a smile, something harder, something that wanted to be a smile but couldn’t commit. He leaned against the counter, arms folded, positioning himself between her and the door. She noticed it without looking directly at it. The geometry of the room had changed.

“You remember details when something goes wrong. The brain locks things in.” He paused. “This station runs on trust, Ingrid. And procedure.”

There it was. The same phrase he had used three days ago when she had asked about the diesel ledger. The same cadence, the same weight on the word procedure, as though it were a wall he could stand behind. Real memory stumbled. It reached for foggy edges, said around then or I think, qualified itself with uncertainty. Erik’s account had no qualifiers. A corridor with no doors.

She closed the notebook, smiled, thanked him. Left the mess hall with the taste of metal in her mouth.

The corridor was narrow enough that she could press both palms to the walls if she stretched. She didn’t. She walked to the common area where Markus sat at the workstation, his back to the door, headphones around his neck. The screen cast blue light across his jaw. The faint tang of ozone from the electronics mixed with the cold air blowing down from the vent above. Anya was at the far table, mending a thermal glove with a curved needle, her movements precise and unhurried. The scratch of her thread through synthetic fabric was the loudest sound in the room.

Ingrid paused near Markus’s chair. She let her gaze drift over his screen, columns of seismic data, timestamps scrolling, before she spoke.

“Markus — Erik mentioned you were in the lab that evening. Seismograph data?”

Markus pulled the headphones off one ear. He didn’t turn around immediately. When he did, his expression was already composed, already waiting.

“Data-entry. I was at the console from 2100 to 2230. Standard logging run.” He replaced the headphone on his ear, a gesture of dismissal, then added, softer: “We all miss her, Ingrid. It’s a shadow on the ice.”

2100 to 2230. The same start time Erik had cited. The same block, the same shape. Ingrid felt the pen in her hand go still. She had not told Markus what Erik had said. She had not mentioned 2100. Yet he had reached into the air and pulled out the exact hour, unprompted, and offered it as though it were nothing, as though memory worked that way, retrieving precise timestamps for unremarkable evenings six months cold.

“2100 to 2230. That’s specific.”

“You log your hours. It’s procedure.”

The word again. The same wall.

She turned, as if casually, toward Anya. The woman’s needle paused, held still in the fabric. She had been listening. Of course she had been listening. The room was too small for anything else.

“I heard him typing when I passed the lab. Around 2145, I think. The data from that period is anomalous. I have noted it in my log.”

Anya’s voice carried its usual composure, each word placed with the same care as her stitches. She did not look up from the glove. The needle resumed its arc.

Ingrid watched her hands. Steady. Too steady. The kind of steady that comes from having practiced the motion before, not the mending, the speaking. The sentence had a shape to it, a pre-formed quality, like a stone that had been tumbled smooth by repetition.

“You’re sure it was 2145?”

“The corridor clock. It’s reliable.”

Ingrid nodded. Smiled. Thanked them both. Walked down the corridor to the archive, her footsteps echoing off the metal floor grating with a sound like a slow, deliberate heartbeat.

The archive was a converted supply closet at the station’s eastern end, four paces by three, lit by a single desk lamp with a green glass shade. The air was cold and dry, carrying the smell of old paper, that particular vanilla scent of decaying lignin, and beneath it dust, and beneath that the mineral tang of the concrete walls. A metal shelving unit held binders going back three years, their spines labelled in black marker. The duty rosters were in the third binder from the left, second shelf. Ingrid pulled it down, set it on the desk, and opened it to the week of Elin’s death.

The pages were stiff with cold. Her breath fogged in the lamplight as she turned to the night in question. The entries were handwritten in at least three different scripts: Erik’s blocky capitals, Markus’s tight leftward slant, and a third hand she hadn’t yet identified, round and careful. The duty roster for that evening read:

2100 — Generator check: E.L.
2100 — Lab data-entry: M.D.
2130 — Perimeter sweep: E.L.
2140 — Lab door secured (corridor lock): logged by A.P.
2145 — Corridor patrol: A.P.

She read it twice. Then a third time, her finger tracing each line, the paper cold enough to sting.

The lab door was logged as secured at 2140. Anya’s handwriting. The corridor lock required a physical key, held by the person locking from outside. If Markus was inside doing data-entry from 2100 to 2230, as he claimed, as Erik confirmed, then someone had locked him in. Or someone had locked the door after he left. But Markus said he was inside the whole time. And Anya said she heard him typing at 2145, five minutes after she herself had logged the door as secured from the corridor.

A sealed door. A corridor lock. And typing heard through it.

Ingrid closed the binder. Opened it again. Read the lines once more, as though repetition might force the contradiction to resolve. It didn’t. It sat there, plain and impossible, a five-minute window where the story collapsed into something that could not be true.

She stood, the chair scraping concrete, and carried the binder back down the corridor to the mess hall. The duty roster was still pinned to the corkboard. She stood before it, the open binder in her arms, and began to trace the overlapping time slots with her finger. Markus in the lab from 2100. Anya locking the lab door at 2140. Anya hearing typing at 2145 through a sealed door she had locked herself.

The generator hummed beneath the floor. The radiator ticked. The brass tack holding the schedule to the board caught the light, a small, bright point. The pin seemed to hold the entire false world in place, a single piece of metal supporting the weight of everything she was beginning to understand.

She thought of the spectrogram. The clean cut. Eleven minutes of silence where sound should have been, spliced with the precision of someone who knew what they were doing. The forged log entry matching that gap, the generator running during those minutes for a reason she could not yet name. And now this: three people, three accounts, fitting together like fingers interlaced. Too neat. Too mutual. The kind of corroboration that doesn’t happen by accident but by design.

The horror wasn’t in a single lie. The architecture of them held the weight. Erik’s timeline supported Markus’s, Markus’s timestamps supported Anya’s, Anya’s observation supported Markus’s presence, and the physical log, the mute paper record, refused to support any of it. Five minutes. A locked door and a sound that couldn’t pass through it. Too small to confront. Too real to ignore.

She closed the binder. Her hands were steady. The cold in the archive had settled into her knuckles, her wrists, the small bones of her fingers. She stood alone in the mess hall, the duty roster above her, the station around her: five rooms, five people, and a silence eleven minutes long that someone had built on purpose.

She was not investigating a death anymore. She was standing inside a conspiracy, and the walls were listening.

She didn’t go back to the dormitory wing. Not yet. Not with the taste of the paper still on her fingers, that particular dust the archive left behind, like dry ink and old adhesive. She walked instead to the communications room, where the station’s terminal glowed its standby amber against the far wall.

Markus was there. Of course he was. Third shift, data-entry backlog. He’d said so himself at dinner two nights ago, and he’d said it again when she’d asked him casually in the corridor yesterday: I was at my terminal the whole time. You can check the log entries, I timestamped every form.

“Busy night?” she asked from the doorway.

He looked up. That same unhurried expression. “Always is, end of quarter.” He gestured at the screen. “Requisition logs, inventory reconciliations, personnel updates. The kind of thing nobody notices until it’s wrong.”

“Sounds tedious.”

“Sounds,” he agreed, and turned back to his work.

She watched him type for a moment. The keystrokes were measured, deliberate, the pace of a man who knew exactly how long each entry took and had factored that into his accounting. She’d seen Erik move with similar precision. Anya had spoken with the same careful cadence, each sentence load-bearing.

Three people who had rehearsed without rehearsing. Who had arrived at the same evening from three directions and produced accounts that interlocked like joinery, no gaps, no contradictions, no excess. The kind of testimony that survived scrutiny precisely because it was too clean.

She left Markus to his timestamps.

The archive drawer held the station log — the master record, the one maintained by the automated system, not by human hand. She pulled it and opened it to the date in question. The entries were sparse: environmental readings, door-cycle counts, system alerts. The kind of data that accumulated without narrative, without intent.

She found the door-cycle log for the relevant hour. The cargo bay door, the one Erik claimed he’d passed through at 22:47, showed a cycle at 22:46: 38. Fine. Close enough. The dormitory door, which Markus said he’d heard close from the corridor at 22:51, showed a cycle at 22:50: 12. Also fine.

But between those two entries, the system recorded a third cycle. The med bay door. 22:48: 03.

Anya had stated — twice, unprompted — that she’d been in the med bay from 22:30 until 22:55. That she’d heard nothing. That the door had remained closed and she had been alone inside it.

A door that stays closed doesn’t cycle.

Someone had opened the med bay door at 22:48, from the inside or the outside, and the system had recorded it. Anya hadn’t mentioned it. Markus hadn’t mentioned it. Erik’s account had no room for it; his trajectory put him in the dormitory corridor by then, moving away.

Three stories. Three alibis. One physical record that said a door opened where no one admitted standing.

Ingrid closed the log. Her fingers left faint marks on the page, the oils of her skin, the cold still caught in her joints. She stared at the closed cover and understood what she was looking at for the first time.

They hadn’t coordinated their stories to hide what happened during those eleven minutes. They had coordinated them to hide what happened during one minute of them. The rest was scaffolding, elaborate, mutual, load-bearing scaffolding built around a single door opening that none of them could explain away.

She returned the log to its drawer. She did not lock the archive behind her.
