Chapter 2

A False Account

────────────────────────────────────────

Erik Larsen’s smile doesn’t reach his eyes as he gestures Ingrid into the lamplight of his office. The door clicks shut behind her, and suddenly the space feels smaller than it did yesterday, the walls pressing inward with the weight of unstated things. A kerosene lantern throws amber shadows across the cluttered desk—navigation charts, maintenance schedules, a chipped mug ringed with coffee stains that have soaked into the grain of the wood.

“Please, sit.” Erik motions to the wooden chair opposite him, his other hand resting on the leather-bound logbook that sits at the center of his desk like an altar piece. His grey wool vest is buttoned precisely, the fabric pulling slightly across his barrel chest. The missing chunk of his left earlobe catches the light as he turns—a childhood frostbite injury, she knows, but in this moment it looks like something taken, something paid.

Ingrid sits. The chair creaks beneath her, and she’s aware of how the sound punctuates the silence between them. Through the frost-rimmed window behind Erik, the January dark presses against the glass, the Arctic winter swallowing what little light the station generates. The hydrophone array lies somewhere out there in the black water, its cables running like veins through the seafloor, listening to things no human ear was meant to hear.

“I wanted to speak with you about Solveig,” Erik says, and Ingrid notes how he uses her sister’s first name—not Dr. Solheim, not the researcher, but the woman. A deliberate choice, she thinks. Designed to make this feel personal rather than procedural. “I know you’ve been reviewing the acoustic data. That’s good. Professional. But I thought you might have questions about that night. About what happened.”

His voice is warm, paternal. The voice of a man who has delivered bad news before and learned to make it palatable. Ingrid watches his hands—steady, practiced, one thumb tracing the spine of the logbook in a slow, hypnotic rhythm.

“I do have questions,” she says, and keeps her tone neutral. She’s thought about this moment since she first saw the eleven-minute gap, rehearsed the approach like a technician calibrating an instrument. Too eager, and she tips her hand. Too passive, and she misses the seams in his story. “The official report says she drowned during a solo equipment check. I’d like to understand the sequence of events better.”

Erik nods, as if this request is entirely expected. Perhaps it is. Perhaps he’s been waiting for her to ask, preparing his answers the way a mechanic prepares an engine for inspection—wiping away the grease, tightening the visible bolts, hiding the deeper problems beneath the housing.

“Of course,” he says. “That night—the night of January fourteenth—it was routine. Ordinary, in the way things become ordinary here. You’ll learn that about station life. The days blur together. The night shifts especially.”

He leans back in his chair, and Ingrid can see him constructing the narrative, pulling details from memory or manufacturing them as needed. The dry heat of the radiator at her back makes her skin prickle. The office smells of old paper and stale coffee, with an undertone of something metallic—machine oil, perhaps, or the faint electrical scent of the equipment humming in the walls.

“Solveig had the overnight hydrophone watch,” Erik continues. “Standard procedure. We rotate the duty so no one person bears the burden too long. She’d been on rotation for six days, I believe. Finn was running diagnostics on the diesel generator, and Anya was processing samples in the lab. I was in my quarters, reviewing the week’s logistics.”

He pauses, and Ingrid recognizes the pause for what it is—a breath, a beat, a moment designed to lend weight to what comes next. She’s seen the same technique in the spectrograms she analyzes: silence as structure, the space between sounds as meaningful as the sounds themselves.

“Sometime around two in the morning, she went out to check the primary array. The connection had been degrading—we’d been getting interference, probably ice movement on the cables. She radioed in at oh-two-fifteen to confirm she was at the junction point. Standard check-in.”

“And after that?” Ingrid asks, though she already knows the official answer. The report sits in her quarters, its language clinical and final: Subject failed to return from scheduled maintenance. Body recovered at 06:47 approximately two hundred meters from station. Cause of death consistent with cold-water drowning.

“After that, we lost contact.” Erik’s face settles into an expression Ingrid can’t quite read—regret, perhaps, or the performance of regret. “When she missed the oh-three-hundred check-in, I went out with Finn to look for her. We found her equipment at the junction point. Her body was recovered at first light.”

The story flows smoothly, each piece fitting into the next like a well-maintained machine. But Ingrid has spent her career listening for the sounds that don’t belong—the clicks and pops and static that reveal what lies beneath the surface. And something in Erik’s account is making her ears prick, some frequency that doesn’t match the ambient noise.

She thinks of the diesel generator, thrumming through the station’s floorboards. She thinks of the maintenance log she glimpsed yesterday, its columns of timestamps and readings marching in neat rows. And she thinks of the entry she noticed, half-buried among the routine notations: 02:47 — Generator shutdown for scheduled maintenance. Duration: 47 minutes.

“The generator was running all night?” she asks, and watches Erik’s hand pause on the logbook. Just for a moment. Just a hitch in the rhythm.

“Of course,” he says. “We run it continuously through the winter months. The heating system requires constant power, and the research equipment—you know this better than anyone—needs stable electricity.”

“But Finn was performing maintenance?”

“A routine check. Visual inspection. He didn’t need to shut anything down.”

Ingrid nods slowly, as if absorbing this information. In her mind, a spectrogram is forming—lines of sound and silence, and in the gap between them, the shape of something wrong. If Finn performed only a visual inspection, why does the maintenance log record a forty-seven-minute shutdown beginning at 02:47? And if the generator was running continuously, as Erik claims, where did those forty-seven minutes of darkness go?

“The diesel log would show the fuel consumption for that night,” she says, and keeps her voice casual. “I’d like to cross-reference it with the acoustic data. Background noise from the generator can interfere with the hydrophone readings. It would help me calibrate my analysis.”

Erik’s smile doesn’t waver, but something shifts behind his eyes—a calculation, quick and cold. His knuckles whiten where his hand rests on the logbook, and she watches him tap a precise rhythm against the leather cover. Once. Twice. Three times.

“The diesel logs are part of the station’s operational records,” he says. “I can arrange for you to see them. But I’d ask that you go through proper channels. Submit a formal request. Procedure exists for a reason, Ingrid. This station runs on trust. And procedure.”

The words land like stones dropped into still water. Proper channels. Formal request. Procedure. She hears what lies beneath them: a gate, a checkpoint, a way to control what she sees and when she sees it.

“Of course,” she says, and means: I’ll find another way.

They sit in silence for a moment, and Ingrid can feel the weight of the station pressing down—the tons of ice and rock above them, the endless dark outside, the few souls huddled together in this manufactured warmth. Erik’s office, with its lamplight and its logbook and its carefully constructed narrative, is supposed to be a refuge. Instead, it feels like a room where the air is slowly being pumped out.

Then the door opens, and cold air rushes in.

Anya Petrova steps through, bringing with her the scent of machine oil and the sharp bite of the Arctic wind. Her parka is dusted with frost, her dark hair pulled back in a severe knot that accentuates the sharp angles of her face. She looks from Ingrid to Erik, and something passes across her features—surprise, perhaps, or the shadow of it.

“Am I interrupting?” Her voice is low, measured, each word placed with precision.

“Not at all,” Erik says, and Ingrid catches the slight edge of relief in his tone. “Ingrid was just asking about the night Solveig died. Perhaps you could add your perspective. You were in the lab that night, weren’t you?”

It’s not quite a question. It’s something closer to a prompt, a line fed to an actor who’s forgotten her cue. Ingrid watches Anya’s eyes dart to Erik—just for a second, a flicker of movement that might mean nothing. Might mean everything.

“I was,” Anya says. She moves to the corner of the office, her movements graceful, economical. She removes her parka and hangs it on a peg, revealing a grey sweater that hangs loose on her thin frame. “Processing water samples. The work requires focus. I wasn’t aware of… of what was happening until Erik raised the alarm.”

“And you didn’t notice anything unusual before that?” Ingrid asks. “Any sounds, any disturbances?”

Anya’s hands, still carrying the chill of the outside, fold neatly in front of her. Her face is a mask of professional calm, but Ingrid sees the way her fingers interlace—tight, tighter, tightest.

“The lab is soundproofed,” Anya says. “The equipment is sensitive. Vibrations from elsewhere in the station can corrupt the data. So I work in isolation. I heard nothing.”

“The generator was running, though,” Ingrid presses. “You would have felt it through the floor. The vibration pattern changes when it shuts down.”

Anya’s eyes meet hers, and for a moment, something unspoken passes between them. Not a confession, exactly. Not even agreement. But something that says: I know what you’re asking. And I cannot answer.

“The generator ran all night,” Anya says, and her voice is flat, controlled. “As it always does. I would have noticed if it stopped. I would have logged it.”

But even as she speaks, her gaze slides to Erik. And in that glance, Ingrid sees the shape of a lie. Not Anya’s lie—someone else’s, a story she’s been told to tell, a truth she’s been forced to bury. The realization settles into her stomach like ice water.

She knows, Ingrid thinks. She knows the generator stopped. She knows because she was here, and she felt it, and someone made her pretend otherwise.

“Thank you, Anya,” Erik says, and his voice carries a note of dismissal. “That’s helpful. Ingrid, I hope this clarifies things.”

Ingrid rises from her chair, and the movement feels heavier than it should. She’s carrying something now—a weight she didn’t have when she entered this room. The knowledge that the station’s official story is a fabrication. That Erik Larsen, the man entrusted with their safety, is lying about something fundamental. And that Anya Petrova, whatever her role, is trapped in that lie alongside him.

“It does,” Ingrid says, and means: You’ve told me exactly enough to know you’re hiding more.

Erik stands as well, extending his hand. His grip is firm, paternal, the clasp of a man accustomed to shaking hands with subordinates. “I’m glad we could talk. This station—it’s a family, in its way. We look out for each other. I want you to feel you can come to me with any concerns.”

Ingrid nods, and the gesture feels like another mask settling over her face. “I appreciate that. I’ll submit my request for the diesel logs through proper channels.”

“Good,” Erik says. “Procedure. It protects everyone.”

She walks to the door, and Anya steps aside to let her pass. For a moment, they’re close enough that Ingrid can smell the machine oil on Anya’s hands, can see the faint tremor in her jaw. Then she’s through the doorway and into the corridor, the cold air of the hallway a shock after the warmth of the office.

The passage is dim, lit by bulbs that cast yellow pools of light at irregular intervals. The station’s bones show here—exposed pipes, riveted metal plates, the skeletal infrastructure that keeps them alive in this hostile place. Ingrid walks slowly, her footsteps echoing against the floor.

She passes the door to the equipment bay, where cables and diving gear hang in organized rows. Further down, the entrance to the generator room hums with the sound of the diesel engine—running now, running continuously, as Erik claimed it ran that night. But Ingrid knows better. She’s seen the log entry, and she’s seen the way Erik’s hand tightened on the logbook, and she’s seen the way Anya’s eyes betrayed her careful words.

02:47, she thinks. Forty-seven minutes of darkness. What happened during those forty-seven minutes that someone needed to erase?

The question follows her down the corridor, past the lab where Anya spends her hours, past the common room where Finn can sometimes be found nursing a drink and staring at nothing. She passes Solveig’s old quarters—still sealed, still waiting for someone to sort through its contents—and feels a pang of something that isn’t quite grief. Something sharper, more urgent.

What did you know, Solveig? What did you find out here, in this frozen place, that cost you your life?

She reaches her own door and pauses, hand on the cold metal of the handle. Inside, the hydrophone tape waits on her desk, its eleven-minute gap a wound in the record of that night. She’s been thinking about it wrong, she realizes. She’s been treating it as a technical anomaly, a puzzle to be solved with calibration and cross-referencing. But it’s not a puzzle. It’s a crime scene.

Someone erased those eleven minutes deliberately. Someone with access to the recording equipment, with knowledge of how to edit tape without leaving obvious traces. Someone who knew what was recorded there, and needed it gone.

The generator shutdown gives her a window—forty-seven minutes when the station would have been dark, when anyone could have moved unseen, when anything could have happened in the shadows. And Erik Larsen, who controls the logs and the narrative, who sits in his warm office dispensing rehearsed stories and paternalistic reassurances, is at the center of it.

Ingrid opens her door and steps inside. The room is cold—colder than she left it, as if the heat has bled away through some crack in the walls. She crosses to the desk and picks up the hydrophone tape, turning it over in her hands. The plastic casing is smooth, anonymous, revealing nothing of its secrets.

She’ll need to review the maintenance logs without Erik’s knowledge. She’ll need to find out who else had access to the recording equipment that night. And she’ll need to understand what her sister was doing in the hours before her death—what she saw, what she heard, what she recorded that someone needed to silence.

The tape sits heavy in her palm, a small thing carrying the weight of a life. Ingrid sets it down and reaches for her notebook. If Erik wants her to submit formal requests and follow proper channels, she will. She’ll give him the performance he expects, the grieving sister satisfied with official answers.

But in the margins, in the spaces between the lines, she’ll build her own investigation. She owes Solveig that much. She owes herself that much.

Outside, the wind howls against the station walls, a sound the hydrophones would pick up if they were listening—a sound like grief, like warning, like something trying to break through from the darkness beyond.

Ingrid opens her notebook and begins to write.

She writes the date, then her sister’s name. The letters blur.

Her pen hovers. Erik’s face surfaces in her memory—the tightness around his eyes when she’d asked about the generator logs, the fraction of a pause before he’d answered. A man lying to a grieving sister, or a man lying to protect someone else? She doesn’t know. Doesn’t know if Anya’s vague corroboration was a warning or an accident.

Don’t accuse. Don’t confront. Don’t let him see you coming.

She underlines the words she’s written: Generator timestamp—contradiction confirmed.

If Erik is involved, an open accusation would end her access to the station, cut her off from the equipment, the logs, the people who knew Solveig. If he’s not involved—if someone higher is pulling strings and Erik is merely the mouthpiece—then showing her hand alerts them both.

No. Better to play the part he expects: the sister who asks questions but accepts answers, who files requests and waits for responses that will never come.

She turns to a fresh page and writes in the neat, careful script she learned in school: Formal request—Solveig’s last recordings. Personal effects. Shift logs.

For him. For the performance.

Then, in the cramped shorthand she developed during her months on the ice, she adds beneath it: Real investigation: diesel log original. Anya—approach alone. Hydrophone data—find backup before Erik’s “review.”

She tears the formal request page free, folds it once, and sets it on Erik’s desk. The other notes she slips into her pocket, close against her chest.

The wind rattles the window. Somewhere below, the hydrophones hum their endless vigil, listening to a darkness that doesn’t know she’s coming.

Ingrid walks out into the station’s fluorescent glare, her face composed, her notebook a hidden weight against her heart.
