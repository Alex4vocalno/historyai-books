Chapter 12

The Sister's Shadow

────────────────────────────────────────

The crayon drawing, faded to ochre and violet, felt absurdly small in Ingrid’s hands. She turned it under the server room’s blue emergency lighting, and the waxy pigment caught a dull sheen against her fingertips. A monster alphabet. She and Solveig had invented it one summer in their grandmother’s attic in Drøbak—two girls crouched over a roll of butcher paper, inventing creatures for every letter of the Norwegian alphabet. Each monster had a name, a sound, and a corresponding symbol: curling horns for A, a fanged spiral for Ø, a triple-pronged trident for Å.

She had found the drawing folded inside the back cover of Solveig’s journal, pressed between the final entry and the cardboard like a forgotten bookmark. For three days she had stared at the journal’s cryptic margins—those scattered symbols that looked like notation, like shorthand, like the idle doodling of a bored technician on a long polar night. She had assumed they were decorative. Ornamental. The kind of marks a person made while thinking about something else entirely.

Now the crayon drawing lay beside the open journal on the server room’s metal table, and the symbols matched.

Ingrid’s hands trembled. She pressed her left palm flat against the cold surface to steady it, and the old soldering scar across her skin stretched white against the chill. The server rack hummed behind her, a low mechanical drone that filled the cramped space with vibration. She could feel it in her teeth.

She positioned the crayon key beside the journal’s first annotated page. The entry was dated nine days before Solveig drowned. Solveig’s precise, small handwriting filled the lines with routine observations—sea temperature, ice thickness, the generator’s hourly output logged in neat columns. But in the right-hand margin, running vertically along the edge, a sequence of symbols traced downward like a second, secret sentence.

The fanged spiral. The triple-pronged trident. The curling horns.

Ø. Å. A.

She mouthed the sounds without voicing them, letting her lips shape each letter in the server room’s stale air. Her finger traced the crayon symbol on the key, then moved to the corresponding mark in the journal. The waxy pigment smudged under her thumb, leaving a faint violet streak across the page. She wiped it away with the frayed cuff of her grey Norwegian navy sweater—Solveig’s sweater—and felt a small, sick lurch at the gesture. Wearing a dead woman’s clothes while decoding her secrets.

The first word resolved: output.

Ingrid sat back. Her chair scraped against the floor, and the sound was loud in the confined space. She breathed in, held it, breathed out. Then she leaned forward again.

The second word took longer. Solveig had used a variant—a reversed trident where the original key showed it pointing upward. A childhood convention: flipped symbols meant plural. Ingrid remembered this suddenly, the way one remembers a game’s rules decades after the last time it was played. The memory arrived with the ghost of her grandmother’s attic—smell of dust and old wool, Solveig’s laughter, the scratch of crayon on paper.

Data.

The third and fourth words came faster as the cipher’s logic reassembled itself in her hands. The symbols were not random. They were not decorative. They were a deliberate, functional code, and Solveig had used it throughout the journal’s margins with the systematic discipline of an adult who had repurposed a childhood game for adult purposes.

Output data falsified.

Ingrid read the phrase again. Whispered it this time, the consonants clicking softly in her throat. “Output data falsified.” The words hung in the server room’s stale air, meaningless and enormous at once.

She turned the page.

The next annotated entry was dated seven days before the drowning. The marginal symbols ran along the bottom edge this time, cramped and small, as though Solveig had written them quickly, furtively, perhaps during a shift change when the observation deck was briefly empty. Ingrid aligned the crayon key and worked through each character with methodical patience.

The decoded text read: Generator logs don’t match reported figures. Weekly output inflated by 18% in transmission to mainland.

Ingrid’s breath caught. She knew what this meant, or she was beginning to know. The station’s funding—its entire operational budget—depended on energy production metrics reported to the Norwegian Energy Directorate. Inflated output figures meant inflated funding. It meant someone had been systematically falsifying data for months, perhaps years, and pocketing the difference between what the station actually produced and what it claimed to produce.

She thought of Erik Larsen. The generator is my responsibility. The data is yours. Stay in your lane. He had said that to her only days ago, his voice carrying the particular flatness of a man defending territory. She had read it as professional rigidity at the time. Now she read it differently.

She turned another page. And another.

The entries accumulated like sediment. Each marginal annotation decoded to a brief, clinical observation: dates, percentages, discrepancies between the generator’s actual output and the figures transmitted south. Solveig had been meticulous. She had cross-referenced the diesel ledger—the fuel consumption logs that Ingrid herself had flagged as anomalous only days ago—and found that the burned fuel did not match the reported energy production. The numbers were impossible. The generator could not have produced what the reports claimed, not with the fuel it had consumed.

Someone was skimming. Someone had been skimming for a long time, and Solveig had found the proof.

Ingrid’s hands had stopped trembling. The grief that usually distorted her perception—the soft fog that made every piece of evidence about Solveig feel personal and raw—had burned away, replaced by something colder. Clarifying. She felt the precise, sickening clarity of a spectrogram showing a clean cut rather than a degradation. An edit. A deliberate, human act.

She turned to the final annotated entry. It was dated two days before Solveig drowned.

The symbols here were different. Smaller. Closer together, as though Solveig had been pressing hard, writing in a constrained space, perhaps in the dark, perhaps while someone was nearby. The crayon key’s correspondences came slowly now—Ingrid had to turn the drawing twice, checking a variant she had nearly forgotten, a doubled curling horn that meant a compound consonant.

The decoded text read: Confrontation scheduled. Evidence secured separately. This is a just transaction—not a threat. They will comply.

Ingrid stared at the words. Her finger rested on the final symbol—the triple-pronged trident, the Å that ended comply—and the crayon smudged under her touch, violet pigment bleeding into the dry ink of Solveig’s adult handwriting. The dissonance was physical. A child’s drawing. A child’s game. And beneath it, the calculated language of someone preparing to use leverage.

This is a just transaction—not a threat.

Ingrid read it again. Then again. The words did not change. She whispered them, and the whisper sounded like someone else’s voice in her skull. A just transaction. Not a threat. The language of someone who had convinced herself that coercion was righteousness. Who had dressed blackmail in the clothes of moral duty.

Solveig. Her sister. The girl who had invented monsters with crayons and laughter. The woman who had drowned in a cove that the tide tables proved was a mudflat when the sea supposedly took her.

Ingrid pressed both palms flat against the table. The metal was ice-cold through her skin. She could feel the server rack’s hum traveling up through the floor, through the table legs, into her bones. The scar on her left palm ached—a phantom response, the body remembering what the mind could not yet process.

Solveig had found evidence of fraud. She had documented it in a code only she and her sister shared—or so she must have believed. And she had planned to confront the person responsible, armed with that evidence, framing it as a transaction. Compliance, not exposure. She had not planned to report the fraud to the Directorate. She had planned to use it.

The question settled into Ingrid’s chest like a stone dropped into still water: Did her sister’s quest for justice cross into something uglier that got her killed?

She gathered the crayon drawing and the journal together, hands moving with a deliberateness that felt foreign. She closed the journal. She folded the crayon key along its original creases and slipped it into the journal’s back cover, where she had found it. She placed both into the archive box and sealed the lid.

Then she stood in the server room’s blue light and did not move for a long time.

The corridor outside the archive was narrow and cold, lit by strips of fluorescent tubing that buzzed at a frequency just below hearing. Ingrid walked toward the common room with her shoulders pulled in and her hands thrust into the sweater’s pockets, fingers curling against the frayed cuffs. She needed coffee. She needed to sit somewhere with other people and not think. She needed to not think for five minutes.

Erik Larsen was already in the common room. He stood at the counter with his back to the door, pouring from the pot with the slow, deliberate care of a man who measured everything. The bitter smell of over-extracted coffee reached her before she crossed the threshold.

“You’re up late,” he said without turning. “Or early. Can’t tell which anymore.”

“Couldn’t sleep.” Ingrid moved to the far counter and pulled a mug from the drying rack. The ceramic was cold. She held it between both hands and did not fill it.

Erik turned. He held his own mug close to his chest, and his eyes moved over her face with an attention that might have been concern in a different context. In this one, it felt like assessment. “You look like you’ve seen a ghost.”

“I look like I haven’t slept.”

“That too.” He sipped. Watched her over the rim. “How’s the review going? The data review.”

“Progressing.”

“Good. Good.” He nodded slowly. “You know, if you need help with anything—any of the technical logs—I can walk you through the filing system. It took me months to learn how Solveig organized things. She had her own system.”

Ingrid felt the words land. Her own system. She tightened her grip on the mug. “I’m managing.”

“You’ve been quiet lately. More than usual.” Erik set his mug on the counter. The ceramic clicked against the metal surface. “I know this is hard. Being here, where she was. Doing this work. If there’s anything you’ve found—anything that doesn’t make sense—I hope you’d tell me. We’re all on the same team here.”

Ingrid looked at him. His face was open, earnest. His voice carried the warm, reasonable tone of a man who believed in procedure and trust. She thought of his words from days ago: This station runs on trust, Ingrid. And procedure. She thought of the generator logs that did not match the reported figures. She thought of stay in your lane.

“Family secrets,” she said, and watched his expression. “Everyone has them.”

Something moved behind his eyes. A flicker—there and gone, fast enough that a less suspicious observer might have missed it. His hand tightened on the counter’s edge. “I suppose they do.”

The silence between them thickened. The fluorescent lights buzzed. Somewhere in the station’s walls, a pipe clanked once and went quiet.

“You know,” Erik said, and his voice had shifted—still warm, but with a new edge beneath it, like a blade wrapped in cloth, “Solveig was complicated. More than I think even you knew. She had… layers. Things she kept close.” He paused. “Sometimes people do things for reasons that seem right to them at the time. Even if, from the outside, they look like poor judgment.”

Poor judgment. The phrase landed in Ingrid’s mind and sat there, heavy and deliberate. She stared at him and said nothing.

He held her gaze for a beat too long. Then he picked up his mug, nodded once—a curt, closing gesture—and walked past her toward the door. His shoulder did not quite brush hers, but she felt the displacement of air, the warmth of his body passing close in the cold corridor.

When he was gone, Ingrid poured coffee into her mug. The liquid was bitter and stale. She drank it anyway, standing at the counter, staring at the empty doorway.

The cove was a mudflat. The sea could not have taken Solveig at the hour the official report claimed. Someone had erased eleven minutes from the hydrophone tape. And Solveig—Solveig had discovered the fraud, documented it in a childhood cipher, and planned to use the evidence as leverage.

This is a just transaction—not a threat. They will comply.

Who had Solveig been about to confront? And was that person still on this station, breathing the same recycled air, pouring coffee in the same common room, watching Ingrid’s face for signs of what she knew?

The coffee tasted like nothing. She set the mug down and pressed her palms flat against the cold metal counter, letting the chill steady her. The scar on her left hand ached. The server room hummed distantly through the floor, a mechanical heartbeat.

Her sister had not been a victim. Or not only a victim. The distinction was a blade, and it cut deeper than any loss.
