Chapter 15

The Edited Truth

────────────────────────────────────────

The question Finn’s confession had left burning — who gave the order, and what were those eleven minutes hiding — carried Ingrid down the corridor to the radio room door. Erik sat with his back to her, bathed in the sickly green glow of the equipment panel, his shoulders hunched like a man waiting for a blade to fall. The hydrophone hummed its eternal vigil, a low frequency pulse that had monitored these waters long before Solveig drowned, and would continue long after everyone who remembered her was gone. Ingrid stepped inside and let the door click shut behind her.

She didn’t lock it. She didn’t need to.

“You sent Finn to edit the tape,” she said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected, cold and clear as the polar air that seeped through the station’s ancient insulation.

Erik’s hand froze over the frequency dial. For a long moment, neither of them moved. The green light painted shadows up the walls, making the cramped room feel like the interior of a submarine running dark. When Erik finally turned, his face was the color of old paper.

“Ingrid.”

“Don’t.” She crossed to the console in three swift strides and slapped Finn’s ledger down between them. The sound cracked through the room like a gunshot. “Don’t pretend you don’t know what this is. Don’t pretend you don’t know why I’m here.”

Erik’s eyes dropped to the ledger. His throat worked, a visible swallow. Ingrid watched him calculate, watched him weigh denial against the evidence sprawled across the pages in Finn’s meticulous hand. The diesel entries. The falsified maintenance logs. The pattern of numbers that traced, with damning precision, a trail of money that should never have moved.

“It’s not what you think.”

“Then tell me what it is.” Ingrid planted her palms on the console and leaned in. The metal was cold through her sleeves, cold as the water that had filled Solveig’s lungs. “Tell me why Finn has a record of every fuel delivery for the past eighteen months, and why those numbers don’t match a single invoice in the station’s official accounts.”

Erik’s gaze flicked to the door, then back to her. She saw him breathing, saw his chest rise and fall with shallow, rapid breaths. The equipment hummed. Somewhere in the station’s guts, a generator coughed and steadied.

“There’s an explanation.”

“There always is.” Ingrid flipped the ledger open to a page she’d marked with a folded corner. “July fourteenth. August third. September twenty-second. Three diesel pickups that never made it into the official logs, but somehow made it into Finn’s records. Someone was skimming, Erik. Someone was selling fuel on the side and pocketing the difference.”

The color drained from Erik’s face. He looked like a man watching his own gallows being built.

“The amounts add up to almost forty thousand kroner,” Ingrid continued. “That’s not a clerical error. That’s embezzlement.”

The word hung in the air between them, heavy and final. Erik’s jaw tightened. His hands, resting on his thighs, curled into fists.

“It wasn’t supposed to—“He stopped himself, pressed his lips together, and started again. “The station was going to be decommissioned. The funding cuts were already announced. I thought if I could bridge the gap, keep us operational until the research grant came through…”

“So you stole.”

“I borrowed.” His voice cracked on the word. “I intended to pay it back. Every krone. But the grant kept getting delayed, and then it fell through entirely, and the hole got deeper, and I couldn’t see a way out.”

Ingrid stared at him. The man she’d worked beside for three years, the station chief who’d signed her paychecks and approved her research requests, sat in front of her now looking like a hollowed-out shell. She’d expected bluster, denial, perhaps even an attempt to turn the accusation back on her. She hadn’t expected this: a confession that came so easily, as if he’d been waiting years to speak it.

“How much?”

Erik’s eyes squeezed shut. “Forty-seven thousand. Give or take.”

“Give or take.” Ingrid laughed, a harsh sound with no humor in it. “You’ve been stealing from the station for two years, and you’re telling me ‘give or take’?”

“I’m telling you because you already know.” Erik opened his eyes. “You didn’t come here to ask about fuel invoices. You came here because of Solveig.”

The name landed like a stone dropped into still water. Ingrid felt the ripples spread through her chest, her throat, the space behind her eyes where tears wanted to form but couldn’t, not anymore, not after all these months.

“Yes,” she said. “Solveig.”

“She found out.” Erik’s voice was barely audible now, a thread of sound beneath the hydrophone’s hum. “She was reviewing the acoustic data from the monitoring buoys. She noticed discrepancies in the power consumption logs. She started asking questions I couldn’t answer.”

“So you killed her.”

Erik’s head snapped up. His eyes went wide, the whites showing all around the irises, and something in his expression shifted. It wasn’t guilt. It wasn’t the calculated panic of a man caught in a lie. It was something rawer, more primal, the look of prey that had just caught a predator’s scent.

“No.” The word came out strangled. “No. God, Ingrid, no. I’m a thief, not a murderer. I took money. I didn’t— I would never—““Then who edited the tape?” Ingrid pressed forward, her voice rising. “Who ordered Finn to cut eleven minutes from the hydrophone recording on the night my sister drowned? Because he says it was you.”

Erik’s face went slack. Ingrid watched the blood drain from it, watched his lips part and his breath come in short, shallow gasps. He looked like a man who’d just been told his execution date.

“The tape,” he whispered.

“The tape.” Ingrid reached across and grabbed a fistful of his shirt, twisting the fabric in her grip. “Eleven minutes erased from the night Solveig died. Finn says you ordered him to do it. He says you told him it was classified, that it was a matter of station security. What were you hiding, Erik? What was on that recording that was worth killing my sister for?”

Erik’s hand came up, not to push her away, but to clutch at her wrist. His grip was trembling, his palm slick with cold sweat.

“I didn’t kill her.” Each word was a ragged gasp. “I swear on my life, I didn’t kill her. I swear on my children’s lives.”

“Then who—““I don’t know!” The words burst out of him, loud enough to make the equipment lights flicker. “I don’t know who killed her. I don’t know what was on that tape. All I know is that someone wanted it gone, and they made sure I couldn’t say no.”

Ingrid’s grip loosened. She stared at him, at the tears now tracking down his cheeks, at the absolute terror carved into every line of his face. This wasn’t the performance of a guilty man. This was genuine, unfeigned fear.

“What do you mean, you couldn’t say no?”

Erik’s hand fumbled in his pocket. He pulled out a folded piece of paper, cheap and wrinkled, and thrust it toward her with the desperation of a man handing over a live grenade.

Ingrid took it. She unfolded it slowly, smoothing the creases with fingers that had gone numb.

The message was typed, the letters slightly uneven, as if produced on an old manual machine. No header. No signature. Just three lines of text:

The hydrophone tape from October 23 needs eleven minutes removed, starting at counter 0489. This is not a request. Your theft has been documented. Evidence will follow.

The paper shook in Ingrid’s hands. She read the words again, and a third time, letting them sink into her understanding like cold water seeping into bone.

“When did you receive this?”

“The day after Solveig died.” Erik’s voice was raw. “It was in my personal mail cubby. No one saw who put it there.”

“And the evidence they mentioned?”

“Two days later, another envelope appeared. Copies of fuel transfer records. Account numbers. Enough to send me to prison for a decade.” He laughed, a wet and broken sound. “As if I needed more incentive. They already knew what I’d done. They knew I couldn’t go to the authorities without exposing myself.”

Ingrid’s mind raced. The pieces were rearranging themselves, forming a new pattern that she hadn’t seen before. Erik had stolen, yes. He’d been blackmailed into ordering the tape edit, yes. But the fear in his eyes wasn’t the fear of exposure. It was something else.

“Who else knows about the embezzlement?”

“I don’t know.” Erik’s hands dropped to his sides. “That’s the truth. I’ve spent eight months trying to figure out who’s behind this, and I have nothing. No names. No faces. Just notes that appear in my cubby and threats that keep me awake every night.”

“Solveig.” Ingrid forced the word out through a tight throat. “She found out about the money. Was she the one blackmailing you?”

Erik’s face crumpled. “I thought so. I thought she was the one sending the notes, that she was using the information to pressure me into resigning. I thought if I just held on, if I just kept my head down, she’d eventually—““But she wasn’t.”

“No.” His voice broke. “The first note arrived the day after she died. Whoever’s behind this wasn’t trying to silence her. They were using her death to get to me.”

Ingrid stepped back. Her legs hit the edge of the equipment rack, and she steadied herself against the cold metal. The room seemed smaller now, the walls pressing in, the green light casting everything in a sickly pallor.

“If you didn’t kill Solveig,” she said slowly, “and if the blackmailer didn’t kill her to silence her about the money, then why? Why edit the tape? Why kill her at all?”

Erik’s eyes darted to the door again. It was a reflexive movement, automatic and full of dread.

“I’ve asked myself that every day for eight months,” he said. “And the only answer I can come up with is that whatever was on that recording was worth more than my career, more than Solveig’s life, more than any of us can afford to know.”

“Tell me.”

“I can’t.”

“Tell me what you suspect.”

“I can’t.” Erik’s voice dropped to a whisper. “They’re watching, Ingrid. I don’t know how, but they are. Every move I make, every conversation I have. The notes don’t just appear. They’re preceded by… signs. A folder moved on my desk. A light left on that I know I turned off. Small things that tell me I’m being observed by someone with access to this station.”

Ingrid’s blood chilled. She thought of the anonymous messages that had appeared in Erik’s cubby, the evidence that had been gathered with surgical precision. Someone on the inside. Someone who knew the station’s rhythms, its routines, its secrets.

“Who has access to your mail?”

“Everyone.” Erik spread his hands. “Station staff. Researchers. Supply crews. Anyone who can walk through the main corridor.”

“That’s dozens of people.”

“Yes.”

Ingrid looked down at the note in her hand, at the uneven letters that had set this entire nightmare in motion. An anonymous threat. A coerced edit. A dead woman whose only crime had been noticing the wrong thing at the wrong time.

“What aren’t you telling me?” she asked.

Erik’s gaze met hers. In the green half-light, his face looked like a drowning man’s, pale and desperate and searching for rescue that wasn’t coming.

“There’s something else,” he said. “Something I haven’t told anyone.”

“What?”

“The notes didn’t stop. They keep coming. Every few weeks, a new envelope appears with instructions. Maintain the equipment logs at certain levels. Divert specific fuel shipments to particular coordinates. Small things, easy to miss if you’re not looking.”

Ingrid’s breath caught. “You’re still being blackmailed.”

“I never stopped.” Erik’s laugh was hollow. “They don’t just want the tape edit. They want access. They want the station’s resources. They want me to look the other way while certain… activities happen in these waters.”

“What kind of activities?”

“I don’t ask.” His voice dropped to a barely audible whisper. “I learned early on that asking questions gets people killed.”

The weight of the words settled over Ingrid like a physical thing. Erik wasn’t just a thief. He was a pawn, a tool being used by someone with a larger agenda. And Solveig, whatever she had stumbled onto, had been caught in the crossfire.

“If I find out you’re lying to me,” Ingrid said, her voice low and steady, “I will make sure every authority that can prosecute you gets a full copy of Finn’s ledger. You’ll spend the next decade in a cell.”

“I know.” Erik’s eyes were dry now, resigned. “I’ve been waiting for that knock on the door since the first note appeared.”

“Then why haven’t you turned yourself in?”

“Because whoever’s doing this isn’t just threatening me.” He leaned forward, and Ingrid saw something in his expression that looked almost like concern. “They mentioned you, Ingrid. In the last note. They said to keep you away from the hydrophone archives. They said if you kept digging, there would be consequences.”

A chill ran through her. She’d expected threats, expected danger, but hearing it stated so baldly made it real in a way that abstract suspicion hadn’t.

“When?”

“Three days ago.”

Ingrid folded the blackmail note and slipped it into her pocket. Her hands were steady now, her mind clear. The cold rage that had driven her to Erik’s door hadn’t dissipated, but it had transformed into something sharper, more focused.

“Whoever’s behind this killed my sister,” she said. “They erased evidence, they manipulated you, and now they’re threatening me. Do you really think I’m going to stop?”

Erik’s face crumpled. “I think,” he said quietly, “that you’re going to get yourself killed.”

“Then at least I’ll die knowing the truth.”

She turned toward the door, but Erik’s hand shot out and caught her arm. His grip was surprisingly strong, his fingers digging into her sleeve.

“Ingrid.” His voice was urgent, barely above a whisper. “Be careful. Whoever this is, they’re not just station personnel. The coordinates they’ve had me send fuel to, the equipment they’ve asked me to maintain, the patterns… it’s bigger than a research outpost. There are ships out there that shouldn’t exist. Signals that don’t match any registry. I don’t know what they’re doing, but it’s organized. It’s funded. And they have no intention of letting anyone stop them.”

Ingrid looked down at his hand on her arm. She should have felt afraid. She should have felt the urge to run, to hide, to let the authorities handle it. But all she felt was the cold certainty that had been building in her since the night Solveig’s body had been pulled from the water.

“Who are they?” she asked.

“I don’t know.” Erik released her arm. “But I think Solveig figured it out. And I think that’s why she’s dead.”

The door clicked shut behind Ingrid as she stepped into the corridor. The station’s fluorescent lights buzzed overhead, casting their sterile glow over walls that suddenly seemed too thin, too permeable. Somewhere in the building, footsteps echoed, and she couldn’t tell if they were coming closer or moving away.

The note sat heavy in her pocket, a folded square of paper that contained more questions than answers. Erik had stolen. He’d been blackmailed. He’d ordered the tape edit under duress. But he hadn’t killed Solveig, and the person who had was still out there, watching, waiting, making sure that the eleven minutes erased from the hydrophone tape stayed buried forever.

Ingrid walked down the corridor toward her quarters, her mind racing through the implications. Erik’s confession had given her a glimpse of something larger, something that extended beyond the station’s walls into the dark waters beyond.

She thought of the coordinates Erik had mentioned, the fuel shipments diverted to unknown destinations. She thought of the ships that shouldn’t exist, the signals that didn’t match any registry. And she thought of Solveig, alone in her research outpost on the night she died, hearing something on the hydrophone that had cost her her life.

The eleven minutes. Whatever was on that erased section of tape, it was the key. It was what Solveig had died for, what Erik was being blackmailed over, what the anonymous threat had been so desperate to hide.

And somewhere, in the physical evidence she’d been gathering, in the tape counter log and the diesel ledger and the tide tables she’d already studied, there had to be a clue. A fragment. A trace of what had been recorded before it was wiped clean.

Ingrid reached her door and paused, her hand on the handle. The corridor behind her was empty, the footsteps having faded into silence. But she could feel it now, the prickling sensation at the back of her neck that told her she wasn’t alone.

Someone was watching. Someone who had killed to protect their secrets.

And Ingrid had just announced, through her actions if not her words, that she wasn’t going to stop until she found them.
