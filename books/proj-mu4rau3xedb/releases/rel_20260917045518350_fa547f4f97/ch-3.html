Chapter 3

Solveig's Hidden Pages

────────────────────────────────────────

The footlocker under Ingrid’s bunk exhaled a sigh of cold, stale air.

She knelt on the thin carpet of her own quarters, fingers curled around the brass latch. The locker had traveled with her from Oslo, shipped ahead in her personal cargo, and she had not opened it since arriving at the station. Inside, wrapped in a Norwegian navy sweater she had not been able to look at since the funeral, were Solveig’s personal effects. The station director had offered to send them south. Ingrid had refused. She wanted to go through them herself, alone, in her own time. That had been four days ago. The sweater still smelled faintly of Solveig’s shampoo, a ghost of cedar and something floral she could not name.

Beneath the sweater were the things the station had returned in a plastic bin: a thermos, a pair of wool socks, three paperback novels with cracked spines, a charging cable, a toiletry bag. Ingrid lifted each item out and set it on the floor beside her. She was not looking for any of these. She was looking for something Solveig would have kept private, something the cursory inventory of a dead woman’s belongings would have missed.

The key lay at the bottom of the locker, wedged between the folded sweater and the metal wall. Small, brass, with a round head and teeth worn smooth by use. Tape held it to the inside of a hardcover textbook on marine acoustics, the tape yellowed and brittle. Ingrid peeled it free and held it under her desk lamp. No markings. No tag. She turned it over between her thumb and forefinger, feeling the weight of it, and thought of the footlocker she had not been able to open in Solveig’s quarters because she did not have the key.

She had asked the station quartermaster for access two days ago. He had unlocked the door, wished her a quiet goodbye, and left her standing in the doorway. The room was untouched. Bunk made, shelf neat, a thin layer of ice dust on every surface from the ventilation draft. She had stood there for a long minute, breathing the cold air, and then walked out without touching anything. The reverence of the dead had stopped her at the threshold.

Now the key sat warm in her palm. She closed her fist around it.

Solveig’s quarters were two corridors and one stairwell down from Ingrid’s, in the section of the station that housed the junior researchers. The hallway lights were dimmed to their nighttime setting, casting a bluish wash over the metal walls. Ingrid’s socks made no sound on the grated floor. She carried her torch, a penlight with a narrow beam, and the key.

The door to Solveig’s room was unlocked, the same way she had left it. The quartermaster had not relocked it. Inside, the air was colder than the corridor. The ventilation in this section ran at a lower setting, and the room felt like a box carved from the ice outside. Ingrid clicked on the torch.

The beam found the bunk, the shelf, the small desk bolted to the wall. Everything was as she remembered. The thin mattress, the folded blanket, the pillow still dented with the impression of Solveig’s head. On the shelf, a row of textbooks identical to the one Ingrid had found the key in. She swept the beam lower. Under the bunk frame, recessed into the wall, was a metal footlocker. It had a brass padlock.

Ingrid knelt. The metal of the padlock was so cold it burned her fingertips. She fitted the key in, turned it, felt the mechanism give with a soft click. The lid rose on silent hinges.

Inside, the air was stale and dry, carrying the faint scent of old paper and machine oil. A stack of notebooks, a cable organizer, a small fabric pouch. And beneath these, held flat by the weight of the items above it, a journal. Its cover was dark leather, worn soft at the corners, with a elastic strap that had been pulled loose and tucked under the back cover. Ingrid lifted it out. The leather was cold against her palms. She sat back on her heels, then moved to the bunk, positioning herself against the wall with the torch angled over her shoulder.

She opened the journal to the first page.

The handwriting was Solveig’s. Ingrid knew it as well as her own: the same narrow loops, the same habit of crossing her sevens with a single slash, the same slight leftward lean that their mother had said came from being left-handed in a right-handed world. The ink was blue, ballpoint, pressed hard enough to leave grooves in the paper.

The first entry was dated six weeks before Solveig’s death.

“Calibration run complete. Hydrophone array 3 showing 0.4 dB attenuation on the low-frequency band. Replaced pre-amp module. Will re-run tomorrow. Dinner was fish. Again. Called Ingrid but she was on shift. Left a message. Hope she’s sleeping enough.”

Ingrid’s throat tightened. She read the entry again, then turned the page. The next entry was two days later.

“Array 3 clean. But array 1 showing something odd in the 20-40 Hz range. Signal strength dropping in a pattern that doesn’t match tidal interference. Could be a connector fault. Will swap cables with array 4 and compare. If the anomaly follows the cable, it’s hardware. If it follows the array, it’s something in the water.”

Solveig. Methodical, precise, ruling out variables one at a time. Ingrid could hear her sister’s voice in the sentences, the flat, patient tone of someone who had grown up taking things apart to understand how they worked. She turned the page.

“Anomaly follows the array. Not hardware. Signal attenuation in a narrow band, consistent across three sampling windows. This shouldn’t exist. Either something is physically blocking the array at specific frequencies, or the data stream is being modified after acquisition. Need to check the processing pipeline.”

Ingrid paused. She read the last sentence again. The data stream is being modified after acquisition. She could hear the engineer in Solveig, the careful, neutral language. But the sentence carried weight. Solveig was not describing equipment failure. She was describing interference.

The entries continued, dated irregularly. Some were short, a line or two. Others filled the page.

“Checked the processing logs. The raw data feed from array 1 does not match the archived output. There is a gap in the spectral envelope between 22 and 27 Hz. The raw feed shows signal present. The archived output shows flatline. Something is stripping that band before it reaches storage.”

Three runs. Same result. The attenuation was not in the water and not in the hardware. It was in the processing chain. Someone or something was editing the data before it reached the archive.

Ingrid set the journal on her knee and pressed her fingers against her temples. The torch beam wavered. She steadied it and kept reading.

The next entry was dated ten days after the first.

“I have not told anyone. I need to understand the scope first. If I report this through normal channels, the data gets flagged and reviewed by the science lead. But what if the science lead already knows? What if the editing is coming from that office? I need to build a case before I make noise. I need evidence that cannot be dismissed.”

Solveig’s handwriting had changed. The letters were smaller, pressed harder, the loops tighter. Ingrid recognized the compression of someone writing in a confined space, or in fear of being seen. She had seen it in her own field notes during stressful deployments, the hand pulling inward as the body tensed.

The entries shifted. Technical observations still appeared, but they were interleaved with something else.

“He asked me today if I was having trouble with the array. I said no. He looked at me the way he does when he already knows the answer and is checking whether you will lie. I smiled. I hate that I smiled.”

“Found something in the backup logs. There is a secondary processing script running on a cron job. It executes every six hours. It strips a specific frequency band from the raw hydrophone data before the archive write. The script is not in the official codebase. Someone installed it manually. I have copied the script source to local storage. I have not told anyone.”

Ingrid’s hands were shaking. She pressed the journal flat against the mattress to still them. The cold air of the room pressed against her face, and she could feel the chill seeping through Solveig’s sweater, the one she wore everywhere now, the oversized grey wool with the frayed cuffs. She pulled it tighter and read on.

The next entry was longer, filling a full page.

“I have been thinking about who has access to the processing pipeline. The list is short. Station systems are locked behind administrative credentials. The science lead has them. The station manager has them. The systems engineer has them. I do not. My access is read-only on the archived data and full access on the raw feed, which means I can see what is being deleted but I cannot stop it. The script runs regardless of my permissions.”

“Whoever did this wanted the 22-27 Hz band gone. That band corresponds to biological signatures. Large marine life. The kind that environmental protection protocols require us to report. If those signatures are absent from the archive, no one can prove they were there. No regulatory filings. No protection triggers. No operational delays.”

“I think someone is making sure the station does not have to report what its hydrophones are hearing. I think someone is editing the ocean.”

Ingrid stared at the page. The words were precise, measured, the language of a scientist building a hypothesis from evidence. But beneath the precision was something else. Solveig had not written I suspect or I believe. She had written I think, which was as close to certainty as her training would allow her to go without proof.

She turned the page. The entries grew shorter, the handwriting more compressed.

Solveig had called Ingrid that night. She had not mentioned the data. They talked about Christmas. Ingrid had sounded tired. Solveig wanted to tell her. She did not. This was not her fight. This was not anyone’s fight yet. But it would be.

“He was in the lab when I arrived this morning. The system log on my terminal showed a login at 03:00. My credentials. I was asleep at 03:00. Someone is using my access. Or someone is making sure I know they can.”

“I have started keeping this journal in the footlocker instead of on my desk. Small precaution. The lock is not much but it is mine.”

Ingrid’s fingers found the edge of the page. She turned it slowly.

The next entry was undated. The handwriting had changed again. Not compressed this time but fast, the pen moving across the page with a speed that left the ink thin and broken in places.

“I confirmed it. The secondary script was installed from an administrative session logged to a terminal in the station manager’s office. The timestamp predates my arrival at the station. This has been running for months. He knows. He has always known.”

The installation log. The script source. The raw data showing the deleted band. The archived output showing the gap. Enough. She had enough.

She had been wrong before. This was not a fight she could win quietly. The data went through systems he controlled. A report would land on his desk. A transmission to the mainland would route through the station uplink, which he monitored. No clean channel. No safe path.

“I will make them”

The sentence stopped.

Ingrid stared at the page. The torch beam was steady now because her hands had gone completely still. The ink at the end of the last word was thick, the pen pressed hard into the paper, and then it dragged. A thin blue line pulled to the right and off the edge of the page, as if the pen had been taken. Not set down. Taken.

She turned to the next page. Blank. She turned through the rest of the journal. Blank. Every page after that final drag of ink was blank.

Ingrid closed the journal. She held it in both hands, the leather cold against her palms, and stared at the far wall of the room. The ventilation hummed. The ice dust on the shelf glittered in the torchlight like ground glass.

She opened it again to the last entry. She read the final sentence. I will make them. The ink bleeding where the pen stopped. The drag to the edge.

Solveig had been interrupted. Or Solveig had been stopped. The distinction sat in Ingrid’s chest like a stone.

She thought of the spectrogram she had pulled from the tape two days ago. The clean cut at the eleven-minute mark. The edit, surgical and deliberate, removing a segment of audio with the precision of someone who knew exactly what frequencies to target and how to erase them without leaving a trace in the surrounding waveform. The same precision as a script that stripped a specific band from a hydrophone data stream. The same method. The same hand.

Ingrid closed the journal and pressed it against her sternum. The cold leather warmed slowly against her body. She sat in Solveig’s bunk, in Solveig’s room, wearing Solveig’s sweater, and for the first time since the funeral, she did not cry. The grief that had sat in her like a slow tide for weeks had crystallized into something harder and colder. It had edges now. It had a direction.

She thought of the people Solveig had written about. The unnamed he who looked at Solveig with knowledge in his eyes. The administrative credentials. The terminal in the station manager’s office. Solveig had not written a name. She had been too careful for that, or too frightened. But she had left a map. Access, motive, method. The shape of a person drawn in negative space.

Ingrid slid the journal into the footlocker, locked it, and pocketed the key. She stood. The room was the same as she had found it, ice dust and silence and the dent in the pillow. She clicked off the torch and stood in the dark for a long moment, breathing the cold air that had been Solveig’s last air, and then she walked out into the corridor and pulled the door shut behind her.

The journal ended mid-sentence, its threat hanging in the cold air of Solveig’s old room. But Ingrid carried it with her now, locked in metal and memory, and the weight of it against her hip was the weight of a new and terrible purpose.
