Chapter 1

The Unlogged Tape

────────────────────────────────────────

The hydrophone tape, labeled in her sister’s neat handwriting, sat in the deck like an accusation. Ingrid Solheim had handled hundreds of these reels over her three years as an acoustic engineer, but this one made her fingers tremble as she threaded it onto the spool. The date stamped in faded ink across the cardboard sleeve read June 14th, 1989. The night Elin drowned.

She glanced at the digital log mounted beside her station, its green phosphor glow casting harsh shadows across the control console. According to the official record, the hydrophone had run continuously from 1800 hours to 0600 hours, capturing the full acoustic profile of the fjord. Standard procedure. No gaps. No interruptions. The Norwegian Research Council’s protocols left nothing to chance, each tape logged and archived with bureaucratic precision.

Ingrid pressed play. The headphones crackled, then filled with the familiar symphony of the Arctic ocean. The groan of shifting ice sheets. The distant percussion of calving glaciers. The rhythmic pulse of winter waves against stone. She closed her eyes and let the sound wash over her, searching for any discontinuity, any hiccup in the continuous record of her sister’s final night.

The tape counter climbed steadily. 00247.00248.00249.

She opened her eyes and checked the digital timestamp. The two should match perfectly, the counter synchronized to the logging system by the station’s central clock. She picked up her pen and began noting the standard markers in her own log, the careful shorthand of a technician verifying routine data.

At counter 00312, her pen stopped.

The digital log read 22:47: 00. The tape counter read 00312. But according to the calibration she’d run that morning, 00312 should correspond to 22:36: 00. An eleven-minute discrepancy.

Ingrid pulled the headphones off and checked the calibration log. The numbers were correct. Her pen scratched against paper as she ran the calculation again. Eleven minutes. The tape counter was eleven minutes behind the digital timestamp.

She pressed rewind and watched the counter roll backward, the reels spinning in the dim light of the control room. The heating vents hummed overhead, but cold still crept through the walls of the station, the Arctic winter pressing against every seal. She stopped the tape at 00298, just before the jump, and pressed play.

Ocean noise. Ice. Water. Stone.

She watched the counter reach 00312. The digital timestamp advanced to 22:47: 00. But the sound continued without interruption, the same ambient ocean roar, the same acoustic fingerprint.

Ingrid stopped the tape. Her breath fogged in the cold air as she leaned closer to the console, her thumb hovering over the rewind button. She pressed it again, watching the counter, watching the digital display. The jump happened at exactly the same point. Counter 00312. Digital timestamp 22:47: 00.

She pulled the tape from the deck and held it up to the desk lamp, examining the magnetic ribbon where it wound across the take-up reel. To an untrained eye, it looked continuous. But Ingrid had spent three years learning to read magnetic tape the way a surgeon reads tissue. She found what she was looking for within seconds: a subtle change in the tape’s surface, a hairline shift in the oxide coating that betrayed a splice.

Someone had cut this tape.

Her hands moved with practiced efficiency as she rigged a magnifying lens over the deck and threaded the tape back onto the spool. She advanced it manually, centimeter by centimeter, until she found the splice point. The cut was clean, professional, almost invisible. Two pieces of tape joined with adhesive so precisely matched that the seam all but disappeared under magnification.

Her frequency analyzer came next, patched directly into the oscilloscope display. The screen flickered to life, painting the audio signal in bright green lines. She advanced the tape to the splice point and watched the waveform jump.

There. A clean vertical break in the signal. Not a fade, not a gradual loss of amplitude. A hard cut. The spectrogram showed the frequency content on either side of the splice: normal ocean noise, then sudden silence, then ocean noise again. But the silence wasn’t true silence. She zoomed in on the waveform and saw the flat line of magnetic erasure, the signature of a bulk demagnetizer passed over oxide to destroy whatever sound it once held.

The spectrogram showed a clean cut, not a degradation. That was an edit.

Ingrid sat back, her breath coming slow and deliberate. The weight of what she was seeing pressed against her chest. She had come to this station six months after her sister’s death, requesting the transfer herself, telling herself it was closure. Reviewing Elin’s work. Understanding what she had loved about this frozen outpost at the edge of the world.

But she had found something else entirely.

She pulled the official incident report from the folder beside her station and flipped to the timeline. Station commander Erik Larsen had documented the night in precise, military prose. 22:15: Elin Solheim leaves the station for a routine equipment check on the shoreline hydrophones. 22:45: Station loses radio contact. 23:10: Search initiated. 23:47: Body recovered from the water.

According to the report, Elin had drowned sometime between 22:15 and 22:45. An accident. Slippery rocks. Cold shock. The Arctic claiming another careless researcher.

But the tape said something different.

Ingrid checked the counter log again. The eleven-minute gap began at 22:36: 00 according to the physical tape counter. The digital log, which someone had manually updated, claimed continuous recording through 22:47: 00. The splice itself occurred at the eleven-minute mark, erasing everything between 22:36 and 22:47.

Those eleven minutes would have captured whatever happened to Elin. And someone had removed them.

She heard footsteps in the corridor outside the control room. Heavy boots on metal grating, the distinctive rhythm of someone approaching with purpose. Ingrid killed the oscilloscope display, pulled the tape from the deck, and slid it beneath a stack of routine hydrophone reels just as the door swung open.

Erik Larsen stood in the doorway, his broad frame blocking the fluorescent light from the hall. The station commander had a face built for authority, square-jawed and unlined despite the harsh winter he’d spent at this latitude. His eyes swept the control room with practiced efficiency before settling on Ingrid.

“Settling in?” His voice was warm, professional, the concerned host checking on a new arrival.

Ingrid kept her hands flat on the desk, her pulse steady. “Still finding my way around the archives. There’s a lot of material here.”

“Elin was meticulous about her records.” Erik stepped into the room, his boots clicking against the floor. “One of the best technicians we’ve had at this station. Her loss was…”

He trailed off, and Ingrid watched him compose his expression into something appropriately somber. A framed certificate hung on the wall behind him, the Norwegian Research Council’s safety protocols rendered in gold leaf, a reminder of the institution’s commitment to procedure. The Authority’s Mask, she thought. Official reassurance dressed in formal language.

“I appreciate you letting me take her post,” Ingrid said. “It helps, being here. Understanding what she did.”

“Of course.” Erik moved to the window, his reflection ghosting against the Arctic darkness outside. “I wanted to check on you personally. The first few weeks at an isolated station can be difficult. The winter night, the confined quarters. It takes adjustment.”

“I’ve done remote postings before.”

“But never somewhere quite like this.” He turned back to face her. “I wanted to tell you about that night. So you understand what happened. So you don’t have to wonder.”

Ingrid’s throat tightened. She kept her face neutral, her posture open. “I read the official report.”

“Reports don’t capture everything.” Erik leaned against the console, his posture deliberately casual. “I was on duty that night. Elin had been restless all week, working late hours, checking and rechecking the hydrophone calibrations. I thought nothing of it at the time. She was dedicated. Passionate about the work.”

He paused, and Ingrid realized he was watching her reaction. She nodded slowly, encouraging him to continue.

“When she didn’t return from the equipment check, I knew something was wrong. Elin was reliable. If she said she’d be back by 22:45, she’d be back by 22:40. The radio silence at 22:45 confirmed it. We launched the search immediately.”

Ingrid noticed the specific times. 22:45. The same timestamp where the digital log showed continuous recording, but the physical tape showed an eleven-minute gap already in progress.

“Where exactly was she found?” Ingrid asked.

“South cove. The rocks there are treacherous when they ice over. She must have slipped, hit her head, fallen into the water. Cold shock would have killed her within minutes, even if she’d been conscious.” Erik’s voice carried the rehearsed cadence of a man who had delivered this account many times. “By the time we reached her, it was too late.”

South cove. Ingrid kept her expression still, but her mind raced. She had reviewed the tide tables that morning. On June 14th, high tide had occurred at 21:30, with a maximum water level of 1.2 meters above chart datum. By 22:30, the tide had receded to 0.4 meters. The south cove was accessible only during high tide. At 22:30, the water level would have been too low for anyone to drown in that location without deliberate immersion.

Unless Elin had gone into the water intentionally. Or unless someone had put her there.

“The hydrophones were running that night,” Ingrid said carefully. “Recording continuously?”

“Standard protocol. The system never shuts down during winter months.” Erik straightened, his hand resting on the console beside the tape deck. “I made sure the logs were complete before filing the incident report. Everything was in order.”

Everything was in order. The words hung in the cold air. Ingrid understood now what she was hearing. The official story, delivered with bureaucratic confidence. The Authority’s Mask, concealing whatever truth lay beneath.

“I’d like to review the tapes from that night,” she said. “For calibration purposes. Understanding Elin’s baseline recordings will help me continue her work.”

Erik’s hand lingered on the tape deck. “Of course. Though I should warn you, the archive organization can be confusing. Elin had her own system. Some of the tapes may be mislabeled.”

“I’ll figure it out.”

“I’m sure you will.” He smiled, but his eyes remained watchful. “If you need anything, my quarters are at the end of the east corridor. This station runs on trust, Ingrid. And procedure. We look out for each other here.”

The implicit warning beneath the words. Trust. Procedure. Stay in your lane.

“I understand,” she said.

Erik nodded once, satisfied. He turned and walked to the door, pausing before he stepped into the hall. “It’s good to have you here, Ingrid. Elin would be pleased someone is continuing her work.”

The door closed behind him.

Ingrid sat motionless for a long moment, listening to his footsteps fade down the corridor. Then she pulled the tape from beneath the stack of reels and held it under the desk lamp, studying the splice again. The clean cut. The professional erasure.

Erik’s account placed Elin at the south cove at 22:45. But the tide tables proved she couldn’t have drowned there at that hour. And the tape he had just touched, the tape he had warned her might be mislabeled, contained an eleven-minute gap precisely when her sister had vanished.

Someone had erased those eleven minutes. Someone had falsified the logs. Someone had constructed a careful, professional cover-up and delivered it with bureaucratic confidence.

Ingrid threaded the tape back onto the deck and pressed record, capturing the ambient silence of the control room over the erased section. She would make a copy of the original, document the splice, preserve the evidence. Then she would listen to every tape from that week, every hydrophone recording, every piece of acoustic data she could find.

The Arctic night pressed against the window, vast and patient. Somewhere in the erased magnetic oxide, the truth waited. And Ingrid Solheim would find it, no matter how many lies stood in her way.
