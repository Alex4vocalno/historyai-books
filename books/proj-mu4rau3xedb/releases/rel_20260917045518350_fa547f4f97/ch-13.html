Chapter 13

The Practical Power

────────────────────────────────────────

The technical bay door sealed behind her with a pneumatic click, and the sound reminded her of a lock engaging. Finn Agaard stood at the far workbench under the humming fluorescents, a reel-to-reel tape deck disassembled before him, its guts exposed like something on a surgical table. He held a splicing block in one hand and a razor blade in the other, and when he looked up, his expression carried the careful blankness of a man who has rehearsed what comes next.

“Good. You came.” He set the blade down on the antistatic mat. “I wanted to show you the proper technique for tape maintenance. The heads on the field recorder build oxide faster than the manual suggests, and if you’re going to be running the hydrophone rig through the winter, you need to know how to keep your stock clean.”

Ingrid pulled a stool from under the bench and sat. The workshop smelled of ozone and solder, the sharp chemical tang of a space where electronics lived and died. A portable cassette deck sat between them on the bench, its housing removed, a test tape threaded through the heads. Green VU meters pulsed faintly with no signal, their needles twitching like small, nervous animals.

“You could have left a note,” she said. “You didn’t need to drag me down here for a lesson.”

“I could have.” He picked up a short length of quarter-inch tape, already cut, already prepared. “But this is better shown than described. Watch the splice. Most people use too much tape. Too much adhesive. The excess changes the spacing between the heads and creates a dropout. You hear it as a click, but what it actually is, that’s a gap in the magnetic signal. A dead spot.”

He placed the tape in the splicing block. His hands moved with the practiced ease of someone who had done this thousands of times. The blade came down at a precise forty-five-degree angle. He joined the two ends with a strip of splicing tape no wider than two millimeters, pressing it flat with his thumbnail. The join was nearly invisible.

Ingrid leaned forward. She could see the splice, but only because she was looking for it. The cut was clean, the angle exact, the adhesive strip trimmed to a width that would pass through the playback heads without catching. Under the sterile fluorescent light, the join was almost beautiful. Almost.

“May I?” She reached for the tape before he answered. He let it go.

She held the strip up to the light. Turned it. The adhesive had been applied with a specific pressure, leaving a faint, characteristic bowing at the edges where his thumbnail had pressed. The bowing was slight. You would never notice it unless you had spent hours staring at a spectrogram of a gap in a hydrophone recording, tracing the magnetic bias signature of the splice that had created it.

The bias signature. The jump in the counter log. The eleven minutes missing from the night Solveig drowned.

“You use a diagonal cut,” she said. “Forty-five degrees. And you trim the adhesive with your thumbnail, not a tool, which leaves a pressure deformation along the leading edge. The deformation is measurable. It shows up in the magnetic bias as a specific waveform. A signature.”

Finn said nothing.

“The gap in the hydrophone tape from August fourteenth has that signature. I’ve been staring at it for three weeks. I can describe the waveform from memory.” She set the tape down on the bench between them. “The spectrogram shows a clean cut, not a degradation. That’s an edit. Your edit.”

The fluorescents hummed above them. The VU meters pulsed once, twice, settling back to zero. Finn’s hands, which had been so steady through the demonstration, went still on the bench. Not resting. Frozen.

He looked at the tape between them. Then at the splicing block. Then at the blade. His jaw worked, and for a moment she thought he would deny it, that he would fall back on the careful blankness he had worn when she walked in.

Instead, he said, “She asked me to teach her this.”

The air in the workshop changed. The hum of the fluorescents seemed louder, the ozone sharper.

“The week before she died. She came to me and asked me to show her how to splice tape. How to make a join that would pass a bias check. I taught her.” His voice was flat, stripped of its usual careful cadence. “I didn’t ask why. She was Solveig. She always had reasons. Good ones. I didn’t ask.”

Ingrid’s fingers went cold. The sensation spread from her hands up through her wrists, and she pressed her palms flat against the bench to stop them from shaking. The metal was cold, too. Everything in this room was cold.

“You edited the tape,” she said. “The morning after she drowned. Erik told you to do it.”

Finn closed his eyes. When he opened them, the blankness was gone. What replaced it was worse: relief. The expression of a man who has been carrying something heavy for a very long time and has finally been told he can set it down.

“Yes.”

The word hung between them, small and enormous. Ingrid stared at the tape on the bench, at the nearly invisible splice with its thumbnail-bowed edges, and she heard the word echoing off the walls of the workshop, off the metal benches and the humming lights and the disassembled tape deck with its surgical exposure.

“Erik came to me at five in the morning. He said Solveig had recorded something on the hydrophone tape that couldn’t get out. He said it was a private conversation. He said it would hurt people. He said I needed to remove it.”

“Eleven minutes.”

“Eleven minutes. I sat at the recording deck and I cut it out and I spliced the ends together and I erased the counter log entry. I did what he told me to do. I did it because he’s the station chief and because I trusted him when he said it was just a private matter.”

Ingrid’s breath came shallow. She could feel the cold bench under her palms, could feel her own pulse in her fingertips. The fluorescents buzzed. The VU meters sat at zero, their needles still.

“What was on the tape?”

“I don’t know. I never listened. Erik told me not to, and I didn’t. I cut blind.”

“You cut eleven minutes from a hydrophone tape on the morning after a woman drowned, and you didn’t listen to what you were removing?”

His jaw tightened. “You don’t understand the noise floor here. You’re listening for ghosts. I did what I was told. I did it because I believed him.”

“Believed what?”

“That it was just a conversation. That it was embarrassing but not criminal. That removing it would protect people.” He paused. “I believed him until I stopped believing him, and by then the tape was already cut, and Solveig was already dead, and there was nothing I could do.”

Ingrid pulled her hands off the bench. She sat back on the stool. The room seemed to tilt, and she gripped the edge of the seat to steady herself. She had come here looking for a killer. She had spent weeks building a theory: someone had erased the tape to hide the sound of a murder, and the someone who had erased it was complicit in Solveig’s death. The spectrogram was her proof. The counter log was her witness. The evidence was clean and technical and damning.

And now Finn sat across from her, shaking, and what he had given her was not a confession to murder. A confession to obedience.

“What was on the tape, Finn? What did Erik need to hide?”

Finn reached for the portable cassette deck. His hand hovered over the stop button. The green VU meter light caught his fingers, and she watched them tremble. Not a twitch. A sustained, visible tremor, the kind that comes from holding something too tightly for too long. His index finger hung above the stop button, the pad of his fingertip just above the plastic, close enough that the light pooled beneath it, casting a green crescent on the cuticle. He held that position. His knuckle whitened. The tremor moved from his finger into his wrist, and still he held it there, as though the act of pressing that button and the act of refusing to press it were two doors leading to two different rooms, and he could not choose.

The tremor moved into his hand. The green light shifted on his skin.

Then he pressed the button. The deck clicked. The reels stopped.

“Money,” he said. “Station funds. Supply allocations. Fuel reserves. Solveig found discrepancies in the ledger. Diesel that was ordered and paid for but never received. Equipment that was logged as received but never unpacked. She went through the accounts line by line. She found the gaps.”

Ingrid’s vision narrowed. The edges of the workshop blurred, and all she could see clearly was Finn’s face under the sterile light, the hollows of his eyes, the tight line of his mouth.

“She confronted Erik. She gave him a deadline. She told him she would report the discrepancies to the institute if he didn’t explain them. She had it on the tape. She had recorded the conversation she’d had with Erik in the instrument bay, and on that tape she named the amounts, the dates, the missing items. She was blackmailing him. She told him she would send the tape to the mainland if he didn’t return the funds.”

The word landed. Blackmail. Not Ingrid’s word. Finn’s word. He said it plainly, without flinching, and the word filled the workshop the way the ozone did, sharp and chemical and impossible to ignore.

Solveig. Blackmailing Erik.

Ingrid’s throat closed. She could not swallow. The cold had spread from her hands into her chest, into her ribs, and her breathing came in short, tight bursts that fogged in the chilled air. She could see her own breath. She could see the tape on the bench. She could see the splicing block and the razor blade and the nearly invisible join with its thumbnail-bowed edges, and she could see Solveig’s face, and the two images would not merge.

“She was extorting him,” Ingrid said. The words came out hoarse, scraped. “She was using the tape as leverage.”

“Yes.”

“For money she believed he had stolen.”

“For funds she believed he had misappropriated. Yes. She gave him a week. She said she would send the tape to the institute director. She said she would make him pay.”

Make them pay.

Ingrid’s hands went to her face. She pressed her fingers against her cheekbones, hard, as if she could hold the shape of her skull together. The phrase from Solveig’s journal. The phrase she had read as righteous anger, as a victim’s determination to see justice done. Make them pay. She had carried those words like a talisman, like proof that her sister had been fighting the right fight, that her sister had been the moral center of this station, that her sister had been a woman wronged.

And now the same words sat in her ears with a different weight. A blackmailer’s ultimatum. A threat dressed in the language of principle.

“I cut the tape because Erik told me to,” Finn said. “He said the conversation would destroy the station. He said the funding discrepancies were a misunderstanding, that he could explain them, that he just needed time. He said if the tape reached the mainland, the institute would shut them down. Everyone would lose their positions. Months of research, gone. He said I had to remove it.”

“And you believed him.”

“I believed him for exactly long enough to pick up the razor. After that, it didn’t matter what I believed. The tape was cut. Solveig was dead. And I was the one who had made the silence.”

The fluorescents hummed. The VU meters sat at zero. The workshop was very quiet.

“You’re telling me the edit was about blackmail. Not about the drowning.”

“I’m telling you what I know. I edited the tape to hide the blackmail. I did not kill Solveig. I did not push her into the water. I did not see her that night. I cut a tape. That is what I did.”

“That is what you did.”

“Yes.”

Ingrid stood. The stool scraped against the floor. She picked up the demo tape from the bench and held it in front of her, the splice catching the light.

“This splice. Your signature. It’s on the hydrophone tape from August fourteenth. I can prove it. I can show the bias deformation to anyone who understands magnetic recording. Your work, and you’ve admitted it’s your work, and Erik ordered it.”

Finn looked at her. The relief was still there, but underneath it, something else. Fear.

“He’ll know I told you.”

“Yes.”

“He’ll say I’m lying. He’ll say I edited the tape on my own. He’ll say I had my own reasons.”

“Did you?”

“No. But that won’t matter. It never matters what’s true. It matters what people believe.”

Ingrid set the tape down. She turned and walked to the door. Her hand found the handle. The metal was warm from the compressor behind the wall.

“If it wasn’t murder,” she said, “then what was it? She fell? She slipped? She went swimming in the dark and drowned by accident, and the only crime anyone committed was erasing evidence of blackmail?”

Finn’s silence filled the room.

She pulled the door open and stepped into the corridor. The cold hit her like a wall. The corridor lights were dimmer here, and her boots echoed on the metal grating. She walked without seeing the walls, without seeing the doors, without seeing the frost patterns on the portholes. She walked until she reached her room and shut the door behind her and stood in the dark, breathing, breathing, her breath coming in clouds that she could see in the light from the hallway under the door.

She found the journal by feel. The cover was familiar under her fingers, the leather soft and cold. She carried it to the desk and turned on the lamp. The pages were dry and stiff, and they made a sound like dry leaves when she turned them. She found the entry. The one from August ninth. Five days before the drowning.

He thinks I don’t see the numbers. He thinks I’m here to play in the water and look at ice. But I see the numbers, and I see what they mean, and I will make them pay.

Ingrid traced the words with her fingernail. The letters pressed into the page, leaving faint impressions on the sheet beneath. She had read this entry a dozen times. She had read it as evidence of Solveig’s courage, of her sister’s refusal to look away from corruption. She had read it as a mission statement.

She read it now and heard a different voice. A voice calculating leverage. A voice counting days until a deadline expired. A voice that understood the practical power of a tape recording in the hands of someone willing to use it.

Make them pay.

Her fingernail stopped on the period at the end of the sentence. The page was cold under her hand. The lamp buzzed. The room smelled of old paper and cold metal and the sea.

If it wasn’t murder, then what? An accident that happened to silence the one person who held proof of Erik’s theft? A drowning that arrived at the most convenient possible moment for the man she was threatening? A death that required no crime at all, only a pair of scissors and a razor blade and a colleague willing to cut blind?

Ingrid closed the journal. She pressed her palm flat against the cover. The leather was warming under her hand, drawing heat from her skin.

She held a confession. She held proof of the edit, proof of who ordered it, proof of why. She held the answer to the question that had driven her across the Arctic Ocean to this frozen station.

And the answer was not an answer. The answer was a door that opened onto another door. Behind the blackmail, behind the theft, behind the eleven minutes of silence, there was still the question of what happened on the water that night. What Solveig heard. What Solveig said. What sound existed in those missing minutes that someone needed gone so badly they cut it out of the record and hoped the sea would take the rest.

The journal lay on the desk. The lamp buzzed. Outside the porthole, the dark pressed against the glass.
