Chapter 18

The Final Argument

────────────────────────────────────────

Markus’s broken voice still echoed in Ingrid’s skull when she shouldered open the bio-lab door. Anya was the last person to see Solveig alive. They were arguing. The words had come out of Markus like bone fragments, jagged and wet with grief, and they had rearranged every assumption Ingrid carried into this final confrontation.

The lab was narrow and cluttered. Reagent bottles crowded the back shelf in no particular order, their labels faded to illegibility. A freezer unit hummed in the corner, its compressor cycling with a mechanical patience that made the silence between heartbeats feel enormous. A single overhead fluorescent tube cast everything in a flat, bloodless glow. The smell of ozone hung in the air, layered over stale coffee that had been sitting in a beaker on the edge of the bench for hours, its surface filmed with a skin of oil. Anya stood with her back to the door, her shoulders pulled tight beneath her sweater, sorting sample vials with the kind of mechanical focus that meant she had heard the door and was choosing not to turn around.

Ingrid set the diesel ledger on the bench between them. The sound was small, deliberate. A soft thud of paper on steel.

Anya’s hands stilled on the vial she was holding. She did not turn.

“You told me you were in your quarters that night,” Ingrid said. “From twenty-two hundred until the alarm at zero-five-thirty. That was your statement. I wrote it down, you signed it.”

“I remember what I said.” Anya’s voice carried its usual precision, each word placed like a specimen on a slide. But something underneath the polish had thinned. She turned now, and her gaze dropped to the open ledger page, then held there.

Ingrid pressed her finger to the entry. The ink was smeared at the margin where someone had pressed too hard. “Generator shed, activated twenty-three-forty. Deactivated zero-one-twelve. Fuel consumption logged manually. Your initials are on the fuel draw. A-P.” She let the letters sit in the air between them. “That’s you, Anya. Not Erik. Not Markus. You.”

For three seconds, the freezer compressor was the only sound in the room.

Anya’s jaw tightened. A vein at her temple pulsed visibly. “The fuel log is wrong. Anyone can initial anything.”

“The spectrogram shows a clean cut, not a degradation. That’s an edit.” Ingrid kept her voice level. She had learned to do that from Solveig, actually. Hold the tone flat. Let the evidence do the rising. “Someone edited the hydrophone tape during those same minutes. The same window. The generator shed is the only space on this station with independent power routing and no camera coverage. And you were there.”

“You are making a connection that does not exist.”

“Then explain the fuel log.”

Anya picked up a pipette from the rack. Set it down. Picked it up again. Her knuckles whitened around the slender glass. “I went to run a diagnostic on the backup generator. The primary was showing fluctuation. I did not want to log it through the main system because Erik would have—“She stopped. Her throat moved.

“Erik would have what?”

Ingrid watched the calculation happen behind Anya’s eyes. The woman was weighing which lie to sacrifice. Ingrid had seen that look before, on Erik’s face, when he had sat across from her in the radio room and admitted to the embezzlement. His hands had been shaking the same way. He had taken station funds, routed them through supply invoices, covered the gaps with doctored requisitions. When she had cornered him with the financials, he had crumbled into a confession about the money. But the terror in his eyes had been disproportionate.

Someone had sent him an anonymous note. They knew about the theft. They had leveraged that knowledge to force his hand. They had made him edit the tape. Erik had described the note’s handwriting as deliberate, unhurried. The penmanship of someone who understood power.

He would not say who. He could not say who. The fear in his voice when he refused had been animal, primal, the kind of fear that came from knowing the threat was close and listening.

Anya was staring at the ledger entry. Her lips were pressed thin.

“You were not running a diagnostic,” Ingrid said. She did not raise her voice. “You were in that shed for over ninety minutes. Nobody runs a diagnostic for ninety minutes. And you did not log it through the main system because you did not want a record.”

Anya set the pipette down a third time. Her fingers were trembling now, a fine vibration that traveled up through her wrists. She gripped the edge of the bench. Her shoulders curved inward.

“I was there,” she said.

The freezer cycled off. In the sudden quiet, the absence of its hum felt like a held breath.

“I was there because I was meeting Solveig.”

Ingrid’s hand was still on the ledger. She felt the paper’s texture under her fingertips, the slight ridge where the page had been creased and refolded. She did not move.

“She came to me that afternoon,” Anya said. Her voice had lost its smoothness. The words came out in uneven bursts, like something being pulled from her chest in fragments. “She said she had found discrepancies in her data set. Three months of acoustic profiling, corrupted. Calibrated wrong. She thought it was an equipment failure. She came to me because I am the bioacoustics lead. She wanted my help diagnosing the error.”

“But it was not an equipment failure.”

Anya’s chin dropped. She stared at the vial rack on the bench, the row of identical glass tubes catching the overhead light. “No. It was not.”

“What was it?”

“I recalibrated her hydrophone array. I adjusted the baseline parameters. I introduced a systematic offset into her frequency filters.” Anya spoke to the vials, not to Ingrid. “It would not show up in a standard diagnostic. The data would look clean. But every measurement she took for three months would be shifted by a factor that made her spectral analysis worthless.”

Ingrid felt the cold from the freezer unit reaching her across the narrow space. The overhead light buzzed, a faint electrical whine.

“Why?”

Anya’s hand came up and pressed flat against her sternum. Her breathing had become shallow. “Because she was leaving. She had the position at the institute. She had the recognition, the publications, the reputation. And I had been here for six years running the same baseline surveys, processing the same data, watching her take every opportunity that should have been mine.”

The words accelerated, tumbling over each other. “The jealousy was not just professional. I hated her for leaving me behind. She got the credit for work I had contributed to. She stood on stages and presented findings from data I had helped collect. And then she was going to walk away to a better position while I stayed here, in this box, in the cold, running the same routines.

So I made sure her final data set was useless. So she could not publish. So she would have nothing to take with her.”

The confession filled the small room like a pressure change. Anya’s shoulders were shaking. She gripped the bench edge with both hands now, her body curved forward as though bracing against a wave.

“She found it,” Anya continued, her voice cracking along the middle of the word. “That night. She came to me and she was furious. She told me she knew. She said she had spent two days isolating the offset and tracing it back to my calibration override. She had proof. She had saved the original parameters before I altered them, and she had the revision log showing my access credentials.”

“So she confronted you. In the generator shed.”

“We met there because she did not want anyone to overhear. She did not want to report it through official channels. Not yet. She wanted to hear me say it first.” Anya’s voice broke apart on the final word. Her knees buckled. She caught herself against the bench, her hip striking the steel edge, and she slid downward until she was half-sitting, half-leaning against the cabinet below. Her hands covered her face. The sound she made was something drier than a sob, more internal, a sound like something structural giving way.

Ingrid watched her. The fluorescent light caught the ink stains on Anya’s fingers where she had gripped the ledger pages. Her shoulders heaved twice, then went still.

“What happened in that shed?” Ingrid asked.

Anya lowered her hands. Her face was drawn, pale under the cold light. “We argued. For forty minutes. She called me a coward. She called me a thief. She was right about both.” Her voice was barely audible now. “She said she was going to report the sabotage to the station director and to the institute review board. She said it would end my career. I begged her not to. I offered to correct the data, to publish a correction, to accept whatever sanction she wanted. She told me it was too late for that. The damage was done.”

“And then?”

“She left.” Anya’s hands dropped to her lap. They were shaking, a visible tremor that she could not control. “She turned around and she walked out of the shed. She was alive. She was angry. She was walking fast, back toward the main building. I stayed in the shed because I could not move. I sat on the floor and I could not move for a long time.”

Ingrid’s grip on the ledger had loosened. She realized her fingers had been pressing into the paper hard enough to leave marks. She straightened.

“Which direction did she go?”

“Toward the main corridor. But she stopped.” Anya’s eyes widened. She grabbed Ingrid’s wrist. Her fingers were cold, clammy, the grip surprisingly strong. “She stopped at the junction. I saw her through the shed window. She stood there for a moment, and then she turned. She did not go back toward the residential wing. She turned toward the generator annex.”

Ingrid felt the shift in the air. Not a physical change. A structural one. The timeline she had built in her mind, the careful sequence of events that placed Anya as the last contact, the final argument, the endpoint, had just cracked open.

“The annex,” Ingrid repeated.

“She was meeting someone.” Anya’s voice was raw, scraped clean of its usual composure. “She told me during the fight. She said, ‘At least someone on this station is willing to help me.’ I asked her what she meant and she would not explain. She just said she had an arrangement. Someone was going to help her with something. She was supposed to meet them after she talked to me.”

“Who?”

“I do not know.” Anya’s grip tightened on Ingrid’s wrist. “I swear to you. She was alive when she left me. She was walking toward the dark. I swear it.”

Ingrid looked down at Anya’s hand on her wrist. The woman’s fingers were stained with ink and trembling. The overhead light made her face look hollowed, the bones visible beneath the skin. She had stopped defending herself. There was nothing left to defend.

Ingrid pulled her wrist free gently. She picked up the ledger. She walked out.

The corridor was empty. The station’s generators hummed through the walls, a low, constant vibration that she felt in the soles of her boots. The overhead lights here were dimmer, spaced further apart, casting pools of yellow on the metal floor. She leaned against the cold wall and closed her eyes.

The pieces rearranged themselves. Erik had embezzled station funds. Someone had discovered the theft and sent him an anonymous note. They had blackmailed him into editing the tape, cutting eleven minutes from the hydrophone recording. Erik had described the note’s tone as controlled, unhurried. The handwriting of someone who understood leverage. He had been terrified. Not of the exposure. Of the person behind it. He would not name them. He could not.

Solveig had discovered the data sabotage. She had confronted Anya. She had proof. But she had also discovered something else. She had an arrangement. Someone on the station was going to help her with something. She had been heading to meet them at the generator annex when she died.

Erik’s blackmailer had known about the embezzlement. Had they known about the sabotage too? Had Solveig found the same person? Had she walked into the generator shed to meet the one person on this station who understood enough about all of it to be dangerous?

The metal wall was cold against her back. The generator hummed through the floor. Somewhere in the station, a door closed with a distant, hollow sound.

Ingrid opened her eyes. The corridor stretched ahead of her, dim and empty. At its end, the junction where Solveig had turned. The generator annex lay on the other side of that turn, a low outbuilding bolted to the main structure, accessible through a covered walkway. The one place on the station where you could have a conversation that no one would hear. No cameras. Independent power. No one logged its use unless they chose to.

Solveig had been alive when she left Anya. She had been alive and walking toward someone. The eleven minutes on the tape had been cut to hide whatever happened in that window. Erik had been forced to make the cut. Someone else had made the arrangement.

The question was not who had the opportunity. Everyone on this station had access to that shed. The question was who Solveig trusted enough to meet there, alone, at midnight. And what that person needed her to help them with.

Ingrid pushed herself off the wall. The metal was cold where her body had pressed against it. She could feel the absence of warmth on her back as she started walking toward the junction.
