Chapter 9

The Tape Teacher

────────────────────────────────────────

The workshop door was half-open, and through it Ingrid could see Finn Agaard’s hands buried to the wrists inside the open chassis of the reel-to-reel recorder, his fingers moving with the unhurried certainty of a surgeon who trusts the architecture of what he’s touching. The bench lamp threw a cone of amber light across the work surface, catching the pale curl of a tape splice he’d left resting on a square of felt, the join so clean it was nearly invisible. The room smelled of hot solder and ozone, that sharp mineral tang that clung to the station’s electronics bay like a second atmosphere, and beneath it the fainter scent of machine oil and the cold steel walls that never quite lost their bite even with the heating circuits running full.

Ingrid paused in the doorway. Her fingers still trembled faintly from the hours hunched over the spectrogram — the same tremor that had started in her thumbs and worked its way up through her wrists into the tight muscles of her forearms. She flexed her right hand, watching Finn’s fingers withdraw a small flathead screwdriver from the recorder’s transport mechanism, setting it down on the felt pad beside the splice with a soft click. His movements were precise. Confident. The kind of hands that knew where every gear sat, where every tension spring lived, where the tape head aligned against the guide pins.

She had come here for a technical answer. The ‘how’ of the eleven-minute gap was answered: a clean edit, a splice made by someone who knew the equipment. But the ‘who’ and the ‘why’ had only grown larger and more dangerous in the cold hours since she’d confirmed it. And Finn was the station’s technical resource. Finn was the person she trusted.

She knocked once on the metal frame of the door. Finn looked up, and his face registered mild surprise before settling into the careful professional cordiality she recognized from months of working alongside him at the station.

“Ingrid.” He wiped his hands on a rag that hung from his belt. “You’re up early. Or late. I’ve lost track of which.”

“Early, I think.” She stepped inside, letting the door drift shut behind her. The latch caught with a soft magnetic thud. They were alone. “I need to ask you something. About the tapes.”

Finn set the rag down and turned to face her fully, leaning one hip against the workbench. The splice on the felt pad caught the light between them, a clean diagonal join held with specialized splicing tape, the kind that required a cutting block and a steady hand and knowledge of where the magnetic oxide could be disturbed and where it couldn’t.

“What about them?”

Ingrid chose her words carefully. She needed this to sound like a technical inquiry, not an interrogation. “The gap I showed you. The eleven minutes. I’ve been trying to understand the method. What kind of skill set would someone need to make a cut that clean on a hydrophone tape without leaving a spectrographic signature.”

Finn’s expression didn’t change. He glanced down at the splice on the felt, then back at her. “You already know the answer. You showed me the spectrogram. That’s a razor splice on quarter-inch stock, made on a proper cutting block. Anyone with basic tape-handling training could do it.”

“Basic training.” She held his gaze. “How basic?”

Finn picked up the splice between his thumb and forefinger, holding it to the bench lamp. The light passed through the magnetic tape at the join, showing the thin line of splicing adhesive. His hand was steady. Perfectly steady. “Simple,” he said, the word landing like a sigh or a shrug, she couldn’t tell which. “It’s not complicated. You mark the tape at the right counter position, you align it on the block, you cut both ends at forty-five degrees, and you join them. The tape head reads the splice as continuous signal. There’s a tiny dropout — maybe a quarter second — but on a hydrophone recording at that sampling rate, it vanishes into the noise floor.”

“You make it sound simple.”

“It is simple. The hard part isn’t the cut. The hard part is knowing where to cut and having the nerve to commit to it.” He set the splice back on the felt pad. “Why?”

Ingrid watched his hands. The way his fingers had positioned the tape on the block, index finger holding tension, thumb guiding the alignment, was the same motion she’d seen him perform a hundred times maintaining the station’s recording equipment. Muscle memory. Deep, practiced, automatic. The kind of expertise that came from years of repetition.

“Solveig,” she said. “Did she know how to do this?”

The question landed in the ozone-scented air between them, and for a moment the only sound was the hum of the recorder’s warming filaments and the distant thrum of the generator through the station’s steel bones. Finn’s hand paused over the felt pad, fingers still extended, the gesture frozen in the amber light like a held breath.

Then he moved. He reached behind him to a shelf bolted to the wall and pulled down a slim binder, dark blue, station-issued, with a white label on the spine that read Audio Recording Systems: Maintenance and Editing Procedures.

“Of course she did,” Finn said. The words came out casually, almost with a note of pride, as though he were recalling a student who’d done well on an exam. “I taught her. She was assigned to hydrophone monitoring for three months before you arrived, and part of the orientation is basic tape maintenance. Splicing, leader tape application, counter alignment. I walked her through it myself.”

He held the binder out to Ingrid. She took it. The cover was worn at the corners, the binding cracked from use. Inside, she could see tabbed sections with diagrams of tape transport mechanisms, cutting block alignment, splice angle specifications.

“When was this?” Her voice was even. She was proud of that.

“Her first week. Maybe second. Standard procedure.” Finn turned back to the open recorder, reaching for the screwdriver again. “She was quick. Good hands. Better than most who come through here.” He paused, and something shifted behind his eyes, not guilt, exactly, but a shadow of recollection. “She asked a lot of questions. Wanted to understand the theory behind it, not just the steps. I gave her the manual so she could study on her own time.”

Ingrid’s fingers tightened on the binder’s spine. She could feel the cracked laminate under her thumb, the slight give of the binding. I gave her the manual. The words settled into her chest like a stone dropped through dark water.

“So the knowledge was documented,” she said slowly. “Written down. Accessible.”

Finn nodded, not looking up from the recorder. “It’s in there. Section four, I think. Splicing procedures. Anyone with access to the technical library could read it. Why?”

“Just trying to understand the full picture.” She closed the binder and held it against her sternum. “Did you teach anyone else? While Solveig was here, or after?”

“After?” Finn’s hands stilled on the screwdriver. He turned to look at her directly, and for the first time she saw something careful and guarded slide behind his expression. “No. No one else needed it. The hydrophone post was hers. Then it was yours.”

The silence between them filled with the hum of the electronics, the faint tick of cooling solder. Ingrid nodded once, thanked him for his time, and walked out of the workshop carrying the binder against her chest like a shield.

The corridor back to her bunk was narrow and cold, the steel walls radiating the perpetual chill of the Svalbard winter pressing against the station’s skin. Overhead lights buzzed in their housings, casting a flat white glare that made everything look like a photograph of itself. She passed the closed door of the common room, the sealed hatch to the generator bay, and the small alcove where the station’s reference shelf held tide tables, weather logs, and the diesel consumption ledger in a row of identical blue binders.

She didn’t stop. Not yet. She needed to be alone with what she’d just learned, needed to hold it up to the light of the diesel ledger clue and see what shape it made.

Her bunk was a narrow rectangle of space: bed, desk, vent. The air from the overhead duct was cold enough to raise the hair on her forearms. She sat on the edge of the mattress, set the technical manual beside her, and pulled the diesel ledger from under her pillow where she’d hidden it days ago.

The pages were copies, not originals. She’d made them on the station’s photocopier during a late shift when no one was watching, feeding each page through the machine with the door propped shut to muffle the mechanism’s whine. The entries for the week of March 12-18 were spread across two sheets, and she found the entry she’d flagged with a penciled dot in the margin: March 14, 23: 40, Generator test, 18.4 liters.

Eighteen point four liters. She had stared at this number before, in the small hours after confirming the spectrogram, and she’d seen the anomaly then: a test logged for a time when the generator should have been running on its normal cycle, consuming fuel at the baseline rate of roughly nine liters per hour. A test that should have shown zero additional consumption showed 18.4 liters, an excess of 9.4 liters that couldn’t be explained by routine maintenance.

But that was before. Before Finn’s casual admission in the workshop. Before she’d watched his hands hold a tape splice with the unconscious precision of someone who’d performed the motion a thousand times. Before she’d held the technical manual in her arms and understood that the knowledge to create the eleven-minute gap wasn’t a rare specialty. It was taught, and the man who taught it was sleeping two doors down from her bunk every night.

She spread the ledger copies on the desk and smoothed them flat. Her hands had stopped trembling. The tremor was gone, replaced by something colder and more focused, a clarity that sat in her stomach like ice water.

The 18.4-liter entry was logged at 23: 40. The hydrophone tape’s counter placed the eleven-minute gap starting at approximately 23: 32, ending at 23: 43. The overlap was almost exact. Whoever had edited the tape had done so during a window when the generator log recorded a test that consumed 9.4 liters more than it should have, enough fuel to run a portable tape deck and a set of headphones in a space away from the station’s main recording bay. Away from the hydrophone’s primary capture system. Away from where anyone might walk in and see what was being done.

Ingrid closed her eyes. She’d read this overlap before. She’d noted it, flagged it, filed it as a coincidence worth pursuing. But she’d read it with Finn as a trusted ally — the helpful technician who maintained the equipment, who answered her questions, who showed her how the systems worked with patient good humor. In that reading, the diesel anomaly was evidence pointing toward an unknown person who had used the generator test as cover for the edit.

Now the reading collapsed.

Finn had taught Solveig how to splice tape. Finn had given her the manual. Finn was the person at this station with the deepest knowledge of the recording systems, the most practiced hands, the clearest understanding of where a cut would be invisible on a spectrogram and where it would show. His helpfulness over the past weeks, the patient explanations, the technical advice, the willingness to walk her through the station’s systems, now carried a different weight. Not generosity. Demonstration. Each time he’d shown her something about the equipment, he’d also been showing her what he could do with it. What he had done with it, perhaps. The knowledge of the splice, the access to the cutting block, the familiarity with the hydrophone tape’s counter positions, all of it flowed through his hands.

She opened the technical manual to section four. The pages were dense with diagrams: cutting block alignment, splice angle specifications, tension calibration. In the margin of one page, in pencil, in handwriting she recognized with a jolt as Solveig’s, the same looping S, the same crossed t-bar, there was a small note: ask F about counter reset timing.

Ingrid stared at the note until the letters blurred. Ask F. Solveig had written questions in the manual. She’d studied it. She’d been curious, engaged, exactly as Finn described. And Finn had answered her questions, had walked her through the procedures, had given her the knowledge that someone had later used to erase eleven minutes of audio from the night Solveig drowned.

The question that coiled in Ingrid’s chest wasn’t whether Finn had the skill. That was answered. The question was whether the skill had flowed in only one direction, from teacher to student, or whether the teacher had kept the darker application for himself. The person who taught the victim could also be the one who knew how to cover up a crime.

She closed the manual and sat very still in the cold air from the vent, listening to the hum of the station around her. The generator’s distant pulse. The creak of thermal contraction in the walls. The faint, almost subliminal hiss of the hydrophone feed running through the conduits, the sound of the sea beneath the ice, constant and indifferent.

Then, from the corridor outside her door, voices.

She couldn’t make out the content. Fragments reached her: a sharp consonant, a rising inflection that might have been a question, Finn’s reply lower and slower, as if calming or deflecting. Then a pause. Then Erik again, and this time the tone was unmistakable: the strained, controlled urgency of someone who needed something understood and didn’t want it said louder.

She couldn’t make out the content. Fragments reached her — a sharp consonant, a rising inflection that might have been a question, Finn’s reply lower and slower, as if calming or deflecting. Then a pause. Then Erik again, and this time the tone was unmistakable: the strained, controlled urgency of someone who needed something understood and didn’t want it said louder.

The exchange lasted perhaps thirty seconds. Then footsteps, two sets, moving in opposite directions down the corridor. The magnetic latch vibrated faintly in its frame as the pressure differential shifted with their passing.

Ingrid sat motionless on the bunk, the diesel ledger copies spread before her, the technical manual open to her sister’s handwriting in the margin. Her breath was shallow and controlled. Every past interaction with Finn, every helpful explanation, every patient demonstration, every casual technical tip, now refracted through a new lens. His hands on the tape. His voice in the corridor. His pride in teaching Solveig. The manual he’d given her. The knowledge he’d shared.

The person who taught the victim could also be the one who knew how to cover up a crime.

Ingrid picked up the diesel ledger copies and folded them once, twice, and slid them back beneath her pillow. She placed the technical manual on the desk, spine out, section four’s tab visible. Then she lay back on the bunk and stared at the ceiling, where the overhead light made a small bright circle on the metal that didn’t waver, didn’t flicker, didn’t change at all.

She would need to check the station’s shared records. The technical library. The access logs for the equipment bay. If others had used the manual, if others had signed out the cutting block, if others had been in the workshop on the nights surrounding March 14, there would be records. The station ran on procedure. Erik Larsen’s procedure. And procedure, for all its rigidity, left traces.

The cold from the vent touched her face. She closed her eyes and listened to the sea beneath the ice, and the sea said nothing, as it had said nothing on the night her sister died, as it would say nothing until someone forced the truth out into the open air where it could no longer be spliced away.
