Chapter 7

A Voice in the Static

────────────────────────────────────────

The sound lab is dark except for the pulsing green trace of the audio analyzer, painting ghosts on the screen. Ingrid had killed the overhead fluorescents an hour ago, maybe longer. The strip of masking tape she’d placed over the power switch had stuck fast, and she hadn’t bothered to peel it back. The dark made it easier. The dark made the headphones a country, and she its only citizen, adrift in a landscape of hiss and pop and the deep, tidal breathing of the sea.

The tape moved. She could feel it through the bench, a faint mechanical tremor in the reel spindles, and the sound through the headphones was the sound of the Arctic at night. Pressure groans. Ice cracking in patterns that seemed almost linguistic. The hydrophone sat in thirty meters of water off the station’s eastern shelf, and it heard everything the sea had to say.

Everything except the eleven minutes that had been cut from this tape on the night Solveig drowned.

Ingrid reached forward and pressed stop. The reel halted. She pulled the headphones down around her neck and sat in the residual hum of the amplifier, breathing through her mouth. The archive had confirmed it. The overlap she’d found, that razor-thin redundancy between the duty log and the tape’s internal timestamp, was too small to confront anyone with. A fraction of a second. The kind of thing you could dismiss as a mechanical artifact, a spindle lag, anything. But it proved the lie. Someone had stopped this tape, spliced or recorded over the gap, and restarted it. The continuity was false. She was not investigating a death. She was investigating a conspiracy.

She lifted the headphones back over her ears. Her fingers found the shuttle wheel, and she rewound.

The tape counter read 23: 47: 12. The gap began at 23: 52: 00, according to the station’s official incident report. Five minutes of audio remained before the silence. Five minutes of whatever had happened in this room, or near this hydrophone, or on the ice, in the hour before Solveig went into the water.

She had listened to those five minutes twice already. Both times, she’d heard nothing but the sea. But Ingrid was an acoustic engineer, not a casual listener. The sea was not silence. The sea was a signal, and a signal could be subtracted.

She pulled the noise reduction unit across the bench toward her and ran a cable from the tape deck’s line out to the unit’s input. The unit was a rack-mounted box with three knobs and a row of amber LEDs that glowed when the circuit engaged. She’d calibrated it this morning against a known sample of Arctic seawater noise, the same frequency profile the hydrophone would have picked up on a calm night in June. If there was a voice beneath the water sound, the filter would strip the sea away and leave the voice behind.

She set the tape counter to 23: 47: 00 and pressed play.

The hiss filled her head. She dialed the first knob, the low-pass threshold, until the deepest pressure groans dropped out. The sound thinned. She adjusted the second knob, the narrow-band notch, targeting the fifty-hertz band where ice cracking dominated. The cracking faded. What remained was a thinner, reedy sound, like wind across a metallic surface.

She dialed the third knob. The high-pass gate, sweeping upward, cutting everything below two hundred hertz. The reedy wind vanished. What was left was almost nothing. A faint, granular texture, like sand on glass.

She leaned closer to the speakers. The analyzer’s green trace shrank to a thin, nervous line, barely pulsing.

Then it jumped.

A shape. A cluster of frequencies, between three hundred and eight hundred hertz, the band where human speech lived. It lasted half a second, maybe less, and then it was gone.

Ingrid stopped the tape. Her heart was a drum in her ears, and she pressed her palms flat on the bench to steady herself. She reversed the tape two seconds and played it again.

The shape returned. A burst of formant energy, unmistakably human, swallowed by the noise floor almost as soon as it appeared. She could not make out words. Not yet. But the sea did not produce formants. Only throats did.

She adjusted the notch filter, narrowing it, carving deeper into the fifty-hertz band, and widened the high-pass gate to one hundred and fifty hertz. The granular texture sharpened. She played the segment again.

A voice. Male. Distorted, compressed by the layers of noise reduction, but a voice. She could hear the cadence of speech, the rise and fall of a sentence, though the words themselves were still buried.

She closed her eyes. She had been in this lab for three days. She had eaten at the same table as four men, slept in the bunk her sister had slept in, walked the corridors Solveig had walked. She had done all of it with the cold certainty that the gap in the tape was deliberate. Now she was hearing the first proof that something had been recorded there, something someone had needed to erase.

The tape played on. The male voice came again, longer this time, a full phrase, and then silence, and then another phrase. The cadence was conversational. Not shouting. Controlled.

She reached for the fine-tune dial and rotated it two degrees clockwise. The noise floor dropped another decibel. The voice surfaced further.

“You shouldn’t have come here alone.”

The words were clear. Not perfect, still threaded with the ghost of the sea, but clear enough that there was no ambiguity. A man’s voice, low, measured, speaking Norwegian with a slight northern accent. The kind of accent you couldn’t fake or lose. Someone who had lived here long enough for the vowels to flatten and the consonants to harden.

Ingrid’s hand moved to the volume knob and then stopped. Her fingers hovered over it. If she turned it up, the voice might solidify into someone she knew — her uncle, or a neighbour, or one of Elin’s old friends.

She turned it up.

The hiss thickened, but the voice held. There was a pause. A breath. Then another voice.

“I know what you did.”

Solveig.

Ingrid’s hand jerked away from the knob as if the metal had burned her. She sat in the dark with the headphones on and the green trace pulsing on the screen and the sound of her sister’s voice filling her skull.

Solveig. Unmistakably. The slight burr of her sibilants, the way she clipped the ends of her sentences, the low, steady pitch that always carried more authority than her small frame suggested. Ingrid had grown up listening to that voice across kitchen tables and telephone lines and late-night conversations in shared bedrooms. She would have known it underwater, in a hurricane, in a crowd of ten thousand.

“I know what you did.”

The sentence landed a second time as Ingrid replayed it. Not grief. Not accusation. Something harder. Certainty. Solveig had known something, and she had said it out loud, and the man had been there to hear it.

Ingrid pressed her palms together and brought them to her mouth. Her breath was hot against her fingers. The air in the lab smelled of hot electronics and the faintly metallic tang of old tape heads. She breathed through it.

The tape was still running.

A gap of silence, two seconds, maybe three. Then a sound that was not a voice. A heavy, percussive thud, low-frequency, a single impact that registered on the analyzer as a sharp peak before dropping back to the noise floor. It could have been a body striking the floor, or a fist striking a table, or a piece of equipment knocked off a shelf. The hydrophone was omnidirectional; it could not locate the source. The thud was simply a thud, and Ingrid could not say whether it was violence or accident.

But the silence that followed was not silence. Erasure. The eleven-minute gap began at that exact point, right after the thud, right after Solveig’s accusation. Whatever had happened next, someone had decided it could not remain on the tape.

Ingrid stopped the reel. The green trace went flat.

She sat in the dark for a long time.

The man’s voice played in her memory on a loop, the same way the tape had played it. “You shouldn’t have come here alone.” Low, measured, northern accent. She tried to match it to the voices she knew at the station.

Erik Larsen was the first she ruled out. She had heard him speak at length during the handover briefing, and his voice was distinctive. He had a pronounced lateral lisp, a sibilant distortion that turned every soft S into a faint whistle. The man on the tape had said “shouldn’t” and “alone” with clean sibilants, no whistle, no distortion. The voice was not Erik’s.

That left three.

She rewound to the male voice and played it again. Then again. Then a dozen times more. Each repetition stripped another layer of distortion from her perception, the way the noise filter had stripped the sea. The accent was northern but not extreme. The pitch sat in the lower baritone range. The cadence was controlled, deliberate, the speech of someone who thought before he spoke. She could not identify him. Not yet. But she had the voice now, etched into her auditory memory like a groove in wax.

She removed the headphones and set them on the bench. Her ears rang in the sudden silence of the lab. She looked at her hands. The faint silver scar across her left palm caught the green light from the analyzer.

She stood, and her knees ached from sitting. She peeled the masking tape off the light switch but did not turn it on. She left the lab in darkness and walked down the corridor toward the mess hall.

The smell hit her first. Reheated stew, the fat congealed to a film on the surface, and the burnt-coffee bitterness that never left the pot. She had not eaten since morning, and her body registered the smell before her mind registered the room.

Three men sat at the long table. Markus Dahl was closest to the door, his spoon halfway to his mouth. He set it down when she entered.

“Solheim. You look like you’ve been in a cave.”

“Sound lab,” she said. “Acoustic calibration for the new hydrophone array. I lost track of time.”

She collected a tray and joined the table. The stew was lukewarm. She ate without tasting it, her eyes on her plate, while the men talked around her.

Markus was speaking about the ice shelf. His voice was a mid-range tenor, clipped and precise, each word placed like a specimen on a slide. He swallowed his vowels slightly, a habit common to men who had spent years in isolation, talking to equipment more than people. Ingrid listened to the shape of his consonants and compared them, silently, to the voice on the tape.

Not a match. But not a clear mismatch, either. The distortion made certainty impossible.

She ate another spoonful and let her gaze move to the man across the table. He was arguing with Markus about the generator maintenance schedule, his voice louder, rougher, a baritone that boomed in the low-ceilinged room. Ingrid measured his pitch against the recorded voice. Too loud. Too much resonance. The man on the tape had been quiet, controlled.

The third man said almost nothing. He ate with his head down and grunted agreement when the others addressed him. His voice, when it surfaced, was thin and reedy, pitched high. Not the voice on the tape.

Three voices. Three eliminations, two partial, one firm. The man on the tape remained unidentifiable, but he was in this room, or he had been, six months ago.

Markus turned to her. “The calibration. Any anomalies in the new array?”

“None so far. The signal chain is clean. I’m still working through the historical tapes, though. Background noise profiles for comparison.”

“Historical.” Markus picked up his coffee. “You mean the tapes from before your arrival.”

“From before. Yes.”

He drank. His eyes stayed on her for a moment longer than was comfortable, then moved away. “The old hydrophone had a narrow band. Anything recorded on it is compromised.”

“I’m aware. That’s why I’m building a noise profile. To compensate.”

He nodded, apparently satisfied, and returned to his stew. Ingrid ate two more spoonfuls, then set her spoon down.

“I’m not hungry,” she said, though no one had asked. She stood, took her tray to the wash bin, and left the mess hall.

The corridor was cold. The station’s heating system labored against the Arctic night, and the walls wept condensation in the spots where the insulation had thinned. Ingrid walked to her bunk and closed the door and sat on the edge of the bed in the dark.

Her hands were still cold. They had been cold since she’d pressed stop.

One of the four men in this station was the last person to have spoken to her sister alive. She had heard his voice. She had heard Solveig’s accusation. She had heard the thud. And she could not yet match the voice to a face.

But she would. She would sit in that dark lab and listen to those words a hundred more times if she had to, and she would compare every phoneme, every sibilant, every breath pattern, until the voice on the tape had a name.

She pulled Solveig’s sweater tighter around her shoulders. The frayed cuffs brushed her wrists. The wool still carried a ghost of her sister’s scent, or Ingrid imagined it did, the way you imagine a voice in static when you need to hear it.

But this voice was not imagined. Recorded. Real. And somewhere in this station, the man who had spoken those words was breathing the same recycled air she was breathing, sleeping under the same steel roof, waiting for the polar night to end.

Solveig had known what he did. She had said so. And eleven minutes later, she was in the water.

Ingrid pressed her cold hands together and held them there until the trembling stopped.
