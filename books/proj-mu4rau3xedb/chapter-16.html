Chapter 16

Counting Off Silent Hours

────────────────────────────────────────

Erik’s terror had followed Ingrid out of the storage closet, clinging to her like cigarette smoke, and she paused in the corridor with her palm pressed flat against the cold metal wall. The generator’s distant thrum filled the silence, but it did nothing to quiet the question that had taken root the moment Erik’s voice had cracked. If he hadn’t killed Solveig to cover the theft, who did, and why? His fear pointed to something larger than financial ruin, something with teeth. She closed her eyes, forcing her breathing to slow. The real killer was still unknown, still watching, and she had just revealed how much she knew. Foolish. Necessary. She pushed off the wall and walked toward her quarters, her boots striking the grating with deliberate rhythm. The counter log evidence against Markus would not wait for her nerves to settle.

Three ledgers fanned across her desk, each entry a silent witness she was about to force to speak. The duty roster lay open to the week of Solveig’s death, its neat columns a chronicle of routine that now read like a list of alibis. Beside it, the whale migration charts sprawled in blue and white, tracking pod movements that had been her sister’s obsession. And at the center, the tape counter log, its final entry circled in red pencil: 23: 47, ID code M.Dahl, Counter reading 847, 293. The night after the drowning. The night after Finn’s edit. Ingrid’s finger traced the entry, feeling the faint ridge where her own pressure had worn the paper. Markus had accessed the recording deck when he had no reason to be there, and he had never reported what he heard.

She pulled the duty roster closer, her eyes moving down the column for that night. Erik had been on generator watch. Anya had the galley.

Finn had been asleep, or so he claimed, and she had already extracted his confession about the edit. But Markus, according to the rotation, had been off-duty in his quarters. Geophysicists did not work the hydrophone arrays unless specifically assigned, and the roster showed no such assignment for that date.

Ingrid flipped to the previous week, checking for any pattern of late-night monitoring. Nothing. Markus kept to his seismographs and his isolation, his name appearing only in the equipment maintenance logs.

She turned to the migration charts, unfolding the largest sheet until it covered half the desk. Solveig had marked the pod positions herself, her precise handwriting tracking the orcas’ slow retreat from the fjord as winter deepened. By the night of her death, the nearest pod was two hundred kilometers north, far beyond hydrophone range.

Ingrid’s throat tightened at the sight of her sister’s careful notation, but she forced herself to read the data coldly. If Markus had been checking for whale calls that night, he would have found only silence.

The station clock ticked in its plastic housing, each second a small hammer strike against her patience. She had what she needed. The counter log placed Markus at the deck after the edit, the duty roster gave him no official reason to be there, and the migration data stripped away his most likely excuse. But evidence was not proof, and proof was not confession. She needed to hear him say it, to watch his face when he realized the trap had already closed. Ingrid gathered the papers into a neat stack, her movements precise, and stood. The corridor outside her quarters stretched empty in both directions, the overhead lights casting their usual yellow pall. Somewhere in the station, Markus was working, believing himself unobserved. She would disabuse him of that notion.

The walk to the sound lab took her past the galley, where the smell of yesterday’s fish stew still lingered, and past the storage closet where Erik had unraveled. She did not slow. The sound lab’s door stood slightly ajar, and she could hear the low hum of the reel-to-reel decks through the gap. She pushed it open and stepped inside.

The room glowed blue with equipment lights, banks of LEDs and indicator lamps casting long shadows across the consoles. Markus stood with his back to her, his silhouette framed against the humming tape decks. He was calibrating a seismograph, his hands steady on the dials, his posture that of a man entirely absorbed in his work. Ingrid let the door close behind her with a soft click, and she saw his shoulders stiffen. He knew someone had entered. He did not turn.

“Markus.”

He finished his adjustment before acknowledging her, a deliberate delay that told her everything about his confidence. When he finally faced her, his expression was the same mask of professional detachment he always wore, thin lips, pale eyes behind wire-rimmed glasses, gray hair cut close to his skull. He looked like a man who had spent decades in remote stations, learning to tolerate human proximity without engaging with it.

“Ingrid.” He glanced at the papers in her hand. “Working late?”

She moved to the console and laid the stack on the nearest flat surface, keeping her finger on the counter log entry. The blue light made the red pencil circle glow like a wound.

“I have a question about this log entry.”

Markus approached, his steps measured, and looked down at the paper. She watched his face, searching for the flicker of recognition, the micro-expression that would betray him. His gaze moved across the entry, and she saw his jaw tighten, a contraction so small she might have imagined it. When he looked up, his expression remained neutral.

“Twenty-three forty-seven. The night after the incident.”

“Your ID code is on that entry.” Ingrid kept her voice flat, stripped of accusation. “You accessed the recording deck at counter reading 847, 293. That’s after Finn’s edit. After the tape was logged and sealed.”

Markus adjusted his glasses, a slow gesture that bought him time. “I was checking for late-season whale calls. The orcas sometimes return to the fjord if the currents shift. I wanted to verify we hadn’t missed anything.”

The excuse landed neatly, a technical explanation from a technical mind. Ingrid let the silence stretch for three heartbeats before she reached for the migration chart and spread it across the console beside the log.

“The pods were two hundred kilometers north by that date. Solveig tracked them herself.” She tapped the relevant notation, her finger landing on her sister’s handwriting. “There were no whale calls to hear. The hydrophones would have picked up nothing but water noise and engine hum.”

Markus’s gaze dropped to the chart. She saw his eyes move across the data, processing it, calculating. His hand lifted toward the console, then lowered. A bead of sweat glinted at his temple, catching the blue light.

“I didn’t know that at the time.”

“The migration data was posted on the bulletin board that morning. Solveig’s final update.” Ingrid did not blink. “You would have seen it if you’d checked.”

The hum of the tape decks filled the silence between them. Markus’s composure began to fracture, hairline cracks spreading across his professional mask. His breathing quickened, shallow breaths that barely moved his chest. His hand came up to adjust his glasses again, and she saw the tremor in his fingers, fine but unmistakable.

“I was being thorough.” His voice had tightened, the detached tone fraying at the edges. “After what happened, I thought someone should review the raw audio. In case the official log missed something.”

“You didn’t log your review.”

“It was unofficial.”

“You listened to the tape after it was sealed.” Ingrid stepped closer, closing the distance between them. “What did you hear, Markus?”

His jaw worked, muscles contracting beneath pale skin. Sweat now glistened at his hairline, catching the blue glow from the equipment. His eyes darted to the door, then back to her, and she saw calculation behind them, a man weighing his options and finding them few.

“I heard nothing unusual. Water noise. Engine sounds. The same as the official log.”

“Then why didn’t you report it?”

“Because there was nothing to report.” His voice cracked on the last word, and his hand gripped the edge of the console, knuckles blanching to bone white. “I was concerned about the station’s reputation. About whether we’d be blamed for negligence. I listened, I heard nothing, and I let it go.”

Ingrid studied him. The tremor in his hands had spread to his shoulders, barely visible but present. His breathing came faster now, and his eyes kept returning to the door, measuring the distance. He was scared, genuinely scared, but not of her. Of something else. Of someone else.

“You’re lying.”

“I’m telling you what happened.” The words came too quickly, tumbling over each other. “I made a mistake. I should have logged my access. I was worried about being implicated, about people asking questions I couldn’t answer. So I kept quiet. That’s all.”

“Someone edited this tape before you accessed it. Finn has already admitted to that much. But you came back. You listened again, after it was sealed, and you said nothing.” Ingrid leaned in. “What was on that tape that frightened you enough to break protocol?”

Markus’s mask shattered. His face contorted, fear and something darker twisting his features. His hand shot out and grabbed the edge of the console, steadying himself, and she saw his knuckles pulse white and release, white and release, a rhythm of panic.

“I didn’t kill her.” The words escaped him like steam from a pressure valve. “Whatever you think, whatever you’re building toward, I didn’t kill her.”

“Then tell me what you heard.”

His mouth opened, then closed. The internal war played across his face, calculations and recriminations, fear and desperation. Ingrid watched him struggle, watched the silence stretch between them like a wire about to snap. The blue light carved deep shadows beneath his eyes, making him look older, hollowed out.

“Someone on this station has been very careful to stay hidden.” Markus’s voice dropped to a whisper, his eyes fixed on hers. “I listened to that tape because I wanted to know if I was next. That’s all. I wanted to know if there was something that could be used against me, the way it was used against others.”

“Used how?”

But Markus shook his head, a sharp jerk that ended the conversation as definitively as a slammed door. He straightened, pulling his professional mask back into place, though it sat crooked now, unable to hide the terror beneath.

“You have your evidence, Ingrid. You’ve proven I lied about my reason for being here. Take that to whoever you need to take it to. But I’m done talking.”

He turned back to the seismograph, his hands shaking so badly he fumbled the dial. Ingrid watched him for a long moment, her mind turning over his words, his fear, the calculations she had seen behind his eyes. He was hiding something. He had accessed the tape, heard something that terrified him, and chosen silence over exposure. But he had not confessed to murder, and he had not named the person he feared.

She gathered her papers and left without another word, the sound lab door clicking shut behind her. The corridor stretched before her, yellow-lit and empty, but she could feel eyes on her back, the weight of being watched. Markus knew she was closing in. Whoever he was protecting, whoever he feared, they would know soon enough.

The walk back to her quarters felt longer than before, each step echoing in the empty passage. She passed the galley, the storage closet, the door to the generator room. The station hummed around her, machines and electronics maintaining their vigil, indifferent to the human drama unfolding in their shadow. When she reached her quarters, she did not turn on the light. She sat on the edge of her bunk, the ledgers still in her hands, and stared at the wall.

Erik had been blackmailed into editing the tape. Finn had confessed to the edit itself. And Markus had accessed the recording afterward, heard something that sent him back into the shadows. Three men, three pieces of the same puzzle, none of them the full picture. The real killer was still out there, hidden behind layers of fear and misdirection. Solveig had been blackmailing someone, and that someone had silenced her. The question was which of the remaining suspects had the most to lose.

She woke before dawn, her sleep fractured by dreams of water and silence.

The station was quiet, the generator’s thrum the only sound in the predawn dark. Ingrid dressed by feel, her fingers finding the familiar fabric of her sweater and trousers without need for light. The ledgers lay where she had left them, fanned across the desk like a hand of cards waiting to be played. She gathered them and stepped into the corridor.

The galley stood empty, the previous night’s dishes still stacked in the drying rack. Coffee, she needed coffee, but the urn was cold and she did not have the patience to wait for it to brew. She filled a cup with water from the tap and drank it standing, her eyes on the bulletin board across the room.

The migration chart hung there, Solveig’s handwriting tracking the pods’ retreat north. Ingrid crossed to it and studied the notation for the week of her sister’s death, the final entry before the chart stopped updating. Two hundred kilometers. The number had become a refrain in her mind, a distance that meant nothing and everything. Markus had used the whale calls as his excuse, and she had stripped that excuse away with the same data. But what remained was not a confession, only fear.

She left the galley and walked to the observation deck, the narrow room that looked out over the dark water. Through the reinforced glass, she could see the fjord stretching toward the horizon, black water against black sky. Somewhere out there, beneath the surface, her sister had drowned. The hydrophone arrays still monitored those depths, still recorded every sound, but the eleven minutes that mattered had been erased, and the people who knew why were hiding behind lies.

The door opened behind her. She did not turn.

“You’re up early.”

Anya’s voice, rough with sleep, carried across the room. Ingrid heard her approach, felt her presence at the edge of her vision, but kept her eyes on the water.

“Couldn’t sleep.”

“Nightmares?” Anya moved to stand beside her, close enough that Ingrid could smell the herbal tea on her breath. “This place breeds them.”

Ingrid said nothing. The water stretched before them, an expanse of darkness that held no answers.

“I heard you had a talk with Markus last night.” Anya’s tone was casual, but her eyes were sharp when Ingrid glanced at her. “He came to the galley after, looking like he’d seen a ghost. Pale, shaking, couldn’t hold his coffee cup steady.”

“I asked him some questions.”

“About the tape.”

Ingrid turned to face her. Anya’s expression was open, curious, but something flickered behind her eyes, a calculation that mirrored Markus’s. Everyone in this station had secrets. The question was which secrets led to murder.

“He accessed the recording deck after Solveig’s death. He lied about his reason.”

Anya absorbed this, her face smoothing into careful neutrality. “And what does that prove?”

“That he’s hiding something.”

“We’re all hiding something, Ingrid.” Anya turned back to the window, her profile limned by the faint glow of the rising sun. “This is a small station in a frozen corner of the world. We live in each other’s pockets for months at a time. Secrets are the only privacy we have.”

“Someone killed my sister.”

“Someone did.” Anya’s voice softened. “But destroying everyone else’s privacy won’t bring her back.”

Ingrid watched her, searching for the crack in her composure, the tell that would betray her. Anya met her gaze steadily, her hands loose at her sides, her breathing even. If she was afraid, she hid it better than Erik or Markus.

“Did you know Solveig well?”

“Well enough. She kept to her work, same as the rest of us. But she had a way of noticing things, of seeing what people wanted to keep hidden.” Anya paused, her eyes drifting to the water. “That can be dangerous in a place like this.”

The morning passed in a blur of routine tasks. Ingrid returned to her quarters and forced herself to work, reviewing the hydrophone logs from the days surrounding Solveig’s death. The numbers blurred together, timestamps and counter readings that should have formed a pattern but refused to cohere. She rubbed her eyes and reached for her cold coffee, grimacing at the bitter taste.

A knock broke her concentration. She set down the cup and crossed to the door, opening it to find the corridor empty. A folded paper lay on the floor at her feet, slipped beneath the door while she worked. She picked it up and unfolded it, her eyes scanning the handwritten message.

The counter log was not the only record. Check the diesel ledger for the night of the drowning. Engine signatures don’t lie.

No signature. No indication of who had sent it. Ingrid turned the paper over, but the back was blank. She stepped into the corridor and looked both ways, but the passage stretched empty in either direction.

The diesel ledger was kept in the generator room, a thick-bound book recording every fuel transfer and engine cycle. Ingrid found it on its usual shelf, its pages dense with Erik’s precise handwriting. She flipped to the date of Solveig’s death and ran her finger down the entries.

The station’s main generator had run its usual cycle, consuming fuel at the expected rate. But at 22: 15, a secondary entry appeared: auxiliary engine, runtime 47 minutes, fuel draw 12 liters. Ingrid frowned. The auxiliary engine powered the research vessels, small boats used for sample collection and equipment maintenance. It should not have been running at that hour, not in the dead of night.

She traced the entry with her fingernail, feeling the indentation of Erik’s pen. Someone had taken a boat out that night, and they had logged it under the auxiliary engine designation. The timestamp placed the departure less than an hour before Solveig’s estimated time of death. The return would have been after midnight, after the tape edit, after everything.

Her hand trembled as she turned the page, checking the following entries. No corresponding log of who had authorized the engine use, no maintenance request, no sample collection report. The entry stood alone, a single line of data that contradicted every official account of that night.

She copied the entry into her notebook, her handwriting cramped with urgency. When she closed the ledger and returned it to its shelf, her mind was already racing ahead. Someone had taken a boat. Someone had left the station and returned under cover of darkness, and Erik had logged the fuel draw without question. Had he known what the engine was being used for? Or had he simply been following orders, the same way he had followed the blackmail note?

That evening, she returned to the sound lab. The blue light greeted her like an old enemy, casting its familiar pall over the consoles and tape decks. She sat at the monitoring station and pulled out her notes, spreading them across the desk in a pattern only she could read. The counter log entry. The migration data. The diesel ledger notation. Three pieces of evidence, each pointing toward a larger truth.

The station’s intercom crackled to life, a burst of static that made her jump. Then a voice, thin with distance, came through the speaker.

“Ingrid Solheim, please report to the main commons. Immediately.”

She gathered her papers and stood, her heart pounding. The walk to the commons felt like a march toward execution, each step measured and deliberate. When she pushed through the door, she found the room arranged like a tribunal. Erik stood by the far wall, his face gray with exhaustion. Anya sat at the table, her hands folded before her. And at the head of the room, Markus stood with his back to the window, his arms crossed over his chest.

“We need to talk.” Markus’s voice was steady, but his eyes betrayed him, darting to Erik, to Anya, to the door behind her. “About what you’ve been doing. About what you’ve been accusing people of.”

Ingrid did not sit. She remained standing, her notes clutched against her chest, her gaze moving from face to face. Three people, each with something to hide, each watching her with a mixture of fear and calculation.

“I’ve found evidence,” she said, her voice flat. “Evidence that someone accessed the tape after the edit. Evidence that a boat was taken out the night Solveig died. Evidence that this station is protecting a killer.”

The silence stretched between them, taut as a wire. Erik’s hand found the wall behind him, steadying himself. Anya’s fingers drummed against the table, a rapid rhythm that betrayed her composure. And Markus, at the head of the room, stared at her with something that might have been hatred, or might have been fear.

“You don’t know what you’re doing.” Markus’s voice dropped to a whisper. “You think you’re finding answers, but you’re just pulling threads. And when you pull enough threads, the whole thing unravels.”

“Then tell me what I’m missing.”

But he only shook his head, his arms tightening across his chest. The window behind him showed the dark fjord, the black water, the endless stretch of winter night. Somewhere out there, beneath the surface, the truth waited. And somewhere in this room, the person who had killed to keep that truth buried watched her with eyes that held no remorse.

Ingrid turned and walked out, leaving them to their silence. The corridor stretched before her, empty and waiting. She had exposed Markus’s lie, but the killer remained hidden, and the net was tightening around them all.

A folded note on the floor by her boot caught her eye. She picked it up. It was a printout from the station’s network, a system alert for a verified log backup. Counter log backup verified. Anomaly detected on secondary server.

She allowed herself a single, measured breath. The preparation had mattered—cross-referencing the duty roster against the migration charts, pulling the backup server logs before anyone could alter them, building her case method rather than confronting Markus on instinct alone. Without that groundwork, he would have walked away.

Now she had to decide what to do with him.

Markus had admitted to accessing the tape, admitted to hiding it. But his explanation had come too easily—the framed technician, his fear of becoming the convenient suspect. He had offered her a motive that cast him as coward rather than killer. Convenient. Rehearsed.

She stopped outside the archives room, her hand resting on the door handle. The secondary logs Erik had confirmed would show exactly when Markus had accessed the tape, and from which terminal. If his story about the lab was true, the access point would match. If he had lied about that too—

The door clicked open under her palm.

Inside, the server hummed in the darkness. She crossed to the terminal, pulling up the access records from the verified backup. The timestamps scrolled past: maintenance checks, routine backups, and there—two days after Solveig’s drowning—a single access entry flagged with Markus’s credentials.

From a terminal in the east wing.

Not the lab. The east wing, where the administrative offices sat empty after hours, where the security cameras had been disabled the night Henrik died.

Ingrid stared at the screen, her reflection ghostly in the monitor’s glow. Markus had claimed he found the tape during routine review. But the east wing terminal had no business pulling hydrophone data. Someone had used his credentials, or he had used them himself to plant evidence against the technician he was so eager to blame.

Her phone buzzed again. Another message from Erik: Found something else. The deleted footage from that night. Meet me at the security station.

She tucked the phone away, her decision crystallising. Markus’s fear was real enough—but fear could make a man kill, and it could certainly make him clever. She had exposed his lie about the whale calls. Now she would find out what else he had buried beneath his convenient confession.

The corridor stretched behind her, empty and waiting. She walked toward the security station, the truth pulling her forward into the dark.
