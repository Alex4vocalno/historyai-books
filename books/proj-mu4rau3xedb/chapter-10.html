Chapter 10

Mudflat Settles

────────────────────────────────────────

Ingrid’s pencil scratches across the tide graph, a lone sound in the silent map room.

The graphite tip leaves a thin grey trail across the gridded paper — longitude and latitude, depth contours, the Norwegian coast rendered in miniature. Outside the map room’s single porthole, the polar night presses black against the glass. Midnight in January. Six hours of twilight at noon, and then this — the long, dark hours when the station feels less like a building and more like a capsule suspended in nothing.

Finn’s voice still loops in her head. You see, the bias calibration is simple once you understand the deck’s routing. Said with that patient, teacherly patience, as if he were doing her a favor. As if the man who had sat at this same desk, who had trained Elin on the recording equipment, who had taught her sister how to thread a reel and set the counter — as if that man were simply being helpful. But the helpfulness had felt like a demonstration. A quiet display of capability. A reminder that the person who taught the victim could also be the one who killed her.

Ingrid sets the pencil down and flexes her cramped fingers. The map room is small — barely three meters by four — and cluttered with decades of accumulated charts. Rolled tide tables lean in a rack beside the door, yellowed paper cylinders of varying diameters, each labeled by year and coastal sector. The air smells of dust, old ink, and the faint chemical tang of the station’s heating system.

She reaches for the rack and pulls out the cylinder marked 1989 — Sektor 7 — Tidalanger. The paper is heavy, slightly waxy, and unrolls with a dry crackle across the desk. Columns of numbers. Hours down the left margin, dates across the top, tide heights in meters in the body of the table. Predicted high and low water for every six-hour cycle, calculated by the Norwegian Hydrographic Service for this specific stretch of coast.

March fourteenth. The night Elin died.

Ingrid finds the column. Her finger traces down the hours. 20:00 — 2.1 meters. 21:00 — 1.4.22:00 — 0.7.23:00 — 0.3.

Zero point three meters. Twelve inches. The height of a coffee table.

She holds her finger there and reaches across the desk for the diesel log. The leather-bound ledger sits open where she left it an hour ago, its pages smoothed flat. The entries are in Erik’s handwriting — tight, controlled script, each number formed with mechanical precision. She found the anomaly two nights ago: March fourteenth, 22:40, generator test, 9.4 liters excess consumption. Nine point four liters unaccounted for. Not the eighteen-point-four she’d initially misread in the dim light — she’d corrected that. The actual figure was 9.4. But the excess was still there, still wrong, still a ghost in the ledger that corresponded to nothing in the operational schedule.

More importantly, it gave her a timestamp. 22:40. The generator had been shut down for what Erik called a “routine test,” and during that window — that precise, logged window — the hydrophone tape had gone silent. Eleven minutes of magnetic bias mismatch. Eleven minutes of erased time, cleanly cut, not degraded. An edit.

And Elin’s last transmission, logged in the station’s communication record, had been at 22:35. Five minutes before the generator test. Five minutes before the tape went blank.

Ingrid pulls the communication log toward her. The entry is in Erik’s hand: 22:35 — Solheim, E. — Routine position check — acknowledged. She has read this line a hundred times. Routine position check. Acknowledged. As if Elin had simply radioed in from the cove, said she was fine, and signed off. The official report stated that Elin had been at Storegga Cove — a deep-water inlet on the station’s northern shore — conducting a solo hydrophone deployment when she was caught by a wave and swept in. The cove was known for its depth and its treacherous current. A dangerous place, the report said. A tragic accident waiting to happen.

But the report also said the drowning occurred sometime between 22:35 and 23:10. And the tide table says that at 22:40, Storegga Cove had 0.3 meters of water.

Ingrid picks up the pencil again. She turns to the bathymetric chart — the depth map of the cove — pinned to the wall behind the desk. Storegga Cove is a small inlet, roughly two hundred meters across at its widest, funnel-shaped, with a steep shingle beach that drops into a basin. At mean high water, the basin is 4.2 meters deep. At mean low water — the spring low tide, the kind that pulls the sea out like a sheet from a bed — the basin drains to 0.6 meters. But 0.3 is lower than mean low. 0.3 is the neap low, the weak tide, the one that happens twice a month when the gravitational pull of the moon and sun are at right angles.

She checks the astronomical notes in the tide table’s margin. March fourteenth, 1989: neap tide. The lowest astronomical tide of that two-week cycle.

Ingrid’s pencil moves to the graph paper. She plots the tide curve — a sinusoidal wave, the water rising and falling in its predictable rhythm. She marks the hours along the x-axis, the tide height on the y-axis. At 20:00, the curve peaks at 2.1 meters — high water. By 22:00, it has fallen to 0.7. By 22:40 — the timestamp from the diesel log, the moment the tape went silent — the curve bottoms out at 0.3.

She stares at the number.

Zero point three meters. Thirty centimeters. A little over a foot of water across the floor of a cove that the official report calls “deep-water” and “treacherous.” Thirty centimeters of seawater covering a basin that, at that tide, is not a basin at all but a mudflat — a vast, dark expanse of exposed silt and shingle, damp but solid, the kind of ground you could walk across without wetting your knees.

In the dim map room, Ingrid’s pencil stops. The tide height number stares back from the graph. Her eyes flick to the window, to the black glass and the nothing beyond it. She pictures the cove — not as deep water, not as the churning inlet of the official report, but as a flat, dark expanse of silt under the polar night. Mud and rounded stones. The smell of brine and decomposition. The kind of landscape that couldn’t drown a child, let alone a grown woman who knew the coast.

The station’s ambient hum — the ventilation, the distant generator, the creak of thermal contraction in the walls — fades from her awareness. It is replaced by a silence that is not silence but the roaring, vertiginous absence of a lie exposed. The sea didn’t take Elin. The story did.

Ingrid sits motionless. Her hand rests on the graph paper, the pencil still between her fingers, the tip resting on the plotted point where the tide curve touches its nadir. She breathes. The air in the map room is warm, close, stale. She can smell the graphite dust, the old paper, the faint residue of her own fear — a metallic tang at the back of her throat.

She thinks of the cove. She has never been there — has not been able to bring herself to visit the place where her sister was said to have died. But she has seen the photographs in the station’s files: steep shingle, dark water, rock walls. At high tide, it looks exactly as described. Deep. Dangerous. A place where a person could drown.

But the photographs were taken in daylight. In summer. At high water.

Nobody photographed the cove at neap low tide in March. Nobody mentioned that the cove empties. That the basin becomes a mudflat. That the water drains out through a narrow channel at the southern end, leaving behind nothing but wet silt and the smell of the sea floor.

Elin could not have drowned at Storegga Cove at 22:40 on March fourteenth, 1989. Not in thirty centimeters of water. Not on a mudflat.

Which means Elin did not drown at Storegga Cove.

Which means someone moved her body. Someone carried her — or drove her, or dragged her — to the cove after she died, and left her there to be found at low tide, and then reported the drowning as an accident at a location that sounded plausible because everyone knew the cove was deep and dangerous at high water. But no one checked the tide table. No one calculated the water height at the time of death. No one did what Ingrid has just done: sat alone in a map room with a pencil and a graph and a diesel ledger and done the arithmetic.

The arithmetic is simple. The arithmetic is irrefutable. The arithmetic says: the official story is a lie, and the lie is geographical, and geography does not negotiate.

Ingrid’s chest tightens. She feels the grief — the old, familiar weight of it — shift inside her, not lightening but hardening, compressing into something denser and colder. For six months she has carried the shapeless, suffocating mass of not knowing. Now the shape is forming. It is sharp-edged. It has a point.

Someone killed her sister. Someone moved the body. And the tide table proves it.

She is still staring at the graph when the map room door opens behind her.

“Ingrid?”

Erik’s voice. Casual, almost conversational. But with a thinness to it — a controlled lightness, like a man walking on ice and pretending not to notice the surface beneath his feet.

She does not turn around immediately. She gives herself one second — one full second — to let her face settle. To school the muscles of her jaw, the skin around her eyes, the set of her mouth. To become the person she was before the number locked in: a woman doing routine checks, nothing more.

Then she turns in her chair. Erik stands in the doorway, his bulk filling the frame. He is wearing his standard station sweater — the grey wool that all the crew were issued — and his face carries the faint sheen of someone who has been near the generator room. The smell reaches her: diesel fuel, warm metal, the particular oily musk of a man who has been servicing machinery. His eyes move past her shoulder to the desk, to the open tide table, to the diesel ledger, to the graph paper with its plotted curve and its damning point.

“What are you doing in here so late?” he asks. The tone is light. The eyes are not.

“Equipment schedules,” Ingrid says. She hears her own voice as if from a distance — steady, clipped, the voice of an engineer answering a routine question. “I’m cross-referencing the generator maintenance log against the tide cycles. Finn mentioned that the diesel intake can pull sediment during extreme low tides — the coastal water gets turbid, and the filter clogs faster. I wanted to see if the maintenance intervals line up with the neap cycles.”

The words come out smooth, plausible, laced with just enough technical detail to be boring. She watches Erik’s face as the jargon lands. His eyes narrow fractionally — not in confusion, but in calculation. He is weighing whether this explanation is genuine or a performance. She can see the gears turning behind his expression, the way a man who has told his own lies recognizes the architecture of someone else’s.

“Finn mentioned that,” Erik repeats. He steps into the room. The map room shrinks around him.

“Last week,” Ingrid says. “When he was showing me the intake system.” She taps the diesel ledger with her pencil — a casual, absent gesture, as if the ledger is incidental, a reference tool, not the axis of the entire investigation. “The March entries seemed irregular, but I think that’s just the neap tide effect. Sediment load in the fuel line.”

She is, she realizes, good at this. The thought arrives with a small, cold shock. She is lying smoothly, convincingly, building a technical fiction that Erik cannot immediately dismantle without revealing that he knows more about the diesel log than he should. If the generator is his responsibility — and he has said so, emphatically, more than once — then he should know whether the diesel intake is affected by tide cycles. He should know whether the March entries are irregular or normal. He should know, and if he doesn’t know, he should ask. And if he does know, he should correct her.

He does neither.

“Right,” Erik says. He nods slowly. His eyes move from the ledger to the tide table and back. “The intake system. Yes, Finn’s been… helpful with that kind of thing.” A pause. “You know, Ingrid, you don’t need to work this late. The schedules will keep. You should rest.”

“I’m almost done,” she says. She turns back to the graph paper and picks up the pencil, as if resuming her work. A dismissal. Polite, professional, the kind of thing a focused engineer says to a well-meaning colleague who is interrupting.

Erik does not leave. He stands behind her for a long moment — long enough for Ingrid to feel the heat of his body, the displacement of air, the weight of his attention on the back of her neck. She keeps her hand steady. The pencil traces a meaningless line on the graph — a continuation of the tide curve past the point that matters, into the hours after midnight, as if she is interested in the full cycle and not the single, devastating number at its base.

“This station runs on trust, Ingrid,” Erik says. His voice has dropped half a register. “And procedure. I know you’ve been… reviewing things. Since you arrived. The tapes. The logs. I understand. You want to understand what happened to your sister. But there’s a point where reviewing becomes… something else.”

“Something else?”

“Distraction. From the work.” He pauses. “From the living.”

Ingrid turns her head slightly. Not enough to face him fully — just enough to show she has heard, that she is considering his words. “The work is fine, Erik. The hydrophone array is calibrated. The tapes are archived. I’m just filling time.”

“Filling time.” He repeats the phrase as if tasting it. “That’s… an honest answer, at least.”

He lingers another beat. Then she hears him step back, turn, and walk away down the corridor. His footsteps recede — heavy, measured, the tread of a man who is thinking. The door remains open behind him, and the draft from the hall carries the cold of the station’s unheated passages, a chill that settles on her exposed skin like a warning.

Ingrid does not move until the footsteps are gone.

Then she sets the pencil down and places both hands flat on the desk. The tide table is still open. The diesel ledger is still open. The graph paper still holds its plotted curve, its damning nadir, its stark and simple truth.

Zero point three meters.

She looks at the number. It is small. It is dry. It is the kind of number that an engineer writes in a logbook without thinking, the kind that a clerk files without reading, the kind that a police report would never check because who would think to question the sea? The sea is always there. The sea is always deep. Everyone knows the cove is dangerous.

But the sea is not always deep. The sea withdraws. The sea leaves. And when the sea leaves Storegga Cove on the night of March fourteenth, 1989, it leaves behind a mudflat, and a mudflat cannot drown anyone.

Elin did not die at the cove. Elin died somewhere else. And someone — someone who knew the station, who knew the diesel log, who knew the generator schedule and the tide cycles and the hydrophone tape and the exact, precise, eleven-minute window when the recording went dark — someone carried her to the cove and left her there and wrote “deep-water” in the report and “routine position check” in the log and “tragic accident” in the official file and thought that would be the end of it.

Because who checks the tide table? Who does the arithmetic? Who sits alone in a map room at midnight with a pencil and a grudge and calculates the height of the sea?

Ingrid does.

She begins to gather the papers. She rolls the tide table back into its cylinder and slides it into the rack, third slot from the left, where it had been. She closes the diesel ledger and returns it to the shelf. She folds the graph paper — the plotted curve, the nadir, the number — and slips it into the inner pocket of her jacket, against her ribs, where it sits flat and hard like a second heartbeat.

She will not leave this here. She will not leave evidence of what she knows in a room where anyone can find it, where Erik can find it, where the open pages and the plotted graph and the pencil marks can speak louder than any words she might say to deflect, to dissemble, to perform the role of the grieving sister who is simply filling time.

She turns off the map room lamp. The darkness closes in. She stands for a moment in the black, listening to the station’s hum — the ventilation, the generator, the creak of the walls — and feels the folded paper against her ribs.

The cold, hard fact of the mudflat settles in her gut. The sea didn’t take Elin. The story did. Someone moved her body.

And Ingrid is going to find out who.
