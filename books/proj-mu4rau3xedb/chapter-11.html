Chapter 11

Tide Tables Tell

────────────────────────────────────────

The bound volume of tide tables lay open on the archive desk, its spine cracked at a date six months ago.

Ingrid ran her finger down the column of printed tide heights, past the entries for March 12, March 13, and stopped at March 14. The ink was faded blue, the type neat and institutional, the work of someone who understood that logs were not suggestions. The record. The ground truth that existed before stories were told to explain what happened on the night Solveig Solheim went into the water and did not come back.

The entry read: 22: 47 — 0.2m.

She held her finger there. The dry, papery smell of the old logbooks filled the small archive space, and somewhere in the walls the heating system ticked, a slow metronome that measured the silence. March 14. The night of the drowning. The night Erik Larssen said Solveig walked down to the cove at approximately twenty-three hundred hours, slipped on wet rocks, and was taken by the current. The night the hydrophone tape lost eleven minutes of audio in a clean, surgical cut that was not degradation but editing. The night the diesel generator ran an unlogged test cycle that consumed fuel no one accounted for.

0.2 meters. She mouthed the number without sound. Two-tenths of a meter. Twenty centimeters of water above chart datum, which itself was already the lowest astronomical tide. At 0.2m, the cove became a basin of exposed sediment, a mudflat ringed by bedrock and kelp stranding lines. Twenty centimeters would not reach Ingrid’s ankles.

The pencil in her other hand had stopped moving. She looked at the number again, then at the adjacent entries. March 14, 21: 00 — 1.4m. March 14, 23: 30 — 0.8m. The tide had been ebbing through the evening, reaching its lowest point at 22: 47, then beginning to flood again after twenty-three hundred. The timeline Erik gave placed Solveig at the cove during the lowest water of the entire tidal cycle. Not just low tide. The lowest of the low. Spring tide, new moon, the sea pulled as far from the shore as it would go that month.

She wrote 0.2m on her notepad, underlined it twice, and closed the logbook. The cover settled with a soft, definitive sound.

Ingrid stood and moved to the library table beneath the window. The nautical chart was where she had left it, rolled and secured with a length of marine cord. She unrolled it across the table’s scarred surface, spreading the large, crackling paper flat, and weighed its corners with three volumes of the station’s oceanographic reference set and a mug of cold coffee she did not remember leaving there. The waxy coating of the chart paper caught the overhead light and threw it back in a dull sheen.

The chart was British Admiralty Series 27, covering the coastal approach to the station and the surrounding fjord system. She had found it in the chart drawer two weeks ago, filed between an obsolete 1974 edition and a current shipping lane overlay. The bathymetric contours were drawn in fine brown lines, each labeled with its depth in meters at chart datum. She found the cove. It sat on the western edge of the station peninsula, a small indentation in the coastline sheltered by a basalt shelf. The contour line at the cove’s mouth read 1.0m. Inside the cove, the contours tightened and shallowed. The inner basin, where Erik said Solveig was found, was marked with a symbol Ingrid recognized from her coastal engineering coursework. A small, crossed circle. Mudflat. Exposed at chart datum.

Chart datum was the reference level. The zero line. The tide log’s 0.2m meant the actual water level was twenty centimeters above that zero. Twenty centimeters above a surface that was, by definition, exposed. The mudflat symbol marked ground that was dry, or near-dry, at the lowest tides. At 0.2m above that, the inner basin would hold a thin film of water over ankle-deep silt. Not a current. Not a channel. Not anything that could pull a woman under and hold her there.

Her finger traced the contour lines, following them inland where they described the shape of the cove floor. The outer approach had depth, a channel that cut diagonally toward the open fjord. That channel would still have water at 0.2m. But it was narrow, shallow, and separated from the inner basin by the basalt shelf. To reach the inner cove at low tide, a person would have to walk across exposed mud and rock. They would have to choose to go there. And once there, the sea could not reach them.

She looked at her notepad. The pencil trembled between her thumb and forefinger. She set it down and pressed both palms flat against the chart, feeling the waxy resistance of the paper, the faint texture of the contour lines beneath her skin.

The cold came up through her hands first, then settled into her wrists, her forearms. Not the room temperature. The library was warm, almost uncomfortably so, with the station’s forced-air heating cycling through its ducts. A different cold. The cold of something that could not be changed. A fact that would remain true regardless of who believed it, who denied it, who constructed a story to bury it.

She looked at the intersection where her finger had stopped. The mudflat symbol sat directly beneath the X she had drawn on the chart two days ago, marking the location Erik had given in his incident report. The X and the symbol overlapped perfectly. The place where Solveig supposedly drowned was a mudflat at the time she supposedly drowned there.

The room contracted around her. The ticking of the heating system grew louder, then faded to nothing, and she heard the blood in her own ears. She was alone in the library. The common room door was closed. Beyond it, she could hear the low murmur of the radio schedule, someone keying the transmitter at the wrong hour, the static that filled the station like white noise. The others were elsewhere in the building, separated by bulkheads and insulation, and none of them knew what she was looking at.

None of them could know. Not yet.

She pulled her hands from the chart and sat back in the chair. The metal frame creaked. She tasted the station’s recycled air, metallic and thin, the flavor of a closed system breathing its own exhaust. The taste sat on her tongue like a coin.

She reconstructed it from memory, from the incident report she had read three times, from the conversation in the common room where he had laid it out with the practiced calm of someone who had rehearsed the telling. Solveig had been unable to sleep. She had taken a walk to clear her head, a habit Ingrid recognized because Solveig had always walked when she was restless. She had gone to the cove because it was the closest point of coastline to the station, a three-minute walk down the gravel path. The night was dark, the sky overcast, and she had slipped on wet rocks near the waterline. The current had taken her before anyone heard her fall. The body was recovered the next morning at first light, washed into the outer channel by the flooding tide.

The story was precise in the way that lies are precise. It included specific details that anchored it to reality. The overcast sky. The wet rocks. The three-minute walk. The flooding tide recovering the body. Each detail was plausible in isolation. Together, they formed a narrative that sounded like an accident because accidents sound like that. Disconnected facts arranged to explain an outcome no one planned.

But the tide log was a disconnected fact of a different kind. Measured, recorded, and filed by someone whose job was to record it accurately. The log existed before the story, before the incident report, before Erik sat in the common room and told Ingrid how her sister died. The kind of record that did not care about narratives. It cared about the moon, and the gravitational pull of celestial bodies, and the shape of the coastline. Beyond manipulation. No one could have falsified it without corrupting every subsequent tidal prediction the station relied on for its operations, and the predictions had been accurate all season. She had checked.

The diesel ledger sat in the same archive, two shelves above the tide log. She had found the anomaly there two weeks ago. March 14, 22: 30 to 22: 41, generator test cycle, 9.4 liters consumed above the baseline draw. Eleven minutes of additional generator load. Eleven minutes, matching the eleven-minute gap in the hydrophone tape. The generator test was logged as routine, but the fuel consumption was wrong. A test cycle drew power for calibration equipment. This draw was heavier. Something with more load had been running during those eleven minutes. Something that required the generator’s full output.

She had noted it privately, held it close, and now the diesel anomaly and the tide log and the nautical chart formed a triangle in her mind. Three points of evidence. The generator ran at full load for eleven minutes during the gap in the hydrophone tape. The hydrophone tape was edited. The tide at the time of the supposed drowning was 0.2m. The drowning location was a mudflat. Each fact pointed to the next. Someone had used the station’s power for something during those eleven minutes. Someone had erased the audio record of what happened. And the place where Solveig was said to have died could not have killed her.

If the cove did not drown Solveig, then Solveig did not drown at the cove. If she did not drown at the cove, she died somewhere else, and someone carried her body to the cove afterward, during the flooding tide, so that the recovery would look consistent with a person swept from the rocks by the current. The mudflat would be underwater by morning. The body would be found in the channel. The story would hold.

But the tide log held better.

Ingrid looked at the chart again. Her X still marked the spot. The mudflat symbol still sat beneath it, patient and indifferent. She picked up the pencil and wrote, in small letters beside the X, the notation she had been trained to use in her engineering work: 0.2m above CD — mudflat exposed. Then she drew a second X, this one in the outer channel where the contour line read 3.0m. Where the body was recovered. Where the current ran. Where a drowning would look right.

Two X marks. One where the story said Solveig entered the water. One where the story said she was found. The first was impossible. The second was convenient.

She understood now that what she was looking at was not a mistake in the report. Mistakes are random. They scatter. They do not align. A wrong tide time might produce an error in the narrative, but the error would not coincide with an edited tape and an unlogged generator cycle and a body found in the one place where it would corroborate the impossible location. Architecture, not mistakes.

Someone had built this. Someone who knew the station’s systems, who understood the tidal patterns, who had access to the hydrophone deck and the generator and the tide log and the charts. Someone who had planned the timing so that the flooding tide would do the work of placing the body where the story needed it to be found. Someone who had erased the audio evidence of whatever happened during those eleven minutes when the generator ran at full load and the sea was not in the cove.

Five people wintered at the station. Five people had access. Five people had told versions of that night that supported Erik’s account, each one adding a small corroborating detail. She had been here for months, listening to them, watching them, cataloging the way their stories matched too neatly, the way their recollections never contradicted. She had thought the pressure of isolation was compressing them into agreement. The station was a closed system, five people in a concrete building on a rock in the North Atlantic, and closed systems produced conformity. What she had believed. What she had allowed herself to believe.

The conformity was not produced by the environment. The event had produced it. They were not agreeing because they were isolated. They were agreeing because they were lying about the same thing.

Her jaw tightened. She closed the notepad, folded it once, and slid it into the inner pocket of her jacket. The chart stayed on the table. She could not take it. Its absence would be noticed. But the data was in her head now, fixed and permanent, as unchangeable as the record itself.

She stood and crossed to the common room door. Pressed her ear against it. The radio schedule had ended. She could hear footsteps in the corridor beyond, the heavy tread of boots on the metal grating. Someone moving toward the kitchen. The clatter of a kettle being set on the stove. Ordinary sounds of a station in winter, people making coffee, living their routines, maintaining the shape of normalcy.

Ingrid opened the door and stepped through. The corridor smelled of diesel exhaust and instant coffee and the particular staleness of air that had been circulated through the same ducts for six months. The overhead lights buzzed at a frequency she had learned to ignore. She walked toward her quarters, nodding once to a figure passing in the other direction, keeping her pace even, her face still.

Behind her, the library door stood open. The chart lay on the table, weighted with books and a cold mug of coffee. The X marks faced the ceiling. The notation beside the first one read 0.2m above CD — mudflat exposed. Anyone who entered the library and looked at the chart would see it. Anyone who understood bathymetric notation would know what it meant.

She did not go back to close the door.

The wind found her when she reached the observation deck, a gust that carried the smell of salt and ice and the deep, cold Atlantic. The sky was the color of wet slate, pressing down on the water, and the tidal channel was visible from here, a dark scar in the landscape, and at this hour, at this tide, it was full of water, gray-green and moving, and it looked like a place where a person could drown.

But she knew what it looked like at 22: 47 on March 14. She knew what it was. A mudflat. A lie. And somewhere beneath both, the thing that had actually happened to Solveig.

The wind picked up. Ingrid turned her back to it and walked inside.

She stopped in the corridor. Her hands were steady. The steadiness — that was the part she noticed, because it meant something had finished happening inside her. The suspicion she had carried for weeks, the careful, scientific habit of holding multiple hypotheses open until the data settled, had closed like a hand around a single fact. Solveig had not drowned. Solveig could not have drowned. The mudflat would have been exposed for a hundred meters in every direction at 22: 47 on March 14. No water deep enough to kill anyone existed in that cove. She knew this the way she knew the freezing point of seawater — not as a belief, but as a number that would not move.

Her phone was in her pocket. She pulled it out and opened the photo of Solveig from the station’s common room — the one taken three weeks before the drowning, Solveig smiling in a yellow rain jacket, her hand on the railing of the observation deck. Ingrid had looked at this photograph dozens of times searching for something she could not name. Now she saw only her sister’s face, and the part of her that had been conducting an investigation went quiet, and the part that was Solveig’s younger sister stood alone with the knowledge that someone had put Solveig in the water after she was already dead.

She put the phone away.

The corridor lights hummed. Somewhere in the station, a door latch clicked — a sound she would not have registered an hour ago. Now she catalogued it. Footsteps followed, heavy, moving toward the library.

The chart was still open on the table. The door was still open behind her.

Whoever had written 0.2m above CD — mudflat exposed in the official log had not tried to hide it. They had simply assumed no one would read it. Or no one who mattered.

Ingrid turned toward the footsteps and did not move to cover what she had found.
