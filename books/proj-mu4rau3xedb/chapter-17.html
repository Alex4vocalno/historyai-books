Chapter 17

Ledger Confrontation

────────────────────────────────────────

Finn’s confession still burned behind Ingrid’s eyes when she pushed open the common room door, the diesel ledger a cold weight beneath her arm. The space was empty, fluorescent lights humming their eternal drone, the laminate tables bare except for a few abandoned mugs and the ever-present condensation creeping down the windows. She had chosen this hour deliberately, the station’s rhythm slowed between shifts, when Markus would come for his evening coffee and find her waiting.

She spread the ledger on the table nearest the door, smoothing the pages to the entry dated the night Solveig died. The forged numbers stared back at her, neat and damning. She had spent hours comparing the handwriting to Markus’s field notes, the angular sevens, the distinctive loop of his eights. The match was unmistakable.

The door swung open.

Markus entered with his usual measured stride, a ceramic mug already in his hand, his attention fixed on the steam rising from its surface. He was halfway to the coffee station before he noticed her, and in that fraction of a second, Ingrid watched his body recognize the trap. His shoulders tensed, his step faltered, and his gaze dropped to the open ledger on the table.

“Ingrid.” He kept his voice even, but his fingers had tightened around the mug’s handle. “You’re up late.”

She didn’t rise. Didn’t smile. She simply turned the ledger to face him and tapped the entry with one finger. “Sit down, Markus.”

He hesitated. The silence stretched between them, filled only by the relentless hum of the heater and the distant groan of ice shifting against the station’s pilings. Then, slowly, he crossed to the table and lowered himself into the chair opposite her. His eyes moved across the page, and she watched the color drain from his face in increments, like ice melting under an unexpected sun.

“I’ve been going through the diesel logs,” Ingrid said. Her voice came out flat, controlled, stripped of the fury that had been building in her chest since she’d first noticed the discrepancy. “Finn’s numbers didn’t add up. Neither did Erik’s explanations. So I started looking at the original entries. The handwriting.”

She slid a page of Markus’s field notes beside the ledger, aligning the samples so the similarities became undeniable. The same sharp angles. The same hurried but precise strokes.

“You want to tell me why you forged a diesel entry the night my sister drowned?”

Markus stared at the evidence as if it might rearrange itself into something less incriminating. His jaw worked, and she could see him cycling through responses, searching for an angle, a deflection, a way out. But the numbers were there, black ink on white paper, and his own hand had written the lie.

“It’s not what you think.” His voice had dropped to barely a whisper.

“Then explain it to me.” Ingrid leaned forward, closing the distance between them. “Because from where I’m sitting, you falsified station records on the exact night Solveig died. You’ve been lying to everyone, including the investigation. So either you start talking, or I take this to the authorities right now.”

The threat hung in the air. Markus’s composure cracked. His hands, which had been resting on the table, began to tremble, a fine vibration that traveled up his arms and into his shoulders. He opened his mouth, closed it, opened it again.

“I didn’t kill her.” The words came out ragged, broken. “You have to believe that. I would never—““Then why the forgery?” Ingrid pressed. “Why the lies?”

He drew a shuddering breath, and when he spoke again, his voice carried the weight of something that had been pressing against his ribs for far too long. “Because I wasn’t where I said I was. I wasn’t in the lab checking for late-season whale calls.”

“Where were you?”

Markus’s gaze dropped to the table. His shoulders curved inward, collapsing under an invisible burden. “I was with Solveig.”

The words struck Ingrid like a physical blow. She had expected an admission of guilt, a confession of some petty crime or bureaucratic cover-up. She had not expected this.

“What do you mean, you were with Solveig?”

“I loved her.” The confession tumbled out, raw and unguarded. “I’ve loved her for years. Since before she married Erik, before any of this. She never knew, or if she did, she never acknowledged it. But that night, she came to me.”

Ingrid’s mind raced, recalibrating. “She came to you? Why?”

“She was upset. Furious, actually. She’d found out about Erik’s embezzlement, the missing station funds. She’d confronted him earlier that evening, and it had turned ugly. She needed somewhere to go, someone who wasn’t involved. She came to my lab.”

The heater hummed. Ice groaned against the pilings. Ingrid’s fingers curled around the edge of the table, knuckles white.

“What happened then?”

“We talked. She cried. I held her.” Markus’s voice cracked. “I thought maybe, finally, she saw me. But she was just using me as a refuge, a place to hide until she could figure out what to do. And then Anya showed up.”

The name dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Ingrid felt the ripples spreading outward, disturbing everything she thought she knew.

“Anya?”

Markus nodded. His eyes had gone red, his face drawn and haggard. “She came looking for Solveig. When she found us, she… she lost control. I’ve never seen her like that. She was screaming, accusing Solveig of ruining everything, of destroying the station, of betraying their friendship. It was more than anger. It was rage.”

“What were they arguing about?”

“I don’t know the specifics. I couldn’t make sense of it. But Solveig was shouting back, and Anya kept pushing closer, and I realized things were escalating beyond words. I stepped between them. I told Anya to leave.”

Markus’s hands had stopped trembling. Now they lay motionless on the table, as if all the life had drained out of them. “Anya stormed out. Solveig followed her a few minutes later. She said she needed to clear her head, to walk along the shore. I offered to go with her, but she said no. She needed to be alone.”

“And then?”

“And then she drowned.” His voice broke completely. “I waited in my lab. I thought she’d come back. When I heard the alarm, when they pulled her body from the water, I knew I should have gone after her. I should have stopped her. But I was a coward. I panicked.”

Ingrid absorbed the confession, her certainty dissolving into something more complicated. “So you forged the diesel entry to hide the fact that you were with her.”

“I forged it because I didn’t want anyone to know I was the last person to see her alive. I didn’t want to become a suspect. I didn’t want my… feelings for her to become public. I was selfish and afraid, and I thought if I could just make it look like I was working, no one would ask questions.”

He looked up at her, his eyes wet. “I didn’t kill her. I swear on everything I have left. I loved her too much to ever hurt her.”

Ingrid studied him, searching for the lie. His grief seemed genuine, his fear too raw to be performative. But she had been wrong before. She had accused Erik of murder when he was only guilty of theft and cowardice. Now she had accused Markus of forgery, and he had admitted it, but the confession had cracked open a different door entirely.

“Anya,” Ingrid said. “You’re telling me Anya was the last person to see Solveig alive. That they were arguing violently less than an hour before she drowned.”

Markus nodded. “I saw them. I heard them. And when Solveig walked away, Anya followed her.”

The words settled into Ingrid’s chest, cold and heavy. Anya Petrova, her colleague, her friend through this investigation, the one who had helped her analyze the hydrophone tapes, who had sat beside her in the recording room and pointed out the eleven-minute gap. Anya, who had been there every step of the way, guiding the investigation toward Erik, toward the embezzlement, away from herself.

“I need to go.” Ingrid rose from the table, her movements mechanical. She gathered the ledger, the field notes, the evidence that had seemed so damning an hour ago and now felt like a distraction.

“Ingrid, wait.” Markus reached for her, but she pulled back.

“Did you tell anyone else about the argument? About Anya?”

“No. I was too afraid. I thought if I said anything, it would come back to me. I thought I could just… disappear into the background, let the investigation run its course.”

“You let Erik take the blame.”

“I didn’t know they would blame him for murder. I thought the embezzlement would be enough, that someone would find the financial discrepancies and stop there. I never thought it would go this far.”

Ingrid’s jaw tightened. She wanted to be angry, to feel the righteous fury that had carried her into this room. But all she felt was a hollow exhaustion, a sense of the ground shifting beneath her feet. She had been so certain. She had built her case, laid her trap, sprung it with precision. And the result was not a confession of murder, but a redirection toward someone she had trusted.

“I need to check something,” she said. “Stay here. Don’t talk to anyone. If you try to leave this station, I will make sure everyone knows what you’ve done.”

Markus slumped in his chair, a man undone. “I’m not going anywhere.”

Ingrid walked out of the common room and into the corridor. The fluorescent lights flickered overhead, casting their sterile glow on the metal walls. Her footsteps echoed in the emptiness, each step carrying her further from the certainty she had carried into that room.

She needed to think. To process. But more than that, she needed to verify. Markus’s story shifted the frame, but it was still just a story, the desperate confession of a man caught in his own lies. She needed evidence.

The recording room was dark when she entered, the equipment humming in standby mode. She had spent countless hours here over the past weeks, poring over the hydrophone tapes, searching for the truth hidden in the static. Now she approached the access terminal with a different purpose.

The log files.

Every access to the recording system was timestamped and attributed to a user ID. If Anya had been in the system the night Solveig died, if she had accessed the tapes or the editing software, there would be a record.

Ingrid pulled up the archive and scrolled to the date of the drowning. The entries cascaded down the screen, a digital fingerprint of the station’s activities. There was Erik’s ID, the edit that had erased the eleven minutes. There were routine system checks, maintenance logs, the usual background noise of a research station’s operations.

And then, near the bottom of the list, she found it.

Anya’s user ID. A timestamp from the night Solveig died. Not during the drowning itself, but hours later, in the quiet hours before dawn, when the station was asleep and no one would notice.

Ingrid stared at the entry, her pulse quickening. Anya had accessed the recording system after Solveig’s death, after the alarm had been raised, after the body had been pulled from the water. She had been in this room, at this terminal, while Ingrid and the others were dealing with the aftermath.

But there was something else. Another entry, unattributed, sandwiched between Anya’s access and the routine system checks. A ghost in the machine. An access point with no user ID, no timestamp, no explanation. As if someone had found a way to touch the system without leaving a trace.

Ingrid leaned back in the chair, her mind racing. Anya’s presence in the log was damning enough. But this ghost entry, this unlogged access, suggested something more. Someone else had been manipulating the system, someone who knew how to erase their tracks.

The hydrophone tape. The eleven minutes that had been edited out. Erik had been blackmailed into ordering the edit, but who had actually performed it? Who had the technical skill, the access, the motive?

Anya was a marine biologist, not a technician. But she had spent enough time in the recording room to learn the systems. She had helped Ingrid analyze the tapes, had pointed out the gap, had guided the investigation toward Erik and his embezzlement.

Had she been guiding it away from herself?

Ingrid’s hand hovered over the keyboard. She could dig deeper, cross-reference the access logs with the tape archives, search for any other anomalies. But the hour was late, and her mind was clouded with the weight of Markus’s confession and the implications of what she had just found.

She copied the log files to a secure drive and cleared the screen. Tomorrow, she would confront Anya. Tomorrow, she would have answers.

But as she left the recording room and walked back through the station’s empty corridors, she couldn’t shake the feeling that she was being watched. The walls seemed to press in around her, the hum of the heater taking on a sinister undertone. Somewhere in this station, someone knew what she had discovered. Someone who had already killed to protect their secrets.

The lights flickered. A door creaked in the distance. Ingrid quickened her pace, her fingers tightening around the drive in her pocket.

She had come to this station seeking justice for her sister. She had found a web of lies, a ledger of deceit, a tape with a missing eleven minutes. And now, at the center of it all, stood Anya Petrova, her friend and colleague, the woman who had been helping her search for the truth.

But the truth, Ingrid was beginning to understand, was not something you found. It was something you survived.

She reached her quarters and locked the door behind her. The station settled into its nighttime rhythms, ice groaning against the pilings, wind howling across the frozen expanse. Ingrid sat on the edge of her bed, the drive clutched in her hand, and stared at the wall.

Tomorrow, everything would change. Tomorrow, she would look into Anya’s eyes and demand to know what had happened on the shore that night, what words had passed between her and Solveig, what fury had driven her to follow a woman into the dark.

But for now, in the silence of her room, Ingrid allowed herself a moment of doubt. Markus had confessed to forgery and cowardice, to love and fear and self-preservation. His story painted Anya as the villain, the last person to see Solveig alive, the one who followed her into the night.

But Markus was a liar. He had forged the ledger, hidden the truth, let an innocent man take the blame. His confession could be genuine, or it could be another lie, another deflection, another way to shift suspicion away from himself.

Ingrid closed her eyes. In her mind, she saw Solveig’s face, her sister’s smile, the way she had looked the last time they had spoken. She saw the hydrophone tape spinning on the playback deck, the counter ticking toward the missing eleven minutes. She saw Erik’s terror, Markus’s tears, Anya’s steady hand guiding her through the investigation.

And somewhere, in the gaps between the evidence, she saw a truth that refused to take shape. A presence that lurked just beyond the edge of perception, watching, waiting, pulling strings from the shadows.

The blackmailer. The unknown threat. The one who had forced Erik to edit the tape, who had set this entire chain of events in motion.

Who were they? And what did they want?

Ingrid didn’t know. Not yet. But she would find out. She had come too far, lost too much, to stop now.

She lay back on the bed, the drive still clutched against her chest, and let the exhaustion wash over her. Tomorrow would bring confrontation, accusation, revelation. Tomorrow, she would face Anya and demand the truth.

But tonight, in the darkness of her room, she allowed herself to feel the weight of everything she had learned. Her sister had been loved by a man too afraid to save her. Her colleague had been arguing with her moments before she died. Her investigation had been built on lies, and the truth was still hiding in the spaces between.

The heater hummed. The wind howled. And somewhere in the station, the killer watched and waited, their secrets safe for one more night.
