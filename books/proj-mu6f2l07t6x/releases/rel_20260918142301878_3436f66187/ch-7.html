Chapter 7

The Siege of the Scales

The needle on the tripod scale hovered and settled. A constable of the North-West Mounted Police, his fingers stiff inside wool gloves, leaned forward to read the mark. The air at the summit of the Chilkoot Pass was thin and cold, carrying the sound of labored breathing up from the slope below. Before him stood a man bent under a pack frame lashed with flour sacks, a coil of rope, and a tin of baking powder—just one of the thousand loads that had waited on the Dyea flats. Behind that man stood another, and behind him another, a line that wound down the ice staircase and into the murk of the canyon. The constable wrote a figure in the ledger, beside a name, beside a date in late September 1897. He did not look up. The next man stepped forward and lowered his load onto the scale.

The scene was bureaucratic in the way that a border is bureaucratic. The pass itself was not.

The Chilkoot rose from the coastal flats through a series of canyons and shelves, narrowing toward a notch in the granite ridge that formed the boundary between the Alaska panhandle and the Canadian Yukon. Below the summit, the slope steepened to a pitch of nearly thirty-five degrees, a face of ice and packed snow that the stampeders had cut into steps. They called it the Golden Stairs. A man climbing it took two steps forward and slid one back, the load on his back pressing him into the snow.

The steps were worn to a slick glaze by hundreds of boots. At the base of the stairs, at a place called the Scales, freight was reweighed before the final trek to the pass. Within sight of the pass, the Scales hosted a small tent city, including six restaurants, two hotels, a saloon, and many freighting offices and warehouses. Native packers often demanded higher packing rates at this point, knowing the climb ahead and knowing that the men who had hired them had no leverage left.

The Mounted Police had set their post at the summit itself, where the border ran between two posts set in a cairn of rocks. The position was deliberate. Every human being and every pound of goods that would enter the Canadian interior had to pass through this notch. There was no other route that did not involve a journey of hundreds of additional miles or the navigation of the White Pass, which ran parallel to the south but was, in the autumn of 1897, barely a trail. The Chilkoot was the artery. The police intended to control what flowed through it.

The orders had come from Sam Steele, the officer commanding the Mounted Police in the Yukon district. Steele was a man who had built his career on the principle that authority arrived before comfort. He had dispatched small detachments to the passes with instructions that were simple in formulation and brutal in execution: no person was to enter the Yukon Territory without a year’s supply of provisions. The figure was set at roughly two thousand pounds of food and gear per individual. The rule was not advisory. It was a condition of entry, enforced at the point of a pen.

Inspector John Moodie commanded the detachment at the summit. He and a handful of constables occupied a small cabin built of logs hauled up the slope, its walls banked with snow for insulation. Inside, a stove burned continuously, fed by coal that had itself been packed to the summit. The ledgers lay on a rough plank table. Each entry recorded the prospector’s name, the date of passage, and a notation of the outfit’s weight. The entries were methodical. They had to be. The volume of humanity moving through the pass in the late summer and autumn of 1897 exceeded anything the police had anticipated. Thousands of men, and some women, were climbing the Chilkoot with the intention of reaching the Klondike gold fields before winter closed the rivers. Most had no idea what they were walking into.

The region they were walking toward was, in the language of the encyclopedia, a tract of the Canadian Territory of Yukon, comprising an area about equal to that of Rhode Island, and lying chiefly to the east of the Yukon River, where it received the Klondike Creek, near the middle of the Alaskan boundary line. The men at the summit did not think in terms of area. They thought in terms of miles. From the summit to Lake Lindeman, where they would build boats, was a descent of several miles through snow and muskeg. From Lake Lindeman down the chain of lakes and the Yukon River to Dawson City was over five hundred miles of water, already freezing at the margins. They would arrive, if they arrived, in a country where the ground was frozen to bedrock and the temperature in January dropped to sixty below.

The ton rule was the mechanism that stood between them and that arrival. It was not designed to be cruel. It was designed to prevent starvation. The North-West Mounted Police had learned from previous rushes in the Canadian north that men who arrived without sufficient provisions became a burden on the state, or on the small communities that could not feed them, or they died. The figure of two thousand pounds was derived from the caloric requirements of a man working in extreme cold for one year, with a margin for loss, spoilage, and the delay that travel imposed. It was a calculation. It was also, in practice, an act of violence against the body.

A man carrying a pack of sixty to eighty pounds would need to make between twenty-five and thirty-three trips over the Chilkoot to move his outfit from the cache at the base to the summit. The distance from Sheep Camp, the last staging post before the climb, to the summit was roughly four miles. The round trip, then, was eight miles, much of it on ice, at altitude, in weather that could close from clear to whiteout in the time it took to cover that distance. A man working at full capacity could make two trips in a long day. Some made three. The mathematics was unforgiving. A man with a full ton of goods and no pack animals needed two weeks of continuous labor to move his outfit to the summit, assuming the weather held and his body did not fail.

The weather did not always hold. In late September, the first storms of autumn moved across the coast range. Snow fell at the summit and rain fell below it, turning the trail to a channel of mud and melted ice. Men slipped on the Golden Stairs and slid back down, their loads scattering. The steps had to be recut. The line of climbers stalled, and behind the stall, the crowd grew. At Sheep Camp, which had been a clearing with a few tents in August, the population swelled to over a thousand people by early October. They waited in canvas shelters, watching the weather, paying prices for food and firewood that would have been absurd anywhere else and were merely ruinous here.

The police at the summit recorded the passage of these people with a fidelity that bordered on the obsessive. The ledgers grew. Name after name, date after date, weight after weight. The entries did not distinguish between the well-equipped and the desperate, between the experienced hand who had packed across the Chilkoot before the rush and the clerk from Seattle who had never carried a pack heavier than a valise. The rule applied to all. A man who arrived at the summit with less than the required ton was turned back. He did not enter Canada. He descended to Sheep Camp and began the process again, carrying more loads, buying more goods from the merchants who had set up at the base, or hiring packers at rates that climbed with each day the weather worsened.

The ledgers were the instrument of a system that operated through paper. A man whose name appeared in the ledger with a satisfactory weight beside it had passed. He was permitted to descend the far side of the pass to Lake Lindeman, where he would begin building the boat that would carry him down the lakes and the river. A man whose name did not appear, or whose weight was insufficient, was stopped at the cairn. The border was not a line on a map. It was a tripod scale and a notebook.

The counterargument was obvious, and it was made, then and later, by men who saw the Klondike as the last great American gold rush, a free-for-all in which fortune favored the bold and the lucky, and in which the role of the state was peripheral, an afterthought imposed on an event that was already essentially over.

The evidence of the summit told a different story. The Mounted Police had arrived before the bulk of the stampeders. They had established their post and their scales and their ledgers before the crowd formed. The crowd did not overwhelm them. It moved through them, processed by them, weighed and recorded and either passed or turned back. The police were not numerous. A mere handful of this fine military force had sufficed to introduce law and order into the country, from the time of the great rush in 1897. The calm that prevailed on the Canadian side of the frontier, and had prevailed from the first, stood in stark contrast to the confusion reigning on the American side, where no authority held sway.

The confusion was real. On the American side of the pass, at Dyea and Skagway, the trails were unregulated. Men were robbed on the White Pass trail by bandits who operated in the fog and the forest. Soapy Smith would build his empire of fraud in Skagway within months, preying on the stampeders who arrived with money and without sense. The American authorities were absent or indifferent. The Canadian side began at the cairn, and at the cairn, authority was absolute.

The authority was not gentle. A man turned back at the summit had invested weeks of labor and hundreds of dollars to reach that point. That flour had gone up the Golden Stairs on his back. The cold and the wind at the top of the pass, within sight of Canada, had been the reward for the labor, and then the verdict: the outfit was short. The descent waited. It was faster than the climb, but more dangerous. Men fell on the ice and broke bones. Men collapsed from exhaustion and exposure. The police at the summit kept a record of injuries and deaths, and the record grew through October as the weather worsened and the line of stampeders continued to arrive, unaware that the season had turned and that the window for safe passage was closing.

The stampeders’ diaries from this period are uniform in their description of the physical ordeal and varied in their response to it. Some wrote of the climb with a kind of grim pride, noting the number of trips made and the weight carried, as though the pass were a test and the ledger entry a certificate of passage. Others wrote of despair, of loads abandoned on the trail, of goods cached and never retrieved, of the realization that the ton rule had converted a journey into a labor that would consume the autumn and leave them stranded at the lakes with boats unbuilt and the river freezing.

The labor had a structure that the stampeders did not choose but could not escape.

A man moved his outfit in stages. He carried a load from Sheep Camp to a cache point partway up the trail, deposited it, and returned for the next load. When he had moved the entire outfit to the cache point, he advanced it to a higher cache, and so on, in a relay that compressed the distance but multiplied the trips. The trail was marked by these caches, piles of flour and bacon and tools covered with canvas or simply left in the open, marked with the owner’s name on a scrap of board.

The Scales, at the base of the Golden Stairs, became a kind of warehouse under the sky, where goods accumulated and where the Native packers who had worked the trail for generations negotiated their rates with men who had no choice but to pay or to carry.

The police at the summit observed this accumulation with a professional interest that was also a strategic concern. The ton rule required that each man carry his own year’s supply. It did not specify that he had to carry it on his back. But the pass allowed no wagons, no carts, no animals in any number. The White Pass was being widened for pack trains, but the work was slow and the trail was not yet passable for animals in the autumn of 1897. The Chilkoot was a foot trail. It had been a foot trail for centuries, used by the Chilkat Tlingit people who had controlled the route to the interior long before the first white man crossed it. The stampeders were now using it at a volume it had never carried, and the trail was breaking down under the traffic.

The breakdown was visible. The steps of the Golden Stairs were worn to ice. The cache points were expanding into dumps. The line at the summit grew longer, and the weather grew worse, and the police continued to weigh and record, their ledgers filling with names that would later be cross-referenced against the customs records at Dawson City, where the same outfits would be inspected again. The system was redundant by design. A man who passed the summit was not free of the ton rule. He would encounter it again at the border of the Yukon district, at the police post at Tagish, and again at Dawson City itself. The rule followed him like a shadow.

The rule also transformed the men it touched. A stampeders who arrived at the base of the Chilkoot with a dream of gold and a sack of flour was, by the time he reached the summit, something different. The flour had been carried on his back, in stages, over ice, in weather that tried to kill him. The line at the scales had been the final test, the needle settling on a number that determined whether he could proceed. The process was complete. The dream of gold remained, but it was now seated inside a body that understood weight, and distance, and the cold mathematics of survival. The ton rule had taken a man who thought he was chasing fortune and turned him into a man who was hauling freight.

This was the transformation the police intended. Not the hauling, which was a consequence of geography, but the understanding. A man who arrived in Dawson City with a full ton of provisions was a man who could feed himself through the winter. Starvation was not a possibility. Begging was not an option. He would not become a charge on the state or a burden on the community. He might not find gold. He might work his claim for a season and find nothing. But he would eat. The ton rule was a welfare policy enforced at the point of a scale, and its enforcement was the first act of governance that most stampeders encountered on Canadian soil.

The governance was physical. The constables at the summit lived in the same cold as the stampeders, ate the same rations, and climbed the same ice. Their authority derived not from force, which they did not possess in any number, but from the ledger and the scale and the simple fact that the pass was a chokepoint through which every man had to pass. A handful of police could control thousands of stampeders because the geography did the work of concentration. The pass was a door. The police stood at the door and checked papers.

The papers were the ledger. The ledger was the law. The law was the ton. And the ton was two thousand pounds of flour and bacon and beans and sugar and tea and tools and clothing, carried on a man’s back, in loads that bent the spine and compressed the breath, up a staircase of ice cut into a mountain, in weather that could kill, for a distance that multiplied with each relay, for a duration that consumed the autumn and pushed the stampeders toward the freeze-up of the lakes with a pressure that was not lessened by the knowledge that the police at the summit were recording every pound.

The records at the summit grew through October. The storms intensified. Snow accumulated on the Golden Stairs and had to be cleared before the steps could be used. The line at the Scales grew longer. Men waited in the cold for their turn to weigh, and the waiting itself consumed time that they did not have, because the lakes were cooling and the river was slowing and the season was turning against them with a finality that no amount of effort could reverse. Some men abandoned the attempt. They sold their outfits to the merchants at Sheep Camp or Dyea and descended to the coast, taking passage south on the steamers that still called at the tidal flats. Others pushed on, carrying their loads in the diminishing daylight, and the police at the summit recorded their passage and let them through.

The summit post was a machine for the production of order. It took the stampede, which was a phenomenon of mass and momentum and individual frenzy, and passed it through a constraint. The constraint was physical, the pass itself, and bureaucratic, the scale and the ledger. What emerged on the far side was not the same crowd that had entered. It was a population that had been weighed, counted, and found sufficient or insufficient. It was a population that understood, in the fibers of its muscles and the ache of its joints, that the journey to the gold fields was not a rush but a siege. The ton rule had converted the stampede into a labor, and the labor had converted the stampeders into something more disciplined and more desperate than they had been when they landed at Skagway or Dyea with their outfits and their hopes.

The discipline was visible on the far side of the pass. At Lake Lindeman and Lake Bennett, where the trail descended to the water, the stampeders who had passed the summit began building boats. The work was methodical. The police had established additional posts at the lakes, and the men who arrived there had already been processed. They knew the routine. They stacked their goods in ordered piles. They cut spruce and pine from the surrounding forest and sawed planks and hammered nails and caulked seams.

The camps at the lakes were crowded and noisy, but they were organized in a way that the camps at the base of the Chilkoot were not. The ton rule had done its work. The men who had passed through the scales were men who had been measured and found adequate. They carried that measurement in their bodies and in the ordered piles of goods that stood beside their boat frames.

The pile of goods was the visible evidence of the rule’s enforcement. Each pile was a ton. Each ton was a year of survival. The piles stood in rows along the shoreline of the lakes, and between them the boat frames rose, and the sound of hammering carried across the water in the cold air. The police at the lakes recorded the progress of the boat building and the accumulation of goods. Their reports, forwarded to Steele and from Steele to the command in Ottawa, described a population that was preparing for the descent of the Yukon River with a discipline that the command had not expected and that the stampeders themselves would not have believed possible a month earlier.

The transformation was not complete. Men still cheated, still smuggled, still arrived at the lake posts with outfits that were short and had to be supplemented before they were allowed to launch. Men still fought, still drank, still collapsed under the strain. But the system held. The scale at the summit had imposed a standard, and the standard had imposed a discipline, and the discipline had converted a mob into a supply line. The supply line moved goods, not gold. The goods were the ton. The ton was the rule. The rule was the border.

The border was where the change happened. On the American side, the trail was a chaos of competing interests, of merchants and packers and bandits and stampeders, each pursuing his own advantage in the absence of any authority capable of imposing order. On the Canadian side, beginning at the cairn on the summit, the trail was a corridor. The corridor was controlled. The control was exercised by a handful of men with a scale and a ledger, and it was sufficient. The contrast was not lost on the stampeders who passed through it.

Many of them noted it in their diaries and letters, the sudden transition from disorder to order, from the noise and theft of Skagway to the quiet of the Canadian camps. The transition was not gentle. It was a door that opened only for those who could prove, by weight, that they were prepared to survive what lay beyond.

The line at the summit did not thin. It thickened. Through October, the arrivals continued, the weather worsened, and the pile of goods at the Scales grew until it was no longer a collection of individual outfits but a mass, a stationary field of flour and bacon and tools and rope, stacked and restacked and marked and waiting, worth more in aggregate than any single claim on any single creek.

The men who owned those goods could not move them fast enough to clear the pass before the snow closed it. They stood among their piles, and the piles stood among other piles, and the merchants and packers who had been working the margins of the trail since August moved in closer, offering to carry, to sell, to buy, to speculate on the difference between what a man needed and what a man could move. The goods were the gold. The pass was the claim. And the men who controlled the movement of goods through the pass were already building the next empire.
