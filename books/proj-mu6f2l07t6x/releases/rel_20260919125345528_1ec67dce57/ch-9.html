Chapter 9

The First Settled Consequence

The first bite of the crosscut saw into a standing spruce at the shore of Lake Bennett made a sound like tearing linen, and the tree fell north, toward the water, its branches cracking against the frozen crust. The date was late October 1897.

The man who sawed was not a lumberjack. He had been a clerk in a shipping office in Seattle ninety days before. Now he stood in sawdust to his ankles, clearing the stump with a broadaxe he had learned to swing by watching the man at the next tree, who had been a bank teller in San Francisco. Between them, along the flat shore where the lake ice had begun to lock the margin, a third man was fitting a keel board to a frame of ribs cut from green timber, the joints fastened with bolts and nails carried over the Chilkoot Pass on a pack strap. The boat taking shape was flat-bottomed, square-sterned, and wide enough to carry a ton of goods and four men down five hundred miles of the Yukon River to Dawson City.

They were not alone. The shore of Lake Bennett, and the smaller camp at Lake Lindeman two miles to the south, held by the end of October what amounted to a city. It had no streets and no charter. It had tents, sawpits, whipsaws, and a population that the North-West Mounted Police attempted to count.

The constables walked the shoreline and recorded names, occupations, and the dimensions of boats under construction. Their tallies, entered in the patrol diaries that Superintendent Sam Steele had ordered kept at every station along the trail, gave the encampment the character of a census.

The Mounties counted because Steele’s regulations required it: every man who passed the summit scales with his ton of goods was now on Canadian soil, subject to Canadian authority, and the police needed to know who was there, what they carried, and what they intended. The prospectors’ journey had begun for many when they climbed the mountains over the White Pass above Skagway and onward across the Canada–US border to Bennett Lake, or one of its neighboring lakes, where they built barges and floated down the Yukon River to the gold fields around Dawson City.

The numbers were large and they grew. Through the autumn weeks, as the last parties came down from the summit and the trail behind them hardened into ice, the population of the two lakes swelled past five thousand and kept climbing. The tents spread along the beach in rows, their guy-lines frozen into the ground. Smoke from cooking fires hung in the still, cold air over the lake. The sound of saws carried across the ice.

Why build boats? The question had a single, hard answer. The Yukon River was the only highway to the Klondike, and it was frozen.

Lake Bennett sat at the head of navigation, the point where the chain of lakes fed the river that ran north and west to Dawson City, five hundred miles away. There was no overland route.

The coastal mountains that the stampeders had crossed formed a wall, and beyond the lakes the country dropped into a river valley that was impassable on foot for any distance once the snow was deep. The river itself, when open, was the road. When frozen, it was a barrier.

The only way forward was by water, and the only craft available were the ones the stampeders built themselves. At Lakes Bennett and Lindeman, the prospectors camped to build rafts or boats that would take them the final 500 miles (800 km) down the Yukon to Dawson City in the spring.

Why were they all here, concentrated at these two lakes? Because they had passed the test. The ton-of-goods rule that Steele enforced at the summit of the Chilkoot Pass, and the corresponding inspection at the White Pass customs point, had functioned as a filter. Every man who stood at the shore of Lake Bennett had carried or packed his year’s supply of provisions across the mountains, had stacked it on the Canadian side of the boundary, and had been counted, weighed, and recorded by a constable of the North-West Mounted Police. The rule had done more than ensure self-sufficiency. It had sorted the stampede into those who could endure the passage and those who could not, and it had deposited the survivors at the same place. The concentration was not accidental. The designed consequence of a regulatory system that treated the route as a corridor and the stampeders as traffic.

Why now, in the deepening cold? Because winter had locked them in. The lake had frozen at the edges by mid-October, and the ice thickened through November until it held the weight of a man and a sled. The river below the lakes was already frozen solid. No boat could move. The stampeders who reached the lakes in October and November found themselves stopped by a force no regulation could override: the subarctic winter, which closed the Yukon to navigation from October to May. The energy of the stampede, which had been kinetic, a matter of movement and distance, now became potential. It had nowhere to go. It turned inward, into construction.

The construction was collective, not by ideology but by necessity. No single man could build a seaworthy boat alone. The timber had to be felled, dragged to a sawpit, ripped into planks, and shaped. The ribs had to be cut and bent. The bottom boards had to be fitted and caulked. A man working by himself might take six weeks to finish a hull that four men could complete in ten days. The stampeders formed partnerships, drawing on the bonds forged on the trail, and the shore of Lake Bennett became a shipyard.

The sawmills came first. Two were operating by November, their engines burning cordwood cut from the surrounding forest, their blades shrieking through green spruce and pine. The stampeders who owned the sawmills charged by the board foot, and the line of men waiting to have logs ripped into planks stretched along the beach. The price was high. A board foot of dressed lumber at Lake Bennett cost more than the same measure in Seattle, and the stampeders paid it because the alternative was a whipsaw, a pit dug in the frozen ground, and two men working in shifts, one above and one below, pulling the blade up and down through a log for hours to produce a single plank. The whipsaw was slow and brutal. The sawmill was fast and expensive. The stampeders chose speed.

Around the sawmills and the boatyards, a civic structure emerged. It was not planned. It grew from the accumulation of needs that a population of several thousand generates in a confined space.

A tent served as a bakery. Another sold hot meals. A third was a saloon, its bar a plank laid across two barrels, its stock a few bottles of whiskey carried over the pass at considerable expense.

A man who had been a doctor in Portland set up a tent with a sign, and the Mounties recorded his presence. A photographer hauled his equipment over the Chilkoot and set up a darkroom, photographing the boats and their builders for a fee, the images destined for newspapers in Seattle and San Francisco. The photographs, several of which survive in archives, show a beach crowded with hulls in every stage of construction, men in mackinaws and fur hats standing beside them, the lake ice behind them white and flat to the horizon.

The police were there because the lakes were in Canada. Steele had established a detachment at Lake Bennett, and the constables who staffed it carried the same authority they had exercised at the summit.

They inspected boats for seaworthiness, a task that fell to them because no other authority existed and because the river below the lakes was dangerous.

The Yukon, between Lake Bennett and Dawson City, was not a gentle current. It dropped through Miles Canyon, a narrow basalt chasm where the water ran fast and deep, and below the canyon it broke over the White Horse Rapids, a stretch of white water that would wreck boats and drown men in the spring.

The police knew this. Steele had ordered that no boat leave the lakes without inspection, and the constables checked hulls for sound construction, adequate freeboard, and the presence of oars. The boats that failed were held until repaired. After many boats were wrecked and several hundred people died, the North-West Mounted Police (NWMP) introduced safety rules, vetting the boats carefully and forbidding women and children to travel through the rapids.

This was the institutional root. The stampede had begun as a rush, a headlong movement of individuals toward a rumor of gold. The passes had winnowed it. The ton rule had structured it. The winter had stopped it. And now, at the lakes, the combination of climate and regulation had produced something the stampeders had not expected: a society. It was temporary. It was built on frozen ground. But it had the attributes of a settlement, because it had a population, an economy, a system of authority, and a shared purpose. The men who felled trees and bent ribs and caulked seams were not prospectors yet. They were not miners. They were boatbuilders, and their common task gave them a common identity that the trail had not.

The diaries kept at the lakes record the transformation. Men who had crossed the passes in a state of anxious urgency wrote about the dimensions of planks, the price of nails, the angle of a stem, the weight of a keel. They recorded the weather, the temperature, the thickness of the ice. They noted the arrival of new parties, the departure of none. They wrote about the sawmills, the saloons, the price of a meal. They did not write about gold. The gold was five hundred miles north, and it was buried under frozen ground, and the river between here and there was solid. The diaries are the record of a community that had accepted its confinement and turned its energy toward the one task that the confinement permitted.

The population was not uniform. The lakes held the same social stratification that the trails had produced, and the stratification was sharpening. The men who had arrived early, who had claimed the best timber and the best beachfront, who had bought sawmill stock or set up supply tents, occupied a different position from the men who straggled in late, exhausted, their outfits depleted, their cash gone. The early arrivals were becoming a kind of establishment. They had space, tools, and materials. The late arrivals had nothing but their ton of goods and their labor, and they traded the one for the other. A broadaxe was a livelihood. A whipsaw was a partnership waiting to happen. Provisions and willingness to work were all a man had to trade for a share in a boat he had not designed.

The distinction between those who held ground and those who did not was hardening into the structure that would define Dawson City in the summer. The men who controlled the lakeshore, the sawmills, and the supply tents were not mining gold. They were mining the stampeders. They were extracting value from the immobility that the winter imposed, just as the packers and roadhouse keepers on the White Pass had extracted value from the trail’s difficulty. The mechanism was the same: the environment created a bottleneck, and the bottleneck created a market, and the market rewarded those who had arrived first with capital or goods. The lakes were the first place where this stratification settled into something like a class structure, because the lakes were the first place where the stampeders stopped long enough for one to form.

The Mounties saw this. Their reports, filed weekly to Steele at the summit and relayed to the divisional headquarters at Dawson, noted the prices of goods, the number of boats under construction, the state of the ice, and the conduct of the population. Disputes arose, mostly over timber rights and boat space, and the constables settled them. The sick and the injured appeared in the logs. So did the dead. The lakes were not violent, but they were dangerous. Men drowned when they fell through thin ice at the lake’s edge. Men were crushed by falling trees. Men lost fingers to saws. Each case went into the patrol diary, and the diary became the record of a community that was, for the winter, the northernmost settlement of any size in the Yukon.

The forest around the lakes was disappearing. By December, the shoreline for a mile in either direction had been stripped of standing timber, the stumps dotting the snow like stubble. The sawmills consumed cordwood for fuel and logs for lumber, and the boatbuilders consumed planks for hulls. The sound of axes was constant from dawn to dark, and the dark was long. The sun cleared the ridge to the south by ten in the morning and dropped behind it by three in the afternoon. The men worked in the light they had, and the light was brief.

The boats accumulated. By January, the count of hulls in various stages of construction passed a thousand. By February, it passed two thousand. The Mounties walked the beach and counted, and the number they entered in their reports grew every week. The boats sat on cradles of log and dirt, their hulls wrapped in canvas or tarred with pitch, their builders waiting for the one signal that would release them: the breakup of the ice on Lake Bennett.

The wait was the point. The winter had imposed a pause, and the pause had imposed a discipline. The men at the lakes were not the men who had climbed the passes. They had been processed. The trail had taken their horses, their money, their certainty. The Mounties had taken their freedom of movement and given them a set of rules. The winter had taken their momentum and given them a task. What remained was a population that had been refined by each of these forces into something harder and more organized than the stampede that had produced it.

The diaries do not record much reflection on this transformation. The men who wrote them were too busy. A sentence about the weather, a sentence about the boat, a sentence about the price of nails. The entries are short and practical. They do not say that the stampede had become a community. They do not say that the individualistic rush had coalesced into a collective enterprise. They say that the planks are cut, the ribs are bent, the caulking is done, and the ice is still solid. The transformation is visible only in the aggregate, in the count of boats and the count of people and the count of sawmills, in the fact that several thousand men were engaged in the same task on the same shore at the same time, bound by the same season and the same river and the same destination.

The counter-explanation holds that the Klondike was the last great American gold rush, a chaotic, individualistic stampede where fortune favored the bold and the lucky, and where state authority was a late and peripheral imposition. The evidence at Lake Bennett answers this. The state was not peripheral. The Mounties were there, on the frozen beach, inspecting boats and counting people and recording prices. The ton rule had determined who was present and what they carried. The regulations had shaped the route, the pace, and the composition of the population. The winter had done the rest, but the winter acted on a population that the state had already structured. The chaos of the passes was behind them. What lay ahead was the river, and the river demanded preparation, and the preparation demanded cooperation, and the cooperation was supervised by a police force that answered to Ottawa.

The men who built boats at Lake Bennett were not free in the way the stampede had promised. They could build. They could choose partners and trade labor for materials. But they could not leave. The ice held them. The Mounties held them. The river held them. For the winter, they were inmates of a prison whose walls were climate and whose wardens were constables with notebooks. The prison was vast and the air was clean and the work was real, but it was a confinement, and the confinement was the condition that made the community possible.

The community’s purpose was narrow. It existed to build boats and to launch them when the ice broke. It had no other reason. The saloons and the bakeries and the doctor’s tent existed to serve the boatbuilders, and when the boats left, the settlement would dissolve. This was the nature of the place: a temporary society, organized around a single task, bound by a shared calendar that was set not by any human authority but by the Yukon River’s annual cycle of freeze and thaw. Build now, the calendar said. Wait. When the ice goes, go.

The ice would not go until May. Between October and May, the men at the lakes built. They built boats, and they built the infrastructure that boatbuilding required, and they built the social arrangements that the infrastructure required, and they built the habits of cooperation that the social arrangements required. Each layer rested on the one below it, and the foundation was the ice.

The sawmills ran until the timber gave out. By March, the forests around the lakes had been largely cut down, the ground stripped to bare rock and snow for a quarter mile inland. The boats sat on the beach, finished or nearly finished, their hulls stacked side by side like shells on a beachhead. The Mounties counted them. The number was staggering. At Lakes Bennett and Lindeman, the prospectors camped to build rafts or boats that would take them the final five hundred miles down the Yukon to Dawson City in the spring. 7, 124 boats of varying size and quality left in May 1898, when the ice broke, and the armada would be the largest flotilla of improvised craft ever assembled on a North American river. By that time, the forests around the lakes had been largely cut down for timber.

The number is exact because the Mounties counted. They stood at the outlet of Lake Bennett, where the river began, and they tallied every boat that passed. The figure is in their reports. It is one of the hard numbers that the Klondike gold rush produced, and it is the measure of what the winter at the lakes had built.

The boats were frozen in place. The ice held them. The men who built them waited in their tents, through March and April, while the temperature rose slowly from thirty below to thirty above, while the snow softened on the ridges and the lake ice began to darken and crack. The saws had stopped. The timber was gone. The boats sat on their cradles, their hulls sealed, their cargoes stacked inside them, their crews idle for the first time since October. The energy that had gone into construction had nowhere to go. It was pent up, stored in the boats and in the men, waiting for the single signal that would release it.

The signal was the breakup. When the ice on Lake Bennett went, the boats would go with it, and the river would carry them north, and the community that the winter had built would dissolve into the current. The men who had been clerks and tellers and lumberjacks and shipwrights would become passengers, and then they would become prospectors, and then they would reach Dawson City, where the gold was, and where the social structure that had formed on the frozen beach would re-form on the creeks and in the saloons.

But that was ahead. The ice held. The boats sat. The men waited. The armada was ready. It numbered in the thousands, and it was frozen in place on Lake Bennett, and it represented a massive, pent-up energy that had been building since October, and it was waiting for the ice to break.
