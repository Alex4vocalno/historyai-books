Chapter 23

The Mounted Police’s Final Tally

Seen from above, the Yukon River was a frozen corridor stretching from Lake Bennett to the flats at Dawson, a silent channel where nothing moved. The route that had once teemed with activity now lay still. The posts and ledgers of the Mounted Police remained, but the men who had filled the river and trails were gone, leaving the apparatus built to contain them to settle into its first winter of silence.

At the Tagish post, a constable closed the boat registry for the season. The customs house at the summit had recorded its last crossing weeks before. The detachment at Bennett, where the forests had been stripped to stumps and the boat slips stood empty, filed its final return. At Fort Constantine on the Peel and at headquarters in Dawson, the officers of the North-West Mounted Police spent the closing months of 1899 doing what no other institution on the continent was equipped to do. They counted. But the counting had changed. The figures traveling south to Ottawa in the winter dispatches were no longer tallies of men passing through. They were tallies of what remained. The Mounted Police had spent three years measuring a flood. Now they measured what the flood had left behind.

Superintendent Sam Steele, who had commanded the Yukon district through the peak of the stampede and had enforced the order that every man crossing into Canadian territory carry a year’s supply of provisions, sat at his desk in Dawson that winter compiling the consolidated report. The report would go to the Commissioner of the Force in Regina and from there to the Minister of the Interior in Ottawa. It would carry the weight of an institutional reckoning. The gold rush had been narrated in the newspapers of San Francisco, Seattle, New York, and London as a romance of individual daring and sudden fortune. Steele’s report would replace that romance with arithmetic. The arithmetic was not kind to the romance.

The process of consolidation was methodical. Officers at scattered posts had kept their own records throughout the rush, and now those records came together. Bennett had been built during the Klondike Gold Rush of 1897 to 1899 at the end of the White Pass and Chilkoot Trails from the nearby ports of Skagway and Dyea in Alaska. Gold prospectors would pack their supplies over the Coast Mountains from the ports and then build or purchase rafts to take them down the lakes and river to Dawson. Now the sawdust and the canvas and the boat slips were abandoned. The forests around the lake, stripped bare for plank and fuel, stood in rows of raw stumps. The detachment’s return recorded what had been a city and was now a siding on a rail line, its economy collapsed when the White Pass and Yukon Route Railroad was completed in 1900, passing the town for Whitehorse.

The figures moved down the chain of posts. The customs returns at the Chilkoot summit showed the tonnage of goods that had crossed the boundary into Canada. The boat registry at Tagish recorded the number of vessels passing through the network of lakes toward the river. At Fort Constantine and the smaller detachments along the waterway, the daily journals logged traffic and incidents. All of these returns now converged on Dawson, where Steele and his staff assembled them into the year-end summary.

The summary was, in effect, the first comprehensive administrative audit of the gold rush. It took the chaos experienced by every stampeder on every trail and reduced it to verified entries in columns. The entries were stark. They recorded the tons of goods imported, the number of boats that had navigated the river, the official tally of gold output by year, the population counts for Dawson and the outlying posts, the crimes reported and the convictions obtained, the deaths from accident and disease, the cases of destitution, and the cost of maintaining the police presence that had made all of this possible to track.

The tonnage figures alone told a story that the newspapers had never captured. The Mounted Police had required each stampeder to carry a year’s supply of provisions, and they had enforced this requirement at the border by weighing loads on tripod scales. The result was that an enormous quantity of food, clothing, tools, and equipment had been carried or packed over the passes and into the Yukon. The customs receipts recorded the aggregate.

The figure was staggering in its implications, because it represented not the gold extracted but the investment made to extract it. The tons of goods imported vastly exceeded the tons of gold produced. The stampede had been, measured on the balance, a net loss for the majority of those who participated.

This was the calculation that no newspaper had performed, because no newspaper had access to the customs returns. The Mounted Police had the returns because they had stood at the border and collected them.

The boat registry told its own version of the same story. The police had recorded the departure of vessels from Bennett and Lindeman in the spring of 1898. The figure that survived into the consolidated report was precise. Boats of varying size and quality had left the lakes for the run down the Yukon to Dawson, and the police had logged each one at inspection points along the route. The number that reached Dawson was smaller than the number that launched. Some boats had been wrecked in the Miles Canyon or at the Whitehorse rapids. Some had been abandoned along the river when their owners lost heart or lost time or found the passage too difficult. The gap between launch and arrival was the measure of the river’s toll.

The population figures carried the heaviest weight. Dawson City, which had been a First Nations camp before 1896 and had become a city of sixteen to seventeen thousand people by 1898, had seen its population collapse. By 1899, the gold rush had ended, and the town’s population plummeted as all but eight thousand people left. The winter census of 1900 would show the decline continuing. The Mounted Police had counted the people as they came in, and now they counted them as they left. The outflow was not a retreat. It was an exodus. The stampeders had come because they believed the creeks were bottomless. They left because the creeks were not.

The gold output figures provided the final answer to the question that had launched the stampede. The consolidated returns recorded the official production by year, and the numbers were large. Gold had been found on Bonanza Creek and its tributaries, and the yields from the richest claims, especially on Eldorado, had been extraordinary.

Among the gold-bearing creeks, Eldorado stood apart: for the two or three miles where the gravel held pay, it yielded more gold per foot than any other channel in the field, surpassing any known placer deposit of comparable length. The gold was real. The fortunes were real.

But they were concentrated on a handful of creeks and in a handful of claims, and the number of men who had come north to find them vastly exceeded the number of claims available. The gold output, divided by the number of stampeders who had made the journey, produced a per capita figure that was devastating. Most men had spent more getting to the Klondike than they had taken out of it.

An estimated 14, 000, 000 ounces of gold would eventually be taken from the area, half from Bonanza Creek alone, but this was the product of decades of industrial work, not the stampeders’ rush.

The crime statistics completed the picture. The Mounted Police had recorded every offense reported in their jurisdiction, from claim-jumping and theft to assault and murder. The conviction rates were high. The police had maintained what they called perfect quiet on the Canadian side of the frontier, and the reports made clear that this was not a boast but a documented fact. A mere handful of this fine military force had sufficed to introduce law and order into the country, from the time of the great rush in 1897. The perfect quiet that prevailed on the Canadian side stood in stark contrast to conditions on the American side, in Skagway, where Soapy Smith’s criminal regime had held sway until the shootout on Juneau Wharf. The police reports did not make the comparison explicitly. They did not need to. The numbers spoke for themselves.

The statistics on destitution and death were the hardest entries in the ledger. The police had dealt with men who arrived at Dawson or on the creeks with no money, no supplies, and no prospect of work. The cold and scurvy had caused many deaths among the prospectors who reached the Yukon exhausted and undernourished, a fate that befell thousands who attempted other routes like the ‘All-American’ passage from Valdez. The reports recorded the names, dates, and causes of those who had died. These figures were small compared to the total number of stampeders, but each entry in the ledger represented a specific person who had come north with a specific hope and had met a specific end. The police had recorded the names where they could. The names were there, in the registers, beside the dates and the causes and the locations.

The process by which these figures were assembled was itself a story of institutional persistence. The Mounted Police had been present in the Yukon before the stampede began. A police post stood at the mouth of the Fortymile River when George Carmack and Skookum Jim and Dawson Charlie came down from Bonanza Creek to register their claims in August 1896. The claims were filed the next day at the post, and word of the strike traveled fast to the mining camps downstream. From that moment forward, the police served as the continuous administrative presence in the territory. They were not late arrivals. They were not imposed after the fact. They were there before the first stampeder crossed the Chilkoot, and they remained after the last one left.

This continuity was what made the final tally possible. No other institution, American or Canadian, had maintained a continuous presence at every point of the stampede’s route. The American customs service had operated at Skagway and Dyea, but the American side of the trail had been, for the duration of the rush, a zone of disorder that the customs service could not police.

The North-West Mounted Police had established posts at the boundary, at the summit, at Tagish, at Bennett, at Whitehorse, at Five Fingers, and at Dawson. River patrols ran between them. Boat inspections were conducted at every station where vessels passed. Loads were weighed at the border. Claims were registered at the recorder’s office. The dead were counted and named.

The final report that Steele assembled was not a reconstruction from scattered sources. It was a synthesis of records kept continuously, by the same force, under the same authority, from the beginning of the event to its end.

The tension between the chaotic reality of the stampede and the orderly numerical version being created in the report was not merely a matter of presentation. It was a tension between two understandings of what the gold rush had been.

The stampeders who had lived through it understood it as a personal experience. The pass was climbed in the cold, one man at a time, with a pack on the back and a rope over the shoulder. Boats were built on the frozen lake, plank by plank, with saws and pitch and oakum. The rapids were run in open water, with the spray coming over the bow. Gravel was dug, pan by pan, from the creek beds. Men froze, starved, got drunk, got rich, got sick, and got out. The experience was individual, immediate, and unrepeatable.

The police report reduced all of this to numbers. The numbers were accurate. They were verified. They were, in their way, the truth of the event.

But they were a truth that excluded the experience, and the exclusion was the point.

The report was not a narrative. It was an audit. Audits do not capture experience. They capture outcomes.

The outcomes, measured against the scale of the endeavor, were sobering. The tons of goods imported, when set against the tons of gold produced, showed a massive imbalance. The investment of labor and capital had been enormous. The return on that investment had been, for the majority, negative. The number of boats that had launched, when set against the number that had arrived, showed the river’s toll. The population at peak, when set against the population in winter 1900, showed the speed of the collapse. The crimes, when set against the convictions, showed the efficiency of the police presence. The deaths, when set against the total population, showed the human cost. Each figure was a column in a ledger. The ledger was the gold rush, stripped of its romance and reduced to its bones.

Steele’s report did not draw conclusions. It presented the figures. The figures were the conclusions. The gold rush had been, by the measure of the Mounted Police’s own records, a managed event. The ton-of-goods requirement had ensured that stampeders arrived in the Yukon with enough supplies to survive. The registration of claims had prevented the chaos of disputed ground that characterized earlier gold rushes. The police presence on the trails and the river had prevented the lawlessness that had made Skagway a byword for corruption. The customs collection had generated revenue for the territory. The medical and relief services had mitigated the worst effects of disease and destitution. The management had not prevented the suffering. The suffering was in the ledger. But the management had prevented the suffering from becoming catastrophe, and the ledger was the proof.

The counter-argument was obvious, and the report did not address it. The popular version of the rush held that the Klondike was a free-for-all where bold men seized fortune by force of will, and that government authority was an afterthought, irrelevant to the real action on the ground. The figures in the report answered this argument without acknowledging it. The gold rush had been neither wild nor uncontrollable on the Canadian side. It had been regulated, supervised, and recorded from start to finish. The regulation had not prevented the stampede. It had channeled it. The supervision had not prevented the suffering. It had contained it. The recording had not prevented the chaos. It had captured it. The capture was the final act of the institution. The gold rush was over. The ledger was closed.

The closure of the ledger was not a symbolic act. It was a bureaucratic one. The figures went to Ottawa. They were filed. They became part of the administrative record of the North-West Territories. They would be consulted by future officials, cited in future reports, and used as the basis for future decisions about the governance of the Yukon. They were the first cold accounting of the gold rush, and they would remain the definitive accounting, because no other record had been kept with the same continuity and the same authority. The newspapers had told stories. The diaries had recorded experiences. The police reports had counted everything. The counting was the institution’s final service to the event it had governed.

The men who had been counted were, by the winter of 1899 and 1900, mostly gone. They had left Dawson and the creeks and the trails. They had gone south, back to the cities and the farms and the jobs they had left behind.

Some had gone to Nome, where a new gold strike on the beaches of the Seward Peninsula had drawn the stampeders who could not stop stampeding; about 2, 500 left Dawson alone during August and September of 1899. Some had gone to Atlin, where gold had been found in August 1898 at the lake at the head of the Yukon River, generating a flurry of interest and a minor stampede that drew men away from the Klondike.

The final trigger for the collapse of the Klondike rush was the discovery of gold elsewhere in Canada and Alaska. The stampeders who had come to the Klondike because it was the only gold field in the news now left because it was not.

The Mounted Police recorded the departure. The ledger showed the outflow. The outflow was the event’s ending.

What remained was the ground. The creeks that had been staked and worked and fought over were still there, and the claims registered at the police post were still on the books. The gold was still in the gravel. The extraction would continue, but it would no longer be the work of individual men with pans and shovels. Starting approximately ten years later, the industrial mining that would follow, with dredges that scooped the creek beds and reworked the landscape, was already on the horizon. The dredges would do what the stampeders had done, but mechanically, systematically, and on a scale that made the individual prospector obsolete. The age of the stampeders was ending. The age of the machines was beginning. The Mounted Police’s final tally marked the boundary between the two.

The tally also marked the boundary between two kinds of reckoning. The stampeders had reckoned their adventure in personal terms. The journey was made, the gamble was taken, the fortune was found or lost, and the stampeder moved on. The police reckoned the adventure in institutional terms. Arrivals and departures were counted, goods and gold were weighed, crimes and convictions were logged, the living and the dead were recorded. The two reckonings described the same event. They did not describe the same experience. The gap between them was the gap between a diary entry and a census return. Both were true. Neither was complete. The diary told you what it felt like. The census told you what it was.

The census returns for the winter of 1900 showed Dawson at roughly eight thousand and falling. The outlying posts showed smaller numbers still. The creeks showed the few hundred men who had stayed to work their claims through the winter, the men who had found enough gold to justify remaining, or who had been unable to leave. These men were on what the records called claimed ground. Their claims were registered. Their names were in the ledger. Their production was recorded. They were the residue of the stampede, the men for whom the Klondike had been, in financial terms, worth the journey. They were a minority. The ledger showed this with the clarity of a column of figures.

The figures were the police’s final word on the event. Every point of the route had been manned, and everything that passed had been recorded. Now the absence was recorded instead. The river was frozen. The trails were empty. The posts were still staffed. The ledgers were still open. But the entries were fewer. The counting was nearly done.

The final, filed tally of the dead hands off the pressure to move from abstract numbers back to the specific, scarred ground where the dream began.

The names in the police registers, the men recorded as having died of scurvy or cold or accident on the creeks and the trails, were not abstractions. They were men who had staked claims on Bonanza and Eldorado and Hunker and Dominion, or who had never reached the creeks at all, or who had reached them and found no ground left to stake. The ledger closed on their names. The ground remained, frozen and quiet, waiting for the thaw and the dredges and the next chapter of extraction.

But the men who had come to find their fortune in the gravel of the Klondike, the men whose names filled the columns of the Mounted Police reports, had been counted, weighed, and recorded. The institution that had measured them stood at the border and at the summit and at the mouth of the river, and it would stand there after they were gone, because standing there was what it did. The gold rush was over. The tally was complete. The ground held what it had always held, and the names in the ledger were the names of men who had come to take it and had, in most cases, failed.
