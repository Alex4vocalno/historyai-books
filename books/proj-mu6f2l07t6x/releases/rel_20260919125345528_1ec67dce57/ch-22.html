Chapter 22

The Last Boat Down the River

In the late summer of 1899, the men who went to Nome were looking for a country without constables, without customs posts, without the ton-of-goods rule that had turned a mountain trail into a administered corridor. What they found at the Bering Sea coast was a beach with no trees, no fresh water, and no law at all, and the freedom they had chased south cost most of them whatever they had left. But for the men still at Lake Bennett, the question was simpler and more final: whether to launch a boat at all.

Two records sit side by side in the archives of the North-West Mounted Police, and the distance between them measures a collapse. On May 29, 1898, the police tally at the Tagish post, where every vessel passing from the headwater lakes toward the Yukon River was counted and inspected, recorded over seven hundred boats in a single day’s count, a figure drawn from the diaries of stampeders who wrote of the armada shoving off from Bennett in a single, chaotic tide, and from the police logs that tried to impose order on what looked like a maritime evacuation. By the last week of August 1899, the same post recorded three boats and four men. The contrast is not merely numerical. It is the difference between a civilization in motion and a trail being reclaimed by silence.

The spring launch of 1898 had been the stampede’s central, unifying ordeal, the moment when every stampeders’ investment of money, labor, and hope converged on a single body of water.

Seven thousand boats, by the police count, left the headwater lakes that May, built from the spruce and pine forests that had been stripped bare from every shore within walking distance of the building sites. The break-up of the ice that spring had been greeted with something close to delirium.

Men who had spent the winter in canvas tents on the frozen lake, sawing planks by hand, caulking seams with pitch and oakum, drinking bad coffee and worse whiskey, waiting for the moment when the ice would release its grip, had rushed to the water’s edge and shoved their makeshift vessels in and pointed them downstream toward Dawson.

The Miles Canyon and the White Horse Rapids waited below, and the police had placed a constable at the head of the canyon to inspect every boat for seaworthiness, but the current was fast and the impatience was faster. Boats swamped. Men drowned. The rest went through.

The North-West Mounted Police had introduced safety rules, vetting the boats carefully and forbidding women and children to travel through the rapids, but the river remained a dangerous, documented bottleneck.

By the late summer of 1899, that current was carrying almost no one.

The Tagish post sat at the outlet of the lake system, where the water narrowed and began its run toward the river. It had been established as a customs and inspection point, part of the police infrastructure that made the route legible and governable, a node in what the records themselves constituted: a paper trail of permits, tallies, and manifests that turned a wilderness crossing into a documented corridor. Every boat that passed was counted. Every man was asked his destination. The constables recorded the numbers in ledgers that now sit in the archives, and the ledgers tell the story of a tide going out.

Through June 1899, the counts fell from dozens per week to single digits. The men who passed in those weeks were different from the men who had passed the year before. They had arrived late. Some had spent a winter cutting wood or hauling freight for someone else’s expedition. Some were sick. Some were broke. Some had simply been unable to accept that the moment had passed them by.

The diaries that survive from this period are shorter than the diaries of the spring before. The entries contract. Where a man in May 1898 might have written pages about the weather, the boat next door, the price of bacon, the sound of the ice cracking, a man at Bennett in August 1899 might write a line. A date. A temperature. Nothing else. The entries read like a man running out of words the way he had run out of money: not all at once, but by degrees, each day’s silence a little deeper than the last.

The traders at Bennett saw it first. The town that had sprung up at the head of the lake, a raw, sprawling settlement of sawmills, saloons, provision stores, and boatyards, had been, for a few months in 1898, one of the busiest places on the continent. Men had queued at the sawmills to buy planks. They had paid exorbitant prices for nails, pitch, canvas, oakum, anything that could make a boat float.

The traders had made their money not from gold but from the stampede itself, from the engine of extraction and consumption that the Mounted Police requirement of a ton of goods had set in motion. The ton of goods regulation had created a market, and the market had created Bennett. One of its establishments was the Arctic Hotel, a combination saloon, restaurant, and hotel set up by Friedrich “Fred” Trump and Ernest Levin, a venture that thrived on the passage of stampeders.

Now the market was gone. The sawmills stood idle. The provision stores had shipped their remaining stock back to Skagway or south to Seattle. The saloons were dark. The boatyards, where hired carpenters had once worked day and night building vessels for men who could not build their own, were dismantled, their lumber sold for scrap or left where it stood. A trader at Bennett who had watched the spring armada depart wrote of the silence that settled over the lake after the last boats had gone, not the silence of a place that had never been inhabited, but the heavier silence of a place that had been full and was now empty.

The boats told the story. Along the shore of Lake Bennett, where the spruce and pine had been cut to the stumps to supply the boatyards, the frames of abandoned vessels lay in the mud and the brush. Some were half-built: a keel laid, ribs bent, planks started and never finished, the builder having run out of money or hope or both before the hull could be closed. Some were finished but never launched, sitting on cradles of logs and dirt, their pitch dried and cracked, their caulking loosened by a winter’s freeze and a summer’s sun. Some had been launched, made it partway down the lake, and been pulled ashore and abandoned when their owners realized they could not make the river or could not pay the customs fee or had simply decided, at the last moment, that the gold was not waiting for them.

The police at Tagish recorded the boats that passed. They did not record the boats that did not. But the boats that did not pass were the more telling figure, because they represented the men who had invested everything in the stampede’s central promise, that if you built a boat and carried a ton of goods and made it down the river, you would arrive at the gold fields and find your claim, and who had lost. The abandoned boat frames on the lakeshore were the physical residue of that loss, the material evidence of a collective endeavor ending not in triumph or disaster but in a slow, exhausted fade.

The causal mechanism was simple and devastating. News had filtered back. The claims on Bonanza and Eldorado were taken, had been taken since 1896, and the men who held them were not selling. The creeks that had produced the gold were being worked by the men who had arrived first, and the men who had arrived in the great armada of 1898 had found, in too many cases, that the gold was already gone.

Some had found work as laborers on the claims of others, digging for wages what they had come to dig for themselves. Some had found nothing.

The stories came back up the river, not the stories of a ton of gold on a steamship in Seattle, but the stories of men walking away from claims they had staked and abandoned, of creeks that yielded nothing, of a town that was becoming, month by month, less a gold rush camp and more a company town where the companies were the men who had been lucky in 1896. The richest gravels were worked out before 1910, and most of the population had left the Klondike for Alaska and other regions; so that Dawson, which for a time was a bustling city of more than 30, 000, was emptying.

Then came Nome. In the summer of 1899, gold was found on the beaches at Nome, on the Alaska coast, and the news hit Dawson like a second stampede, this time heading out. The men who had come to the Klondike to find their fortune and had not found it packed their gear and headed down the Yukon to St. Michael and the coast. The men who had found a little gold, enough to live on but not enough to justify staying, did the same. The exodus was immediate and dramatic. Dawson emptied. The saloons closed. The merchants who had made fortunes selling supplies to stampeders saw their customers leave in a single season. About 2, 500 left Dawson alone during August and September 1899, a flood of prospectors from across the region heading for the new goldfields, marking the end of the Klondike Rush.

The departure of the last boats from Bennett in the late summer and fall of 1899 marked the definitive end of the stampede’s central, unifying ordeal. The route that had defined the Klondike experience, the climb over the Chilkoot or the White Pass, the winter on the lakes, the building of the boat, the run down the river, was no longer a route. It was a memory. The men who still passed the Tagish post in those final weeks were not part of a stampede. They were the residue of one.

The Mounted Police at Tagish recorded the passing of these last boats with the same bureaucratic discipline they had applied to the seven thousand. Each boat was logged. Each man was asked his name and destination. The entries in the ledger for August and September 1899 are sparse, a few lines per week where there had once been pages per day. The constable who made the entries was doing his duty, recording the end of something he had been stationed to oversee, and the very spareness of the record carries its own weight. The paper trail that had made the stampede legible was now making its dissolution legible. The same system that had counted the armada was counting its disappearance.

What the ledger could not record was the state of the men in those last boats.

They were, by every account that survives, a different order of traveler from the men who had passed the year before. The men of May 1898 had been hopeful, frightened, excited, ignorant, and determined, a volatile compound that had fueled the stampede and that the police had worked to channel into something survivable.

The men of August 1899 were something else. They were the men who had not given up, which is not the same as saying they were hopeful.

They were the stubborn, the unlucky, and the lost. Some did not know that Nome had drawn the crowd away. Some did not know that the claims they were heading for had been worked out or were underwater or had never existed.

They were heading down a river toward a town that was emptying, carrying supplies for a gold rush that was over.

The contrast with the spring launch was not merely in the numbers. It was in the nature of the departure. In May 1898, the departure from Bennett had been a collective act, thousands of men shoving off on the same day, the lake covered with boats, the air filled with shouting and the sound of hammers and the smoke of campfires. In August 1899, the departure was solitary. A single boat. Two men. Sometimes one. The lake was open water, empty except for the occasional steamer heading north with freight for the few traders still at Bennett, and the shore was littered with the debris of the year before: old campfire rings, collapsed tents, the skeletons of boats.

The forests around the lakes told their own version of the story. The land that had been stripped bare for timber in 1897 and 1898 was growing back. The stumps were sprouting new shoots. The sawdust that had covered the ground at the building sites was being washed away by rain and covered by moss and grass. The physical landscape was reclaiming what the stampede had built, and the reclamation was faster than anyone would have expected.

The trees had been cut in a frenzy, but the soil was good and the rain was heavy and the growing season, short as it was, was enough to start the process of erasure. In a few years, a traveler passing Lake Bennett would see a forest, not a boatyard, and would have to look closely to find the stumps and the sawdust and the rusted nails that marked where seven thousand boats had been built.

The White Pass and Yukon Route Railroad, completed in 1900, would soon run right to Whitehorse, passing the town. This led the entire economy of Bennett, based on stampeders and river travelers, to collapse, rendering the silent lake and its abandoned boatyards a permanent relic.

The traders who remained at Bennett watched the traffic slow and then stop. Some of them had been there since the beginning, men who had set up provision stores in 1897, when the first wave of stampeders arrived at the lake and needed food and tools and lumber and advice. They had made their money from the stampede, not from the gold. Their profit was in the passage, not the arrival. Now the passage was ending, and with it their livelihood. Some packed up and followed the stampeders north to Nome, where a new passage was beginning and a new market was opening. Some stayed, watching the lake go quiet, selling the last of their stock to the last of the travelers, and then closing the door.

The boats that passed the Tagish post in those final weeks were often not the boats their builders had intended to take. Men who had arrived at Bennett with plans to build a sturdy scow or a flat-bottomed river boat had, in many cases, given up on the building and bought a boat from someone who was leaving, someone who had built a boat, made it down the lake, and decided, at Tagish or at Bennett, that the river was not for them.

These secondhand boats were often in poor repair, their caulking loosened, their planks warped, their fittings rusted. The police at Tagish inspected them as they had inspected every boat, checking for seaworthiness, and in some cases turned them back. A boat that could not make the river was a boat that would kill its crew, and the police had seen enough drownings in the canyon and the rapids to know the difference.

The men who were turned back did not always go back. Some sold their boats on the spot to other travelers, men heading south, back to Skagway, back to the world, who wanted a boat for the lake crossing on the way out. The price was low. A boat that had cost a hundred dollars to build might sell for ten, or five, or for the price of a meal. The scrap lumber was worth more than the vessel, because the vessel had no destination and the lumber could be burned for heat.

This was the final economy of the stampede, not the economy of gold, not the economy of supplies sold at a premium to men heading north, but the economy of departure, of selloff, of liquidation.

The ton of goods that the police had required each man to carry, the regulation that had made the stampede a structured, survivable event and that had created the market for suppliers and packers and traders, was now being sold for pennies.

Men who had hauled a ton of goods over the Chilkoot, who had spent a winter at Bennett eating through their supplies, who had built a boat and loaded it with their remaining provisions and headed down the river, were now selling those provisions and that boat to get the cash to go south. The stampede engine, which had run on the self-reinforcing logic of requirement and supply and regulation, was sputtering out.

The fuel was gone. The gold that had drawn the men was still in the ground, but the ground was taken, and the men who owned it were not sharing.

The police at Tagish were among the last to see the stampede end, because they sat at the bottleneck. Every man who came down the lakes had to pass their post. Every boat was counted. The ledger entries for the fall of 1899 are the last record of the stampede as a physical event, the last evidence that men were still coming, still building, still launching boats on a river that no longer led to a gold rush. After the last entry, the ledger goes blank. The constable closed the book, or the book was closed for him, and the post at Tagish became what it had been before the stampede: a quiet station on a northern lake, staffed by a policeman whose job was no longer to count a tide but to watch an empty water.

The men who built the last boats and launched them on Lake Bennett in the fall of 1899 were, in a sense, the truest stampeders, the men who held on longest, who believed most stubbornly, who were last to accept that the world had moved on. They were also the men who had least to show for their belief. The gold they sought was not waiting at the end of the river. The town they aimed for was emptying. The claims they hoped to stake were already worked or already filed. They were heading down a river toward a future that had already happened, and the river itself, the Yukon, the great, broad, fast, cold river that had carried seven thousand boats to Dawson, was carrying them toward a town that no longer needed them.

The last boats on Lake Bennett were not the last boats on the Yukon River. The river was still carrying traffic, steamers heading north and south, freight boats carrying supplies to the mining camps, poling boats carrying men to their claims. But those boats were part of a different economy, the economy of extraction, of corporate mining, of a settled industry that had replaced the stampede. The amateur-built boats, the scows and flat-bottoms and makeshift vessels that had defined the stampede experience, were gone. The last of them passed the Tagish post in the weeks before the ledger went blank, and the men in them were the last men to experience the stampede as the stampeders had experienced it: as a journey, an ordeal, a passage from the world they knew to the world they hoped to find.

The world they hoped to find was no longer there. Dawson was still standing, the claims were still producing, the gold was still coming out of the ground. But the Dawson they reached was not the Dawson they had imagined. It was not a town of opportunity, where a man with a pick and a pan and a ton of goods could find his fortune. It was a town of companies, of wage laborers, of men who had been there long enough to have stopped dreaming. The stampede was over, and the last men to arrive were the men who had missed it.

Along the shore of Lake Bennett, where the water lapped at the stumps of the cut forest and the mud held the rusted nails and the rotted planks of a thousand abandoned boats, the physical evidence of the stampede was already being absorbed back into the land. The boat frames that had been built and never launched. The half-finished hulls. The cradles of logs and dirt that had held vessels through the winter. The campsites where men had lived and worked and waited. All of it was going back to the forest and the lake, the wood softening and sinking, the metal rusting into the soil, the sawdust washing into the water.

The image of the abandoned boat frame on the lakeshore, a keel laid, ribs bent, planks started and never finished, the whole structure listing in the mud as the forest grew back around it, was the image of a finished event. It demanded an accounting. Not the romantic accounting of gold found and fortunes made, but the institutional accounting of what had been spent, what had been required, what had been lost, and what had been gained. The Mounted Police, who had stood at the summit of the Chilkoot and the head of the canyon and the outlet of the lake, who had counted every boat and weighed every load and recorded every name, were the ones who would make that accounting. Their tallies, their customs records, their claim registries, their reports, the paper trail that had made the stampede legible and governable, were now the record of its end.
