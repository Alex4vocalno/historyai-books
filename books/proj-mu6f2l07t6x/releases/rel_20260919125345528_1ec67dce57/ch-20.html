Chapter 20

The Women of the Dance Halls

Even as the merchant steamer crates were being unloaded on the beach at Nome in the spring of 1899, back in Dawson the previous summer, a different kind of economy was being built.

The merchants trailed the miners north, and the miners chased the gold, but the women who came after followed something less obvious: the architecture of need that the stampede had left behind. Dawson in the summer of 1898 held perhaps thirty thousand people. Eight percent of those living in the Klondike territory were women, and in towns like Dawson this rose to twelve percent. Many arrived with husbands or families. Others travelled alone. Most came for the same reasons as the men, to make money, to escape what they had left, to find a life the settled states could not offer.

What they found was a town built of wood, isolated, unsanitary, plagued by fires and epidemics, where the wealthiest prospectors spent extravagantly in the saloons and where the indigenous Hän, who had lived along the Yukon for generations, had been moved into a reserve to make way for the stampeders, and many died. Into this demographic imbalance walked women who understood that the imbalance was a market.

Two scenes from the same summer frame the hierarchy. The Palace Grand Theater opened on Front Street in 1899, a wooden structure built to hold the overflow of men who wanted entertainment more elaborate than the saloons provided. The floor was cleared for dancing. A stage ran across the far wall. The performers, singers and dancers, were the elite of Dawson’s female economy, highly paid and publicly celebrated, their names printed in the Dawson newspapers alongside mining deals and steamship arrivals.

On the same night the Palace Grand held its opening performance, the police court docket across the street recorded the arrest of a woman from Paradise Alley, the row of small cabins behind Front Street where sex workers lived and worked, for creating a disturbance. The juxtaposition was structural. The same town paid one woman to dance on a stage and another to stand in a lineup, and the line between the two economies was drawn by capital, by the degree of control a woman held over her labor, and by the willingness of the male majority to distinguish between entertainment they could watch and services they could purchase.

At the top of the female hierarchy stood the women who arrived with capital or acquired it quickly.

Belinda Mulrooney reached the Klondike in early 1897, before the mass of stampeders, carrying a consignment of cloth and hot water bottles. She sold the cloth to miners who needed tents, clothing, and blankets. She sold the hot water bottles to men suffering from cold or illness. With the profits she first built a roadhouse at Grand Forks and later a grand hotel in Dawson. Mulrooney’s path was the merchant’s path, the same supply-chain logic that drove the traders on Front Street, but she navigated it as a woman in a town where women were scarce and therefore visible. A woman who owned a hotel in Dawson was a figure of social authority.

Her establishment served as meeting place, lodging house, and venue for the informal negotiations that drove the mining economy. She leveraged that authority, investing in various mining and business ventures with her brother, and became one of the wealthiest women in the Klondike.

Wealthier women with capital might invest in mines and other businesses. Mulrooney was the most prominent example, but she was not alone.

Martha Black arrived in 1898 and invested in mining claims, eventually becoming a figure of social and political standing in the Yukon. These women operated within the same commercial framework as male merchants and prospectors, buying and selling, filing claims, recording transfers. Their sex did not exclude them from the economic machinery of the stampede. It made them conspicuous within it, and that conspicuousness, in a town where the ratio of men to women was roughly eight to one, gave them a form of leverage unavailable in the settled south.

A woman who walked into a mining recorder’s office to file a claim was noticed. A woman who opened a restaurant on Front Street was noticed. The noticing carried the assumptions of a male-dominated society, but it provided a platform, and some women used that platform with precision.

Beneath the entrepreneurs sat the performers, women whose fame was a convertible asset. The dance halls of Dawson were economic engines, generating revenue through ticket sales, drinks, and the commissions dancers earned on the men they persuaded to buy champagne or whiskey at inflated prices. The women who worked in these halls occupied a position that was public, celebrated, and contingent. Their earnings depended on their ability to attract and hold male attention. Their fame, amplified by the newspapers and by the dense social life of a town where thirty thousand people lived in a space meant for a fraction of that number, could be parlayed into wealth or a ticket out.

Kathleen Rockwell, whose memoirs provide one of the most detailed accounts of the dance hall economy, described a world where a woman’s earning power was tied to her visibility and her skill in managing the desires of men who had gold and loneliness in equal measure. The dance hall was a marketplace, and the commodity was the performance of intimacy, the illusion, sustained for the length of a dance or a drink, that the man was not alone.

Nellie Lamore, known as “the Pig,” was another celebrated performer, and her nickname suggests the rough, carnivalesque texture of Dawson’s entertainment culture.

The women who performed under such names were public figures, known by the names they used on stage, and their reputations were built on the same principles that governed the mining economy: discovery, claim, extraction. A performer who could draw a crowd claimed a portion of the town’s disposable wealth, and that portion could be substantial. The newspapers that had encouraged so many to travel to the Klondike chronicled the doings of these women with the same attention they gave to gold strikes and steamship arrivals.

A dance hall queen in Dawson occupied a social position with no exact equivalent in San Francisco or Seattle. She was famous within the town’s borders, and that fame was a form of claimed ground, a territory as definite within its sphere as any mining claim staked on Bonanza or Eldorado Creek.

The parallel is exact. The mining claim was a legal territory, bounded by stakes and recorded in a ledger, that gave its holder the right to extract gold from a specific piece of ground. The dance hall performer’s fame was a social territory, bounded by the walls of the theater and the columns of the newspaper, that gave its holder the right to extract money from the pool of male desire. Both were claims. Both were recorded. Both were transferable, contestable, and subject to the economic forces that governed the town. Both depended on the continued influx of men willing to spend, men who had gold or the expectation of gold, and who were willing to part with it for warmth, beauty, or the relief of human company.

At the bottom of the hierarchy, and in many ways at its foundation, were the women who worked on the line in Paradise Alley. The line was a row of small cabins behind Front Street where sex workers sold their labor to the miners and laborers of Dawson. Their economic reality was harsh. They earned less than the performers, less than the entrepreneurs, and they bore the brunt of the town’s violence, disease, and moral judgment.

But their work was structured, and the structure was imposed by the North-West Mounted Police. Under Sam Steele’s command, the police regulated the line with the same bureaucratic precision they applied to the customs inspections at the passes and the ton-of-goods requirement on the trails.

The women on the line were required to register, to submit to medical inspections, and to confine their work to the designated area. The police court records from Dawson in 1898 and 1899 contain entries for arrests on charges of creating a disturbance, vagrancy, and disorderly conduct, charges applied almost exclusively to the women of Paradise Alley.

The regulation was not humane. It was administrative. The Mounted Police did not regulate the line out of concern for the women who worked there. They regulated it because unregulated sex work in a town of thirty thousand men, most of them young, armed, and drinking, would have produced violence and disease that would have overwhelmed the detachment and threatened the commercial order on which the Klondike economy depended.

The regulation of the line was an extension of the ton-of-goods rule: a measure designed to make the stampede survivable, not by protecting the individual but by preserving the system. The women on the line were part of that system. Their labor, like the labor of the packers on the Chilkoot and the merchants on Front Street, kept the machinery running. They provided a service for which there was enormous demand, and they did so under conditions determined by the demographic facts of the stampede and the regulatory framework of the police.

The numbers ground the point. Of the estimated thirty to forty thousand people who reached Dawson during the gold rush, roughly fifteen to twenty thousand became prospectors. The rest spent their savings, their time, and their health in the Klondike and left with nothing.

The women of the dance halls, the hotels, and the line served this majority. They served the men who had failed to find gold and who, in failing, had become the raw material of a different economy, one built not on extraction but on consumption. The stampede engine, which had begun with the requirement that every stampeder carry a ton of goods, had by 1898 and 1899 absorbed the failure of the stampeders themselves.

The men who had carried their loads over the passes, built their boats at Lake Bennett, run the Yukon to Dawson, and found no gold became the market for the women who had followed them north. The system did not care whether a man found gold. It cared that he had money to spend, and it ensured there were places to spend it.

The transformation of Dawson from a ramshackle boom town into a more sedate, conservative municipality was well under way by 1899, and it affected the women as much as the men. Modern luxuries appeared: zinc bathtubs, pianos, billiard tables, Brussels carpets in hotel dining rooms, menus printed in French and invitational balls. The raw energy of the early stampede was giving way to something more ordered and more closely governed. The dance halls did not disappear, but they were subject to the same pressures of respectability that shaped every other aspect of Dawson’s life. The newspapers that had chronicled the dance hall queens with fascination began to treat them with a more measured tone. The police court records show an increase in arrests and fines as the regulatory apparatus tightened.

The telegraph, which by 1899 stretched from Skagway to Dawson, accelerated the shift. Instant international contact meant the news from Dawson was no longer delayed by weeks or months. The actions of the police, the decisions of the courts, the conditions on the line in Paradise Alley could be reported to the outside world as they happened. The knowledge that the outside world was watching imposed a discipline the town had not felt during the isolated period of the early rush. The women of Dawson were now operating in a town connected to the world, and that connection brought the expectations of the society they had left behind.

Mulrooney’s trajectory illustrates the range of outcomes. She arrived with goods, sold them at a profit, invested in real estate and mining claims, built the Fairview Hotel, and became one of the wealthiest business figures in the Klondike. Her wealth was the product of the same calculation that drove the merchant supply chain: identify a demand, supply it, reinvest the profit in the infrastructure of the demand. She identified the demand for shelter and warmth and supplied it.

In a town where women were scarce, her visibility was an advantage, a form of conspicuousness that drew customers and gave her social standing she could leverage in negotiations. She claimed ground in Dawson not by staking a mining claim but by building a hotel, and the hotel was as much a form of claimed ground as any piece of creek bed. It was a territory she controlled, from which she extracted profit, and which she could defend against competitors.

The performers’ economy operated on a different principle. Rockwell and Lamore did not own real estate or mining claims, at least not initially. Their asset was fame, and fame was a product of the town’s social infrastructure: the theatres, the newspapers, the gossip networks, the enclosed social world of a town where thirty thousand people lived in a space meant for far fewer. In this environment a performer’s reputation could be built quickly and could translate into substantial earnings. But the earnings were contingent. They depended on the continued flow of men into Dawson, on the willingness of those men to spend, and on the performer’s ability to maintain her position in a hierarchy that was competitive and fluid. The dance hall was claimed ground that could be lost overnight, to a younger performer, to a change in fashion, to the departure of the most generous patrons.

The women on the line had the least secure position. They were subject to police regulation, to the violence of individual clients, to the diseases that spread through the crowded cabins, and to the moral judgment of a town that simultaneously demanded and condemned their services. Their earnings were lower, and their ability to accumulate wealth or leave the line was limited by the same forces that had driven them there: the lack of capital, the lack of alternatives, and the demographic fact that in a town where twelve percent were women, the demand for sex was constant and the supply of women willing to meet it was small enough to be regulated, contained, and exploited by everyone except the women themselves.

The North-West Mounted Police records from Dawson in 1898 and 1899 show the pattern of enforcement. Women on the line were arrested for disturbing the peace, for being drunk in public, for fighting, for failing to comply with medical inspection regulations. The arrests were frequent. The fines were small, typically five or ten dollars, amounts that could be earned in a single night but that accumulated over time into a form of taxation falling most heavily on those least able to pay. The police were not acting out of malice. They were acting on the same bureaucratic logic that had driven the ton-of-goods requirement at the Chilkoot and the customs inspections at the border. Regulate, contain, make survivable. The women on the line were not the beneficiaries of the regulation. They were its subjects.

The contradiction was structural. The same demographic imbalance that created the market for women’s labor ensured that women would be a minority in every sphere of the town’s life. The women who profited did so by navigating this contradiction.

They operated within a narrow but potent sphere of influence, using their visibility, their skills, and their control over specific forms of labor to secure independence and wealth in a world dominated by men. Their success did not challenge the domination.

It exploited it. The men who spent their gold in the dance halls and the cabins of Paradise Alley were spending the proceeds of a mining economy built on the labor of men who had carried a ton of goods over the passes, and the women who received that gold were participating in the same chain of extraction and consumption that defined the stampede. The gold moved from the creek beds to the miners, from the miners to the women, from the women to the merchants who supplied them with food and shelter, and from the merchants back to the mining operations in the form of investment and credit. The women were a link in this chain, and their labor was as integral to the stampede’s machinery as the pack animals that had died on the White Pass.

The peak of Dawson’s social economy coincided with the beginning of its decline. In the summer of 1899, gold was discovered around Nome in western Alaska, and the newspapers that had encouraged so many to travel to the Klondike lost interest almost overnight. The men who had filled the dance halls and the cabins of Paradise Alley began to leave. They went to Nome, following the gold as they had followed it to the Klondike. The women who had served them faced a choice: follow the men, or stay in a town that was emptying. Some followed, carrying their skills, their capital, or their labor to the new field. Others stayed in Dawson, where the town’s transformation into a more conservative municipality continued, and where the opportunities for the rapid, speculative wealth of the boom years were narrowing.

The women who stayed found themselves in a different economy. The dance halls that had generated fortunes through sheer volume of customers were operating on thinner margins. The hotels that had been full were half-empty.

The line in Paradise Alley saw fewer clients, and the police court records show a corresponding decline in arrests. The town that had been a boom camp was becoming a municipality, and the women who had thrived in the boom were operating in an environment that valued stability over speculation, respectability over notoriety, and order over the raw energy that had driven the social economy at its height. The instability of that economy, its dependence on a demographic imbalance that was temporary, had been visible from the beginning.

The men came to find gold, and when the gold was found or exhausted or proved illusory, they left. The women who followed were subject to the same cycle. The dance hall queen who earned a fortune in the summer of 1898 might find herself without an audience in the summer of 1899. The sex worker on the line might find the clients gone to Nome. The entrepreneur who had built a hotel on the assumption that miners would keep coming might find the miners had stopped, and the hotel was a building with no guests and a mortgage that could not be paid.

The peak of Dawson’s social economy, built on demographic exploitation, signals an instability that requires a new form of order. The police court docket in Dawson, through the summer and fall of 1899, recorded fewer arrests on the line. The dance halls cut their hours. The hotels dropped their rates. On Front Street, where the Fairview still stood, the traffic of men carrying gold dust in tin cans and leather pouches had thinned to a trickle, and the women who had learned to convert male loneliness into a living watched the street empty and understood that the market they had served was dissolving, and that the town they had profited from was becoming the kind of place where a woman with a past could not afford to be remembered.
