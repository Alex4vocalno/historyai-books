Chapter 27

The Reckoning of the River

Seen from above, the whole scarred map of the north country tells one story: the syndicates left behind their tailing piles and stripped bedrock, while north of the creeks, along the watery corridor, a different kind of ruin settled into the mud. The Klondike Gold Rush, a migration by an estimated 100, 000 prospectors between 1896 and 1898, had transformed the Chilkoot and White Pass trails into mainstream transportation routes, but its most enduring physical legacy was not the gold extracted but the profound scar left upon the landscape and the transportation networks of the Yukon.

From above, had anyone been able to take such a view in the spring of 1902, the Yukon River between Lake Bennett and Dawson City would have appeared not as a highway but as a graveyard. The water ran high with snowmelt, as it did every year, and the current carried the familiar brown sediment of the drainage. But along the banks, in the sloughs and back-channels, in the eddies behind gravel bars and in the soft mud of the cutbanks, lay the detritus of the stampede.

Sternwheelers and scows, skiffs and pole boats, raft hulls and paddle-wheel housings, built in the spring of 1898 by men who had no training as boatbuilders, caulked with whatever pitch could be found, launched in a frenzy when the ice went out of Lake Bennett and never intended to survive a single season. Most had not survived. They had been beached, abandoned, swamped, stove in, or simply left to rot where their owners stepped off them at the Dawson waterfront and walked up the hill to register their claims.

The river held them. The ice crushed some. Others sank in place, their timbers swelling in the current, their fittings rusting to stains on the mud.

They were a hazard to navigation and an offense to the Crown’s authority over its waters, and in the spring of 1902 the Canadian government decided to count them.

A contract was issued. It bore a date and a signature, and it authorized the removal of wrecked and abandoned vessels from the Yukon River between Lake Bennett and Dawson City. The document specified the work: the vessels were to be identified, surveyed, and either salvaged or destroyed, their wreckage cleared from the navigable channel and the shoreline. The contractor was to report the number, type, condition, and location of every vessel found. The contract was the first formal acknowledgment that the stampede had left behind a physical debt, and that the cost of collecting it would fall not on the stampeders who had fled to Nome but on the government that had let them pass through.

The contract moved through the Department of Public Works in Ottawa, a department more accustomed to bridges and dredging operations on the Rideau Canal than to the salvage of a fleet of ghost boats on a subarctic river. But the Yukon was now Canadian territory in more than name. Dawson had been incorporated as a city in 1902, meeting the criteria for municipal status under the act of that year, and the machinery of governance had arrived with a weight and a permanence that the stampeders themselves had never achieved. The city retained its incorporation even as its population plummeted. The laws outlasted the people. And the river, which had carried the stampede north with such indifferent efficiency, now required the same bureaucratic attention as any other Canadian waterway.

The contractor’s first report arrived in due course. It was an inventory of failure. Along the stretch between Lake Bennett and Dawson, the report documented hulls in various states of decay. Some were still recognizable as boats. Others had collapsed into the mud, their ribs sticking up like the bones of animals that had died trying to cross. The report listed the types: sternwheelers, scows, flat-bottomed pole boats, raft platforms with their planks loosened and scattered. Some had been built by men who knew carpentry. Most had not. The joints were rough. Caulking crumbled at the touch. Soft iron fastenings had rusted through where the river water sat in the seams, and whole strakes came away from the frame when a man pulled them.

The inventory ran to pages. Each entry was a vessel that had carried a man or a family or a ton of goods northward, had delivered them to the mud bank at Dawson, and had been left there. No one had come back for a boat. There had been no reason to. The boats had been built to go one way.

The report’s plainness was its force. It did not romanticize the wreckage. It counted it. And the count revealed a scale of abandonment that even the government’s most pessimistic estimates had not anticipated. The stampeders had not only crossed a landscape. They had littered it with the means of their crossing, and those means were now rotting into the water table, leaching creosote and rust and the chemical residue of industrial paints into the river that was the territory’s only reliable transportation corridor.

The Yukon River was the artery. Everything that moved into or out of the Klondike moved on it. The sternwheelers that carried freight from Whitehorse to Dawson, the scows that hauled supplies to the creeks, the small boats that carried mail and passengers and the occasional shipment of gold downstream to St. Michael—all of them depended on a navigable channel. The wrecks were not merely unsightly. They were dangerous. A submerged hull in the current could hole a sternwheeler’s paddle housing. A tangle of wreckage in a shallow channel could trap a scow and spin it into the cutbank. The river was not forgiving. It ran fast and cold, and a man in the water had minutes, not hours. The wrecks were a hazard to the living, paid for by the dead ambitions of the stampeders who had left them.

The contract proceeded. The contractor’s crews worked the river in small boats, marking wrecks with buoys or poles, then returning with winches and jacks and the labor of men hired at northern wages. The work was slow. The river was wide in places, narrow in others, and the wrecks were distributed along its entire length. Some were accessible from the bank. Others sat in water too deep to wade and too shallow for a barge, requiring the construction of temporary platforms and the use of grappling hooks on long ropes. The timber, where it was sound enough, was salvaged and sold. The rest was burned where it lay, the fires fed with pitch and oil until the ribs collapsed into ash and the mud reclaimed the space.

The cost mounted. Northern labor was expensive. Northern transport was expensive. The equipment had to be brought in by steamboat from Whitehorse, itself a supply point dependent on the White Pass and Yukon Route railway, which had reached Whitehorse in 1900 and had replaced the old trail system with a permanence that the stampeders had never imagined.

The railway was part of the same story. The trail that the stampeders had climbed on foot, the Chilkoot Pass with its stone steps cut into the snow and its long line of men bent under packs, had been replaced by steel rails and a narrow-gauge locomotive. The White Pass route, where Soapy Smith and his gang of conniving and murderous thieves had once ruled Skagway and where stampeders had died in the Palm Sunday avalanche, was now a corporate right-of-way. The individualistic chaos of the stampede had been absorbed into corporate infrastructure, and the cost of that absorption was recorded in the ledgers of the railway company and the Department of Public Works.

The river cleanup was one half of the reckoning. The other half was on the trails.

The Chilkoot Trail, which had been the main route from Dyea over the pass to Lake Bennett, had been transformed by the stampede from a footpath used by the Chilkat Tlingit for trade into a mainstream transportation route. Tens of thousands of men and women had climbed it in 1897 and 1898, carrying their ton of goods on their backs, in stages, caching supplies along the way and returning for them, wearing a path so deep into the mountain that the stone steps at the summit, the Golden Stairs, became one of the iconic images of the rush. After the stampede, the trail became more or less deserted. Prospectors late to the gold rush now made their way to the Yukon on the new White Pass and Yukon Route railroad, which took them all the way to Whitehorse.

But disuse did not mean disappearance. The trail remained, and so did what the stampeders had left on it. The first comprehensive surveys of the Chilkoot and White Pass corridors, conducted in the same years as the river cleanup, documented a landscape that had been physically altered by the passage of human traffic.

The surveyors found erosion. The thousands of boots and sled runners had cut through the vegetative cover on the slopes, exposing the soil to the rain and the snowmelt, and the runoff had carved gullies into the trail surface that would take decades to heal. The permafrost, where it had been exposed by the cutting of the trail, had thawed and subsided, creating depressions and sinkages that made the path treacherous even in summer. The trees had been cut for fuel and for boat-building at Lake Bennett, and the denuded slopes showed the scars of axes and saws, the stumps standing in clearings that had once been forest.

The surveys also found goods. The stampeders had carried their ton of goods over the pass, but they had not carried all of it forward. The trail was littered with what they had discarded or cached and never retrieved. Cans of food, rusted and collapsed. Stoves, cast-iron and heavy, dumped when their owners calculated that the weight was not worth the labor. Tents, torn and frozen. Sleds, broken. Clothing, boots, tools, the entire material culture of the stampede scattered along the route like the leavings of a retreating army. The surveyors inventoried it. They did not have the mandate to remove it, not yet, but they counted it and photographed it, and the photographs became part of the record that the Canadian government was assembling of what the stampede had cost the land.

The photographs showed a landscape that looked like a battlefield after the armies had gone. The Chilkoot Pass, with its stone steps still visible in the snow, was empty of people but dense with debris. The White Pass, wider and lower, showed the same pattern: erosion, discarded goods, the remains of camps and way stations that had sprung up to serve the stampeders and had been abandoned when the traffic stopped. The photographs were stark and black-and-white, and they carried the same quality that had made the images of the stampede’s ascent—recorded by photographers like Eric A. Hegg—so widely distributed a few years before. Then, the photographs had shown men climbing into hope. Now, they showed what hope had left behind.

The parallel between the river and the trails was exact. Each corridor had served the stampede briefly and intensely. Each was abandoned when the railway and the steamboats made it obsolete. Each carried a physical debt that the land could not settle on its own. And each was now being reckoned by the same government that had imposed the ton-of-goods rule, the same Mounted Police that had stood at the summit with their tripod scales and their customs ledger and their insistence that every prospector carry enough supplies to survive a winter. The police had created a system. The system had saved lives. But the system had also concentrated the stampede’s impact on a few routes, and those routes now bore the scars.

The paper trail outlasted the physical structures. The contract for the river cleanup, the survey reports for the trails, the cost accounting submitted to Ottawa—these documents would survive long after the last wreck was cleared and the last can was removed from the Chilkoot. They were the record of a reckoning, and they would be filed in the archives of the Department of Public Works and the Department of the Interior, where they would sit in folders and boxes, indexed and retrievable, long after the stampeders themselves were dead. The paper was the most durable artifact of the stampede. Boats rotted. Cabins collapsed. Tailing piles would eventually be reworked and reworked until the gold was gone. What survived was the government’s memory, filed and indexed, permanent.

The final cost accounting for the river cleanup was submitted to Ottawa. The figures were specific. The contractor had removed a documented number of vessels. The tonnage of wreckage, where it had been weighed or estimated, was recorded. The cost per vessel, the cost per mile of river cleared, the total cost of the operation—all of it was entered into the ledgers. The amount was not trivial.

It exceeded what most individual stampeders had ever gained from the Klondike. The arithmetic was cruel in its simplicity. The average prospector had spent a thousand dollars to reach the Klondike, had carried a ton of goods over the pass at a cost of additional labor and time, had staked or bought a claim, had worked it for a season or two, and had taken out less than he had put in. The government, which had collected customs duties on every ton of goods at the summit, which had collected mining recorder fees on every claim, which had collected licenses and permits and fines, was now spending that revenue on cleaning up the mess that the stampeders had left behind.

The circle was complete. The stampede had generated revenue. The stampede had generated wreckage. The revenue paid for the wreckage. And the wreckage, once cleared, left only the paper.

Gold mining production in the Klondike peaked in 1903 after heavier equipment was brought in. The dredges and the hydraulic nozzles and the industrial operations of the syndicates were producing more gold than the individual miners ever had, and they were producing it from the same ground that the stampeders had worked with picks and pans and rocker boxes. The peak was an industrial peak, not an individual one.

It belonged to the corporations and the syndicates, not to the men who had carried their ton of goods over the pass. And it coincided almost exactly with the reckoning of the river. The same year that the government was tallying the cost of clearing the wrecks from the Yukon, the syndicates were recording their highest output. The two figures, read together, told the story of the Klondike’s transformation. The stampeders had come and gone. The syndicates had stayed. The government had cleaned up after both.

The river was cleared. The trails were surveyed. The bill was tallied. And the landscape that remained—the Yukon River running clear of wrecks, the Chilkoot Trail still scarred but slowly healing, the creeks still running through tailing piles and diverted channels—was the landscape that the stampede had made. It was not the landscape the stampeders had found when they arrived. They had found a wilderness, or something close to it, a place where the creeks ran undisturbed and the trails were footpaths and the river was navigable without hazard.

They left it a place of scars. The scars would fade. The river would flush itself clean. The vegetation would reclaim the slopes. The permafrost would re-establish itself where the trail had cut through it. But the process would take decades, and in the meantime, the landscape was a record of what had happened, as legible in its way as the paper trail that sat in the archives in Ottawa.

The stampeders had gone to Nome. Some had gone to other gold fields. Some had gone home. The boats they had built were ash on the mud or salvage timber in a government yard. The goods they had carried were rusted cans on a mountain trail or, where they had been recovered, refuse in a landfill. The claims they had staked were syndicate property. The city they had built was incorporated and shrinking. The Mounted Police who had weighed their goods and checked their supplies were still there, still filing reports, still maintaining the system that had made the stampede survivable. The contracts and ledgers outlasted the physical structures, and the physical structures were being erased.

The river ran clear. The cost was paid. The paper remained.
