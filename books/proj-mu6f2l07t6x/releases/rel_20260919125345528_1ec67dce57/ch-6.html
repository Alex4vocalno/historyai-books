Chapter 6

The Ton of Goods

The tide flats at Skagway stank of low water and churned mud in the last week of August 1897. A man stood at the edge of them, beside a pile of canvas sacks and tin cans stacked on a borrowed plank. His name was not recorded.

He had come off a steamer that morning, one of hundreds who poured onto the beach in the weeks after the Portland’s gold reached Seattle, and he was staring at what he had bought. Flour. Bacon. Beans. Rice. Dried fruit. A coil of rope. A pickaxe. A gold pan. A small tent. The sacks had been weighed at the merchant’s scale in town, and the total came to just over nine hundred pounds.

He needed eleven hundred more. He knew this because someone had shown him a notice, copied by hand and pinned to the wall of a tent that served as a provisions store, bearing the words of the North-West Mounted Police: anyone entering Yukon Territory must bring a year’s supply of food. The notice did not say please.

The man on the tide flats did not know yet what the rule would cost him. He knew only that he had spent nearly everything he had saved for the passage north, and that the pile before him was less than half of what the police at the summit would require. Behind him, Skagway was a grid of mud and canvas, a place without streets or law, where a member of the North-West Mounted Police would later describe the settlement as little better than a hell on earth. Fights, prostitutes, and liquor were ever-present. The con man known as Soapy Smith had not yet arrived, but the ground he would claim was already being cleared. Every tent sold something. Every merchant had heard the same rumor: the police at the border were turning back anyone without a year’s supply. The rumor was true.

Six weeks earlier, on August 16, 1896, on Bonanza Creek, near Dawson, about fifty miles east of the Alaskan border, gold was discovered. George Carmack, Skookum Jim (Keish), and Tagish Charlie (Dawson Charlie) were prospecting a creek the local people called Rabbit Creek. Skookum Jim found the gold, but because of concerns that the mining recorder at the nearby town of Forty Mile would not allow a Native to claim discovery, Jim and Carmack made a pact: Carmack would stake the discovery claim and then give a half-interest to Jim. The discovery was formally recorded the following day at the police post at Fortymile, and from there the news traveled slowly north and west by dog team and river steamer until it reached the outside world on July 17, 1897, when the Portland landed in Seattle.

Now, six weeks after that landing, the first wave of stampeders stood on the mud at Skagway and Dyea, and the rule that would shape them had already been issued from an office far to the north, in a country most of them could not find on a map.

The rule came from the Canadian government, through the North-West Mounted Police, and the man responsible for enforcing it on the ground was Inspector Samuel Benfield Steele. Steele was forty years old that summer, a veteran of the force that had policed the Canadian West since 1873. He had been ordered north to the Yukon in the spring of 1897, before the Portland’s cargo was known, before the stampede had begun, to bring order to a territory that Ottawa feared would be overrun. The fear was not abstract. The Yukon was a remote region with no roads and no supply lines, and a winter that lasted eight months. No farms existed anywhere in the territory. If thousands of men entered without enough food, they would starve. The North-West Mounted Police would have to feed them or bury them, and the Canadian government had no intention of doing either.

The order was simple in its wording and brutal in its consequences. Every person entering Yukon Territory must carry a year’s supply of food, roughly 1, 100 pounds of provisions, in addition to the tools, clothing, and gear necessary to survive and to mine. The total weight required was close to a ton, or 2, 000 pounds. The police would weigh it. They would inspect it. They would record it. And if a man did not have it, he would not cross the border.

The rule was a paper requirement before it was a physical one. It existed as a list, a regulation, an entry in the police orders. But the paper trail it created would become the mechanism through which the stampede was governed—every stampededer weighed, recorded, and either passed or turned back at the summit of the Chilkoot Pass. The rule turned a route into a corridor and a corridor into a system. Before a single man climbed the pass, the system was already operating in the staging towns below, where merchants raised their prices and stampeders opened their purses.

The man on the tide flats at Skagway was not alone. Across the shallow Taiya River delta, at the nearby settlement of Dyea, the beginning of the Chilkoot Trail, the same scene was repeating. Prospectors who chose the Chilkoot were ferried to Dyea by small boats from the steamers that anchored in the inlet, and they arrived to find a settlement even less developed than Skagway. A few cabins stood on the tidal flat. The trailhead began in mud and ended at a mountain.

Dyea was built on the delta of the Taiya River, a wide, shallow expanse of gravel and silt that shifted with every tide. The town had no streets, no drainage, no foundation. Tents stood in rows on ground that flooded at high water. The smell of rotting fish and human waste hung over the flat. Men unloaded their supplies from boats, carried them to campsites, and began the work of sorting, weighing, and repacking what they had.

The merchants had arrived before the stampeders. They had come north on the same steamers, or they had been there already, trading with the Chilkat Tlingit who controlled the passes. Now they sold flour at prices that would have been impossible in Seattle or San Francisco. A sack of flour that cost four dollars in the south cost twelve at Skagway. Bacon sold for double its southern price. Beans, rice, dried fruit, coffee—each carried a markup that reflected not the cost of the goods but the desperation of the buyer. A merchant who had invested a few hundred dollars in provisions in Seattle could sell them for two or three thousand at Skagway. Some made more. The merchants who arrived earliest, with the largest stocks, made fortunes before the first snow fell.

The stampeders needed the supplies because the rule required them. The rule required them because the Canadian government feared starvation. The fear of starvation was justified by the geography.

The Yukon Territory was a vast, remote region, comprising an area about equal to that of Rhode Island, lying chiefly to the east of the Yukon River, where it receives the Klondike Creek, near the middle of the Alaskan boundary line. No railroads reached it. Everything a man needed to survive—food, clothing, tools, shelter—had to be carried in over the mountains. The passes were the only routes.

The Chilkoot Trail spanned between 28 and 33 miles from sea level at Dyea to Lake Bennett, British Columbia, at an elevation of 2, 602 feet. The White Pass, beginning at Skagway, covered similar terrain by a different route. Both were mountain trails, usable only on foot, in summer through mud and rain, in winter through snow and ice. No wagons. No horses, except on the White Pass, and those died by the hundreds. The supplies had to be carried on a man’s back or on a sled pulled by hand.

The arithmetic was simple and crushing. A ton of goods, divided into loads of fifty pounds each, required forty trips over the pass. The pass was a series of stages: from Dyea to Sheep Camp, from Sheep Camp to the Scales, from the Scales to the summit, from the summit down to Lake Lindeman.

A man could carry one load at a time. He would carry it forward, cache it, walk back, pick up the next load, carry it forward, cache it, walk back. The distance multiplied. Forty loads times the full length of the trail meant hundreds of miles of walking, most of it under a pack. The work took weeks. Some men spent the entire winter of 1897 to 1898 moving their supplies over the pass, trip after trip, through snow and wind and cold that froze sweat in their clothing and numbed their fingers until they could not feel the pack straps.

The ton of goods was calculated to sustain a man for one year in the Yukon, where the growing season was negligible and resupply was impossible during the winter. The food component alone was roughly 1, 100 pounds: flour, bacon, beans, rice, dried fruit, sugar, coffee, tea, salt, baking powder. The remaining 900 pounds included clothing, bedding, a tent, a stove, cooking utensils, mining tools, pans, picks, shovels, maybe a rocker for washing gravel, and miscellaneous gear such as rope, candles, matches, and medical supplies. The list was specific. The police at the summit would check not only the total weight but the contents. A man with a ton of whiskey and gambling equipment would be turned back. A man with a ton of flour and no tools would be told to buy tools or go home. The rule was a condition of entry, not a suggestion.

For the stampeders, the rule transformed the journey from a simple migration into a logistical operation of extraordinary scale. A man who had imagined himself walking to the goldfields with a pack on his back now faced the reality that he was a freight hauler, a beast of burden, and his own cargo. The dream of gold became the labor of a mule. The men who had rushed north on the strength of a newspaper headline now stood on the mud at Skagway and Dyea and reckoned with the weight of what they had volunteered to carry.

The cost was staggering. A year’s supply of food, purchased at the inflated prices of the staging towns, could cost five hundred to a thousand dollars. Add the tools, the clothing, the tent, the stove, and the total could reach fifteen hundred dollars or more—more than a year’s wages for a laborer in the cities they had left. Men who had saved for months to buy a steamship ticket now found that the ticket was the smallest expense. The supplies were the real cost. And the supplies were mandatory. A man could not choose to travel light, to buy food in Dawson, to live off the land. The land would not feed him. Dawson had no stores. The rule existed because the land and the town could not be relied upon, and the police had no intention of letting men enter a wilderness where they could not survive.

The merchants understood this before the stampeders did. The rule created a market. The market was not in gold but in supplies. The man who sold flour at twelve dollars a sack was not mining gold. He was mining the stampeders. And the stampeders had no choice but to be mined, because the rule required them to buy. The stampede engine was beginning to turn. The requirement for goods created demand. The demand created supply. The supply created prices. The prices created fortunes. And the fortunes created more stampeders, who arrived with more money, which created more demand. The cycle fed itself. The rule that was designed to prevent starvation became the mechanism that enriched the merchants, the steamship companies, the outfitters, and the traders who lined the route from Seattle to Skagway.

In San Francisco and Seattle, the outfitters were already advertising. They sold complete Klondike outfits—food, tools, clothing, everything a man needed to meet the ton requirement—for a fixed price. The advertisements appeared in the newspapers that had spread the news of the Portland’s gold. The same headline that had triggered the stampede now triggered the market. Men walked into outfitters’ shops with their savings and walked out with a pile of goods they did not know how to carry. The outfitters did not explain the carrying. They sold the goods. The carrying was the stampeders’ problem. The rule was the police’s problem. The gap between the two was where the ordeal lived.

At Dyea, the Taiya River delta was filling with piles of goods. Men stacked their supplies on the flats above the tide line, covered them with canvas, and began the slow process of moving them up the trail. The trail began at the edge of town and climbed through a rain forest of spruce and hemlock, dark and wet, the ground soft with moss and rot. The first section to Sheep Camp was roughly twelve miles, and a man with a fifty-pound pack could cover it in a day.

But a day’s walk for one load meant a day’s walk for each of forty loads. The arithmetic was relentless. A man who moved one load per day would spend forty days moving his supplies to Sheep Camp alone.

Then he faced the climb to the summit. Then the descent to Lake Lindeman. Then the boat building. Then the river. The journey was a campaign.

Some men hired packers. The Chilkat Tlingit, who had controlled the trail for generations, offered their services as packers and guides. They charged by the pound, and their prices reflected the difficulty of the work. A man who could not carry his own ton could pay someone else to carry it, but the cost could double the price of the supplies. Other men bought pack animals—horses, mules—and drove them up the trail. The White Pass, beginning at Skagway, was wider and less steep than the Chilkoot, and some men attempted it with animals. The animals suffered. The trail was rocky, narrow, and slick with rain. Horses slipped off the edge and fell into the canyon below. The carcasses accumulated. The trail became a graveyard of pack animals, and the stench of rotting horse flesh joined the smell of mud and human waste that hung over the staging towns.

The police watched. Inspector Steele had established his post at the summit of the Chilkoot Pass, on the Canadian side of the border. He had a small force of constables, a set of scales, and the authority of the Canadian government. His orders were clear: no man enters without a ton of goods. No exceptions. No appeals. The police would weigh the supplies, inspect the contents, and record the name of the man and the weight of his outfit. The record was the point. The paper trail mattered as much as the physical trail. Every man who crossed the border was entered into a ledger. His name, his weight, his supplies—all recorded, all filed, all sent to Ottawa. The stampeders were being counted before they had mined an ounce of gold.

Steele’s purpose was not merely bureaucratic. The North-West Mounted Police had been created in 1873 to bring law to the Canadian West, and the Yukon was the last frontier they would be asked to police. The force was small. A mere handful of constables sufficed to introduce law and order into the country from the time of the great rush in 1897. The quiet that prevailed on the Canadian side stood in stark contrast to conditions on the American side, where Skagway ran wild.

Steele’s achievement was not in fighting crime but in preventing it. By requiring the ton of goods, he ensured that no man entered the Yukon unprepared. By weighing and recording every outfit, he ensured that every man who entered was known to the police. The rule was a filter. It kept out the destitute, the desperate, and the unprepared. It also kept out men who might have survived on luck or nerve alone. The rule replaced luck with weight and nerve with provisions.

The American side of the border was different. Skagway was American soil, and the North-West Mounted Police had no jurisdiction there. The town was beyond their reach. Soapy Smith would arrive in Skagway in the fall of 1897 and build his empire of graft and violence on the American side of the line, where the police could not touch him. The contrast between the two sides of the border would become one of the defining features of the stampede. On the Canadian side, order, inspection, and the ton. On the American side, chaos, crime, and the free market. The line between them ran along the summit of the Chilkoot Pass, where Steele set his scales.

The stampeders at Skagway and Dyea did not know Steele yet. They knew only the notice on the tent wall, the rumor of the rule, and the weight of the goods they had bought. They were civilians, not soldiers. They were clerks, farmers, laborers, sailors, gamblers, schoolteachers, and sons of merchants. None had trained in freight hauling. None had experience in mountain travel. They had read a newspaper headline about gold and boarded a ship. Now they stood on a mud flat in Alaska, staring at a pile of flour and bacon, and the pile was not heavy enough.

The merchants sold more. The stampeders bought more. The piles grew. The weight accumulated. Men repacked their supplies into fifty-pound loads, wrapping them in canvas, lashing them to pack frames or tying them into bundles that could be carried on the back. They practiced lifting them. Some could not stand under the weight. Others staggered a few steps and dropped the load. The work had not begun. The work was the carrying, and the carrying was weeks of walking, back and forth, up and down, through rain and snow, over a trail that climbed from sea level to the summit of the Chilkoot Pass and down again to the lakes where they would build boats for the next stage of the journey.

The rule had done its work before the first load was carried. A stampede had become a procession. A mob had become a workforce. A dream had become a job. The men who had imagined themselves walking to the goldfields now understood that they would be hauling freight, and the freight was their own survival, weighed and recorded and certified by a police officer at the top of a mountain.

The ton of goods was the operating engine of the stampede. The demand it created filled the staging towns with merchants. The labor it imposed filled the trail with men carrying loads. The bottleneck it produced would form at the summit, where the police and their scales waited. The record it generated made every stampededer legible to the Canadian government. And the cost it exacted would break some men before they reached the goldfields—would send them home broke and broken, their supplies sold at a loss to merchants who would resell them to the next wave of stampeders at the same inflated prices.

The merchants did not care who succeeded. They sold flour and bacon to every man who could pay. The police did not care who found gold. They weighed and recorded and passed or turned back. The stampeders cared. They cared about the gold they had not yet seen and the mountain they had not yet climbed. They stood on the mud at Skagway and Dyea, in the last warm days of August 1897, and the mountain was above them, and the rule was upon them, and the ton of goods lay at their feet in sacks and tins and coils of rope, waiting to be carried.

The tide came in at Dyea and covered the lower end of the flats with shallow water. Men moved their supplies above the tide line, stacking sacks on planks, covering them with canvas. The Taiya River shifted its channel overnight, and a man who had cached his goods on a gravel bar found them washed away by morning. The mountain was visible from the flats, a wall of gray rock and green forest, rising steeply from the valley floor, with a notch at the top that marked the pass. The notch was small. It looked close. It was neither small nor close. The trail to it would take weeks, and the weight on a man’s back would make every mile longer.

At Skagway, the mud was deeper. The town had no river delta, no tidal flat, just a beach of gravel and clay that turned to soup in the rain. The rain came in August and did not stop. Tents sagged. Supplies soaked. Men worked in the wet, repacking, weighing, sorting, carrying loads to the trailhead. The trailhead was a mud path that left the edge of town and entered the trees. Beyond the trees, the trail climbed. The White Pass was wider than the Chilkoot but longer and more exposed. The choice between the two routes depended on which town a man had landed in, which trail he had heard was easier, which rumor he believed. Neither was easy. Both required the ton.

The stampeders were beginning to understand that the ton of goods was not a detail of the journey but the journey itself.

The gold was an abstraction. The ton was real. It had weight and bulk and cost. It occupied space on a steamer, on a beach, on a man’s back. It dictated the pace of travel, the choice of route, the need for packers or animals, the size of a boat at Lake Lindeman, the draft of that boat on the Yukon River. The ton was the material fact around which everything else organized.

The rule that created it had been issued from an office in Ottawa or Regina, by men who had never seen the Chilkoot Pass, who had calculated the caloric needs of a man in the Arctic and multiplied by twelve months. The calculation was sound. The rule was necessary. The consequence was a migration that would be governed not by the goldfields but by the weight of supplies a man could carry over a mountain.

The first men began carrying in late August. They loaded fifty pounds onto their backs and walked up the trail from Dyea to Sheep Camp, or from Skagway to the first camp on the White Pass. They came back down. They loaded another fifty pounds. They walked up again. The trail was a line of men going up and a line of men coming down, passing each other on the narrow track, stepping aside for the pack trains of horses that some men drove up the White Pass. The horses were nervous and overloaded. Some bolted. Some fell. The men kept walking.

At the summit, Steele and his constables were setting up the scales. The tripod was a simple device: three poles lashed together, a beam, a hook, a counterweight. It could weigh a man’s entire outfit in stages. The constables had a ledger. They wrote names and weights. They checked contents. They turned back the men who did not have enough. The turned-back men stood at the summit and looked down at their supplies, stacked at the Scales or at Sheep Camp, and understood that they would have to carry more, buy more, or go home. Some went home. Most carried more.

The ton of goods had created the stampede’s first economy. Not the economy of gold, which would come later, or not at all for most men. The economy of supplies. The merchants at Skagway and Dyea sold provisions at prices that reflected the desperation of the buyers and the scarcity of the supply. The outfitters in Seattle and San Francisco sold complete outfits to men who had not yet left the south. The steamship companies sold tickets at premium rates. The packers sold their labor by the pound. The Tlingit sold their knowledge of the trail. Every transaction was driven by the rule. The rule created the market. The market created the prices. The prices created the fortunes that would be made not from gold but from the stampeders’ need to carry a ton of goods over a mountain.

The irony was lost on no one who looked. The Portland had carried a ton of gold south. The stampeders would carry a ton of goods north. The gold had enriched the men who found it. The goods would enrich the men who sold them. The men who carried them would labor for weeks on a trail that tested their strength and their resolve, and most would arrive at the goldfields to find that the claims had been staked, the creeks had been worked, and the gold they had come for was already in the hands of others. But that was ahead. For now, in the late summer of 1897, the immediate reality was the weight. The weight was the rule. The rule was the mountain. And the mountain was waiting.

The tide went out at Dyea. The flats emerged again, wide and gray, streaked with channels of water. The sun broke through the overcast for a few minutes, and the mountain showed its face—rock and ice and the dark green of spruce forest, rising to the notch of the pass. The light moved across the flats and caught the canvas of a thousand tents, the tin of a thousand cans, the steel of a thousand pickaxes. The piles of goods stood in rows, each one a man’s year of survival, weighed and counted and waiting to be carried. The stampeders stood among them, bent under the first loads, and the trail above was already crowded with men who had started before them, moving slowly, under the weight, toward the summit where the scales stood and the police waited and the border of Canada began.
