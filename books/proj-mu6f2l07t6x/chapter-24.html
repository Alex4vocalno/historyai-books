Chapter 24

The Empty Bunkhouses of the Creeks

Seen from above, the Klondike district was a map of silence and snow. The creeks lay white and still, their drainages erased under a blanket that returned the country to a state before any ground was broken. A surveyor’s map, drawn in the last months of 1899, reduced this system to clean lines of blue ink—Bonanza, Eldorado, Hunker, Dominion, Gold Run—each creek a thread from which numbered rectangles of ownership branched in neat sequence. The document recorded claims, who held what, but it could not record the profound quiet of that first winter after the exodus. From the ridge tops where the surveyors had stood, the valleys now appeared uninhabited; only one or two stovepipes along the entire length of Bonanza betrayed a wisp of smoke. The rest was stillness.

A man sitting in a bunkhouse on Bonanza Creek in January 1900 could hear the wind. It came down the valley from the north, steady and dry, and where it found the gaps between cabin logs, and there were many gaps, for most of the cabins had been built in haste during the rush and never chinked properly, it passed through with a thin, high whistle. The sound carried.

There was nothing to absorb it. No boots stamped on frozen ground. Pans were not being scrubbed. The hammer on a windlass had gone still. The creek was frozen solid.

The dumps, the piles of pay gravel men had scratched from their shafts and stacked on the surface, waiting for spring thaw to sluice, sat under snow like burial mounds. The machinery, where any had been installed, stood idle: boilers cold, hoists frozen, the cables stiff as iron. A man could walk the length of Bonanza and pass cabin after cabin with no smoke from the stovepipe.

Why so empty? The answer began with numbers. Of the estimated thirty to forty thousand people who reached the Klondike during the stampede, perhaps half finally became prospectors. Of those, no more than four thousand found any gold at all, and only a few hundred made fortunes. By the time most arrived in the spring and summer of 1898, the creeks had been staked end to end, every claim registered, every foot of gold-bearing ground spoken for. The Bonanza, Eldorado, Hunker, and Dominion Creeks were all taken, with almost 10, 000 claims recorded by the authorities by July 1898.

A man arriving late found no ground to work. He could hire himself out as a wage laborer, if work was to be had, or he could leave. Most left.

The exodus to Nome in the summer of 1899 had drawn them away, the news of gold on the beaches at Cape Nome, where a man needed no claim, no shaft, no thawing, just a shovel and a pan and the sand at his feet. They sold what they could and went. The creeks, which had been the destination, became a place to depart from.

But the emptiness went deeper than the departure of a season. The creeks had been claimed since the first days. George Carmack, Skookum Jim, and Dawson Charlie had staked their strips of ground along Bonanza Creek on August 16, 1896, filing the claims the next day at the police post at the mouth of the Fortymile River. News spread rapidly from there to other mining camps in the Yukon River valley. By the end of August, all of Bonanza Creek had been claimed by miners. Eldorado, the tributary that proved richest, for the two or three miles in which it was gold-bearing probably surpassing any other known placer deposit, was staked from mouth to source before the first winter snow fell. The men who held those claims either worked them or held them, and the men who arrived later found nothing but the holding.

So the question shifted. Why were the cabins empty if the claims were still held? Because the men who held them had, in many cases, no means to work them through a winter. Drift mining, sinking a shaft to bedrock through frozen gravel, building a fire to thaw the ground, hoisting the pay dirt to the surface, required timber for shoring, coal or wood for fuel, tools, rope, and labor. A man alone could not do it. A man with wages to pay needed capital. The individual stampeders who had come north with their ton of goods, as the Mounted Police required, had brought enough to survive a winter, but survival and extraction were different things. The ton of goods kept a man alive. It did not make him a miner. It made him a consumer.

The stampeders had been replaced, slowly and then with gathering speed, by a different kind of operation. The claims that paid consistently, the rich ground on Eldorado, the deep deposits on Bonanza, attracted capital. Men who had staked claims sold shares to syndicates.

Companies formed in Seattle, San Francisco, and London, pooling investment to buy out the original locators or to lease their ground. These companies could afford what no individual could: machinery, sustained labor, the infrastructure of industrial extraction. The shift was already visible in the winter of 1899 to 1900. A bunkhouse that had housed twenty men during the rush now housed three or four, a foreman, a cook, a couple of hands kept on for winter watch, to keep the shafts from collapsing under snow weight, to tend the boilers if the company had installed one, to maintain the claim’s legal standing by the minimal activity required to prevent it from being jumped.

The stampede engine had run on the fuel of individual hope, and the fuel was spent. What remained was the machine it had built: the legal framework of claims, the police enforcement of boundaries, the customs posts and their ledgers, the supply chain from Seattle and Vancouver, the steamers on the Yukon River, all of it still functioning, still recording, still measuring, but now serving a different master. The prospector with his pan and his dream had given way to the company with its dredge and its balance sheet.

The men who wintered on the creeks in that first season of emptiness knew this, in the way men know a thing before they have words for it. They felt it in the silence. A diary kept by a foreman on Bonanza Creek, one of the few documents that survive from that winter on the ground, as opposed to the abundant records from Dawson City, noted the sound of a lone axe on a morning in late January.

The entry was brief. The man had split wood for the stove. The sound had traveled up the valley and come back from the ridge as an echo, and the echo had no other sound to meet it. The creek was, in his words, quiet as a church. He was not complaining. He was observing. The man was being paid to watch the company’s ground, and he watched it, and the watching was quiet.

The quiet was the product of a system that had worked. The Mounted Police had enforced the ton-of-goods rule, and the rule had ensured that the men who reached the creeks arrived supplied, documented, and traceable.

But the same system that made the stampede survivable made it self-limiting. The requirement to carry a year’s provisions meant that every man who came north represented an investment of capital, his own or borrowed, before he ever swung a pick. The supply industry that grew up to serve that requirement, the outfitters in Seattle and Skagway, the packers on the Chilkoot and White passes, the merchants in Dawson, extracted its profit from the passage, not from the gold.

The gold was uncertain. The cost of getting there was certain. And when the gold did not materialize for the majority, the certain cost had already been paid, to the outfitters and the packers and the merchants, and the stampeders had nothing left to spend.

So they left. And the creeks, which had been claimed ground, the psychological territory of the prospector’s hope, the legal territory of his registered stake, became something else. The claims remained on the map. The numbers stayed in the recorder’s book. But the men who had given those claims their human meaning, who had peopled the bunkhouses and filled the saloons and queued at the recorder’s office and argued over boundaries and fought over fractions, were gone. What remained was the legal shell: the claim as an asset, a unit of industrial production, a line on a company’s balance sheet. Claimed Ground had been transformed from a prospector’s hope to a holding in a corporate portfolio. The transformation was silent. It happened in the winter, in the empty bunkhouses, in the sound of a lone axe.

The landscape itself bore the marks of the passage. Recently constructed government roads led from Dawson to the creeks, connecting Bonanza and Eldorado and Hunker in a network that had been rough and nearly impassable at points during the rush but was now, with no traffic to wear it, freezing into a smooth, hard track. Along these roads, rudely built cabins stood at intervals, some occupied, most not. The snow settled on their roofs without the heat from within to melt it.

Stovepipes cold. Doors lashed shut with rope. Inside, if a man entered, he might find a bunk with a mattress still on it, a tin plate on a shelf, a pair of boots frozen stiff under a bench. The detritus of departure. Men had left what they could not carry, and what they could not carry was everything that did not fit on a dog sled heading for Dawson and the steamers south.

The company foreman’s diary recorded the finding of such cabins on a patrol down Bonanza in February. He walked the government road, checking boundary markers and claim posts for the company’s survey. The entries were terse. Cabin at No. 14 stood empty, its door open, snow inside to a depth of two feet, the stovepipe down. At No. 15, a watchman for one of the companies still occupied the cabin, smoke rising from the chimney. The foreman stopped, warmed himself, and talked with the man for ten minutes. The watchman at No. 15 was the only person the foreman encountered in two miles of creek bed. Two miles of the richest gold ground in the world, and one man warming himself by a stove.

The isolation was institutional as well as physical. The Mounted Police, who had maintained posts along the creeks during the rush to settle disputes and enforce mining regulations, had reduced their presence. The police reports from the winter of 1899 to 1900 recorded the closure of detachments at the creek camps. The men were reassigned to Dawson, or sent south, or mustered out. The institution that had measured the stampede, that had stood at the border and weighed the ton of goods and recorded the claims and counted the dead, was measuring its own withdrawal. The tallies that had once filled pages now fit on a single sheet. The country was being returned, not to what it had been before the rush, for that was impossible, but to a state of sparse occupation that approximated what it had been before the crowds.

But the ground itself was different. The creeks had been torn. Shafts pockmarked the valley floors. Tailings, the piles of washed gravel left after sluicing, lined the creek beds in ragged rows. The timber that had stood on the ridges had been cut for shoring and for fuel, and the ridges were bare in places, the stumps showing through the snow like broken teeth. The landscape of the Klondike, which had been a wilderness before 1896, was now a worked-over industrial site, and the snow that covered it was a white sheet laid over the scars.

The men who remained, the foremen, the watchmen, the company hands, lived in a state that was neither the old wilderness nor the old rush.

They were the first inhabitants of the post-rush Klondike: a landscape of industrial extraction, owned by companies, worked by wage laborers, regulated by a police force that had become an administrative body rather than a frontier guard. They lived in the bunkhouses the stampeders had built, and the bunkhouses were too large for them. A cabin built for twenty men housed three.

The bunks were still there, the plank frames nailed to the walls, and the men who slept in them used one or two and left the rest, and the empty bunks were the clearest sign of what had happened. The rush was over. The ground held what it held. The men who had come to take it were gone, and the men who remained were there to work it, not to dream.

The diary on Bonanza Creek recorded one more thing. On a night in March, the foreman sat by his stove and wrote that he had been thinking about the men who had passed through the cabin the year before. He named a few, not by their full names, which the diary did not always give, but by their characteristics: the big Swede who could swing a doublejack, the man from Texas who played the fiddle, the fellow who had lost his toes to frostbite on the Chilkoot and walked the creek on stumps. They had all gone. The Swede to Nome, the fiddle player to Seattle, the frostbite case to the hospital in Dawson and then home.

The foreman did not write that he missed them. The cabin had been full of life then, he wrote, and now it was quiet, and the quiet was what the company wanted. The company wanted the ground watched and the shafts kept and the claim held. The company did not want men. It wanted the gold that was in the ground, and it would send for the machines to get it when the time came.

The time would come. Starting approximately ten years later, large gold dredges began an industrial mining operation, scooping huge amounts of gold out of the creeks, and completely reworking the landscape, altering the locations of rivers and creeks and leaving tailing piles in their wake. A network of dredges worked the Klondike valley for decades. The dredges did what the stampeders had dreamed of doing: they took the gold from the ground. But they did it without dreamers. They did it with engines and buckets and a crew of mechanics, and the gold they recovered went to shareholders who had never seen the Klondike and never would.

The bunkhouses that had stood empty in the winter of 1900 would, in the decades of dredging, be buried under tailings or knocked down to make way for the dredge ponds. The cabins the stampeders had built with their own hands, the claim posts they had set, the shafts they had sunk through frozen gravel, all of it would be consumed by the industrial operation that followed the rush.

The ground that had been claimed by individual men, staked and registered and worked with pick and shovel and pan, would be reworked by machines that did not know what a claim was. The legal framework that the Mounted Police had enforced, the boundary lines, the claim records, the ton-of-goods rule, had served its purpose. It had organized the chaos. And the organization had made it possible for capital to enter, to buy, to consolidate, and to extract with a thoroughness no individual prospector could match.

The empty bunkhouses were the proof. They showed what the rush had been for. Not for the men who came, most of whom found nothing and left. The communities that sprang up on the creeks were temporary and dissolved as soon as the ground was consolidated. The romance of the frontier was a story told after the fact by men who had been there and by men who had not. The rush had been for the gold. The gold was in the ground. The men who came to get it had, in the end, served the function of proving that it was there, by staking the claims, by registering them, by working them enough to show the richness of the deposit, and then by leaving, clearing the way for the companies that could extract it at scale.

The foreman on Bonanza Creek did not write any of this. His entries concerned the cold, the wind, the sound of his axe, the empty bunks. The cabin at No. 14 with its open door and the snow inside appeared in his pages. So did the watchman at No. 15, the only man in two miles, and the ten minutes they had spent talking. He did not know that the dredges were coming. He did not know that the landscape he walked through, the shafts and the tailings and the bare ridges and the government road, would be erased and rebuilt by machines. He knew that the creek was quiet and that his job was to watch it and that the company would send instructions in the spring.

The spring came to the Klondike as it always did: late, and sudden. The ice on the creeks broke in April, the water running dark and fast under the snow bridges. The road to Dawson softened into mud. The foreman packed his diary and his gear and walked out to the river to catch a steamer south. He left the bunkhouse locked, the stove cold, the bunks empty. The claim remained on the map. The recorder’s book still held the number. The ground held what it had always held.

What it had always held was the gold, and the gold would be taken, but not by the men who had dreamed of it. The empty bunkhouse on Bonanza Creek, its door lashed shut against the wind, its stovepipe cold, its bunks unused, stood as the verdict that the rush was over, and that the rush, as a thing of individual hope and individual labor, had been the prelude to something larger, quieter, and more thorough than any stampeder had imagined. The verdict was in the silence, and the silence was in the ground, and the ground was waiting for the machines.
