Chapter 3

The Winter of the Secret

The claims had to be filed before the river closed. That was the first pressure, the first institutional consequence of what George Carmack and Skookum Jim had pulled from the gravel of Bonanza Creek. The gold was in the pan. The gold was in the ground. But until a man stood before a mining recorder and swore to what he had found, the gold existed in a kind of legal suspension—present, physical, undeniable, but without an address. It belonged to no one in the eyes of the law. The law had a physical location. It sat at Fortymile, at the confluence of the Fortymile River and the Yukon, in a small office where a recorder kept his ledgers and his stamps.

Fortymile, in the second week of September 1896, became the first institutional organ of the Klondike gold rush. The settlement was a cluster of log cabins and cached supplies serving the prospectors who worked the upper Yukon drainage. The nearest recording post to the Klondike Valley sat roughly fifty miles downriver from the discovery. The office was small. The recorder kept his ledgers on a table. The stamps he used to validate claims were the instruments by which raw ground became legal property. Men arrived at his door in a steady press, each carrying a written description of the ground he had staked, each demanding that his parcel be entered before another man’s.

Carmack came to register the discovery claims. He filed for two strips of ground for himself as the discoverer and one each for Skookum Jim and Tagish Charlie. These claims were registered the next day at the police post at the mouth of the Fortymile River, and news spread rapidly from there to other mining camps along the Yukon and its tributaries. The recorder took the descriptions, applied his stamps, and entered the names and the creek locations into the ledger. Bonanza Creek entered the written record. So did the names. The paper trail had begun.

What the ledger could not contain was the velocity of what followed. Within days of the registration, prospectors from Fortymile, from Circle City, and from the scattered workings along the Yukon began to converge on the Klondike Valley. Some traveled by boat where the river still ran open. Others came by dog-sled where the first ice had already bridged the smaller streams. The creek beds of Bonanza and its tributary Eldorado were narrow. A claim was a strip of ground five hundred feet long, running from rim to rim across the valley floor. Only so many such strips existed on Bonanza. Fewer existed on Eldorado, which was shorter. Those who arrived first staked the ground nearest the discovery. Later arrivals took what remained. The last to come found nothing left and began to probe the feeder streams and the hillside benches, looking for gold that had not yet been claimed.

The claim records from September and October 1896 show the rapid parceling of the two creeks. The entries follow a sequence: a name, a date, a location, a number. The numbers run in order along the creek bed. Claim No. 1, No. 2, No. 3, each abutting the next. The progression is orderly at first. The men who staked them were experienced miners who understood the conventions of mining law and who had worked the Yukon gravels for years. They knew how to measure a claim, how to post a notice, how to file. The recorder’s ledger absorbed their filings, and the creek was divided into legal parcels with the precision of a surveyor’s grid.

The order did not hold. By late September, the best ground on Eldorado was gone. The creek was short, and the gold-bearing stretch ran for only two or three miles. Eldorado, for that limited distance, was richer than Bonanza itself, richer perhaps than any other placer deposit then known. Those who had staked early on Eldorado held ground that would produce extraordinary returns. Late arrivals on Bonanza, or men pushed to the secondary creeks, held ground that was poorer or unproven. The ledger recorded all claims with the same stamp, the same format, the same legal weight. The paper did not distinguish between the rich and the poor. The ground did.

The discovery on August 16 had been the work of George Carmack and Skookum Jim. Jim was the one who actually found the gold, but because of concerns that the mining recorder at Fortymile would not allow a Native to claim discovery, Jim and George made a pact. George would file as the discoverer. The recorder’s office, with its stamps and its ledger, would recognize George Carmack’s name. It would not recognize Keish. The first legal document of the Klondike gold rush was built on a necessary fiction, a concession to the racial structures of the mining community that determined whose name could appear on the paper and whose could not.

The pact between Jim and George was a private arrangement. The ledger recorded only the public fact. Those who arrived at Fortymile to file their own claims, and those who arrived at the Klondike Valley to stake ground, saw only the public fact. Carmack’s name appeared on the discovery claims. The creek was being divided. What the arrivals did not see, or what most did not see, was the full measure of what the ground contained. The early pans on Bonanza had been rich. The early pans on Eldorado were richer still. But the full extent of the deposit would not be known until the ground was worked, and the ground could not be worked until the claims were secured and the tools brought in and the shafts sunk through the muck to the bedrock where the gold lay.

The claim records show something else. They show disputes. The orderly progression of claim numbers broke down where two men staked the same ground, or where the boundaries of a claim were unclear, or where a man who had staked early sold his claim to a man who arrived late and the transfer was contested. The mining community had its own mechanisms for resolving these disputes. The miners’ meetings, informal assemblies of the men working a creek, could arbitrate. The recorder could adjudicate. The North-West Mounted Police, whose presence in the Yukon predated the discovery, could enforce. But the mechanisms were strained. The ground was limited. The stakes were high. And the men who had been working the upper Yukon for years, who had shared camps and provisions and the hardships of the northern winter, now found themselves divided by the arithmetic of fortune.

The old sourdough code, the informal compact by which prospectors shared information and respected each other’s ground, buckled under the weight of what Eldorado and Bonanza contained. The code had worked when gold was scarce and the creeks were poor. It had worked when a man’s claim was worth a season’s labor and not much more. It did not work when a claim on Eldorado could yield in a season what a man could not earn in a lifetime at any other trade. The code fractured. Men who had shared cabins now refused to share information. Partners who had prospected together now contested each other’s boundaries. The mining community, closed and intimate and governed by custom, split along the lines that the gold had drawn.

Jealousy was the first solvent. Those who had arrived late, who had staked the poorer ground or no ground at all, watched the men on Eldorado take out the first pans. The pans were heavy with coarse gold. The color was unmistakable. The men who held the rich claims knew what they had. Those who did not knew what they had missed. Rumor did the rest. Reports traveled up and down the creek, magnified with each telling. A pan worth a dollar became a pan worth five dollars. A claim yielding an ounce a day became a claim yielding five. The social fabric of the mining community, such as it was, stretched and tore under the pressure of numbers that no one could verify and everyone believed.

New hierarchies formed quickly. The holders of the discovery claims and the early claims on Eldorado became, overnight, the most powerful men in the Yukon. Not because of skill or experience or standing in the community, but because of the ground they held. Their authority was territorial. It derived from the legal parcel assigned to them by the recorder’s stamp. They were the lords of claimed ground, and their claims gave them a stable social identity that men without claims could not match. Those without claims were transient. Laborers, or prospectors looking for unworked ground, or men who had come to the Klondike too late and found the creeks already divided. They existed in a different relation to the land. The unclaimed ground gave them no identity, no standing, no legal address.

The recorder’s ledger, with its names and numbers and stamps, was the first paper trail of the Klondike gold rush. It was the document by which the discovery became a legal fact, by which the ground was divided, by which the claims were secured. But the paper trail was local. It existed in a single office, in a single ledger, in a settlement accessible only by river. The claims were registered. The news was not. The outside world had no access to the recorder’s office, no knowledge of the ledger, no awareness that the names in it represented fortunes in gold. The paper trail ended at the wall of the Yukon winter.

The freeze came in October. The Yukon River, the only artery connecting the interior to the coast, began to change. Where the current ran slow, the water formed a skin of ice along the banks. The skin thickened, crept toward the center, and met. The river closed. Ice bridged the channel from shore to shore, and the channel became a road of frozen water, passable only by dog-sled or on foot. The steamers that had carried supplies and mail and passengers upriver from St. Michael, at the mouth of the Yukon in Alaska, stopped running. The last boats of the season made their final trips, and then the river was shut.

The freeze-up was not a single event. It was a process that took weeks, during which the ice formed and broke and reformed, during which the river ran with slush and floes that could crush a boat or trap it. Those who had come to the Klondike in September and October, who had staked their claims and filed their papers, were now trapped. They could not leave. The river was closed. The passes over the mountains to the south, the Chilkoot and the White, were deep in snow. The trails were passable only by dog-sled, and a dog-sled could carry a man and a small load but not a fortune in gold. The news of the discovery, the gold itself, the letters that would carry word to the outside world, all of it was imprisoned by the freeze.

The winter of 1896 to 1897 settled over the Yukon Valley. The temperature dropped. The days shortened. In December, the sun rose at mid-morning and set in early afternoon, and in the deep winter, the solstice, the sun did not rise at all. On the creeks, men worked their claims by sinking shafts through the muck, the layer of frozen earth and gravel that lay above the bedrock. They built fires on the surface, thawed the ground below, and dug down. The work was slow. The shafts were narrow. Men worked in frozen darkness, in shafts barely wide enough to turn in, chipping at the frozen gravel with picks, hauling the muck to the surface in buckets, and building new fires to thaw the next layer down.

The gold came from the bedrock, from the crevices and the gravel where the river had deposited it in some ancient geological age. The gold was coarse. The nuggets were large. The dust was heavy. Those who worked the rich claims on Eldorado found gold in quantities that confirmed the early reports and exceeded them. They panned the gravel they had thawed and dug, and the pans showed color that was unmistakable. The gold went into containers, into cans and sacks and the leather pouches that prospectors used for their dust. It accumulated. Through the winter, it accumulated.

But it could not move. The gold was trapped. Those who owned it could not carry it out of the Yukon Valley because the river was frozen and the passes were snowbound. They could not send word of what they had found because the mail routes were closed. The North-West Mounted Police, who maintained the posts along the Yukon and its tributaries, could send dispatches by dog-sled, but the dispatches traveled slowly, and the nearest telegraph station was at Juneau, hundreds of miles to the south, across the mountains. The news of the Klondike discovery, the fact of the gold, the dimensions of the find, all of it was sealed inside the winter.

Word of the gold reached Circle City, the largest settlement on the upper Yukon, before the river closed. Circle City was a supply point and a social center for the miners working the American side of the border. Despite the winter, many prospectors immediately left for the Klondike by dog-sled, eager to reach the region before the best claims were taken. They traveled over the frozen river, following the Yukon upstream to the mouth of the Klondike, and from there to Bonanza and Eldorado. They arrived to find the best ground already staked. They took what remained.

The outside world was still largely unaware of the news. Canadian officials had received reports through the police posts, but the reports traveled slowly and the information was incomplete. The newspapers in San Francisco, Seattle, New York, and London had no correspondent in the Yukon. The wire services carried no dispatches. The world outside the Yukon Valley continued through the autumn and winter of 1896 to 1897 without knowledge that the largest gold deposit found in North America in the nineteenth century had been located on a tributary of the Klondike River.

The silence was total. It was enforced by the geography of the Yukon Valley, by the distance from the coast to the interior, by the mountain ranges that separated the Canadian Yukon from the Pacific ports, and above all by the season. The winter was the mandatory silence. No human agency imposed it. No government ordered it. No conspiracy maintained it. The river froze, the passes filled with snow, and the news stopped. The gold was in the ground. The claims were on the ledger. The men were in their cabins and their shafts. And the world outside went about its business without knowing that anything had changed.

Inside the Yukon, the winter was not silent. The mining community, closed and trapped, buzzed with the knowledge of what had been found. Men on the creeks talked. They showed each other their pans. They measured their dust. They calculated the value of their claims. The social rupture that had begun in September, when the first claims were staked, deepened through the winter. Those who held rich claims and those who held poor claims lived in the same camps, ate the same food, endured the same cold, and occupied different worlds. The jealousy and the rumor and the new hierarchies intensified in the confinement. The closed world of the sourdoughs, the experienced prospectors who had spent years in the north, was the first crucible of the gold rush. The winter was the second.

The cold tested them. The work was brutal. The isolation was complete. Those who survived the winter of 1896 to 1897 were men who had endured the Yukon at its worst, who had worked their claims through the dark months, who had held their ground against the cold and against each other. They emerged in the spring with gold that had been dug from the frozen gravel, and with claims that had been tested and proven. They also emerged with grievances, with disputes that had festered through the winter, with boundaries that had been contested and boundaries that had been crossed. The mining community that entered the winter was not the same community that emerged from it. The gold had changed it.

The thaw came in May. The Yukon River began to break. The ice that had bridged the channel since October cracked and shifted and broke into floes. The floes moved downstream, grinding against each other, piling up in jams that backed the water up and flooded the low ground along the banks. The river ran with ice and mud and the debris of a winter’s accumulation. The break-up was violent. It was also the signal that the silence was ending.

The first boats launched at Lake Lindeman and Lake Bennett, where the rivers that drained the coastal mountains gathered the snowmelt and began their run to the interior. The boats were small. They were canoes and scows and schooners built by men who had spent the winter at the lakes, waiting for the thaw. They carried supplies and mail and men. They did not carry gold, because the men who built them had not come from the Klondike. They were coming to the Klondike, not leaving it.

Those who were leaving the Klondike, the men who had spent the winter on Bonanza and Eldorado, prepared their own boats. They built scows and loaded them with the gold they had taken from the creek beds. They loaded their supplies and their tools and their mail. They wrote letters to families and to business partners and to the trading companies that had provisioned them. They sealed the letters and packed them with the gold. And they waited for the river to clear.

The Yukon cleared in June. The ice jams broke. The floes moved out. The river ran open, high with snowmelt, brown with sediment. The first boats launched from the Klondike carried men and gold and letters down the Yukon toward the coast. The voyage was long. The distance from Dawson City to St. Michael, at the mouth of the Yukon, was roughly sixteen hundred miles by river. The boats traveled with the current, but the current was fast and the channel was choked with the debris of break-up. The voyage took weeks. Those who made it carried in their boats the first physical evidence of what the Klondike contained.

The letters they carried were the first paper trail to reach the outside world. The claims on the recorder’s ledger at Fortymile had been the first, but they were local. The letters were the first documents to travel beyond the Yukon Valley. They carried the news of the discovery to the people who had sent the prospectors north, to the families who had not heard from them in months, to the trading companies that had provisioned them. These were private documents. Not official dispatches. Not newspaper reports. Personal correspondence, written by men who had found gold and who wanted someone to know.

The gold they carried was the first physical evidence. The nuggets and the dust, packed in cans and sacks and leather pouches, were the proof that the letters could not provide. The gold was the argument. It was the evidence that could not be disputed, could not be exaggerated, could not be dismissed as rumor. When the first boats reached St. Michael, and the gold was transferred to the steamers that ran the Alaska coast to San Francisco and Seattle, the evidence would be complete. The secret of the Klondike, held by the freeze of a river for eight months, would be broken.

The first physical packets of gold and letters began their slow voyage down the thawing Yukon River toward the coast in June 1897. The river carried them. The current pulled them north and west, away from the Klondike Valley, away from the creeks where the gold had been found, away from the claims on the ledger and the shafts in the frozen ground. The boats moved with the water, and the water moved toward the sea. The secret was ending.
