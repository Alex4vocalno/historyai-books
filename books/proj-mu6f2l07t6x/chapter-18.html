Chapter 18

The Exodus to the Beach

On a morning in the spring of 1899, with the light no longer falling from Jeff Smith’s Parlor across the mud of Skagway behind him, a man stood on Bonanza Creek with a sack of flour balanced on a stump and offered it for sale to anyone passing. He had carried that flour, sack by sack, over the Chilkoot Pass the year before. He had hauled it down the chain of lakes and through the White Pass rapids, cached it on the ice of Lake Bennett, and floated it down the Yukon to this creek.

A buyer stopped, examined the stitching, and offered a dollar and a half. The original price in Seattle had been four dollars. The freight, in muscle and time, had been incalculable. The miner accepted.

He sold his pick for fifty cents, his gold pan for a quarter, and his tent for what the buyer would give. Then he walked down the creek trail toward Dawson, carrying nothing he could not fit in his pockets, and booked passage on a steamer heading west.

A few weeks later, the Dawson waterfront presented the panoramic inversion of that sale. The steamer docks along the Yukon River were jammed with men and their remaining goods, and the vessels at the wharf, sternwheelers with names like the John J. Healy and the Portus B. Weare, sat low in the water, packed to the guards with passengers who had paid for deck space with the last of their Klondike earnings.

The crowd on the bank was leaving, not arriving. The flow that had come upriver through the previous two seasons, filling Dawson to perhaps thirty or forty thousand people by the summer of 1898, had reversed. Built of wood, isolated, and unsanitary, Dawson had suffered through fires, epidemics, and a winter of inflated prices that made paupers of men who had come expecting to be rich. Now the town was emptying, and the direction of the traffic told the story the Klondike Nugget could not quite bring itself to print: the creeks had not delivered.

The question of what would replace the order Soapy Smith had imposed on Skagway was, for Dawson, already becoming irrelevant. Smith had been dead since the previous July, shot on the Juneau Wharf, and the civic revolt that killed him had settled Skagway’s immediate problem in the only way available to a frontier town without a functioning court. But Dawson’s problem was different in kind.

Skagway’s crisis had been one of authority, whose rules would govern the gateway. Dawson’s crisis was one of arithmetic. Of the estimated thirty to forty thousand people who had reached the Klondike during the rush, no more than four thousand had struck gold, and only a few hundred had become genuinely rich. The rest, the fifteen to twenty thousand who had actually become prospectors rather than mere transients, and the larger mass who had never gotten past the labor of survival, faced a reckoning that no constable and no committee could resolve. The ground had been measured, staked, and worked, and the yields for the majority were thin.

By the spring of 1899, the telegraph line that now stretched from Skagway to Dawson carried news that did more than report events. It transmitted the specific, corrosive information that there was gold on a beach in western Alaska, and that the beach was open, unstaked, unclaimed, and free. The telegraph had arrived in stages, and by this year it connected Dawson to the outside world in something approaching real time. A rumor that would once have taken weeks to travel by dog team now moved in hours through the wire.

The news from Nome was simple and devastating: a man named Jafet Lindeberg, who had come to Alaska in 1890 and married an Indigenous woman, had been prospecting along the Bering Sea coast. In the autumn of 1898, on a stretch of beach near the mouth of the Snake River, he and two partners had found gold in the sand. Not in buried gravel reached by shaft and drift. In the sand itself, on the surface, within arm’s reach of the tide.

The contrast with the Klondike was exact and cruel. In the Klondike, gold lay beneath feet of frozen muck, and reaching it required sinking shafts through permafrost with wood fires, thawing the ground inch by inch, hoisting the gravel to the surface, and then, when the ground thawed in summer, running it through sluices fed by ditch water hauled from miles away. The work was industrial in its demands and individual in its rewards. A man on a good claim on Eldorado Creek could hire a crew and make wages that defied belief. A man on a poor claim, or on no claim at all, could work a season and emerge with less than he had brought.

On the Nome beach, the gold was in the sand. A man needed a shovel and a pan. The rumor carried the promise that the most brutal features of the Klondike, the frozen ground, the heavy machinery, the capital costs, the legal complexity of staking and maintaining claims on a Canadian creek, could be bypassed entirely.

The departure began as a trickle and became a flood. The passenger manifests of the steamers departing Dawson for St. Michael, the port at the mouth of the Yukon, told the quantitative story. Through May and June of 1899, the sternwheelers that had spent the previous season bringing stampeders upriver now carried them back down, and the numbers on the manifests climbed week by week. Men who had spent their last dollars securing passage up the Yukon now spent their last dollars securing passage down it. The John J. Healy, the Portus B. Weare, and the other vessels that had been the arteries of the stampede inward became the arteries of the stampede outward, and the direction of their travel was the most honest assessment of the Klondike’s promise that the season would produce.

The departure followed a precise economic logic that the records of the Mounted Police, the customs declarations, and the miners’ own letters make visible. A man who had staked a claim on a Klondike creek and found it barren, or found it productive enough to cover costs but not enough to justify another season of frozen shafts and thawed muck, faced a choice. He could remain on his claimed ground, working a marginal prospect through another winter, or he could sell what he had and move. The claim itself, the legal and psychological territory that had given him identity and purpose since his arrival, was worth something only if the ground beneath it produced gold. If it did not, the claim was a piece of paper and a hole in the permafrost. Abandoning it meant surrendering an identity the stampeders had crossed mountains to obtain.

The letters sent from St. Michael, where the Yukon River steamers transferred their passengers to ocean-going vessels for the passage to Nome, carried the texture of the disillusionment.

Men wrote to families in Seattle, in San Francisco, in towns across the American Midwest, and their accounts shared a common structure. Frozen shafts and wood fires burning through the night to thaw a few inches of ground appeared in letter after letter, alongside the gravel hoisted by hand windlass and the sluices that ran only when the ice broke and the ditches flowed. The yields appeared too: a few dollars a day, sometimes less, sometimes nothing. The cost of living in Dawson appeared as well, where a meal that cost fifty cents in Seattle cost a dollar and a half, and where a pair of boots that cost four dollars in the south cost twelve.

And then the news from Nome arrived through the telegraph and the newspapers with the force of a second gold strike, except that this one promised what the Klondike had not: easy gold, surface gold, gold for any man with a shovel.

The newspapers in Dawson, the Klondike Nugget and its competitors, tracked the departure with an anxiety that showed through the commercial advertising. Real estate listings appeared with falling prices. Lots on Front Street that had sold for thousands of dollars during the height of 1898 were offered at markdowns that would have been unthinkable a season before. Merchants advertised clearance sales on mining outfits, the same picks, pans, and provisions that had been hauled over the passes at such cost. The Nugget’s editorial pages argued for patience, for the staying power of the Klondike’s proven yields, for the reliability of deep ground over the speculative promise of a beach. But the argument was directed at men who had already heard it from the ground itself, and the ground had not been persuasive. The steamer manifests told a different story than the editorials, and the manifests were winning.

The North-West Mounted Police (NWMP), who had spent two years imposing order on the Klondike, weighing outfits, checking registrations, enforcing the law along the creeks and in the saloons of Dawson, now recorded the departure in their daily reports. The constables at the river landings counted passengers. The customs officers at the port of entry recorded the outbound cargo. The structure that Superintendent Sam Steele had built, the system that had made the stampede survivable by requiring every man to carry a ton of goods, now oversaw the dismantling of the population it had protected. The same regulatory apparatus that had weighed packs at the Chilkoot summit and checked registrations at the border now processed the exodus, and the efficiency of the departure was evidence of the same institutional discipline that had made the arrival possible. The Mounties did not prevent men from leaving. They counted them out.

The journey from Dawson to Nome was itself a passage through the geography of the stampede’s reversal. The Yukon River, which had carried the stampeders inland from St. Michael through the long summer of 1897 and the spring of 1898, now carried them back.

The sternwheelers ran downstream, past the mouths of the Klondike and the Sixtymile, past the flatlands of the Yukon Flats where the river widened and shallowed, past the bluff at Fort Yukon where the boundary line crossed, and on through the delta to the coast. The passage took weeks. The boats were crowded, and the men on the decks were not the hopeful stampeders who had come up the river with their ton of goods. Veterans of the passes, survivors of the lake, builders of boats and runners of the Whitehorse Rapids, sinkers of shafts and panners of gravel on a dozen creeks, they were leaving with less than they had brought.

The ton of goods that the Mounted Police had required, the flour, the bacon, the beans, the picks and pans and stoves and tents, had been consumed, sold, or abandoned. They carried what fit in a pack, and the pack was light.

At St. Michael, the transfer to ocean steamers completed the reversal. The port that had been the entry point for the upriver stampede became the staging ground for the coastal one. Men who had arrived at St. Michael in 1897 or 1898 and had immediately booked passage upriver now arrived from upriver and immediately booked passage west, along the Bering Sea coast, to Nome.

The journey from St. Michael to Nome was short, a day or two by steamer, depending on weather, and the vessels that ran it were the same type of coastal steamers that had served the Klondike trade. The difference was in the cargo.

The stampeders going upriver had carried tons of goods. The stampeders going to Nome carried almost nothing. They had learned, in the most expensive way available, that the weight that ensured survival was not the weight that ensured fortune, and that fortune did not, in the end, follow from preparation. They traveled light because the Klondike had stripped them of everything else.

The beach at Nome, when they arrived, was unlike anything the Klondike had prepared them for. The gold lay in the sand along the shore, deposited by the sea and the action of the tides over centuries, and the extraction required none of the infrastructure of a deep-placer mine. Men shoveled sand into rockers and pans, washed it in the surf, and found color, sometimes coarse gold, sometimes fine, but enough to sustain the conviction that the beach was richer than the creeks. The diggings stretched along the coast for miles, and the beach was open to anyone who could find a spot. There were no shafts to sink. There was no permafrost to thaw. The beach was the antithesis of the Klondike creek, and for men who had spent a season or two battling frozen ground, the simplicity of the work was its own argument.

The Nome beach was, in its way, the final verdict on the Klondike. Not because Nome was richer, for the richest ground at Nome, like the richest ground at the Klondike, was concentrated in a relatively small area and was quickly claimed, but because the exodus to it demonstrated the essential character of the gold rush phenomenon. The stampeders who abandoned their Klondike claims and booked passage to Nome were not deserting a proven opportunity for a speculative one. They were fleeing a place that had promised transformation and delivered labor, and they were fleeing toward a place that promised the same thing. The cycle was the point. The stampede was a condition of motion, sustained by the belief that the next creek, the next beach, the next strike would be the one where the labor would end and the fortune would begin.

The records of the departure, the steamer manifests, the customs declarations, the letters from St. Michael, the newspaper reports from Dawson and Seattle, all converge on the same quantitative reality. The population of Dawson, which had peaked at perhaps thirty to forty thousand during the summer of 1898, began a decline that would reduce it to a fraction of that number within a year. The merchants who had sold goods at inflated prices found their customers gone. The saloons that had served the wealthy and the hopeful found fewer of both. The real estate that had commanded ten thousand dollars a plot during the boom found no buyers at any price, and the wooden buildings that had housed the commerce of the gold rush stood empty along Front Street, their false fronts facing a river that carried fewer and fewer boats.

The North-West Mounted Police (NWMP), who had been the institutional spine of the Klondike from the first moment a constable set a tripod scale at the Chilkoot summit, recorded the departure with the same methodical attention they had given to the arrival. Along the creeks, the detachment reports noted the abandonment of claims. At the river landings, the daily counts tallied outbound passengers.

From Dawson itself, the reports described a town whose population was draining through its streets toward the docks, and the constables who wrote them understood, with the clarity that comes from counting things, that the Klondike was not ending because the gold was gone. The richest claims on Eldorado and Bonanza were still producing. The gold was still there. What was gone was the population that had come to extract it, and what had driven them away was not the absence of gold but the distribution of it.

The counter-argument, that the Klondike was the last great individualistic gold rush, where luck and daring were what mattered and the state was a peripheral imposition on a fundamentally wild event, collapses under the weight of the departure records. The men who left were not the timid or the unlucky who had been scared away by hardship. Veterans of the passes, survivors of the lake, builders of boats, sinkers of shafts: these were the men who boarded the steamers. Bold enough to reach the creeks, tough enough to work them, they left because the creeks, for the majority, did not pay, and because the institutional structure that had made the stampede survivable, the Mounted Police, the customs, the ton-of-goods requirement, had also made the disappointment legible. The same system that had weighed their outfits and counted their arrival now counted their departure, and the count was the judgment.

The Nome beach, in the summer of 1899, received the Klondike’s veterans and gave them what the Klondike had not: a diggings where the gold was visible, where the labor was simple, and where the legal apparatus of staking and claiming was, for the moment, irrelevant. The beach was unclaimed ground, and the men who worked it were not staking claims. They were digging sand.

The simplicity was intoxicating and temporary, and within months the legal structure that had organized the Klondike, the recording of claims, the resolution of disputes, the policing of boundaries, would arrive at Nome too, imposed by the same American authorities who had been absent from the Klondike and present, in the form of the Mounted Police, throughout it.

But for a few weeks in the summer of 1899, the Nome beach was what the Klondike had never been: a place where any man could dig, and where the digging was enough.

The men who stood on that beach, rocking sand in the surf of the Bering Sea, were the product of the Klondike in a sense that the gold they found there could not capture. Hardened by the trails, seasoned by the creeks, taught at a cost that none would fully calculate how to survive in the country, they carried the Klondike’s most lasting product with them. That product was the population that went out on the steamers: a mobile, experienced, and professionalized cadre of prospectors who had learned the trade in the most demanding conditions the continent could offer and who would carry that trade to Nome, to Fairbanks, to every subsequent strike that rumor could reach and a steamer could serve. The gold rush had not made them rich. It had made them competent, and their competence was now headed for the next beach.

The exodus from the Klondike to the Nome beach was the Klondike’s verdict, rendered by the men who had done the work, in the only language they had: their feet. They walked down the creek trails. They boarded the steamers. They went down the Yukon. They transferred at St. Michael. They landed at Nome. And the beach they stood on, with its gold in the sand and its tide at their feet, was the same promise, in a different place, made by the same rumor to the same men, and the cycle that had begun on Bonanza Creek in August 1896 was now, in the summer of 1899, beginning again.
