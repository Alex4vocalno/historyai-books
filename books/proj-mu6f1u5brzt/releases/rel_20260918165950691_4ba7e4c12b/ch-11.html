Chapter 11

Army Brigadier General Frederick Funston

The gap between intention and execution—between the decision to sacrifice city blocks and the actual cutting of firebreaks—would consume the afternoon and much of the night, leaving hours in which the fire continued its unopposed advance. By mid-morning of April 18, 1906, this interval of paralysis had produced its own desperate logic. The unified wall of flame, now stretching from the waterfront to Mission Dolores, was too large for civilian authority, too destructive for ordinary methods. The city’s fire chief, Dennis T. Sullivan, lay dying from injuries sustained when bricks crashed through the roof of his engine house; its mayor was scrambling through rubble-strewn streets, and its water system lay in fragments beneath the broken pavement. Into this vacuum stepped a man who had spent his career creating such vacuums and filling them with his own authority.

Brigadier General Frederick Funston stood in the commandant’s office at the Presidio, a coastal military reservation three miles west of the burning city. He was forty years old, five feet four inches tall, and already the possessor of a Medal of Honor for actions in the Philippines that his superiors had officially deplored and privately celebrated. The Presidio’s telephone lines still functioned, and through them came fragments of catastrophe: the city hall dome collapsed, the water mains severed, fires spreading unchecked from the industrial district toward the commercial core. Funston did not wait for orders from Washington. He did not consult with Mayor Eugene Schmitz, whose authority he would shortly supersede. He signed deployment orders with his own hand and sent mounted couriers galloping toward the barracks.

The mechanism began to turn.

By 10:30 a.m., elements of the 22nd and 29th Infantry Regiments were moving down Lombard Street toward the Marina District, rifles slung, fixed bayonets catching the morning light. They encountered their first refugees streaming in the opposite direction—women carrying sewing machines, men with mattresses on their backs, children clutching family Bibles. The soldiers had no written orders beyond Funston’s verbal command to preserve order and assist the fire department. They had no legal authority to perform either function. The Constitution assigns control of domestic civil disorder to state and local authorities; federal troops may intervene only at the request of a governor or, in extreme circumstances, by presidential order. California’s governor, George Pardee, was in Sacramento, cut off by damaged telegraph lines. President Theodore Roosevelt was in Washington, unaware that San Francisco existed in anything other than its usual condition. Funston acted anyway.

This was the Catastrophic Default: the automatic reversion to military control when civic leadership was physically or morally incapacitated. It would define everything that followed.

The first patrols reached Market Street shortly after 11:00 a.m. Sergeant Edward O’Brien, commanding a squad from the 22nd Infantry, later filed a report describing great excitement among the population, many persons having lost their homes and all their possessions, wandering aimlessly about the streets. His men established a cordon at Third and Market, turning back looters—or those they identified as looters—at bayonet point. The distinction between scavenging and survival was not one the Army had trained its soldiers to make. A civilian watching from the doorway of a hotel on Market Street recorded that the soldiers appeared nervous, fingering their triggers, while the crowd pressed against their line. The nervousness was mutual. The population had not requested military occupation. It had not been consulted about it.

Funston’s unilateral decision had created a dual chain of command that would complicate every subsequent choice. Mayor Schmitz, when he finally reached his office in the undamaged portion of city hall, found that federal troops already controlled the principal thoroughfares. The fire chief who had assumed command upon Dennis Sullivan’s incapacitation discovered that soldiers were assisting his department—under their own officers, according to their own priorities.

The Army after-action reports, compiled in the weeks following the disaster, reveal a command structure that Funston improvised hour by hour. At 12:15 p.m., he telegraphed the War Department that a serious earthquake had occurred in San Francisco that morning, followed by fire; that he was cooperating with civil authorities; and that he had the entire garrison at the disposition of the mayor. The formulation was masterful. He was not requesting permission. He was not even accurately describing his actions—he had not consulted Schmitz before deploying troops, and the cooperation was entirely one-directional. He was establishing a paper trail that would retroactively legitimize what he had already done.

The telegrams between Funston and Washington, preserved in the National Archives, trace the construction of this alibi in real time. At 2:40 p.m., he reported that fire was now sweeping the whole city, that the water supply was cut off, and that he was using troops to assist the fire department and preserve order. At 4:15 p.m., he declared the situation desperate and announced that he had assumed military control of the city. The progression is instructive.

First cooperation, then assistance, finally assumption. Each telegram reached a War Department that was itself uncertain of its authority. Secretary of War William Howard Taft was traveling; Acting Secretary Robert Shaw Oliver had no precedent for federal intervention in a municipal disaster. The replies, when they came, were carefully noncommittal: continue cooperation with civil authorities. Funston interpreted this silence as authorization. He was, by training and temperament, a man who created facts and let others adjust to them.

The immediate consequences of this assumption of power fell unevenly across the city. For soldiers, it meant improvising roles for which they had no preparation. Private James McCafferty of the 29th Infantry spent the afternoon of April 18 guarding a grocery store at Fifth and Mission while its owner, at gunpoint, distributed inventory to refugees. The General’s orders were to prevent looting, McCafferty later testified, but it seemed hard to call a hungry man a looter when he took bread. The Firebreak Calculus was already taking shape: human safety explicitly subordinated to property defense in the strategic necessity of the moment.

For civilians, the appearance of uniformed troops produced contradictory responses. Emma Houghton’s letter to her sister in Boston, written April 20 from a refugee camp in Golden Gate Park, expressed profound relief at seeing the soldiers, who have restored some measure of security. Henry Clews, a banker who had watched his Montgomery Street office burn, recorded in his journal that the military presence feels like occupation, not assistance—we are treated as a conquered population.

Both observations were accurate. The dual chain of command meant that authority was now exercised twice: once by the civilian officials who retained nominal power, once by the military officers who possessed actual force. The friction between these systems generated its own casualties. At approximately 3:00 p.m., a patrol from the 22nd Infantry encountered a group of men attempting to move furniture from a burning building on Mission Street. The soldiers ordered them to desist; the men, who later proved to be the building’s owners, refused. Accounts differ on what followed. The Army report states that the patrol fired warning shots and the persons dispersed. The civilian complaint filed with the mayor’s office claims that soldiers bayoneted a man without provocation, causing serious injury. The discrepancy was never resolved. Funston’s command structure had no mechanism for investigating civilian complaints against his troops.

The dynamiting operations that began Wednesday afternoon illustrated the hazards of this divided authority. Funston had placed an officer of the Corps of Engineers in charge of demolishing buildings to create firebreaks, working in supposed coordination with the fire department. The coordination was largely theoretical. The engineer’s teams used black powder and guncotton on structures that fire officials believed could still be saved; they demolished buildings that were not in the fire’s path, on the theory that a wide cleared zone is preferable to a narrow one. The fire chief protested these decisions in a message to Funston at 5:30 p.m., noting that engineer officers were destroying property without reference to fire department requirements and requesting an immediate conference. The request was denied. Funston was already committed to his strategy of maximum clearance, and he had the troops to enforce it.

The evening of April 18 found San Francisco governed by two incompatible systems. Mayor Schmitz, operating from a borrowed desk in the Mechanics’ Pavilion, was attempting to organize relief supplies and identify shelter for refugees. General Funston, at the Presidio, was directing military patrols, dynamiting operations, and the establishment of detention points for suspicious persons. The city had not declared martial law; Funston had simply begun exercising its prerogatives. The distinction mattered legally—martial law suspends civilian authority entirely, while Funston’s arrangement left Schmitz nominally in charge—but it mattered hardly at all on the streets, where soldiers’ orders took precedence over mayoral proclamations.

The night brought no relief from this confusion. At 9:15 p.m., Funston issued orders expanding his troops’ authority to shoot looters on sight. The order was oral, transmitted through the chain of command to company commanders in the field. It had no legal foundation. The Army’s own regulations required written authorization for lethal force against civilians; Funston later claimed that the emergency precluded compliance with normal procedures. The consequences were immediate and, in some cases, fatal. Private Albert Morrison, patrolling the ruins of the South of Market district, fired on a figure he believed to be running from a burning warehouse. The figure proved to be a forty-seven-year-old teamster who had been searching for his missing daughter. The man died at the receiving hospital at 10:40 p.m. The Army record lists him as civilian, killed while resisting military authority. No investigation was conducted.

The Firebreak Calculus operated with equal coldness on property. By midnight, the engineer’s demolition teams had cleared a swath four blocks wide along Valencia Street, destroying an estimated two million dollars in real estate to create a barrier against the fire’s southern advance. The fire ignored it, jumping the gap on a wind shift at 1:30 a.m. And consuming the very buildings the operation had been designed to save. Funston’s response, recorded in a 2:15 a.m. Telegram to Washington, was characteristic: demolition plan partially successful; will expand operations at dawn. The partial success was invisible on the ground; the expansion was already ordered.

The dual chain of command produced its most surreal effects in the treatment of Chinatown. The Chinese quarter, occupying some fifteen blocks between Bush and Broadway, had suffered severe damage in the earthquake and was now threatened by fires approaching from three directions. Mayor Schmitz, informed by his police chief that Oriental criminals were using the disaster to conceal looting, requested military assistance in evacuating the district.

Funston interpreted this request broadly. At 6:00 a.m. On April 19, troops began systematic clearance of Chinatown, directing residents to evacuation points at Portsmouth Square and the waterfront. The operation was efficient and brutal. Families were separated; baggage was limited to what could be carried; resistance was met with bayonet prods and rifle butts. The Chinese consul filed a protest with the State Department on April 22, alleging that American military forces had dispossessed peaceful residents of their lawful property without process of law. The protest was acknowledged and filed. The clearance continued.

Funston’s decision to deploy had rested upon a particular reading of military necessity that his subsequent telegrams would carefully obscure. The Presidio itself, with its stone barracks and reinforced magazines, had suffered remarkably little damage in the earthquake—three chimneys collapsed, one water tower cracked, no casualties among the garrison. This physical security gave Funston a communications advantage that civic authorities lacked.

While Mayor Schmitz scrambled through streets where every second building threatened to fall, Funston could receive reports, issue orders, and maintain the appearance of systematic response. The contrast was not lost on observers. Major General Adolphus Greely, arriving from Washington three days later to assess the situation, noted in his official report that General Funston’s position at the Presidio, with telephonic and telegraphic connections intact, placed him in effective control of information before any other authority could organize. The control of information preceded and enabled the control of force.

The specific composition of the forces Funston commanded shaped the character of the occupation. The Presidio garrison in April 1906 consisted of approximately 2, 400 regular Army troops, supplemented by several hundred medical personnel and a detachment of the Signal Corps. These were not occupation troops in the Philippine mold; they were garrison soldiers, trained for routine duties and small-unit discipline, suddenly thrust into urban emergency management. The 22nd Infantry had arrived from Fort Crook, Nebraska, only six months earlier; the 29th Infantry was permanently stationed at the Presidio. Neither regiment had trained for riot control, firefighting, or mass evacuation. Their officers, products of the Spanish-American War and the Philippine insurrection, possessed experience in unconventional warfare against irregular forces—experience that translated poorly to the streets of an American city.

Captain John H. Reeves, who commanded the first detachment to reach the Ferry Building, later admitted in a private letter that his men treated the civilian population with the same suspicion they had directed toward Filipino villagers, seeing potential insurgents in every frightened refugee.

This institutional inheritance affected every interaction between soldiers and civilians. The Army’s small-unit tactics emphasized firepower and aggressive patrolling; its disciplinary code prioritized immediate obedience to orders over legal nuance. When Funston’s oral authorization to shoot looters circulated through the ranks, it encountered men already conditioned to accept lethal authority in ambiguous circumstances. The regulations that should have constrained this tendency—specifically General Orders No. 100, the Army’s code of martial law derived from the Lieber Code of 1863—required written proclamation, judicial oversight, and clear definition of military necessity. Funston satisfied none of these requirements. His command operated in a deliberate legal gray zone, neither fully subject to civilian law nor formally governed by military law, answerable ultimately to his own assessment of emergency.

The War Department’s response to this constitutional improvisation revealed the institutional incentives that would sustain it. Acting Secretary Oliver, receiving Funston’s telegrams in Washington, faced a dilemma that would have paralyzed a more scrupulous administrator. To repudiate Funston’s actions would be to acknowledge federal military intervention in a domestic emergency without proper authorization; to approve them retroactively would be to legitimize a precedent for unilateral military assumption of civil power. Oliver chose a third path: studied ambiguity. His replies emphasized cooperation with civil authorities without inquiring whether such cooperation existed; they acknowledged receipt of Funston’s situation reports without endorsing his specific measures; they promised additional supplies and personnel while remaining silent on the legal basis for their deployment.

This bureaucratic evasion had practical consequences. Funston interpreted Oliver’s silence on legal questions as tacit approval; subsequent Army historians would cite the War Department’s continued correspondence as evidence of official sanction; and the constitutional questions raised by the occupation would remain unresolved, available for invocation in future emergencies.

The personal dimensions of Funston’s authority deserve attention, for they illuminate how the dual chain of command operated at the level of individual encounter. Funston was, by contemporary accounts, a man of extraordinary physical and psychological intensity—small in stature but commanding in presence, capable of sustained exertion without apparent fatigue, indifferent to personal comfort or conventional propriety. These traits, developed during his years as a filibuster in Cuba and an officer in the Philippine constabulary, made him exceptionally effective in chaotic environments and exceptionally resistant to institutional constraint. His Medal of Honor, awarded for capturing Filipino insurgent leader Emilio Aguinaldo through a ruse involving disguised scouts, represented precisely the kind of extra-legal initiative that his superiors officially deplored. The pattern was consistent: Funston created situations that required his particular talents, then exploited them with complete commitment. The earthquake emergency offered the largest stage yet for this performance.

The strain of the dual command fell most heavily on those caught between its competing claims. Fire Chief John Dougherty, who had assumed command upon Sullivan’s death, found his department effectively subordinated to Army engineers within hours of organizing his own response. His formal protests to Funston, preserved in the municipal archives, trace a progressive recognition that military cooperation meant military direction.

At 3:00 p.m. on April 18, Dougherty requested Army assistance in guarding fire apparatus; by 6:00 p.m., he was objecting to engineer officers who countermanded his decisions on building demolition; by 9:00 p.m., he had ceased to issue orders that might conflict with military priorities. The fire department’s institutional knowledge—its understanding of building construction, water pressure, wind patterns, and the specific vulnerabilities of San Francisco’s street grid—was systematically disregarded in favor of the engineer’s geometric approach to firebreak construction. The result was demolition without strategic purpose, property destroyed without corresponding protection of adjacent structures, and the eventual burning of areas that competent fire management might have saved.

The economic logic of military control extended beyond the immediate emergency to shape the city’s recovery. Funston’s authority to requisition supplies, to direct labor, and to control transportation created opportunities for profit and loss that privileged those with military connections. The Army’s quartermaster corps became the effective distributor of food, blankets, and medical supplies; its engineering officers determined which buildings would be demolished and which spared; its patrols controlled access to the undamaged portions of the city. These powers were exercised without systematic accounting or external audit.

By the morning of April 19, Funston’s improvised command structure had become the effective government of San Francisco. His troops controlled all major intersections; his engineers directed the demolition operations; his officers determined who would be detained, which buildings would be destroyed, and where refugees would be directed. Mayor Schmitz remained in his office, issuing proclamations that soldiers enforced or ignored according to Funston’s priorities. The Committee of Fifty, a self-appointed group of businessmen that had formed to coordinate relief, found that its decisions required military approval to be implemented. The fire department, lacking water and now subordinate to Army engineers, could only watch as its traditional authority was usurped.

The established fact of military control in the streets created the pressure for Mayor Schmitz to reassert civilian authority.
