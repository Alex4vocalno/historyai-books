Chapter 27

A New City Upon the Ashes, But Not For All

Seen from above in the summer of 1909, the geometry of a remade city came into focus. The official map of the rebuilt downtown showed a San Francisco improved by catastrophe, its tangled past rationalized into a grid of straight avenues and regular blocks. The harbor lay clean against the eastern edge, while widened streets and a grand new civic center dome marked where the fire had burned hottest. This view pleased the eye with order, noting fire-resistant zones and future exposition grounds. What the map did not mark were the properties held in escrow, the lots where working-class houses had stood, or the new location of Chinatown, shifted from its original ground.

The descent from that aerial perspective lands first on a document dated March 3, 1909: Ordinance 5339, passed by the Board of Supervisors and signed by Mayor Edward R. Taylor, who had replaced the convicted Eugene Schmitz.

The ordinance amended the municipal code to establish “fire limits,” zones within which only specified materials and methods could be used in construction. The stated purpose was safety. The practical effect, recorded in the planning commission minutes of February 12, 1909, was to prohibit the rebuilding of wood-frame structures throughout the South of Market district, where the workingman’s cottages and boarding houses had packed the streets before April 1906. The commission’s secretary noted that “undesirable elements” had previously congested the area, and that the new zoning would encourage “a better class of improvement.”

The fire limits did not extend to the industrial zones along the waterfront, where employers still needed cheap labor within walking distance of the docks. They fell instead on the residential blocks where families had rented rooms by the week.

The evidence of intent accumulates in the property records of the same period. The Relief and Red Cross Corporation, which had administered $9 million in private and federal aid, maintained a property department whose function was to acquire damaged lots, clear titles, and resell to approved developers. Its correspondence, preserved in the California Historical Society archives, shows a consistent pattern: offers to Chinese property owners ran at sixty to seventy percent of assessed value, while offers to white owners in comparable districts approached ninety percent. When the Six Companies, representing Chinatown’s mercantile interests, petitioned in December 1908 to rebuild on the original Dupont Street ground, the relief corporation’s director responded that concentrated Oriental settlement presented fire hazards and that dispersal would benefit both the Chinese and the city. The petition was denied.

The new Chinatown would rise on former marshland west of Stockton Street, where the fill had not yet settled and where the sewers backed up in winter rain.

This was the physical reconstruction celebrated in the engineering reports of 1908–1909: the Municipal League’s San Francisco Rebuilt, the Southern Pacific’s promotional California for the Settler, the professional journals that praised the new steel-frame buildings and the revolutionary slow-burning construction required by the amended codes. The Palace Hotel reopened in December 1909 with electric elevators and a steel skeleton that could, its managers claimed, withstand any shock. The new Merchants Exchange Building on California Street incorporated lessons from the fire in its fireproof vaults and automatic sprinkler systems. The engineers had learned. The city had advanced. The reports measured progress in cubic yards of concrete, tons of structural steel, miles of improved water mains, the redundancy that Chief Sullivan had never lived to see, the five independent systems that now fed the hydrants where his single pipe had failed.

Dennis T. Sullivan, mortally wounded when the California Hotel collapsed onto his apartment in the Bush Street fire station, had become in death a symbol of the city’s debt to its public servants.

The fireboat named for him launched in 1909 from the Union Iron Works, its pumps capable of 9, 000 gallons per minute, its hull designed to survive the shock of collision with burning piers. The new residence for fire chiefs on Franklin Street carried his name in bronze above the door.

These memorials honored a man who had believed in prevention, in inspection, in the systematic reduction of risk through professional attention. They also distracted from the question of why his department had lacked the tools his successor now commanded.

The new equipment testified to loss rather than foresight. The city had not funded Sullivan’s 1903 proposal for an emergency water supply system from reservoirs on the city’s high peaks; it funded them after he was dead.

The contrast between the triumphal narrative and the documentary record sharpens in the zoning resolutions of 1908–1909. The Municipal Art Commission, established by the new charter of 1898 but empowered only after the fire, produced a plan for the rebuilt city that its chairman, Daniel Burnham, had presented in September 1905 and that the catastrophe had seemed to make possible. Burnham’s plan had proposed a grand civic center, diagonal boulevards, a park system connecting the bay to the ocean.

The 1909 implementation retained the civic center and the widened streets, but abandoned the public housing provisions, the distributed parks, the requirements that new construction include light courts and ventilation shafts. What survived was the geometry that served property values; what disappeared was the social program that might have constrained them. The Burnham plan had envisioned the city as a coherent organism; the rebuilt city was a portfolio of discrete investments, each regulated to protect its neighbors’ returns.

The mechanism of this transformation operated through the permits and the inspections that the building department now controlled. A property owner seeking to rebuild in the fire limits had to submit plans certified by a licensed architect, pay fees for structural review, and demonstrate compliance with the new codes. The process added six to eight months to construction timelines and raised costs by an estimated thirty percent. For the owners of small residential properties, those who had rented rooms to laborers, who had carried mortgages of $2, 000 to $5, 000, the delay meant default. The relief corporation acquired such properties through tax sales and resold them to developers who could afford the compliance costs. The records of these transactions, filed with the county recorder, show a concentration of ownership: by 1910, three development companies controlled forty percent of the South of Market lots where working-class housing had stood in 1900.

The Chinese community experienced this concentration as displacement and dispersal. The new Chinatown, officially designated in the 1909 zoning maps, occupied twelve blocks where the old had occupied eight, but the additional space was marginal, filling land, subject to flooding, distant from the banks and the customs house that had anchored the old mercantile district. The Six Companies protested again in April 1909, noting that the relocation would destroy established businesses and fragment a community that had maintained mutual aid societies, language schools, and family associations in geographic proximity. The protest was recorded in the supervisors’ minutes and referred to committee, where it remained. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 had already restricted immigration; the 1909 zoning completed the work of exclusion by rearranging the ground itself.

The working-class districts of the Mission and the Potrero, which the fire had spared, faced a different pressure. The new building codes did not apply there, but the infrastructure investments did not reach there either. The water department’s post-fire improvements concentrated on the high-value districts: the new mains ran down Market and Mission and the western avenues, while the eastern and southern margins continued to rely on the pre-1906 pipes. When the city engineer reported in 1909 that San Francisco now possessed the finest fire protection system in the United States, he meant the system that protected the downtown and the western residential districts. The eastern waterfront, where the sugar refineries and the canneries employed thousands, drew its water from the same mains that had failed in 1906. The redundancy was selective. The protection was tiered.

This tiering appears in the mortality records as well as the maps. The city health department’s annual report for 1909 noted a decline in overall death rates and attributed it to improved housing stock in the rebuilt districts. It did not note the continued high rates in the Mission and the Potrero, where tuberculosis and pneumonia remained endemic, or the spike in infant mortality in the relocated Chinatown, where the drainage problems the Six Companies had predicted had materialized in contaminated wells and flooded cellars. The statistics that demonstrated progress were collected from the districts where progress had been built. The districts where conditions had stagnated or deteriorated were statistically invisible, their populations excluded from the narrative of recovery.

The graft trials of 1907–1908 had exposed the corruption of the Schmitz administration without altering the structures that had enabled it.

Abraham Ruef, the political boss who had collected bribes from the traction companies and the gas monopoly, served four years in San Quentin and emerged to practice law again. Mayor Schmitz, convicted of extortion and later acquitted on appeal, spent his later years in real estate speculation. The corporations that had paid the bribes, the United Railroads, the Pacific Gas and Electric Company, retained their franchises and their rate structures.

The new mayor, Taylor, had campaigned on reform, but his administration continued the practice of delegating public functions to private committees. The Committee of Fifty, which had governed the emergency in April 1906, had formalized itself into the Citizens’ Committee for the Reconstruction of San Francisco, which advised, effectively directed, the allocation of relief funds and the priorities of rebuilding.

The same men who had ordered the dynamiting of firebreaks now sat on the boards of the development companies that profited from the cleared ground.

The dynamiting itself, that desperate calculus of sacrificing one zone to save another, had established a template for decision-making that outlasted the emergency. The official report of the Board of Fire Commissioners, published in 1908, justified the destruction of the Mission district’s industrial buildings as necessary to prevent the fire’s spread to the residential hills. The report did not address the fact that the dynamiting had begun before the fire reached the Mission, or that the destroyed buildings had belonged to competitors of the utilities whose executives served on the commission. The pattern was not conspiracy but convergence: the men who made the decisions in the crisis were the men who stood to benefit from their outcomes, and no institutional mechanism existed to separate their interests from the public’s.

This convergence shaped the rebuilt city’s social geography. The widened streets and the new building codes had cleared the tenements and the boarding houses; the zoning ordinances had prevented their return. The labor force that served the downtown hotels and the financial district commuted now from the outer Mission, from Daly City, from the East Bay ferries, journeys of an hour or more that had been unnecessary when workers could walk from the South of Market. The relief corporation’s annual report for 1909 celebrated this dispersal as “decongestion,” a public health benefit. It did not calculate the cost in time and fare, or the effect on family life, or the loss of the street-level economies that had sustained the old neighborhoods. The city had been made safer for property. The people who had lived in the unsafe city had been made mobile.

The mobile population included the refugees who had camped in Golden Gate Park and the Presidio in April 1906, who had received tents and rations and medical attention from the Army and the Red Cross. By 1909, most had found permanent housing, though not in the city. The relief corporation’s resettlement program, operated in conjunction with the Southern Pacific and the Santa Fe, had assisted more than 20, 000 San Franciscans to relocate to Los Angeles, to Portland, to the agricultural valleys of the Central Valley.

The assistance was voluntary in form: a ticket and a small cash grant in exchange for a signed agreement not to return for twelve months. The form obscured the pressure. The refugees who lacked property titles, who could not demonstrate ownership of burned lots, were ineligible for rebuilding permits in any case. The railroads needed labor; the city needed to reduce its dependent population; the arrangement served all parties except those who departed.

Among those who remained, the distribution of recovery followed the distribution of political influence. The Anglo-Saxon Protestant neighborhoods of Pacific Heights and the Richmond received priority in infrastructure improvements and school construction. The Catholic working-class districts of the Mission petitioned for sewers and paved streets and received promises that the 1909 city engineer’s budget did not fulfill. The Chinese community, formally excluded from naturalized citizenship, had no electoral leverage and received only the relocation that the Six Companies had resisted. The Japanese community, smaller but commercially significant, negotiated a partial exception: the zoning board permitted the retention of the Japanese quarter near the waterfront, though it required the demolition of wood-frame structures and their replacement with brick commercial buildings that raised rents and displaced residential tenants.

The brick commercial buildings rose through 1908 and 1909, their construction documented in the Pacific Builder and Engineer and the Architect and Engineer of California: the new Merchants Exchange, the rebuilt Mills Building, the Flood Building on Market Street with its steel frame and its terra-cotta facing. These were genuine technical achievements. The engineers had learned from the fire’s destruction of the so-called fireproof structures of 1906, whose brick shells had collapsed when their wooden interiors burned. The new codes required concrete floors, metal lath, the elimination of concealed combustibles. A building constructed to the 1909 standards would survive a fire that would have destroyed its 1900 equivalent. The knowledge was real. The application was selective.

The selectivity appears in the contrast between the downtown commercial core and the residential periphery. The new codes applied by law only within the fire limits; beyond them, construction remained largely unregulated. The result was a city of two building stocks: the dense, expensive, technically advanced center, and the scattered, cheap, vulnerable margins. The 1906 fire had demonstrated that fire traveled across such boundaries, that the protection of one district required the protection of its neighbors. The 1909 system ignored this demonstration. It protected the valuable properties and accepted the risk to the less valuable ones, calculating that the next fire, if it came, could be contained by the new water mains and the new fireboats before it reached the core.

This calculation was the legacy of the three days of fire: not the lesson that cities must protect all their inhabitants, but the lesson that protection could be rationed according to assessed value. The men who had ordered the dynamiting of the Mission had learned that destruction could be strategic, that the sacrifice of some interests could preserve others. The rebuilt city embodied this learning in its concrete and its steel, its widened streets and its excluded populations. The physical reconstruction was genuine and impressive; the social reconstruction was equally genuine, equally deliberate, and far less visible in the booster literature that proclaimed San Francisco’s resurrection.

The invisibility was itself an achievement of the post-fire narrative. The newspapers that had printed casualty figures and property losses in April 1906 had, by 1909, settled on a story of triumphant recovery. The Chronicle and the Examiner competed in their celebrations of the new buildings, the new infrastructure, the Panama-Pacific Exposition that would demonstrate to the world what American energy could accomplish.

The exposition, planned for 1915, would occupy the reclaimed marshlands of the northern waterfront, the same ground where the relief corporation had settled the refugees in 1906, where the city had dumped the rubble of the burned buildings, where the fill had not yet stabilized. The symbolism was unconscious but precise: the celebration of the new city would rise on the buried remains of the old, on the compressed ashes of the Chinatown that had burned and the South of Market that had been dynamited and the working-class houses that had been zoned out of existence.

The buried remains included the records of the decisions that had shaped the rebuilding. The relief corporation’s files, transferred to the city archives in 1910, were incomplete: the property department’s correspondence with Chinese owners, the planning commission’s discussions of “undesirable elements,” the instructions to inspectors about which violations to enforce and which to overlook. What survived was the official story: the engineering reports, the building permits, the zoning maps that showed a rational city emerging from chaos. The chaos had been rational too, in its way, a systematic application of power to rearrange the distribution of land and people according to the interests of those who held power.

The final measure of this rearrangement lies in the population statistics of 1909 and 1910. The federal census of 1910 recorded a San Francisco that had recovered its 1900 population total but had changed its composition. The Chinese population had declined by thirty percent; the Japanese by fifteen; the Italian and Irish working-class populations by smaller but significant margins. The increase came in the professional and managerial classes, in the native-born white population, in the residents of the new apartment buildings and the single-family homes that replaced the tenements. The city had been filtered. The fire and the zoning and the relief policies had accomplished what the exclusion laws alone could not: a demographic reconstruction that matched the physical one.

The filtering continued in the decade that followed, as the Panama-Pacific Exposition approached and the booster narrative hardened into civic mythology. The earthquake that had initiated the disaster was increasingly forgotten, its mention discouraged by the commercial interests who understood that “fire” was insurable and “earthquake” was not. The rebuilt city rose on foundations that denied the ground’s instability, just as it rose on a social order that denied the displacement and segregation that had constructed it. The denial was not conspiracy but structure: a set of institutions and practices that made certain outcomes inevitable while distributing responsibility so broadly that no individual could be held accountable.

Dennis T. Sullivan had believed in accountability. His reports as fire chief, preserved in the municipal archives, had named the owners of unsafe buildings, the blocked exits, the inadequate water pressure. He had understood that catastrophe was not accidental but cumulative, the product of neglected warnings and deferred maintenance. The city that named its fireboat and its fire chiefs’ residence for him had not adopted his methods. It had adopted his memory as decoration, while continuing the practices that had made his death predictable. The new water mains and the new building codes were genuine improvements, but they were improvements applied selectively, within a system that still calculated the value of human lives against the cost of protection.

The new city stands materially rebuilt, but its foundations are the permanent, intentional scars of segregation and displacement, the final, concrete consequence of the three days of fire.
