Chapter 13

The Military and Civic

The pattern established by Mayor Schmitz’s proclamation—civic authority licensing military violence against civilians—had by Thursday morning, April 19, 1906, become the operating system of San Francisco’s improvised government. Where Wednesday’s shoot-to-kill order sacrificed individual lives to the imperative of order, the dawn of the second day would demand a larger sacrifice: entire districts, chosen for destruction by the same men who claimed to be saving the city.

The dynamiting had begun almost immediately after the shock. In principle, the method was sound. Create gaps too wide for flames to cross. Deprive the advancing wall of fuel. In practice, the results had been catastrophic. The explosives available were military-grade, designed for breaching fortifications rather than surgical demolition in an urban environment. The troops handling them knew destruction, not fire behavior. Charges were placed hastily, often in buildings already ignited, and the resulting explosions scattered burning debris across adjacent blocks, seeding new fires faster than the original conflagration could have spread on its own. A report filed later by Army officers acknowledged what participants had observed in real time: the piecemeal dynamiting of Wednesday afternoon and evening had accelerated the disaster it was meant to contain.

This failure created the conditions for a more systematic gamble. The fire was moving east to west through the downtown core, having already consumed the financial district and the hotels along Market Street. Its advance was not uniform. It jumped, paused, surged again according to wind and available fuel. But its general direction was clear. Before noon on Thursday, observers at the Presidio could see a continuous column of smoke rising from the eastern half of the city. The western half, including the mansions of Nob Hill and the residential districts toward the Pacific, remained untouched. Between these two conditions lay Van Ness Avenue.

Before 1906, Van Ness Avenue was known as the city’s grandest boulevard, lined with Victorian mansions and impressive churches. The street had been renamed in honor of James Van Ness, the sixth mayor, in an era when San Francisco was asserting its metropolitan ambitions against the older capitals of the East. It ran north-south for more than a mile, from Market Street to Bay Street, its width exceeding that of any comparable residential thoroughfare in the West.

The avenue’s eastern sidewalk marked the boundary of the burned district as it stood Thursday morning. Its western edge opened onto intact blocks of wealthy homes, the hospitals and churches of the Western Addition, the approaches to Golden Gate Park and the Presidio itself.

If the fire crossed Van Ness, the western half of the city would face the same destruction that had already consumed the eastern half. If Van Ness could be made into a firebreak, the advance might be stopped.

The decision to attempt this did not emerge from a single moment of deliberation. It accumulated through the morning hours of April 19, as reports converged on the Presidio and as the Committee of Fifty, assembled by Schmitz at the Merchants Exchange Building, processed information from runners and the occasional functioning telephone. The Committee’s composition reflected the city’s power structure: bankers, railroad executives, merchants, attorneys, and the political boss Abe Ruef, who had placed Schmitz in office and now inserted himself into emergency governance. Their presence together in one room—military officers in uniform, civilian officials in soot-stained suits, businessmen with direct financial stakes in which buildings burned and which survived—represented a new institutional form: the automatic reversion to military-business collaboration when formal civic structures proved inadequate.

General Funston’s authority in this arrangement was de facto rather than de jure. He had declared martial law without legal foundation, deploying troops from the Presidio before receiving any request from civilian officials. The Mayor’s proclamation of Wednesday evening had provided a veneer of civilian authorization for military control. By Thursday morning, this improvised command structure faced a binary choice. Previous dynamiting had failed because it was reactive, piecemeal, conducted without plan or coordination. The fire’s relentless eastward march rendered all such efforts obsolete. The only remaining option was to move from fighting the existing fire to sacrificing an unburned district in the hope of saving what lay beyond it.

The technical dimensions of this gamble were stark. Van Ness Avenue was wide, but its mansions were wooden, their construction dating from the 1870s and 1880s when the city had not yet absorbed the lessons of its own fire history. The explosives were military-grade and in the hands of troops, not engineers. The action required convincing or compelling wealthy, influential homeowners to allow the destruction of their property—homeowners who were, in many cases, the same men sitting on the Committee of Fifty or their immediate associates. The psychological distance between abstract acceptance of emergency measures and concrete assent to the demolition of one’s own residence was considerable. Yet the alternative—letting the fire continue unimpeded—offered no prospect of preserving any property at all.

The planning session that formalized this decision left no verbatim record. What can be reconstructed from subsequent military reports, newspaper accounts printed across the bay in Oakland and Berkeley, and the later testimony of participants suggests a convergence of opposed pressures rather than a single dramatic choice. The fire’s advance provided the urgent timeline. Every hour of delay reduced the margin for preparation and increased the risk that the conflagration would reach Van Ness before the firebreak could be established. The failure of previous dynamiting provided the negative lesson: uncoordinated destruction had proven worse than useless. The presence of military explosives and trained personnel at the Presidio provided the means. The Committee of Fifty provided the political cover, its commercial membership ensuring that the decision would be understood as business necessity rather than military imposition.

What emerged was a plan of radical scope. Rather than dynamiting individual buildings as the fire approached, troops would systematically destroy every structure on the eastern side of Van Ness Avenue, creating a continuous gap of rubble and broken timber. The width of the avenue itself would provide the additional buffer. If successful, the fire would reach the demolished strip, find no fuel to sustain its advance across the open space, and die against the barrier. The western half of the city would be saved. The eastern half, including Van Ness itself, would be sacrificed.

This represented a final, desperate pivot in method and in logic. Where previous efforts had attempted to preserve property by fighting fire, this effort would preserve property by destroying it: choosing which destruction to inflict rather than accepting whatever destruction the fire chose. The martial logic that had governed the shoot-to-kill proclamation, the summary detention of suspected looters, the military patrol of commercial districts, here extended to property itself. Civic order and military necessity merged in the authorization to demolish the homes of the city’s elite. The narrative was adapted for financial and legal liability management: what might appear as military overreach or municipal failure could be represented as decisive action, the only possible response to an unprecedented emergency.

The implementation of this plan required specific allocations of material and personnel. Explosive charges had to be transported from the Presidio to the line of demolition. Troops had to be assigned to the work of placement and detonation, with whatever engineering supervision could be improvised. Property owners had to be notified or bypassed, their objections overridden by the emergency authority that Schmitz’s proclamations and Funston’s troop deployments had constructed. The western side of Van Ness had to be cleared of inhabitants, its residents sent to the refugee camps already forming in Golden Gate Park and at the Presidio itself. These practical arrangements consumed the morning and early afternoon of April 19.

The contrast between the failed dynamiting of Wednesday and the systematic operation now authorized was visible in the changed appearance of the streets. Where previous explosions had been scattered, reactive, accompanied by confusion and the visible spread of fire, the new work proceeded in ordered sequence. Blocks were cleared before charges were placed. Detonations followed a pattern designed to collapse structures inward rather than scatter debris outward. The destruction was no less complete for being controlled. Mansions that had survived the earthquake itself now fell in deliberate sequence. But the purpose had shifted from desperate improvisation to calculated sacrifice.

This transformation in method reflected a deeper transformation in authority. The Committee of Fifty, meeting through Thursday morning, had effectively displaced the formal structures of city government. The Board of Supervisors, the regular departments, the legal procedures for eminent domain or public safety, all were superseded by the emergency collaboration of military command and commercial elite. The presence of Abe Ruef signaled the continuity of power beneath the surface disruption. The emergency measures that would reshape the city’s physical form were being authorized by the same networks that had shaped its political and economic form before the disaster.

The specific decision to make Van Ness the line of sacrifice carried particular weight because of what the avenue represented. This was not the anonymous commercial district south of Market, not the immigrant tenements of the Barbary Coast, not even the financial center whose stone facades had proven as vulnerable as wooden construction to the combined effect of earthquake and fire. Van Ness was the residential display of San Francisco’s established wealth, the address of families whose names appeared on the boards of the Committee of Fifty itself. To destroy it deliberately was to acknowledge that no property, however grand, however well-insured, however politically connected, stood outside the logic of emergency. The fire made everyone equal in vulnerability. The response made everyone equal in subjection to military-commercial decision.

Yet this equality was partial and provisional. The same Committee that authorized destruction on Van Ness was already contemplating reconstruction, and the terms of that reconstruction would favor the same interests that the emergency had mobilized. Within a week of the fire’s end, Ruef would form a Subcommittee on Relocating the Chinese, seeking to prevent the return of Chinatown to its valuable downtown location. The insurance negotiations, the bond issues for rebuilding, the revision of building codes, all would proceed through channels that the emergency had fortified rather than disrupted. The dynamiting of Van Ness was a sacrifice, but it was a sacrifice that preserved the structure of power even as it destroyed its physical symbols.

Thursday afternoon saw the final preparations. The eastern side of the avenue was cleared, its residents joining the stream of refugees moving westward. The explosive charges were distributed to the troops assigned to the work. General Funston, commanding from the Presidio, coordinated with the officers who would supervise the demolition. Mayor Schmitz, his authority now entirely derivative of this military-civic collaboration, added the formal authorization of his office. The Committee of Fifty, its existence still extra-legal, its membership self-selected, provided the appearance of representative deliberation. All the elements of the improvised regime were in place: the military explosives, the trained personnel, the cleared ground, the political cover, the desperate necessity.

The psychological weight of this decision fell unevenly across the participants. For General Funston, the Van Ness firebreak represented the vindication of a military approach to civil disaster—systematic, decisive, willing to accept collateral damage for strategic gain. His earlier unauthorized deployment of troops had been criticized in some quarters as usurpation; the success of the firebreak, if it succeeded, would silence such objections and establish a template for military intervention in urban emergencies. The general had spent his career in frontier campaigns and colonial administration, contexts where the destruction of property and the displacement of populations were accepted instruments of control. The burning of San Francisco, viewed through this lens, became a theater of operations like any other, its civilian population a logistical problem to be managed rather than a citizenry to be consulted.

Mayor Schmitz occupied a more precarious position. His proclamations had provided the legal fig leaf for military control, yet he remained nominally the chief executive of a civilian government. The dynamiting of Van Ness tested the limits of this arrangement. To authorize the destruction of the city’s grandest residential district was to acknowledge the comprehensive failure of municipal institutions—the fire department overwhelmed, the water system destroyed, the normal mechanisms of governance reduced to irrelevance. Yet to refuse authorization, to insist on civilian prerogatives in the face of military expertise and urgent necessity, would have exposed the emptiness of his position. Schmitz chose collaboration, as he had chosen it from the first hours of the disaster, and in doing so confirmed the subordination of elected office to emergency command.

The members of the Committee of Fifty experienced the decision through the prism of their individual stakes. For the bankers and railroad executives, the firebreak was a calculation of portfolio preservation: the western half of the city contained substantial real estate holdings, and the systematic destruction of Van Ness, however costly, was preferable to the random destruction that uncontrolled fire would bring. For the attorneys present, the authorization of military demolition raised questions of liability and indemnification that would occupy the courts for years to come—questions deliberately deferred in the urgency of the moment. For Abe Ruef, the decision represented an extension of the political machine’s accustomed methods: the concentration of discretionary power in a small circle of decision-makers, the representation of private interest as public necessity, the transformation of crisis into opportunity.

The homeowners of Van Ness Avenue, those who could be reached, responded with the full range of human reaction to imminent loss. Some accepted the necessity with the stoicism of their class, recognizing that partial destruction was preferable to total loss. Others protested, invoking property rights and insurance policies and the limits of emergency authority, their objections overridden by the same martial law they had tacitly endorsed when it applied to poorer districts. A few attempted to remove valuables, to salvage heirlooms, to witness the destruction of their homes and thus claim some measure of agency in the catastrophe. The troops, under orders to clear the eastern side of the avenue, allowed such gestures within the constraints of their timeline, their tolerance varying with the rank of the householder and the urgency of the approaching fire.

The technical execution of the firebreak revealed both the capabilities and the limitations of improvised military engineering. The explosives—dynamite and gunpowder drawn from Presidio stores—were deployed in quantities calculated to collapse structures rather than disintegrate them, a distinction that required more judgment than the available personnel consistently possessed. The wooden construction of the Van Ness mansions, built in an era of architectural ornament and structural optimism, responded unpredictably to explosive force: some buildings collapsed inward as intended, others splintered outward, their decorative elements—turrets, cupolas, elaborate cornices—becoming projectiles that endangered adjacent structures and the troops themselves. The heat of the approaching fire complicated placement and timing, forcing detonations before optimal preparation and occasionally igniting the very buildings scheduled for destruction.

The systematic nature of the Thursday operation, however, did produce measurable differences from the chaotic dynamiting of Wednesday. Supervising officers established sequences of demolition that allowed for retreat and reassessment, that prevented the simultaneous detonation of adjacent structures, that created cleared zones where fire from premature ignition could be contained. The width of Van Ness Avenue itself proved decisive: even when explosions scattered burning debris, the distance to the western edge was sufficient that most embers died before reaching intact buildings. The firebreak was not a single act but a continuous process, extending through Thursday afternoon and into the night, as new reports of the fire’s advance dictated extensions of the demolition zone north and south along the avenue.

The human cost of this calculated destruction extended beyond property loss. The residents cleared from the eastern side of Van Ness joined the mass of refugees already straining the resources of the western districts, their social distinction providing limited protection against the general conditions of displacement. The troops engaged in demolition worked in proximity to the fire’s advance, their labor illuminated by the glow of burning blocks to the east, their safety dependent on the same wind shifts that determined the fire’s path. The psychological effect of systematic destruction—of placing charges in intact, even elegant structures, of detonating them by order rather than necessity—produced its own casualties, recorded in later accounts of nervous exhaustion and disciplinary problems among the demolition details.

The success of the Van Ness firebreak, measured by the containment of the fire at Franklin Street, validated the decision in retrospect while obscuring the contingencies that had shaped it. Had the wind shifted, had the fire advanced more rapidly, had the explosives proved less controllable, the same operation might have been recorded as a final, catastrophic failure. The margin between controlled sacrifice and uncontrolled disaster was narrow, and recognized as narrow by those who directed the operation.

What remained was execution. The fire continued to advance, its progress visible in the rising smoke, the glowing sky after dark, the reports of runners who had seen the flames reach new blocks. The margin for error was narrow. If the dynamiting proceeded too slowly, the fire would reach Van Ness before the firebreak was complete. If it proceeded too hastily, with the same lack of coordination that had doomed previous efforts, the explosions might scatter fire across the avenue rather than contain it. The width that made Van Ness suitable for a firebreak also made it vulnerable to failure: too much distance to cross with burning debris, too many structures to demolish in limited time.

To stop the fire, rows of buildings were dynamited. In this way many fine mansions on Van Ness Avenue were destroyed, and the westward advance of the conflagration was stopped at Franklin Street, one block west. General Frederick Funston, in command at the Presidio, had directed this operation without civilian request and without legal authority, establishing a precedent that would outlast the emergency itself. The order to create the Van Ness firebreak was given, handing off the explosive charges and the troops to execute it.
