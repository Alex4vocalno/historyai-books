Chapter 20

Using Relief Committee Ledgers

On the morning of April 21, the Relief Committee clerk at the Presidio recorded fifty tents distributed to Section B, Grid 7, each assigned to a named head of household with a corresponding ticket number for blanket allocation. The entry ran three lines in a ledger bound in red cloth, the handwriting level and unhurried. That same morning, in Golden Gate Park, a woman wrote to her sister in Oakland on paper borrowed from a stranger, describing five people huddled under one blanket while not fifty yards away, a party from the St. Francis Hotel had built themselves a comfortable shelter from doors and shutters salvaged from a wrecked house on Fulton Street.

The contrast was not lost on those who lived inside it. Within seventy-two hours of the earthquake, the relief apparatus had bifurcated into two systems operating under the same military authority but producing radically different conditions of survival. The Presidio camps, administered directly by Army quartermasters, functioned as extensions of military logistics: tents in geometric alignment, latrines dug to regulation depth, water barrels on numbered stands. Golden Gate Park, by contrast, had become an ungoverned commons where refugees staked claims by arrival sequence, where the strong secured position and the weak waited for what charity might reach them.

This was not the plan. There had been no plan. The city’s Committee of Fifty, hastily convened in the ruins of the Hall of Justice on April 18, had delegated relief coordination to subcommittees whose authority dissolved at the edge of the burned district. What emerged instead was improvisation pressed into structure by necessity, and the structure revealed what the emergency preferred.

The Army’s virtual town at the Presidio rose on ground that had been, until April 18, training fields and parade grounds. By April 20, the quartermaster corps had organized three thousand tents into a grid complete with designated streets and addresses. As one report noted, “The Army constructed a virtual town with large residential barracks [with temporary] tented housing, latrines and bathhouses, laundries, and other services.” The Presidio’s commander, General Funston, had not waited for civilian authorization to deploy these resources; his troops had begun clearing ground while fires still burned in the Mission District. The result was a relief operation that functioned with the efficiency of an expeditionary force, which in essence it was.

Funston’s authority, never formally declared as martial law yet exercised as such, extended to the civilian relief committees through a system of military liaison officers who reviewed distribution lists and requisitioned supplies. The Relief Corporation that would eventually account for $2, 500, 000 in congressional appropriations operated within parameters set by this military oversight. Its ledgers record the transaction: tents issued, rations distributed, medical supplies allocated by post surgeon’s requisition. The clerks who kept these records worked under Army direction; their precision served both humanitarian and administrative purposes, documenting need while establishing control.

Golden Gate Park offered no such documentation. The park’s transformation into a refugee city occurred without central direction, the product of thousands of individual decisions made in the hours after the fire drove residents from their homes. The Outside Lands, that undeveloped western expanse of dunes and scrub that Olmsted’s design had only partially civilized, became the default destination for those who could not secure passage across the bay or find shelter with friends in the unburned districts.

Of the twenty-six official homeless encampments in the Golden Gate Park region, twenty-one were under the control of the United States Army, which housed twenty thousand people in military-style camps, sixteen thousand of them at the Presidio. By April 20, estimates placed the park population at twenty thousand, though no systematic count existed. The figure appeared in newspaper correspondence published from Oakland, attributed to a gentleman who had walked the grounds, and was repeated until it attained the authority of fact.

The park’s geography shaped its social organization. The Chain of Lakes, that series of artificial reservoirs constructed for irrigation, provided water where the city’s broken mains could not. Metson Lake, with its capacity of over 1.1 million gallons, became a critical resource; refugees drew water for drinking and washing, and the overflow that fed South Lake established a secondary supply point. But access to water, like access to everything else, followed patterns of prior advantage. Those who arrived early secured positions near the lakes; those who came later, often from more distant burned districts, found themselves on higher ground where sand replaced soil and wind carried ash across unprotected sleepers.

The disparity in shelter quality reproduced the city’s class geography with startling fidelity. The wealthy refugees—those from Nob Hill, from the vanished palaces of Van Ness Avenue, from the apartment houses of the Western Addition—had resources that the poor lacked: wagons to transport salvaged goods, social connections to secure information, the confidence to negotiate with military authorities for preferred positions. The woman writing from the park noted that the St. Francis Hotel party had employed two Chinese laborers hired from the street to carry their salvaged lumber. The observation carried no comment on the arrangement; it required none.

The Chinese refugees faced conditions of particular severity. Chinatown’s destruction, complete by April 19, had dispersed a population of approximately twenty-five thousand into a city that had long regarded their presence as provisional. The relief apparatus, such as it was, made no provision for linguistic difference or cultural practice. Chinese refugees who reached Golden Gate Park found themselves segregated by default into the least desirable areas, near the park’s western edge where sand dunes made tenting difficult and wind exposure severe. Military authorities, when they addressed the Chinese presence at all, treated it as a security concern rather than a relief priority; Funston’s staff had circulated warnings about suspicious characters among the refugee population, a category that folded together the actually criminal, the merely destitute, and the racially undesirable.

The Committee of Fifty’s subcommittee on Chinese relief, constituted on April 20, operated with a budget and authority dwarfed by the military-administered mainstream. Its records, fragmentary compared to the Army’s systematic documentation, suggest an operation overwhelmed by scale and constrained by political calculation. The merchant associations that had governed Chinatown’s internal affairs attempted to organize mutual aid; their efforts intersected unevenly with official relief, sometimes complementing it, sometimes competing, often simply operating in parallel without acknowledgment.

The photographic record captures this stratification with documentary precision. Arnold Genthe’s famous images of Chinatown’s destruction, taken in the fire’s immediate aftermath, show a landscape of ruins that would become, in subsequent days, a landscape of encampment. Other photographers, working for relief agencies and newspapers, documented the park refugees: the family groups arranged before improvised shelters, the cooking fires, the lines at water distribution points. These images, collected by the Carnegie Institution in its 1908 survey and later dispersed to the Museum of Modern Art and the Oakland Museum, preserve visual evidence of conditions that the official ledgers record only indirectly. A photograph of a Presidio tent street shows canvas aligned with military regularity; a photograph of Golden Gate Park shows shelter constructed from doors, shutters, and salvaged lumber, the materials of destroyed houses recycled into emergency architecture.

The Red Cross Relief Corporation, which would complete its work in 1908, attempted to impose systematic accounting on this chaos. Its final report, published after two years of operation, catalogued expenditures: for the relief of the hungry, for the sick and injured, for housing and rehabilitation of individuals and families. The categories suggest a uniformity of need that the actual distribution belied. The ledgers reveal, in their granular detail, how resources followed channels of access. Tent assignment at the Presidio correlated with prior residence in unburned districts, with military or political connection, with ability to present documentation of property ownership. The truly destitute, those who had fled with nothing, often found themselves excluded from the formal relief rolls, dependent on the informal charity of religious organizations and neighborhood committees whose operations left no paper trail.

The army’s geometric grid at the Presidio represented one solution to the problem of mass displacement: the imposition of military order on civilian catastrophe. The park’s chaotic encampment represented another: the survival of informal social networks under extreme stress. Between these models, and overlapping with both, ran the fractured experience of a population sorted by class, race, and prior advantage into radically unequal conditions of emergency shelter.

By April 22, the contrast had generated its own political consequences. The Oakland newspapers, receiving correspondence from refugees who had secured passage across the bay, published accounts of conditions in the park that contradicted the official optimism of military bulletins. One correspondent described latrine trenches dug too shallow and too close to water sources, the accumulation of refuse, the first reported cases of illness among concentrated populations. These reports reached the Committee of Fifty and its military liaisons, prompting inspection tours that produced their own documentary record: memoranda on camp sanitation, requisitions for additional medical personnel, orders for the relocation of undesirable elements to more distant park areas.

The relocation orders reveal the administrative translation of social prejudice. A directive dated April 23 directed that Chinese camps be removed to the western section; another ordered that gypsies and vagrants be segregated from the main population. The categories shifted with each iteration, but the principle remained constant: the relief apparatus would sort the refugee population according to criteria of respectability and security, and the sorting would distribute hardship unequally. The western section of Golden Gate Park, where Chinese refugees were directed, lacked the tree cover and water access of the eastern grounds; the segregated camps for those deemed vagrant received no tent allocation at all, their occupants dependent on such shelter as they could construct from salvage.

The Committee of Fifty’s own records, examined against the military documentation, show a civilian authority struggling to maintain relevance within a military-dominated system. The committee’s subcommittees met daily in borrowed offices, compiling lists of the displaced, appealing for contributions, attempting to coordinate with the Red Cross and with private charitable organizations. Their minutes record frustration: requisitions delayed, information withheld, decisions made by military officers without civilian consultation. Yet the committee also exercised real power in certain domains, particularly the allocation of reconstruction contracts and the disposition of relief funds contributed by private donors. This divided authority—military control of immediate survival, civilian influence over longer-term recovery—would shape the politics of San Francisco’s rebuilding for years to come.

The refugee camps thus became, within days of their establishment, arenas of contested governance. The Presidio’s ordered grid represented military efficiency and military surveillance; the park’s improvised settlements represented civilian resilience and civilian disorder. Both models would influence the permanent reconstruction, as the city debated whether to replicate the emergency’s improvisations or to impose the planning that the emergency had lacked.

For the individuals who lived inside these systems, the abstraction of governance translated into concrete experience: the quality of sleep, the security of possessions, the predictability of meals. The park correspondent’s letter, continued over several days as she found more paper and more time, traced this translation. By April 23, she reported, her family had secured a second blanket through the kindness of a gentleman from the Relief Committee who came through asking for names of those in greatest need. The committee’s ledger would record this distribution: one blanket, family name withheld, grid reference approximate. The entry would join thousands of others in the systematic documentation of improvised charity, a record of generosity that also served as an instrument of control.

The Chinese refugees in the park’s western section left fewer written records. Literacy rates among the displaced Chinatown population were lower, and the destruction of community institutions disrupted the networks through which information had flowed. What survives comes primarily through official documentation: the relocation orders, the sanitary inspection reports, the eventual claims for compensation filed with the city and with the merchant associations. These records, read against the grain, suggest conditions of particular hardship: exposure to wind and sand, limited access to the water sources that sustained the eastern park population, exclusion from the employment networks that developed as the city began to rebuild.

The differential experience of the relief camps thus encoded, in miniature, the social structure of pre-earthquake San Francisco. The wealthy, even as refugees, retained resources of connection and information; the poor, even with official assistance, struggled to secure basic needs; the Chinese, long subject to discriminatory practice, found that practice extended and intensified by emergency conditions. The relief apparatus, for all its claims to uniform humanitarian purpose, reproduced these distinctions in its distribution of tents, blankets, and water access.

By late April, the camps had begun their transformation from emergency shelter to longer-term residence. The earthquake shacks that would eventually house thousands of displaced families began to appear in Golden Gate Park, simple wooden structures constructed by the city with federal relief funds. These shacks, intended as temporary, would remain for years, some eventually moved to permanent locations, others absorbed into the park’s developing infrastructure. Their construction represented a recognition that the emergency would not quickly resolve, that the displaced population would require sustained support rather than immediate relief.

The Presidio camps maintained their military organization longer, the Army reluctant to relinquish control of a population it had successfully managed. The transition to civilian administration, when it came in May and June, proceeded gradually, with military officers retaining advisory roles in camp governance. The geometric grid of streets and addresses persisted, becoming the foundation for a permanent military housing development that would outlast the emergency by decades.

The relief committee ledgers, compiled through this period of transition, thus document more than the distribution of material aid. They record the encounter between military and civilian systems of organization, between formal and informal networks of survival, between the uniform categories of humanitarian accounting and the stratified realities of social experience. The precision of the Presidio entries—tent numbers, grid references, named recipients—contrasts with the approximate notations of park distribution, and both contrast with the absence of documentation for those who fell outside the formal relief apparatus entirely.

This absence was itself a form of record. The refugees who built shelters from salvaged doors, who shared blankets in family groups, who drew water from irrigation lakes without official authorization—their experience survives primarily through the complaints they generated, the sanitation reports that noted their presence as problems to be solved, the newspaper correspondence that described their conditions for distant readers. The relief system saw them as residual, as failures of its own organization to reach everyone in need; they experienced themselves as active agents of survival, improvising within constraints they did not choose.

The chapter of emergency shelter was closing by early May, as reconstruction began and refugees dispersed to temporary housing, to relocated shacks, to departure from the city entirely. But the patterns established in those first weeks would persist. The differential distribution of relief, the sorting of population by class and race, the tension between military efficiency and civilian participation—these would shape the politics of rebuilding and the memory of the disaster. The ledgers that recorded tent assignments would become evidence in disputes over compensation; the photographs of camp conditions would illustrate competing narratives of official competence and failure.

What the documentation cannot fully capture, and what the survivors’ letters approach only partially, is the experience of living inside this system: the daily negotiations for position and resource, the observation of inequality among those supposedly united by common catastrophe, the gradual recognition that the emergency was not temporary in its effects. The park correspondent, writing from her improved circumstances in early May, noted that the city was said to be rising again greater than before, but she wondered for whom. The question, posed without answer, suggests the awareness that was growing among the displaced: that the disaster’s aftermath would be shaped by the same forces that had shaped its experience, that the relief apparatus was already constructing the terms of recovery, and that those terms would not be equally distributed.

The revelation that official relief tallies obscure stratified suffering arrived not as theory but as lived recognition, in the contrast between ledger entries and camp conditions, between the geometric order of military administration and the improvised survival of the park refugees. A clerk’s steady handwriting in a red-bound book, a woman’s borrowed paper and unanswered question, a relocation order sorting population by race—these concrete particulars accumulated into pressure for more organized and more contested action, as civilian committees challenged military prerogative, as displaced communities organized mutual aid, as the question of who would rebuild and on what terms moved from emergency improvisation toward open political struggle.
