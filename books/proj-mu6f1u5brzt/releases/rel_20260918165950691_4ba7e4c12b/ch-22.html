Chapter 22

The Earthquake That Did Not Exist

The totals accumulated. Somewhere in that accumulating arithmetic, a different kind of reckoning had already begun.

On the morning of April 26, 1906, ten days after the shock at 5: 12, the Merchants Exchange Association gathered in temporary quarters near the Ferry Building. The building itself had survived, though its interior was a chaos of displaced desks and cracked plaster. The association’s secretary, James Rolph—later mayor, now a young shipping executive—read aloud the draft of a cable to be sent to eastern financial centers. The text, preserved in the association’s minutes, referred to the great conflagration which has devastated our city. It mentioned fire four times. It did not mention earthquake.

That same morning, Brigadier General Frederick Funston submitted his first formal report to the War Department. The document, filed from his headquarters at the Presidio, described in precise military language what his engineers had observed. The surface of the ground was traversed by fissures in many places. Buildings were thrown from their foundations. The shock was of such violence as to render the water supply system useless by breaking the mains in numerous locations. Funston, who had seized control of the city without civilian authorization, had every institutional reason to emphasize fire over seismic cause: fire could be fought, organized against, mastered by troops. Yet his report, unlike the Merchants Exchange cable, named the earthquake as primary agent.

Between these two documents—one private, one official; one commercial, one military—lay the fault line of what would become the most systematic act of narrative control in the disaster’s aftermath. The city was recovering. It was also being renamed.

The mechanism of that renaming can be traced through the surviving records of the Mayor’s Committee of Fifty, the ad hoc body that had formed within hours of the shock. The committee’s minutes for April 27, 1906, record a motion by Abraham Ruef, the political boss who had appointed himself to membership, to establish a Subcommittee on Relocating the Chinese. Ruef’s intervention, coming before the fires had fully cooled, suggests how quickly certain interests moved to capitalize on destruction. But the more significant motion, passed the same day, authorized the committee’s executive officers to prepare statements for the press embodying the facts of the fire and the progress of relief. The word fire appears in the original. The word earthquake does not.

This was not oversight. The committee’s own correspondence reveals a deliberative process. On April 28, an architect who would become a leading voice in reconstruction wrote to the committee’s chairman suggesting that public statements avoid emphasis upon the seismic character of the disaster, which can only alarm capital and retard the return of population. The letter, preserved among the Reconstruction Committee papers that succeeded the Fifty, argued that the Fire should be capitalized in all official communications, as one would speak of the Chicago Fire, or the London Fire of 1666. The comparison was tendentious: Chicago’s disaster had been purely conflagration, London’s the consequence of a bakery accident. Neither city had experienced ground movement that threw buildings from their foundations.

Yet this terminology spread with remarkable speed. The Relief and Red Cross Funds Committee, which opened its headquarters at the Palace Hotel ruins on April 29, adopted the formulation in its first public appeal. The Great Fire has left thousands without shelter, the appeal began. Your contribution will help rebuild a city destroyed by flame. The architectural metaphor was precise: flame destroys, but leaves ground intact. One could rebuild on the same footprint, using the same assumptions, without acknowledging that the ground itself had proven unstable.

The photographic record offers the necessary counter-evidence. A German-born photographer who had documented Chinatown before its destruction returned to the ruins with his camera in the last week of April. His surviving negatives, now at the Bancroft Library, include a series taken on Sacramento Street showing a fissure that had opened in the pavement, running diagonally across the frame for sixty-three feet. The edges of the fissure display clear shear displacement: the northeast side had dropped relative to the southwest, a movement consistent with the lateral strike-slip motion that geologists would later identify as characteristic of the San Andreas fault. The caption, written on the negative envelope, identifies the location and date. It does not mention fire.

He was not alone. A commercial photographer who had developed specialized equipment for aerial photography chartered a balloon to document the destruction from above. His panoramic views, taken on April 28 and 29, show a cityscape in which the pattern of damage contradicts the official narrative. Burned areas appear as dark rectangles, uniform in texture. But scattered throughout these zones, and concentrated along a diagonal line running from the Mission District to the Marina, stand buildings that have clearly collapsed without burning: walls sheared at the foundation, roofs folded like broken cards, streets buckled into waves. He sold his photographs to newspapers and to the Army Corps of Engineers. The newspapers printed the fire damage. The engineers filed the seismic evidence.

The divergence between these uses was not accidental. The Army’s Board of Engineers, convened by Funston to assess infrastructure damage, produced a confidential report dated May 3, 1906, that described in technical detail what public statements denied. The water supply system failed primarily through seismic displacement of pipe joints, the report noted. The conflagration which followed was rendered uncontrollable by this primary failure. The board’s recommendation—that any rebuilt system include seismic-resistant jointing—was ignored in the public reconstruction plans announced the following week.

The financial logic of this suppression becomes visible in the correspondence of the Bank of California, whose president had also appointed himself to the Committee of Fifty. On May 1, he wrote to his counterpart at the National City Bank of New York, explaining that the immediate cause of our losses was fire, which is fully covered by insurance, rather than earthquake, which is excluded under standard policy terms. The distinction was legally consequential. Standard fire insurance policies of the period contained explicit exclusions for earthquake, volcanic eruption, or other convulsion of nature. The letter, which urged his correspondent to correct any misapprehension among eastern investors that the disaster was primarily seismic, was copied to the Mayor’s office and to the editorial board of the Chronicle.

The Chronicle’s transformation offers a case study in institutional adaptation. On April 18, the paper’s extra edition had carried the headline EARTHQUAKE AND FIRE: CITY IN RUINS. By April 25, the headline formula had shifted: FIRE SWEEPS SAN FRANCISCO. By May 2, the earthquake had become a dependent clause: Great Fire Follows Earthquake Shock. The editorial page completed the inversion. The San Francisco Fire, a May 4 editorial declared, will take its place among the historic conflagrations of the world, like Chicago in 1871 and Boston in 1872. The earthquake was mentioned once, in the fourth paragraph, as the preliminary disturbance. The editorial did not explain how a preliminary disturbance had killed Fire Chief Dennis Sullivan, crushed the California Hotel onto his quarters, and severed the water mains before any fire had begun.

Sullivan’s death posed a particular problem for the emerging narrative. The chief had been mortally wounded when a neighboring building collapsed onto the fire station housing his official apartment on Bush Street. His injuries—crushed chest, internal hemorrhage—were unequivocally the result of structural collapse, not flame. He lingered for four days, conscious at intervals, attended by his wife and by doctors who recorded his symptoms in clinical detail. His death on April 22 was reported in all newspapers. Yet within a week, the official summaries of fire deaths issued by the coroner’s office began to exclude his case, categorizing it separately as earthquake casualty. This statistical segregation served the narrative: the fire could be presented as the primary killer, while earthquake deaths were minimized as a separate, smaller category. The chief who had died because the ground moved became, in the official count, an exception that proved the rule.

The physical evidence of seismic damage continued to accumulate, documented by sources with no stake in the official story. The Stanford University campus, where a staff photographer recorded the destruction in the last week of April, showed patterns of damage that could only be explained by ground motion. The university’s Memorial Arch, completed in 1900, had rotated on its foundation, its base sheared from the pedestal while the structure above remained intact. The sandstone facings of the quadrangle buildings had shaken loose in waves, following the propagation of seismic waves through the alluvial soil. The photographs, included in the California State Earthquake Investigation Commission’s eventual report, were available to anyone who wished to see. Few wished to see.

The commission itself, chaired by a University of California geologist, had been authorized before the disaster, in response to smaller earthquakes in 1892 and 1898. Field parties began systematic observation in the first week of May, tracing the fault rupture from the Point Reyes Peninsula to San Juan Bautista. Their preliminary findings, circulated in confidential memoranda to the State Mining Bureau, identified surface displacement of up to twenty feet in some locations. The geologist urged caution in public statement: The scientific facts, he wrote to a colleague on May 5, must be established before they can be communicated without exciting alarm. The delay between observation and publication—ultimately two years—allowed the Great Fire narrative to consolidate without contradiction from official science.

The Reconstruction Committee, formally established on May 5 with Mayor Schmitz as honorary chairman and Ruef as effective director, completed the institutionalization of the new terminology. Its charter, printed in full in the Examiner, committed the committee to rebuilding from the ashes of the Fire a greater and more beautiful San Francisco. The phrase from the ashes carried theological overtones: purification through flame, the phoenix myth. It implied that what had been destroyed was combustible, temporary, replaceable. It did not suggest that the ground itself had proven treacherous, that the city’s location on faulted terrain was a permanent condition rather than a single event.

The insurance dimension of this narrative control has left the most extensive documentary record. A private organization funded by underwriters began daily reports on May 1 that systematically classified all damage as fire-caused unless conclusively proven otherwise. The burden of proof, in practice, fell on claimants. A policyholder whose building had collapsed before burning found the company’s adjuster arguing that structural failure due to seismic shock was excluded coverage, while the same company, faced with a building that had burned after collapsing, argued that the proximate cause was fire and therefore the exclusion did not apply. The inconsistency was flagrant, but the legal resources available to individual claimants were negligible. The organization’s reports, compiled into a summary submitted to the National Board of Fire Underwriters in June 1906, became the statistical foundation for the industry’s position that the San Francisco disaster was essentially a fire catastrophe.

The human cost of this classification appears in scattered individual petitions to the Mayor’s relief office, preserved among the Committee of Fifty records. On May 3, a Mrs. Catherine O’Rourke of Mission Street wrote to request assistance, explaining that her house fell down in the shaking before any fire came near. Her petition was marked with a notation: Earthquake case—refer to special committee. The special committee, when it met, determined that pure earthquake losses were not eligible for fire relief funds. Mrs. O’Rourke’s house had been destroyed by the ground moving. Therefore, she did not qualify for assistance from funds raised to address the Fire.

This administrative logic extended to the refugee camps established in Golden Gate Park and the Presidio. The official registration forms, printed in early May, required applicants to state date of fire loss. Those who wrote April 18, before the fire received follow-up inquiries. Those who persisted in dating their loss to the earthquake were directed to separate processing lines, where the available resources were substantially smaller. The registration clerks were not following personal malice. They were implementing a classification system that had been designed to match the narrative of the Great Fire.

The most revealing document of this period may be a handwritten memorandum among Ruef’s papers, dated May 7, 1906, and apparently intended for the Mayor’s private reading. The word earthquake frightens capital, it notes. The word fire suggests a solvable problem. We must speak consistently of the Fire, the Relief of the Fire, the Rebuilding after the Fire. The earthquake was an incident. The fire was the disaster. The memorandum goes on to propose a systematic course of press communication to ensure that eastern papers receive only such photographs and descriptions as emphasize the fire aspect. The proposal was implemented. The photographs of fissures, of sheared foundations, of buildings collapsed without burning, were not sent east. The photographs of flames, of heroic firefighting, of dynamiting operations along Van Ness Avenue, were distributed with captions emphasizing the battle against the conflagration.

The Army’s role in this narrative management was contradictory. Funston’s troops had enforced order without legal authority, had participated in the dynamiting that destroyed as much property as it saved, had stood guard while the city burned. Yet the Army’s own engineering reports, with their frank acknowledgment of seismic cause, were classified and circulated only within military channels. The public face of military involvement was the photograph of soldiers fighting fire, not the memorandum describing broken water mains. This double record—public heroism, private acknowledgment—created an archival legacy that would confuse subsequent historians, who found in the same institutional records both the evidence of earthquake and the official denial of it.

The psychological dimensions of this suppression attracted contemporary notice from unexpected quarters. A Harvard philosopher and psychologist traveled to San Francisco in early May at the invitation of Stanford’s president. His observations, later published as On Some Mental Effects of the Earthquake, noted the positive attitude of the survivors and their remarkable capacity for improvising services and creating order out of chaos. But he also recorded something else: the systematic avoidance among educated informants of any discussion of the earthquake’s possible recurrence. They speak of the Fire, he wrote in his field notes, as one might speak of a departed relative whose name must not be mentioned. The ground is solid again. The subject is closed. His analysis, published in 1907 after the narrative had fully consolidated, suggested that the denial was commercial and psychological at once: a collective defense against the intolerable knowledge of permanent vulnerability.

The commercial interests had no such vulnerability. By mid-May, the terminology of the Great Fire had achieved sufficient currency to appear in federal legislation. Senator Frank Flint of California introduced a bill for relief of the sufferers of the San Francisco Fire, appropriating $2.5 million for reconstruction loans. The bill’s text mentioned earthquake only in its chronological clause: following the earthquake of April 18, 1906. The operative provisions addressed fire losses, fire refugees, and fire reconstruction. The Senate debate, recorded in the Congressional Record, included no challenge to this framing. The disaster had been officially renamed.

The geological reality, meanwhile, persisted in its indifference to nomenclature. Field parties continued their measurements through May and June, mapping surface ruptures that extended for nearly three hundred miles. The displacement they documented—horizontal offsets of streets and fences, vertical drops of stream beds—could not be attributed to fire. Yet when the State Earthquake Investigation Commission’s final report appeared in 1908, its title acknowledged the secondary status to which science had been relegated: The California Earthquake of April 18, 1906: Report of the State Earthquake Investigation Commission. The earthquake had recovered its name, but only in a technical publication that policymakers could safely ignore. The public name, the name that appeared in insurance policies, in bond prospectuses, in the booster literature that drew immigrants and investment to the rebuilt city, remained the Fire.

The decision to brand the disaster a fire had immediate material consequences. Building codes adopted for reconstruction, announced in July 1906, addressed fire resistance with unprecedented stringency: wider streets, fireproof materials, expanded water storage. They did not address seismic design. The same alluvial soils that had amplified ground motion in 1906 remained the preferred sites for new construction. The same fault trace that had ruptured in April ran beneath streets that were paved and built over without geological survey. The city that rose from the ashes was architecturally magnificent and seismically blind, committed by its own narrative to a future repetition of the disaster it had refused to name.

On May 15, 1906, the Reconstruction Committee held its first formal meeting in permanent quarters on Montgomery Street. The room had been hastily furnished with desks salvaged from burned buildings, their surfaces still bearing the scorch marks that the committee’s narrative attributed to the Fire.

The chairman called the meeting to order and read a communication from the National Board of Fire Underwriters, pledging support for the rebuilding of San Francisco after its great conflagration. No one present remarked that the scorched desks had been damaged not by flame but by the collapse of the building that had housed them, that the very room in which they sat had been shaken to its foundations before any fire had approached. The narrative had achieved sufficient density to exclude such observations from conscious consideration.

The committee proceeded to its agenda: street widening, harbor improvements, the location of a new civic center. The ground beneath their feet, which had moved three weeks before and would move again, had been rendered officially solid by the simple expedient of not speaking its motion.

In the Presidio, meanwhile, the Army engineers filed their classified reports in steel cabinets. In Sacramento, the State Mining Bureau locked its confidential memoranda in vaults. In Washington, the Congressional Record preserved the debate that had never occurred. And in the refugee camps at Golden Gate Park, the registration clerks continued their work, turning pages, accumulating totals, separating those who had lost homes to fire from those who had lost them to something that had no name in their forms. The decision to brand the disaster a fire handed off a city now committed to a narrative that would dictate its physical and legal reconstruction. The clerks did not know this. They knew only that the forms required a date of fire loss, and that those who could not provide one must wait in a different line.
