Chapter 9

The Birth of the First Major

The morning wind came off the ocean with enough force to move a flame across open ground, carrying the fires that had been burning since dawn on April 18. At 5: 12, when the earthquake struck, it had been a light breeze from the west. By 6: 00, it had steadied into something more determined, a twenty-mile-per-hour current of air that would prove as decisive as any broken water main in determining what burned and what survived.

At the corner of Hayes and Gough streets, in a working-class district west of downtown, a wood-frame apartment house began to burn. The fire department’s after-action reports, compiled in the weeks following the disaster, identified this as the first of the major conflagrations, the first fire that would not be contained, that would jump streets and merge with other outbreaks, that would establish the template for the three days of destruction.

The specific ignition source was recorded variously: a toppled stove in one account, a severed gas line in another, electrical arcing in a third. The reports do not resolve this discrepancy. What they agree upon is the time, approximately 6: 15 a. m., and the consequence: within twenty minutes, three buildings were involved, and the single hose company that responded could not establish a water supply. This blaze would later be known as the Ham and Eggs fire, destroying a 30-block area.

The Hayes Street fire began in a building type that defined much of San Francisco’s residential fabric and much of its vulnerability. The structure was a three-story, wood-frame six-flat apartment house, built in the 1880s, with a pressed-tin cornice and a foundation of brick set in soft mortar. It had survived the earthquake with its walls intact but its plaster cracked, its chimneys fractured, and its gas lines stressed to the point of failure. The city’s building codes had required masonry construction in the downtown core since the 1850s, but this district lay outside the fire limits, and wood-frame construction remained not merely permitted but economically dominant. The presence of such buildings, cheap to erect, profitable to own, dangerous to inhabit, would shape both the fire’s spread and the political calculus of what came after.

Engine Company No. 25, quartered at 144 McAllister Street, had responded to the earthquake with its full complement: a steam pumper, a hose cart, and six men. They reached Hayes and Gough by 6: 25, according to the company journal, to find the initial building fully involved and fire spreading to the structures on either side. Captain Thomas J.

Carr attempted to connect to a hydrant at the northeast corner of the intersection. The hydrant was dry. The company then tried to draft from a cistern located behind the apartment house, only to discover that the earthquake had cracked the cistern’s walls and drained its contents into the sandy subsoil. Carr ordered his men to proceed south to Mission Street, where the gravity-fed mains from the reservoirs on Twin Peaks might still hold pressure. By the time they returned, nearly forty minutes later, the fire had crossed Gough Street and established itself in a second row of buildings.

This sequence, arrival, failed connection, improvisation, delay, and return to a transformed situation, would repeat across the city throughout the morning. What made the Hayes Street fire definitive was not its uniqueness but its typicality, combined with its timing. It was the first major fire to reach a scale that precluded control, and it did so before the fire department’s scattered companies had accumulated enough field experience to recognize what they were facing. The men of Engine 25 still operated under the assumption that water could be found, that the fire could be contained, that the normal procedures of urban firefighting remained applicable. The evidence accumulating around them suggested otherwise, but institutional training resisted evidence when evidence contradicted established practice.

The wind drove the fire eastward, toward the more densely built blocks between Gough and Franklin. This was not the random motion of flame in an enclosed space; it was the systematic advance of a firefront across a fuel bed that had been prepared by decades of building practice.

The buildings on Hayes Street stood fifteen to twenty feet apart, separated by narrow alleys and wooden fences. The fences burned first, creating connecting paths of flame that allowed the fire to treat the block as a continuous surface.

The San Francisco Call, printing from Oakland by mid-morning, reported a solid wall of fire moving down the street, though this description, filed by a reporter who could not approach closer than two blocks, likely conflated multiple burning structures with a single unified front. The distinction mattered less than the reporter supposed. Whether one fire or several, the effect was identical: the destruction of everything in the path.

Residents of the burning buildings had evacuated with what they could carry. Witness letters preserved in the Bancroft Library describe the same pattern repeated with variations: the initial shock, the assessment of damage, the smell of gas, the first sight of flame, the gathering of family and portable possessions, the departure into streets already crowded with others making the same journey.

One resident at 318 Hayes Street wrote to her sister in Chicago on April 22 that she had just got the children dressed when she saw smoke coming from the house behind them. Her husband said they had time to save the furniture, but she insisted on the children first. They took what they could carry and walked toward Market Street. By the time they reached Van Ness, their house was burning. The letter does not mention water or firefighters; in her account, the fire arrives as a natural force, unopposed by human intervention.

This perception was not inaccurate. Engine 25, after its return from Mission Street, attempted to establish a line of defense at Franklin Street, where a wider thoroughfare and a masonry church building offered some prospect of containment. Captain Carr deployed his single pumper with its 600-gallon-per-minute capacity against a fire that by 7: 00 a. m. Involved an estimated 50, 000 square feet of built surface. The mathematics were implacable. A fully involved wood-frame building of three stories required approximately 1, 000 gallons per minute for suppression; Carr’s pumper, even operating at rated capacity, could address one such building while dozens burned. The company held its position for thirty minutes, according to the journal entry, before falling back toward Market Street as the fire crossed Franklin through a row of wooden sheds behind the church.

The retreat was not cowardice; it was the recognition of impossibility. But retreat carried its own consequences. Each withdrawal abandoned structures that might have been defended with adequate resources, and each abandonment expanded the fire’s fuel base for its next advance. The Hayes Street fire, by 8: 00 a. m., had established a perimeter of approximately twelve square blocks, bounded roughly by Market Street on the south, Van Ness Avenue on the east, Golden Gate Avenue on the north, and Laguna Street on the west. Within this perimeter, the fire continued to generate new ignitions as collapsing walls and wind-borne embers spread flame to buildings not yet directly involved. The process was self-sustaining and self-accelerating.

At the high command level, awareness of this development arrived piecemeal and late. Mayor Eugene Schmitz, who had spent the early morning hours touring the downtown damage, received his first report of a serious fire in the western district shortly after 7: 30. The report came not from the fire department, whose telegraph lines were down and whose chief lay dying at the Southern Pacific Hospital, but from a police sergeant who had observed the smoke column from the roof of the Hall of Justice. The mayor’s response, recorded in the minutes of the Committee of Fifty formed later that day, was to dispatch a messenger to the Presidio requesting military assistance. This request reached General Frederick Funston at approximately 8: 15, by which time the Hayes Street fire had already achieved a scale that would require not assistance but transformation of the entire response structure.

Funston’s decision to commit federal troops to firefighting operations, made without explicit authorization from Washington, rested partly on this early intelligence. The general later testified that he understood by 8: 30 a. m. That the city was threatened with conflagration and that the ordinary means of fire suppression had proved inadequate. His understanding was correct as far as it went, but it did not yet encompass the full geometry of the disaster. The Hayes Street fire was not merely one of several simultaneous outbreaks; it was the prototype of a new kind of event, one that would merge with other fires to create a unified front of destruction. This merging would begin before noon.

The mechanism of merger was both physical and institutional. Physically, the Hayes Street fire advanced eastward while a separate outbreak, originating near Fourth and Mission streets in the South of Market district, advanced northward. The two fires were separated by approximately one mile at 8: 00 a. m., with the dense commercial core of Market Street between them.

By 10: 30, according to Army observation posts established on the dome of City Hall, the eastern edge of the Hayes Street fire and the western edge of the South of Market fire were visible as separate smoke columns. By noon, the columns had merged.

The interval between these observations is sparsely documented; the fire department companies in the intervening zone were fully occupied with structure protection and did not file position reports. What is established is that by 1: 00 p. m., fire was continuous across an east-west band extending from the waterfront to Van Ness Avenue, and that this band was moving northward with the wind.

The institutional mechanism of merger was more complex and more consequential. The fire department, operating without central command, had assigned its available companies to the protection of specific structures or blocks. This assignment system, appropriate to contained urban fires, created a patchwork of defended and undefended zones that the fire exploited. A building protected by a hose stream might survive while its unprotected neighbor burned; the burning neighbor would then expose the protected building to radiant heat from a new angle, forcing the company to withdraw or extend its line.

The process multiplied demands on a fixed supply of equipment and personnel until both were exhausted. By 10: 00 a. m., according to a compilation made by Assistant Chief John Dougherty from surviving company journals, seventeen of the department’s fifty-two engine companies had abandoned their equipment and retreated to positions where water was available, while fifteen companies reported their pumpers disabled by debris or fuel exhaustion.

The Hayes Street fire had demonstrated this dynamic first, but not last. Its significance lay in establishing the pattern: fire unchecked by water, spreading across prepared fuel, forcing tactical retreats that expanded rather than contained the disaster. The implicit calculus of firebreaks, present in every decision to withdraw, to sacrifice one zone for another, was being made by individual captains without coordination or higher authorization. The cost of these decisions would become visible only in aggregate, as the separate fires joined and the city’s geography was rewritten by flame.

By 9: 00 a. m., the temperature in the burning zone had reached levels that made close approach impossible. The Army’s after-action report, compiled by Major General Adolphus Greely from field observations, noted that the heat at a distance of two hundred yards from the fireline was sufficient to blister exposed skin, and that the noise of burning structures, combined with the rush of wind, rendered verbal communication useless at ranges beyond fifty feet. These conditions transformed firefighting from a technical operation into an endurance test, and then into an evacuation operation. Companies that had held positions through the morning began to receive orders, often shouted rather than written, to withdraw to predetermined fallback lines. The predetermined lines had been established by Chief Sullivan in the 1890s, as part of a conflagration plan never fully implemented; their existence in 1906 was known to senior officers but not to most company commanders.

The fallback line for this district was Van Ness Avenue. This wide boulevard, 120 feet between curbs and planted with street trees that would themselves burn, had been designated in Sullivan’s plan as the final defensive position before the city’s elite residential district. The designation reflected both topographical reality (Van Ness marked the eastern edge of the flat terrain) and political calculation. The mansions of Nob Hill lay beyond Van Ness, and their protection was understood as a priority even in planning documents that never explicitly said so. The Hayes Street fire’s advance toward this line, which became visible to observers on Telegraph Hill shortly after 11: 00 a. m., thus carried significance beyond its immediate physical threat.

The deliberate framing of disaster to manage blame and liability would later emphasize the heroic defense of Van Ness Avenue. This framing was not yet available to participants at 9: 00 or 10: 00 a. m., when the fire’s direction remained uncertain and its ultimate scale unimaginable. What was available was the immediate experience of failure: failed water supply, failed communication, failed command structure, and the growing recognition that individual bravery could not compensate for systemic collapse. A letter from Fireman Patrick H. McCarthy, published in the Oakland Tribune on April 20, described fighting with nothing to fight with, and noted that the chief would have known what to do, but the chief was gone. The reference to Dennis Sullivan, who would survive until 4: 00 p. m. On April 19, was already part of the explanatory vocabulary of disaster.

The physical template established by the Hayes Street fire (ruptured gas lines feeding flame, wood-frame construction providing fuel, wind driving spread, absent water preventing control) would replicate across the city throughout the day. But replication was not mere repetition. Each new fire outbreak interacted with those already established, creating feedback loops of heat and smoke that modified local weather conditions and accelerated combustion rates. The meteorological station at the Presidio recorded wind speeds increasing from 22 miles per hour at 6: 00 a. m. To 31 miles per hour at 11: 00 a. m., with gusts to 45 miles per hour in the burning zones. These speeds were not independent of the fires; the thermal columns rising from multiple square miles of burning city created pressure gradients that drew in surrounding air and intensified its velocity.

The consequence was visible at ground level as the firefront approached Van Ness Avenue. Witnesses described a wall of flame extending above the roofline of three-story buildings, with individual tongues of fire reaching twice that height. The color of the flame changed from orange to white as combustion temperatures increased, indicating that the fire had moved beyond the consumption of ordinary building materials to the pyrolysis of structural timbers and the vaporization of volatile compounds. These were not the conditions of ordinary urban fire; they approached the conditions of a firestorm, in which the fire generates its own weather and becomes effectively unstoppable by any means available to nineteenth-century firefighting.

Engine companies attempting to hold the Van Ness line reported temperatures that made hose handling impossible without wet blankets wrapped around hands and faces. The blankets dried and charred within minutes. Water applied to the firefront flashed to steam before reaching the burning surfaces, creating a superheated atmosphere that drove firefighters back even when their hose streams maintained nominal pressure. The pressure itself was failing; the merged fires had consumed so many distribution mains that even areas theoretically served by alternative supplies found their hydrants reduced to trickles.

By noon on April 18, the Hayes Street fire and its successors had established a destruction zone of approximately 200 acres, with a perimeter of roughly four miles. This figure, compiled from insurance maps and post-fire surveys, represents burned area rather than continuously burning area; within the zone, some structures remained standing while others were reduced to ash. The distinction mattered little to the inhabitants. The zone included an estimated 2, 800 buildings, of which perhaps 400 were masonry structures that had survived the earthquake only to succumb to fire. The remainder were wood-frame construction of the type that had fed the initial outbreak.

The cost of this destruction, measured in property loss alone, would eventually be calculated at approximately $100 million for the first day’s burning, roughly one-quarter of the total disaster loss. But cost was not yet a meaningful category to those experiencing the fire. Cost required distance, assessment, comparison to alternatives. What existed at noon on April 18 was immediate: heat, smoke, the collapse of familiar structures, the displacement of populations into streets already crowded with others similarly displaced. The refugee columns moving south from the burning district toward Market Street, and thence toward the Mission District or the waterfront, represented a human flow that would soon overwhelm the city’s capacity for shelter and sustenance.

The template was set. The individual fire, born of specific ignition and local failure, had become a system of destruction that would operate according to its own logic for three days. The mechanisms of this system (thermal feedback, wind generation, fuel continuity, and the collapse of defensive coordination) were not yet understood by those caught within them. Understanding would come later, in the reports and inquiries that followed. What came first was the experience of powerlessness, repeated across thousands of individual stories that would coalesce into the narrative of disaster.

At 12: 47 p. m., according to the Western Union telegraph log preserved in the National Archives, the last commercial message was transmitted from San Francisco to points east. The transmission was interrupted mid-sentence; the operator reported fire in the building and did not resume. The silence that followed was not absolute (military circuits remained operational, and some telephone connections persisted into the evening), but it marked the effective end of normal communication between the burning city and the outside world. The Hayes Street fire had grown to encompass this silence, to make it part of its territory. What burned in San Francisco on April 18 was not merely buildings but the infrastructure of connection itself: the wires and cables and confident assumptions that had made this city a node in national and global networks.

The fire would continue. The specific outbreak at Hayes and Gough, indistinguishable by afternoon from its merged successors, would be remembered only in after-action reports and insurance claims. But its role as prototype, as the first demonstration of what was possible when earthquake damage met atmospheric conditions and institutional vacuum, would shape every subsequent decision about how to fight, where to retreat, and what to sacrifice. The calculus of firebreaks had begun with the failure at a single dry hydrant on a corner in the Western Addition, and it would not end until the city itself had been rewritten by flame.
