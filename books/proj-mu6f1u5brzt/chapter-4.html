Chapter 4

2 to His Presence

The subterranean stress has reached its breaking point; the fault is ready to slip. Above ground, the city moves through its evening unaware—this is the night before the morning of April 18, 1906.

At Engine Company No. 2 on Bush Street, Chief Dennis T. Sullivan completed his inspection shortly after seven o’clock. The brass fixtures gleamed under electric lights—he had trained his men to polish couplings until they served as mirrors, a discipline that measured readiness in small, visible ways.

The horses stood in their stalls, eleven pairs citywide, each animal known by name and temperament to the drivers who slept beside them. Sullivan ran his hand along the flank of the lead horse, feeling for heat or swelling that might signal lameness, then tested the alarm telegraph himself, watching the needle register his signal at the central station three blocks away.

The system had been his own design, installed after years of argument with the Board of Supervisors: fifty-three alarm boxes wired directly to firehouses, bypassing the telephone exchange that might fail or jam. It had cost eighteen thousand dollars in 1898, and he had defended this expenditure before committees who asked whether the old bell-ringing method that had served since volunteer days was not sufficient.

The men on duty stood at attention while Sullivan moved through the apparatus room. He was fifty-three years old, white-haired now, with the solid build of a man who had spent three decades climbing ladders and handling hose. Born in Ireland, raised in San Francisco from age four, he had joined the department as a call man in 1874, before paid service existed, when firefighters were compensated only when they fought fires and maintained their equipment through subscription and social standing. He had risen through every rank, learned every trick of wood construction and brick collapse, survived the great fires of the 1880s that taught him what a city without water pressure could become. In 1892 the Board made him chief, and he had spent fourteen years preparing San Francisco for the disaster he knew was coming.

Sullivan had grown up in Utica, New York, and moved to San Francisco in the late 1870s, working as a blacksmith before joining the Fire Department at twenty-five.

The preparation had taken specific, material forms. Sullivan had pushed through construction of the high-pressure system along the waterfront, twelve miles of mains fed by Pumping Station No. 2 at the foot of Van Ness Avenue, capable of throwing streams to the roofs of six-story warehouses lining the commercial district. He had standardized hose couplings after the 1901 fire at the Haslett Warehouse, when incompatible threads cost twenty minutes of critical time. He had established the relay system for outlying districts, where ordinary pressure could not reach, and trained his engineers in the use of water towers—those awkward, effective devices that could lift a stream above the roofline when everything else failed. The department counted 585 men in 1906, organized into thirty-five engine companies, twelve truck companies, four chemical companies, and three water towers, with a budget of $602, 000 that Sullivan considered barely adequate and the Board of Supervisors considered extravagantly high.

What he could not secure was independence from the water supply that the Spring Valley Water Works controlled. The private company held monopoly on San Francisco’s water, drawing it from watersheds on the Peninsula and in the East Bay, and from the reservoir that filled the valley behind coastal hills. The system served 343, 000 people through 550 miles of mains, and its engineers had assured the city that aqueducts and tunnels were proof against any conceivable shock. Sullivan had his doubts. He had walked the lines himself, seen the brick arches of the tunnel, noted the unreinforced masonry of distributing reservoirs on the hills. But the assurance held, politically and financially. The city had invested too much in the Spring Valley contract to entertain alternatives, and Sullivan’s requests for supplementary cisterns and emergency reservoirs had been cut from the budget in three consecutive years.

He left Engine No. 2 at half past seven, walking three blocks to the California Hotel where he and his wife maintained their official quarters. The arrangement was unusual—most chiefs had lived in private residences—but Sullivan had insisted on proximity to the central firehouse after his appointment, and the city converted the upper floor of the engine house adjoining the hotel into an apartment. The location gave him direct access to the alarm system and placed him at the intersection of Market and Kearny, where he could observe the city’s daily commerce and read its vulnerabilities.

Margaret Sullivan was waiting with his evening clothes laid out. They were dining out, attending a musical performance at the Columbia Theater on Powell Street, a rare indulgence for a man whose social life normally consisted of inspections, funerals for fallen firefighters, and the endless round of political meetings his position required. The Sullivans had married in 1882, raised two sons who had followed their father into the service, buried a daughter who had died in infancy. Margaret had learned to live with irregular hours and sudden departures, nights when the alarm bell sent him running downstairs while she lay awake listening for his return. She had never grown accustomed to the waiting, but she had learned not to speak of it.

They walked to the theater through streets that still held the April light, past produce markets closing for the evening, past saloons filling with the six o’clock trade. The Columbia Theater seated 1, 800 patrons in the ornate style that the 1890s had made standard: plush seats, gilded plaster, electric chandeliers that dimmed on cue. The performance that night was a touring company doing The Merry Widow, the Lehár operetta that had conquered Broadway the previous year and was now working through provincial circuits.

Sullivan found the music pleasant without engaging his full attention; his mind kept returning to the inspection he had completed, the small maintenance issues he had noted, the letter he needed to write to the Board about the condition of hoses at Engine No. 7. Margaret watched him more than the stage, recognizing the particular quality of his stillness that meant he was working through problems she would not be asked to share.

After the performance they took supper at a restaurant on Geary Street, one of the new establishments that catered to the theater trade with late hours and electric lighting. Sullivan ate sparingly—he had learned early in his career that a full stomach interfered with ladder work—and drank a single glass of beer, watching the street through the window while Margaret talked of their younger son’s recent promotion to lieutenant. The conversation was ordinary, domestic, the kind that fills hours of a marriage that has passed through crisis into comfortable habit. They had survived the deaths and disappointments, the political attacks and physical dangers, and had arrived at late middle age that offered, if not contentment, at least absence of immediate threat.

The restaurant closed at eleven. They walked back to the California Hotel through streets that had emptied of the theater crowd, past darkened shop fronts and the occasional patrolman checking doorways. The night was clear and cool, with a light wind from the west that carried the smell of ocean over the hills. Sullivan noted this automatically—wind direction and velocity were variables in every fire calculation—and found nothing alarming. The conditions were normal for April, when the rainy season had ended and summer fog had not yet established its dominion.

At the hotel, he made his final rounds. The night man at the desk reported no alarms; the telegraph in the engine house next door showed only routine test signals that the central station sent every hour. Sullivan climbed the stairs to their quarters, removed his formal clothes, put on the dressing gown he wore for sleeping. Margaret had already turned down the gas in the bedroom. He sat for a few minutes in the front room, reviewing the next day’s schedule: another inspection, a meeting with the chief engineer of the Spring Valley Water Works, the quarterly report to the Board that he had been drafting for a week. The work was never finished; there was always some deficiency to correct, some political obstacle to overcome, some new construction in the city that added to risk without adding to resources.

He went to bed at half past eleven. The room was small but adequate, furnished with heavy pieces that Sullivan preferred, the bed placed against the interior wall away from the window overlooking Bush Street. Margaret fell asleep quickly, as she always did; Sullivan lay awake longer, listening to sounds of the street below, the occasional carriage, the distant bell of a cable car on Market. He had developed this habit of wakefulness over the years, a kind of professional insomnia that kept him alert to any change in the city’s nocturnal rhythm. Tonight nothing disturbed it. The alarms remained silent. The horses in the stable below shifted in their stalls, settled, were still.

The city that surrounded him was, by measures of 1906, thoroughly modern. Cable cars ran on 112 miles of track, carrying 100, 000 passengers daily. Electric streetlights illuminated 250 miles of roadway. The telephone exchange connected 18, 000 subscribers, and telegraph wires carried the commerce of the Pacific to the financial district’s banks and brokerages. The buildings that Sullivan had spent his career protecting included structures of nine and ten stories, steel-framed, theoretically fireproof, that represented the architectural ambition of a city believing itself immune to constraints that limited older urban centers. San Francisco had burned six times between 1849 and 1851, twice more in the 1850s, again in 1863 and 1875 and 1880; each time it had rebuilt larger and more confident, convinced that modern infrastructure had eliminated conditions that made conflagration possible.

Sullivan knew better. He had studied reports from Baltimore in 1904, when a fire destroyed 2, 500 buildings and required assistance from fire departments in four states. He had read accounts of the Iroquois Theater fire in Chicago, the General Slocum disaster in New York, the endless catalogue of industrial accidents that the new century was compiling. The technical knowledge existed to prevent such catastrophes; what was missing was political will to apply it consistently, the willingness to spend money on prevention rather than compensation, to regulate construction and inspect occupancy with the same attention the city devoted to collecting taxes and issuing licenses. He had made these arguments for fourteen years, had won some victories and lost more, and had arrived at late-career understanding that his function was less to prevent disaster than to mitigate it when it came.

The water supply worried him most. The Spring Valley system depended on a single point of vulnerability: the network of tunnels and aqueducts that brought water from Peninsula reservoirs across the fault line running through coastal hills. The company’s engineers had dismissed his concerns, citing the depth of tunnels and flexibility of iron pipe carrying the flow. Sullivan had commissioned his own study in 1903, hiring a geologist to examine the fault trace where it crossed the water lines; the report had been inconclusive, noting evidence of past movement without predicting future behavior, and the Board had declined to fund recommended redundancy. The city would have to trust Spring Valley assurances, as it trusted assurances of building inspectors who certified fireproof construction of new skyscrapers, the gas company that promised safety of its mains, the electrical utilities that swore their wires would not arc in a storm.

He turned on his side, seeking the position that eased chronic pain in his right shoulder, the legacy of a collapse at the Mills Building fire in 1895. Margaret stirred but did not wake. The clock on the mantel ticked through the minutes, marking the approach of midnight and the new day that would bring its ordinary challenges: the inspection of Engine No. 7, the meeting with the water engineer, the quarterly report. Sullivan had requested a transfer for the captain at No. 7, a man whose drinking had become impossible to ignore; the paperwork sat on his desk awaiting formal signatures that would make it official. There would be resistance from the man’s political connections, the usual negotiation between efficiency and patronage that consumed so much of his administrative energy. He would handle it as he had handled similar problems before, with patience and the accumulated authority of his record.

The building was quiet now. The California Hotel had been constructed in 1890, six stories of brick and stone with an interior frame of Douglas fir, the kind of construction that Sullivan had learned to distrust without being able to prevent. The hotel’s owner had resisted installation of automatic sprinklers, citing cost; the fire escapes were adequate but not generous, the stairwells enclosed but not pressurized against smoke. These were compromises that Sullivan had accepted because he could not win every fight, because the political cost of pressing too hard was loss of influence that might be needed for more critical battles. He had chosen his priorities over the years: the high-pressure system, the alarm telegraph, the training of the men. The rest he had noted, filed, postponed.

He slept finally, as the city slept around him, the cables still on their tracks, the theaters darkened, the markets preparing for morning’s commerce. In the Presidio to the north, Brigadier General Frederick Funston lay in quarters that he had occupied since December, commanding the Department of California with a small staff and limited resources. Funston was forty-five years old, a veteran of the Cuban and Philippine campaigns who had won the Congressional Medal of Honor for his capture of Filipino insurgent leader Emilio Aguinaldo in 1901. His command in San Francisco consisted of the 22nd Infantry Regiment, recently returned from Alaska, and various artillery and engineering detachments totaling about 1, 600 men. The Army’s presence in the city was residual, a holdover from the coastal defense mission that had established the Presidio in the 1850s, now sustained more by bureaucratic inertia than strategic necessity.

Brigadier General Frederick Funston was a veteran of the Spanish–American War and the Philippine–American War, where he had received the Medal of Honor. Known as ‘Fighting Fred,’ he commanded the Department of California from the Presidio.

Funston had spent the evening reading military journals and drafting a report on coastal artillery requirements that he knew would be ignored by a War Department more concerned with pacification in the Philippines than fortification in California. His marriage was in its final dissolution—his wife had left him the previous year, citing his absences and drinking—and he had developed the habits of a solitary man, early to bed and early to rise, with a mechanical regularity that masked the restlessness that had driven him through three wars on two continents.

He would wake at five, ride his horse through the Presidio grounds, breakfast with his officers, attend to the endless paperwork that constituted garrison duty in peacetime. The routine was stupefying after the improvisations of guerrilla warfare, and he had begun to contemplate requests for transfer, for another expeditionary command, for anything that would break the monotony of inspections and requisitions.

The two men—Sullivan and Funston—had met only formally, at civic ceremonies where the Army’s presence was required and the Fire Department’s cooperation was noted. They moved in different circles, answered to different authorities, shared no common experience that would have created personal understanding. Yet their paths would cross with decisive effect in the hours ahead, the one bringing institutional knowledge of a lifetime spent fighting urban fire, the other the executive authority of military command. The city had placed them in separate compartments of its governance, with no protocol for coordination, no plan for joint operation, no recognition that their functions might suddenly become interdependent. This was the normal condition of American cities in 1906: fragmented authority, overlapping jurisdictions, improvised response to unanticipated crisis.

Sullivan’s breathing settled into the rhythm of sleep. The alarm telegraph in the room below registered nothing; the night operator dozed at his post, waking every hour to check the circuits. The horses dreamed standing, as horses do, their weight shifting from leg to leg in the darkness of the stable. Outside, the street was empty, the cable tracks gleaming faintly under electric arc lamps, the buildings massed in their irregular silhouette against the stars that were visible despite the city’s glow. The fault lay thirty feet below the surface, locked in its accumulated strain, the Pacific Plate grinding northward against the North American Plate at the rate of two inches per year, the movement measured and understood by geologists who had no power to warn the city above.

The quiet image of Chief Sullivan retiring in his quarters atop the California Hotel hands off the concentrated authority and latent vulnerability that will be shattered in the next moment.
