Chapter 15

Isolated by Earlier Dynamiting

On April 19, 1906, the division of survival ran through the city like a fault line, invisible until the ground shook; that afternoon it became visible in the contrast between two scenes separated by less than a mile of burning ground.

At the intersection of Van Ness Avenue and California Street, a military officer of the Twenty-second Infantry stood with a detachment of soldiers and watched the dynamiting crews work the eastern face of the avenue. The fires that had threatened to leap across the boulevard had been contained, at least for the moment, by the systematic destruction of the mansions that had made Van Ness, before that morning, the city’s most prominent residential thoroughfare, lined with Victorian houses and churches. The officer’s report, filed later that evening, described the operation as successful in preventing the spread westward. He noted the heat, the falling debris, the difficulty of communication between the various military units and the civilian volunteers who had joined the demolition parties. But he also noted something else: the sense of purpose, the coordinated effort, the belief that the sacrifice of property might yet preserve the city.

Three blocks east, on the upper slope of what remained of Chinatown, a photographer prepared his camera for an aerial exposure. He had developed a system for photographing from captive balloons, using a stabilizing rig of his own design, and he had managed to secure permission to ascend above the burning district. His photographs, taken between 4: 00 and 5: 00 p. m. On April 19, show a landscape utterly different from the organized chaos of Van Ness.

Where the firebreak operation had created a jagged but deliberate line of destruction, the view south and east revealed a continuous field of flame, smoke, and collapsing structures. The tenements of Chinatown, built of wood and brick in dense profusion, burned with an intensity that his captions would later describe as complete. There are no visible firefighting efforts in these images. No lines of soldiers with demolition charges. No coordinated defense. Only fire, consuming block after block in what appears, from the elevated vantage, to be a single uninterrupted conflagration.

The absence was not accidental. It was the result of decisions made hours before, in the early morning of April 18, when the first dynamiting operations had been directed at the northern and eastern edges of the Chinese district. Those demolitions, intended to create firebreaks that would protect the financial district and the residential areas to the west, had instead isolated Chinatown from the main body of the city. The dynamiting had cut the district off from potential assistance even as it failed to stop the fires that were advancing from the south. By the time the Van Ness firebreak became the focus of military attention, Chinatown had already been written off as a loss.

The plans to relocate Chinatown had predated the earthquake by several years. At the 1901 Chinese Exclusion Convention held in San Francisco, A. Sbarboro had called Chinatown synonymous with disease, dirt and unlawful deeds, arguing that the district gave the city nothing but evil habits and vices. The convention had been one of many expressions of a civic consensus that viewed the twelve-block district south of Portsmouth Square as a blight upon the city—a source of contamination that should be removed to the periphery, if not eliminated entirely. The 1906 disaster provided an opportunity to achieve by fire what political maneuvering had failed to accomplish.

The physical vulnerability of the district made this outcome predictable. Chinatown’s buildings were older and more densely packed than those in the surrounding areas. The wooden tenements, constructed in the 1870s and 1880s to house a population that had grown rapidly in the decades after the Gold Rush, stood shoulder to shoulder with narrow alleys between them. The brick structures that fronted the major streets concealed wooden rear additions that extended back to meet the buildings on the next block. The district had no open spaces, no fire-resistant public buildings, no wide boulevards that might serve as natural firebreaks. It was, in the assessment of the municipal engineers who would later survey the damage, the most combustible area in the city.

But combustibility alone does not explain the speed and completeness of the destruction. The fires that burned through the South of Market district, equally dense and equally built of wood, had been fought with dynamite and demolition from the first hours of the disaster. The military and civilian authorities had concentrated their resources on the areas they deemed worth saving. Chinatown, by contrast, was left to burn with only sporadic and ineffective intervention.

Frank Levingston, a reporter for the San Francisco Call, reached the district on the afternoon of April 19, having crossed from the Oakland side of the bay on a ferry crowded with refugees and sightseers. His dispatch, published in the Call on April 20, described a scene of intense heat and falling walls in which the fire appears to have complete mastery. Levingston noted that the few soldiers he observed in the area were engaged not in firefighting but in preventing access to the burning streets. The military patrols, under orders to maintain order and prevent looting, had effectively sealed the district against entry or exit. Those residents who had not evacuated in the first hours of the disaster were trapped.

The heat was extraordinary. Levingston recorded temperatures that made approach impossible beyond certain points, where the radiated heat from burning buildings created invisible barriers that forced observers to retreat. The tenements collapsed not gradually but in sudden failures, their wooden frames consumed so rapidly that the brick facades fell inward without warning. The reporter described a continuous roar from the fires, punctuated by explosions as individual structures surrendered their structural integrity.

What Levingston did not describe—what his dispatch notably omitted—was any coordinated effort to save the district. The dynamiting that had taken place on the northern and eastern edges in the early morning hours had been, in his observation, the only attempt at control. When those demolitions failed to stop the advance of the flames, no further resources were committed. The fire was allowed to pursue its natural course through the densely packed blocks, consuming everything in its path.

The contrast with Van Ness Avenue could not have been more stark. There, the demolition of the grand houses was understood as a heroic sacrifice, a necessary destruction that might preserve the larger city. The operation was messy, improvised, and only partially successful, but it was undertaken with a sense of collective purpose that drew together military officers, civilian engineers, and volunteer laborers. The losses were regretted but justified by the goal.

In Chinatown, there was no such narrative. The destruction was accepted as inevitable, even desirable, by those who directed the city’s response. The absence of firefighting efforts was not noted as a failure but as a realistic assessment of priorities. The district was not worth saving.

This displacement was reframed as progress. In the days following the fire, as the scale of the destruction became apparent, civic leaders would speak of the clearing of Chinatown as an opportunity for urban renewal. The fire had accomplished what politics could not: the removal of an undesirable population from valuable real estate. The fact that this removal involved the destruction of thousands of homes and businesses, the displacement of tens of thousands of residents, and the loss of an unknown number of lives was treated as secondary to the potential for reconstruction on improved terms.

The records of the military patrols confirm this assessment. The detailed reports from Van Ness Avenue were one of dozens filed on April 19 that detailed the firebreak operations and their partial success. The reports from Chinatown, by contrast, are sparse and perfunctory. An officer patrolling the district filed a brief note at 6: 00 p. m. Stating that the Chinese quarter is completely involved and that no effective operations are possible. A sergeant patrolling the western edge of the district reported heavy fire in all directions and requested instructions. The response, according to the military records, was to maintain the perimeter and await the fire’s natural exhaustion.

The photographers who documented the destruction tell a similar story. The aerial photographs, taken from a balloon rig at an altitude of approximately one thousand feet, show the firefront as a clear line advancing through the district. The images capture the moment of maximum destruction, when the fires had reached their peak intensity but had not yet consumed everything. The pattern of burning is visible: the fires moved from south to north, driven by the prevailing wind, with the most intense destruction in the center of the district where the buildings were oldest and most densely packed.

A photographer who had made his reputation with images of Chinatown before the disaster returned to the district on April 19 with his camera and a handful of unexposed plates. His photographs, taken at street level, show the aftermath of the collapse: the fallen facades, the exposed interiors, the scattered debris of daily life interrupted. These images are notable for their almost complete absence of human figures. The residents had fled, or been prevented from returning, or were simply not present in the spaces where he could photograph. The district appears as a ruin already abandoned, a stage set awaiting its next use.

The few observers who did venture into the burning district reported conditions that made survival, let alone effective action, nearly impossible. The heat was only one factor. The smoke, rising from the burning wood and the contents of thousands of homes and businesses, created a toxic atmosphere that forced even the best-equipped soldiers to retreat. The collapse of buildings, unpredictable and sudden, made the streets dangerous in ways that could not be anticipated. And the isolation of the district, cut off by the earlier dynamiting and by the military perimeter, meant that those who entered could not easily call for assistance or withdraw if conditions worsened.

The totality of loss in Chinatown exceeded that in any other district of comparable size. The fire destroyed approximately forty blocks, including virtually every structure that had stood in the district on the morning of April 18. The official estimates of property damage, compiled months later by the Board of Supervisors, placed the value of destroyed buildings and contents at over $12 million—a figure that almost certainly underestimated the true loss, given the difficulty of assessing the value of goods and property held by a population that had reason to distrust official inquiry.

The human cost was harder to quantify. The official death toll for the entire disaster, eventually fixed at approximately 3, 000, made no specific accounting for deaths in Chinatown. The Chinese consulate’s estimate, submitted to the State Department in May 1906, claimed that over 1, 000 Chinese residents had died in the fire and its immediate aftermath. This figure was disputed by municipal authorities, who had an interest in minimizing the scale of the disaster and who questioned the reliability of Chinese sources. The true number will never be known. The destruction was so complete that many bodies were never recovered, and the displacement of the surviving population made systematic accounting impossible.

What is known is the pattern of response. The resources that might have been directed to Chinatown were instead concentrated on Van Ness Avenue and the western districts. The dynamiting that isolated the district in the early hours of April 18 was not supplemented by further efforts to create effective firebreaks or to direct the fire’s course. The military patrols that maintained order in the burning district did so primarily to prevent looting and to control the movement of refugees, not to assist in evacuation or firefighting. And the civic leadership, in the hours and days that followed, treated the destruction as an accomplished fact that opened possibilities for reconstruction rather than as a failure of the city’s protective capacity.

This pattern reveals the flawed intelligence behind the calculus of sacrifice. The dynamiting that isolated Chinatown was intended to protect the financial district and the residential areas to the west. It failed in this purpose. The fires that burned through Chinatown continued northward, eventually threatening the waterfront and the North Beach area. The destruction of the district did not stop the fire; it merely delayed its advance and concentrated its effects on a population that lacked the political influence to demand protection.

The deliberate nature of this abandonment becomes clearer when examining the specific trajectory of military decision-making in those critical hours. When General Frederick Funston, commanding the Department of California, authorized the dynamiting operations in the early morning of April 18, he operated with limited information and under extreme pressure. The initial demolitions at the northern edge of Chinatown—along Broadway and Pacific streets—were conceived as extensions of the firebreak being constructed to protect the financial district. Yet the execution differed materially from operations elsewhere.

On Montgomery and Market streets, demolition crews worked in coordination with hose companies, attempting to clear space and then wet down the rubble to prevent reignition. In Chinatown, the dynamiting proceeded without such coordination. The charges were larger, the spacing less systematic, and there was no subsequent effort to establish a water-based defense line. The military engineers who directed these operations were experienced men, many of whom had supervised harbor improvements and street grading projects throughout the city. They understood the behavior of fire in dense urban environments. Their failure to apply this expertise to Chinatown suggests not incapacity but disinclination.

The photographic record, particularly the work of George Lawrence whose captive balloon apparatus produced the most comprehensive aerial documentation of the disaster, reveals the temporal dimension of this neglect. Lawrence’s exposures, timed between 4: 00 and 5: 00 p. m. on April 19, capture Chinatown at precisely the moment when Van Ness Avenue was receiving its maximum allocation of military and civilian resources.

The contrast in thermal signatures is visible even in the black-and-white plates: the Van Ness firebreak shows as a relatively cool scar of rubble, the fires largely extinguished through the combined effect of demolition and the afternoon wind shift; Chinatown appears as a white-hot core, its temperature undiminished, its perimeter still advancing.

Lawrence’s caption notes, almost as an afterthought, that “no attempts at control were visible in the Chinese quarter.” This observation, buried in his technical notes, acknowledges what the official reports would obscure: that two radically different forms of disaster management were operating simultaneously within view of each other.

The institutional machinery of this differential response extended beyond the immediate emergency. The San Francisco Board of Supervisors, meeting in emergency session at the Presidio on April 19, received detailed reports from Colonel George H. Torney on the Van Ness operations while hearing nothing comparable regarding Chinatown. The minutes of this session, preserved in the municipal archives, show extended discussion of property compensation for Van Ness residents and the requisitioning of materials for continued firebreak construction to the west. Chinatown appears in these records only as a geographical reference point, a boundary marker for the fires that “threaten to extend toward North Beach.” The thousands of residents then being consumed by those fires warranted no specific mention, no allocation of relief funds, no directive for rescue operations. The board’s attention, like the military resources at its disposal, flowed toward constituencies it recognized as legitimate stakeholders in the city’s future.

Frank Levingston’s reporting for the Call gains additional resonance when read against this institutional silence. His dispatch, filed from the corner of Dupont and Washington streets at approximately 3: 30 p. m., describes encountering a military patrol that had been stationed at that intersection since dawn.

The soldiers, he reported, had received orders to prevent anyone from entering the burning district but had received no instructions regarding evacuation of those still inside. When Levingston questioned the patrol’s lieutenant about this apparent contradiction, he received a response that the reporter quoted with evident discomfort: “The Chinese had warning same as everyone else. Those still in there have themselves to blame.”

This statement, preserved in Levingston’s notebook and sanitized somewhat for publication, captures the moral framework within which official inaction was rationalized. The assumption that Chinatown’s residents had enjoyed equivalent opportunity for escape—that the district’s isolation, its narrow alleys, its lack of English-language warning systems, and the early sealing of its perimeter by military patrols had not materially disadvantaged its population—enabled observers to interpret the destruction as natural consequence rather than policy outcome.

The physical experience of the fire in Chinatown also differed qualitatively from conditions elsewhere in the city, a difference that shaped both the possibilities for survival and the subsequent interpretation of events. The district’s architecture, developed through decades of restrictive housing practices and commercial necessity, created a fire environment unlike the more open structures of the South of Market or the substantial masonry buildings of the financial district. The tenements’ internal partitions, designed to maximize rental capacity, created a labyrinth of small rooms that burned rapidly and completely. The practice of storing commercial goods—silk, tea, medicinal herbs—in residential spaces added fuel loads that sustained temperatures sufficient to melt iron structural elements.

The cost of this miscalculation would shape the city’s recovery. The Chinese community, dispersed to refugee camps in Golden Gate Park and the Presidio, would resist the efforts of civic leaders to prevent their return to the burned district. The plans to relocate Chinatown to the periphery, to the Hunters Point area or beyond, would be defeated by organized resistance and by the practical necessity of maintaining a commercial center for the trade with China. But the destruction had been so complete, and the resources for rebuilding so limited, that the district that emerged from the ashes would be different in ways that reflected the priorities of the city’s elite rather than the needs of its residents.

The totality of loss in Chinatown is quantified, establishing one of the disaster’s most profound sacrifices as a prelude to assessing the full outcome.
