Chapter 17

The Fire Crowns Nob Hill

In the 1870s, the stone façades of Nob Hill revealed nothing. From the street, the Huntington mansion at California and Taylor stood as a granite cliff of respectability—rusticated quarried blocks three feet thick, corner towers, a copper mansard roof green with age. The Hopkins place two blocks south carried the same weight of permanence, as did the Stanford residence on the corner of Powell and the Crocker castle occupying the full block between California, Sacramento, Taylor, and Jones. These were structures built to outlast generations, to transmit inherited fortune across centuries, to announce that San Francisco had arrived at a condition of eastern solidity.

What the stone concealed was timber: balloon frames of Douglas fir and redwood, interior partitions of clear cedar, staircases and wainscoting of mahogany and oak, the accumulated fuel of decades of construction stacked beneath ornamental shells. The architects’ drawings, preserved in the Crocker papers and later photographed by the Carnegie Institution of Washington, DC, in 1908, reveal the contradiction in cross-section: masonry veneers four inches thick, structural wood sixteen inches deep, hollow spaces between floors that functioned as flues, attic chambers packed with the detritus of domestic life.

The fire department could have explained this vulnerability had anyone asked. But the fire department, in the person of Acting Chief John Dougherty and the surviving crews, stood at that hour on Van Ness Avenue, four miles south and west, feeding dynamite into the advancing wall of flame that threatened to breach the last organized defense of the western city. Chief Dennis T.

Sullivan, who had understood these buildings and their risks, had been mortally wounded at 5: 15 on the morning of April 18, when the California Hotel collapsed onto the Bush Street engine house where he slept. His injuries, inflicted by falling brick and timbers, would claim his life three days later, leaving the department without the man who had spent thirty years mapping the city’s particular dangers.

In 1903, Sullivan had proposed an emergency water supply system for the fire department to use water from reservoirs to be constructed on the city’s high peaks. Dougherty, promoted by catastrophe, faced a choice that Sullivan’s experience might have clarified: whether to abandon the Van Ness line and attempt a rescue of the hill, or to hold his position and sacrifice the mansions to the larger calculation of city survival.

He held the line. The decision was not recorded in any formal order; it emerged from the impossibility of the alternative. The water system remained shattered. The auxiliary supply from the Golden Gate, fed by fireboat hoses stretched across two miles of burning waterfront, could maintain pressure at Van Ness or nowhere. To shift engines uphill would require withdrawing them from a position where, for the first time since the earthquake, the fire’s advance had been checked. The hill would burn without opposition.

At 10: 00 a. m. On Friday, April 20, the first visible signs appeared. The fire that had consumed Chinatown through the night had not been extinguished; it had been delayed by the collapse of frame buildings into compacted fuel beds that burned slowly, releasing heat and gases that accumulated in the ruins. The steep grades of Nob Hill—California Street rising at a 16 percent slope from Kearny to Taylor, Powell climbing nearly as sharply from Market—functioned as chimneys. The morning wind, blowing fresh from the bay, pushed the heated air upward through the street canyons. Witnesses on Russian Hill, looking south, reported a discoloration in the smoke column that hung over the downtown ruins: a brightening at the base, a yellow-orange core that suggested new ignition rather than cooling embers.

The Mark Hopkins Institute of Art, occupying the former Hopkins mansion at the corner of California and Mason, was the first to attract attention. The building had been donated to the University of California in 1891, converted from a private residence to a public institution housing the James C. Flood collection of paintings and decorative objects. Its position on the crest of the hill, 376 feet above sea level, placed it above the immediate threat from the south; the fire would need to climb two full blocks before reaching its doors. This apparent security had encouraged the staff to remain on site through Thursday night, organizing the removal of portable valuables while assuming that the stone structure itself was defensible.

The assumption collapsed with the arrival of the fire front from the east. At approximately 11: 30 a. m., according to the account compiled by Professor H. Morse Stephens for the university’s report, flames appeared on the roof of a neighboring residence on Mason Street, ignited by wind-borne embers that had traveled half a mile from the burning wholesale district. The institute’s custodian, William H. Meyers, organized a bucket brigade using water from the building’s cistern, which had survived the earthquake intact. The effort lasted twenty minutes. The cistern held 15, 000 gallons; the roof fire required continuous application; the wind carried new embers faster than they could be extinguished.

At 11: 52, Meyers ordered evacuation. Staff and volunteers carried out seventeen paintings, including works attributed to Corot and Rousseau, and deposited them in the street. The remaining collection, estimated at 400 paintings, plus tapestries, porcelains, and the Flood family’s accumulated decorative arts, was abandoned to the building’s protection.

The fire entered through the roof at 12: 15 p. m. The Mark Hopkins Institute burned from the top down, a reversal of the ordinary pattern that testified to the intensity of the wind-driven assault. Photographs taken by George R. Lawrence from his captive balloon, and later by G.K. Gilbert of the U.S. Geological Survey, show the sequence: first the mansard roof consumed, then the upper windows vomiting flame, finally the granite walls standing as a shell around a hollow of incandescent timber.

The stone did not collapse; it cracked, spalled, and blackened, but maintained its structural geometry even as the interior was reduced to ash. This architectural survival, documented in Lawrence’s panoramas and later collected by the Museum of Modern Art in New York, the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art, and the Oakland Museum of California, created a visual record of destruction that emphasized the contrast between external permanence and internal vulnerability.

The Huntington mansion fell two hours later. Collis P. Huntington, the Southern Pacific railroad magnate, had died in 1900; the house remained occupied by his widow, Arabella, and their collection of European paintings and decorative objects. The staff had begun removal operations on Thursday afternoon, following the burning of the Palace Hotel, but the scale of the collection exceeded available transport. Arabella Huntington herself, according to a letter preserved in the Huntington Library archives, supervised the selection of items for rescue, directing that priority be given to small items of irreplaceable value, miniatures, illuminated manuscripts, jewelry, while accepting the probable loss of major furniture and tapestries. Her personal intervention continued until noon on Friday, when the approach of flames from the east and south forced departure.

The house burned with particular violence. Its construction, completed in 1886, had incorporated the most advanced fireproofing techniques of its era: iron floor beams, plaster partitions, asbestos insulation in the service areas. These measures, designed to prevent the spread of fire from room to room, proved irrelevant against external attack. The fire entered through window casements, ignited draperies and upholstery, and found in the mansion’s vertical organization, a central atrium rising four stories, staircases encased in wood paneling, an ideal pathway for rapid combustion. The temperature at the center of the structure, estimated by fire engineers who examined the ruins, exceeded 2, 000 degrees Fahrenheit, sufficient to melt the silver service in the dining room and reduce the famous walnut library to carbonized dust.

The Stanford residence, on the corner of Powell and California, presented a different case. Leland Stanford had died in 1893; his widow, Jane, had maintained the house as a memorial and operational base for her management of the university endowment. Following her murder in 1905, poisoned strychnine in her Honolulu residence, a crime never solved, the property had passed to the university trustees, who occupied it pending decisions about its future. The fire reached the Stanford house at approximately 2: 00 p. m., advancing simultaneously from the east along California Street and from the south up Powell. The staff, forewarned by the burning of the Mark Hopkins Institute, had completed evacuation by 1: 30, removing the Stanford art collection, primarily California landscapes and family portraits, to temporary storage in the Presidio.

The house itself was allowed to burn without intervention. This was not abandonment but calculation: the trustees, consulting with university officials who had reached the site by horseback, determined that the structure was architecturally compromised by the earthquake and that salvage operations would risk lives for property already effectively lost. The decision, recorded in the minutes of the Stanford Board of Trustees for April 22, 1906, reflects the emerging discipline of triage that governed official response throughout the disaster. The mansion’s destruction was complete by 4: 00 p. m., leaving only the stone entrance portico standing, a classical fragment that would be photographed repeatedly in the following days as emblematic of the hill’s catastrophe.

The Crocker mansion, largest and most elaborate of the four palaces, burned last. Charles Crocker had died in 1888; the house remained in family possession, occupied by his widow and children. Its position on the western slope of the hill, fronting Sacramento Street, placed it slightly apart from the direct line of fire advance; the prevailing wind, from the northeast, carried the flames past its eastern flank toward the already-burning Mark Hopkins and Huntington properties. For a brief interval, observers on Russian Hill believed that the Crocker place might survive, protected by its location and by the exhausted fuel bed of the intervening blocks.

The reprieve ended at 3: 30 p. m. The fire that had consumed the Stanford residence generated sufficient thermal updraft to draw cooler air from the west, reversing the local wind pattern and carrying embers across the intervening space to ignite the Crocker roof. The house’s famous tower, modeled on a French château and rising 120 feet above street level, functioned as a chimney; flames were visible from the Presidio, from Oakland across the bay, from ships standing offshore to escape the burning waterfront.

The Crocker family had evacuated on Thursday evening, removing personal effects but leaving the accumulated furnishings of three decades: the silver service commissioned from Tiffany, the tapestries purchased in Paris, the library of Californiana assembled by Crocker himself. The tower collapsed at 5: 00 p. m., a structural failure witnessed by thousands and recorded in the most widely reproduced photograph of the disaster, Lawrence’s “San Francisco in Ruins,” taken from his balloon at 2, 000 feet.

The destruction of Nob Hill’s palaces occurred without military intervention. Brigadier General Frederick Funston, whose authority over the city had consolidated through the preceding forty-eight hours, had deployed troops throughout the burned district for purposes of looting suppression and refugee control; the forces available for firefighting had been committed to the Van Ness Avenue line, where the decisive struggle for the western city was understood to be occurring. The hill’s isolation, surrounded by fire on three sides, accessible only by steep grades that exhausted horses and blocked wheeled transport, made it effectively unreachable by organized relief. Individual soldiers and volunteers attempted ascent through the afternoon, carrying water in buckets or blankets, but their efforts were symbolic rather than operational. The fire crowned Nob Hill through the accumulated energy of its own combustion, requiring no assistance and accepting no opposition.

The social geometry of the loss would be debated for years. The Big Four, Huntington, Hopkins, Stanford, Crocker, had embodied the railroad-era transformation of California, their fortunes extracted from the construction of the Central Pacific and invested in the physical assertion of San Francisco’s metropolitan ambition. Their houses, built within a decade of each other in the 1870s and 1880s, had announced the arrival of a new western aristocracy, self-created and self-celebrating, determined to match the established wealth of Newport and Fifth Avenue. The earthquake and fire destroyed this architectural program in a single afternoon, reducing four monuments of accumulated capital to identical ruins of cracked stone and carbonized timber.

The symbolic weight of this convergence was not lost on contemporary observers. James D. Phelan, former mayor and future senator, walking through the ruins on Saturday morning, noted in his diary the democratic uniformity of the destruction. The houses of the wealthy and the shacks of the poor, the stone palaces and the frame cottages, all were alike before this furnace. The observation was accurate as description but partial as analysis. The laborer’s shack, in the South of Market and Mission districts, had burned without photographic record, without newspaper attention, without the compensating interest of insurance adjusters and architectural historians. The millionaire’s palace burned as spectacle, its destruction witnessed, documented, and memorialized in ways that would shape the narrative of the disaster for generations.

The practical consequences unfolded through the weekend. By Saturday morning, April 21, the fire had exhausted the fuel available on Nob Hill and died against the open spaces of Russian Hill and the already-burned western slope. The ruins remained too hot to approach; the Crocker tower, collapsed but not fully consumed, smoldered for three days. Refugees who had sheltered in Lafayette Park, on the northern face of the hill, emerged to find their vantage point transformed into a overlook of devastation, the familiar landmarks replaced by a landscape of blackened stone and twisted metal.

The institutional response to the hill’s destruction revealed the emerging structure of recovery. The Committee of Fifty, meeting in emergency session at the Presidio on Saturday, allocated relief funds without distinction between the displaced elite and the displaced poor, a procedural equality that masked substantial practical differences. The wealthy burned had resources: bank accounts in New York, insurance policies with eastern companies, social networks that could provide temporary housing and replacement furnishings. The poor burned had the relief stations at Golden Gate Park and the Presidio, the standard ration of bread and coffee, the uncertainty of employment in a city whose economic base had been substantially destroyed.

The rebuilding of Nob Hill would follow a different pattern from the reconstruction of the commercial district or the working-class neighborhoods. The Crocker heirs, inheriting both the ruins and the accumulated railroad fortune, would eventually donate the site to the Catholic Archdiocese of San Francisco, enabling the construction of Grace Cathedral on the footprint of the destroyed mansion. The Stanford trustees, relieved of their architectural memorial, would concentrate resources on the university campus at Palo Alto. The Huntington and Hopkins collections, partially saved through the priority given to portable valuables, would form the nuclei of the Huntington Library in San Marino and the Hopkins marine station at Pacific Grove. The dispersal was creative as well as destructive: the fire that eliminated the concentrated display of wealth on Nob Hill contributed to the wider distribution of cultural resources across California.

This long-term outcome was invisible to the participants in the immediate catastrophe. For the residents and servants who had attempted salvage through Friday morning, for the firefighters who watched from distant positions, for the soldiers maintaining order in the surrounding streets, the burning of Nob Hill represented the final, irrefutable demonstration of the fire’s power. The city’s most defensible structures, its most substantial investments in architectural permanence, had proven as vulnerable as the frame tenements of Chinatown or the wood-shingle cottages of the Mission. The fire had crowned the hill not through strategic calculation but through the autonomous dynamics of combustion, wind, and fuel. Human agency, which had briefly seemed capable of directing the disaster through dynamite and firebreaks, reached its limit against the geological fact of the hill’s exposure and the physical fact of timber construction.

With Nob Hill’s conquest complete, the fire’s dominant phase ended. The thermal mass that had driven three days of continuous combustion, consuming 28, 000 buildings across 4.7 square miles, had exhausted the concentrated fuel of the downtown and its surrounding districts. What remained was the slow burning of isolated structures, the cooling of ruins too hot to approach, the shift of human priority from fighting to surviving. The city that emerged from this transformation would be measured against the city that had existed at 5: 12 a. m. On April 18, and the comparison would shape every decision about what to rebuild, how to rebuild it, and who would bear the cost.
