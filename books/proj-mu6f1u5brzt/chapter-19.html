Chapter 19

The City Under Martial Law

The meeting ended with a telegram to the War Department requesting additional supplies, additional troops, and, significantly, additional authority to requisition property and labor. Back in April 1906, this was not yet the settled procedure of occupation but the improvised scramble of a city whose civilian instruments had failed. The fires still burned when Brigadier General Frederick Funston, commander of the Presidio of San Francisco, began consolidating what he had already begun: the transformation of a damaged municipality into a military district governed by patrol routes, curfew hours, and summary discipline.

Two documents from the morning of April 20 illustrate what this transformation meant. In a military logbook kept at the temporary headquarters established in the undamaged Ferry Building, a captain recorded the routine change of guard at the Market Street checkpoint: relief at 0600, post quiet, no incidents. The entry runs to five lines, neat copperplate, the prose of an institution that believes itself in control. On the same date, in a pocket diary later deposited with the California Historical Society, a resident named Helen McLean recorded a different experience. She had walked toward that same checkpoint hoping to reach the ruins of her boarding house on Mission Street. A single shot, she wrote. Then nothing. The soldiers would not say what happened, and she did not ask again. The checkpoint remained operational. The silence remained unexplained.

Between these two records lies the substance of martial law as it actually operated in San Francisco: not the official proclamation that never came, but the daily exercise of armed authority over a population that had nowhere else to turn.

Funston had declared martial law on the morning of April 18, though he possessed no legal authority to do so and though the formal state of emergency was never ratified by the civil authorities whose powers he assumed. The declaration was characteristic of the man. He had earned his Medal of Honor in the Philippines by leading a party of scouts through enemy lines to rescue imprisoned American soldiers, an operation that combined personal daring with disregard for the chain of command that might have delayed it. Now, with the city’s fire chief, Dennis T. Sullivan, dying of injuries sustained when the California Hotel collapsed onto his quarters on Bush Street, with the water mains ruptured and the dynamiting of firebreaks proceeding without systematic plan, Funston acted as if the absence of effective civilian leadership constituted permission to substitute military hierarchy.

By April 20, the fires that had consumed the South of Market district and surmounted Nob Hill were beginning to exhaust their fuel. The great conflagration had reached the western edge of the built city and, checked by the Van Ness Avenue firebreak and the open ground of the Presidio, could advance no farther. The moment when catastrophe turned into occupation had arrived. The 1, 700 federal troops and National Guardsmen already deployed, numbers that would shortly increase with the arrival of additional regular Army units from posts across the West, transitioned from emergency firefighting to systematic patrol. Their orders, transmitted through a command structure that bypassed the mayor’s office and the board of supervisors, established the patterns that would govern San Francisco for the following weeks.

The military system rested on three operational premises: the prevention of looting, the regulation of movement, and the enforcement of a curfew that emptied the streets from dusk to dawn. Each premise derived from a plausible assessment of post-disaster conditions. The assessment was not wrong. The execution transformed assessment into structure.

Looting presented the most immediate justification for armed patrol. The earthquake had shattered the physical security of the commercial district. Safes stood exposed in collapsed walls. Merchandise lay scattered across streets that no longer possessed functioning police stations. The initial response of the civilian authorities—Mayor Eugene Schmitz’s proclamation authorizing troops and police to kill any person found engaged in looting or other crime—had created a framework of lethal permissiveness that the military command adopted without modification. The proclamation, posted throughout the refugee camps and surviving business districts on April 18, remained in force without formal renewal. Its language of authorization, directed at federal troops, members of the regular police force, and all special police officers, effectively subordinated the remaining civilian law enforcement to military coordination.

What this meant in practice can be traced through the records of military police operations and the scattered civilian testimonies that survived the destruction of personal papers. At the corner of Kearny and Clay, a detail of regular Army infantry established a checkpoint to control access to the damaged but still standing U.S. Mint, where several million dollars in gold and silver lay in vaults that the earthquake had compromised but not breached. The soldiers’ orders were specific: admit no one without written authorization from the military command or the Treasury Department’s regional inspector. Residents of the surrounding blocks, attempting to recover belongings from threatened buildings, encountered fixed bayonets and repeated demands for identification papers that the earthquake had often destroyed. The checkpoint functioned. The gold remained secure. The residents learned that their property rights now required military certification.

This pattern repeated across the surviving city. At the Hall of Justice on Portsmouth Square, where the county jail had partially collapsed and prisoners required evacuation, National Guardsmen maintained a perimeter that admitted only authorized personnel. At the post office on Mission Street, regular troops guarded the mail sorting facility and the federal records stored in its basement. At private commercial buildings where owners had secured military protection—the Merchants Exchange, the Palace Hotel’s surviving annex, several bank headquarters on Montgomery Street—soldiers checked passes, examined bundles, and occasionally confiscated items they deemed suspicious or excessive.

The confiscations extended beyond commercial property. Military patrols, operating under general orders to prevent the accumulation of contraband that might facilitate disorder, seized alcohol, firearms, and ammunition from private citizens. The rationale was preventive: drunkenness might lead to violence, armed civilians might resist authority or engage in extralegal enforcement. The practice was systematic. In the refugee camps established in Golden Gate Park and the Presidio, soldiers searched arriving wagons and persons, removing bottles of whiskey and wine, revolvers and hunting rifles, cartridges and powder. The seized items were inventoried—some records survive in the National Archives—and held for eventual return or, in many cases, eventual disappearance.

The curfew completed the architecture of control. From sunset to sunrise, the streets of San Francisco belonged to military patrols. The order was enforced with varying strictness. In the undamaged residential districts west of Van Ness Avenue, where wealthy homeowners had organized private watch patrols and where military presence was lighter, the curfew functioned largely as a warning. In the burned districts and the refugee camps, where population density was highest and military presence most concentrated, enforcement was immediate and physical.

Soldiers challenged pedestrians after dark, demanded explanations, and escorted violators to temporary detention facilities established in surviving school buildings and church basements. The facilities were not prisons in the formal sense. They were holding areas where military police processed curfew violations, vagrancy charges, and suspected looting before releasing detainees with warnings, transferring them to civilian authorities where those still functioned, or—in a small number of documented cases—referring them for court-martial.

The court-martial records, preserved in the Judge Advocate General’s files, reveal the friction points where military discipline encountered civilian resistance.

A private named William H. Johnson, stationed with the 22nd Infantry, faced charges of assault with a deadly weapon after firing his rifle during a confrontation at a Mission Street checkpoint on the night of April 21. The confrontation had begun when a civilian, later identified as a merchant who ran a grocery on Valencia Street, attempted to pass the checkpoint carrying a sack of personal belongings. Johnson demanded to inspect the sack. The merchant refused. The details of the subsequent exchange are disputed—Johnson testified that the merchant reached for something in his coat; the merchant testified that he merely gestured emphatically—but the outcome was not: Johnson fired, wounding the man in the shoulder.

The court-martial, convened hastily on April 23, acquitted Johnson on grounds of reasonable fear for his safety. The wounded merchant, still recovering, was charged with disturbing the peace and fined twenty dollars by a military tribunal that possessed no statutory authority to impose fines.

The case was not exceptional. It was representative of a system in which military personnel made determinations of guilt and innocence, imposed punishments, and reviewed their own conduct, all without reference to civilian legal process. The system operated under the cover of emergency, though the emergency that justified it, the active firestorm, had largely ended by April 21. The continuation of martial law beyond the immediate crisis was not accidental. It reflected Funston’s assessment, shared by Mayor Schmitz and the Committee of Fifty that had coalesced to direct relief efforts, that the city’s social order remained fragile and that military discipline provided the necessary framework for reconstruction.

The Committee of Fifty—businessmen, politicians, and civic leaders invited by Schmitz to his office on the afternoon of April 18—had initially functioned as an emergency coordinating body. By April 20, it had become the administrative interface between military command and civilian society. The committee’s subcommittees, organized to address food supply, shelter, transportation, and finance, operated within parameters established by Funston’s headquarters. Requests for supplies, for labor, for access to damaged areas, flowed through military channels. The committee’s authority was real in the sense that its members made decisions about distribution and allocation. Its authority was derivative in the sense that every decision required military ratification and could be overridden by military order.

This arrangement solved certain problems. It prevented the chaos of competing jurisdictions that had characterized the first hours after the earthquake, when fire companies, police precincts, and neighborhood committees had acted without coordination. It ensured that relief supplies reached designated distribution points rather than being diverted to private hoarding. It maintained, however imperfectly, the physical security of the remaining commercial and financial infrastructure on which eventual reconstruction would depend.

The arrangement also created new problems that the official reports acknowledged only obliquely and that civilian testimony recorded with greater frankness. The differential application of military control—heavy in poor and working-class districts, lighter in wealthy neighborhoods—reproduced and intensified the city’s existing social stratifications. Chinatown, already devastated by fire and by the deliberate dynamiting of buildings to create firebreaks, experienced military occupation as a continuation of disaster. Soldiers patrolled its ruined streets, established checkpoints at its surviving perimeter, and enforced the same curfew that applied elsewhere. But the enforcement carried additional weight in a district where residents suspected—correctly—that city officials had long desired their removal. The Chinese community’s resistance to relocation, which would become a significant political issue in the following weeks, began in these days of military patrol, when armed soldiers determined who could enter and leave the ruins of their neighborhood.

The differential policing of neighborhoods operated here in its most explicit form. The dynamiting along Van Ness Avenue, which had saved the western districts by sacrificing the mansions of the wealthy, had been accompanied by less systematic destruction in the South of Market and the Mission, where working-class housing burned without deliberate intervention. In Chinatown, the destruction was deliberate and comprehensive. Military engineers, working with city fire officials, had blown buildings to create a zone of clearance that would protect the financial district. The protection succeeded. The clearance removed a neighborhood.

Funston’s own reports to the War Department, composed in the evenings of April 20 and 21, emphasized order and progress. The city is now under complete military control, he wrote on April 20. Looting has been suppressed. The fires are dying. Relief operations are proceeding systematically. The prose is confident, the assessment optimistic. It omits the complaints that were already reaching his headquarters from civilian relief workers, from property owners denied access to their buildings, from refugees detained at checkpoints for lacking proper identification.

The complaints were not trivial. They indicated a fundamental tension between military efficiency and civilian autonomy that would shape the entire reconstruction period. The military system prioritized security and order; it treated individual claims to property, to movement, to due process, as secondary considerations that might be accommodated when they did not conflict with operational requirements. The prioritization was not gratuitous oppression. It was the logical extension of a command structure designed for combat, applied to a population that had not surrendered and had not been conquered.

The transformation of San Francisco into a military district also transformed the relationship between the federal government and local authority. Funston’s seizure of control, however extra-legal, had the practical effect of federalizing the disaster response. The War Department became the primary source of supplies, of personnel, of the authority that made relief distribution possible. Mayor Schmitz retained his office and his formal title, but his effective authority derived from his willingness to collaborate with military command rather than from any independent institutional base. The Committee of Fifty operated as a civilian auxiliary to military administration.

This federalization had consequences that extended beyond the immediate emergency. The reconstruction of San Francisco would be shaped by federal priorities, federal standards, and federal oversight to a degree unprecedented in American urban history. The military occupation established the precedent that local self-governance had failed and required external supervision. This precedent would influence the politics of relief funding, the design of new infrastructure, and the eventual decision to frame the disaster as a fire rather than an earthquake in official accounts.

The daily reality of martial law, as experienced by the city’s surviving population, can be reconstructed from the fragmentary records that escaped destruction. Morning brought the relief of curfew, the opportunity to move through checkpoints with proper authorization, the search for food and water at military-supervised distribution points. Daylight hours were filled with the labor of salvage and evacuation, carried out under the observation of patrols that challenged unexplained movements and suspicious gatherings. Evening brought the curfew’s return, the emptying of streets, the concentration of population in designated camps or in burned neighborhoods where soldiers permitted temporary shelter.

The refugee camps themselves became exercises in military administration. Golden Gate Park, with its open ground and distance from the burned district, received thousands of displaced persons who were organized into company-sized units, issued rations according to military schedules, and subject to the same discipline that governed troop encampments. The Presidio, already a military reservation, integrated civilian refugees into its existing structure with less friction but equal comprehensiveness. In both locations, the military command determined who would receive tentage, bedding, and medical attention, and in what order.

The medical attention was not negligible. Army surgeons, supplemented by civilian volunteers who accepted military coordination, treated injuries from the earthquake and fire, managed the sanitation problems of concentrated displacement, and attempted to prevent the epidemic diseases that threatened to follow physical disaster. Their success was partial but real. San Francisco did not experience the cholera or typhoid outbreaks that had accompanied comparable disasters in other cities and other eras. The achievement was genuine, purchased at the cost of individual autonomy.

The cost is harder to measure than the achievement. It appears in the letters of refugees who described their treatment as prisoners rather than as disaster victims. It appears in the diary entries that record unexplained shootings, unacknowledged detentions, the gradual recognition that normal legal protections had been suspended without formal announcement. It appears in the silence that followed certain questions, the absence of records that might have documented the full extent of military discipline.

By the evening of April 21, the fires had essentially ended. The city that remained was a landscape of ruin, occupied by a population that had survived immediate destruction only to enter a period of controlled dependency. Funston’s telegram to the War Department, requesting additional authority to requisition property and labor, marked the transition from emergency response to systematic reconstruction. The authority was granted. The requisitions followed. The military administration that had begun as improvisation solidified into structure.

The concrete friction between military control and civilian attempts to organize aid established the constrained environment within which all subsequent relief efforts would operate. Private charity, religious organization, neighborhood mutual assistance—all functioned within parameters set by military command. The relief committees that would eventually compile the detailed ledgers of distribution and need did so as authorized auxiliaries, their records subject to military review, their personnel subject to military direction. The city under martial law was not a temporary aberration but the foundational condition of San Francisco’s recovery, a system of surveillance and discipline that would govern the terms of survival for the months and years that followed.
