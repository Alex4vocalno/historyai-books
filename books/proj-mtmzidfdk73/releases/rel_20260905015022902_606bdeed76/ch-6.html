Chapter 6

The Microswitch and the Missed Moment (1998-2001)

The click was a punctuation mark in the palm of your hand. It happened in a Tokyo arcade in the autumn of 1998, but the sound was detached from the roar of the cabinets and the rattle of tokens.

A player held a newly purchased Neo Geo Pocket, its metallic-blue shell cool and dense, his thumb resting on the tiny joystick crowning the d-pad. He pushed forward. A definitive, mechanical click answered through the plastic, a crisp auditory and tactile confirmation of input. He pushed again, left, right, each direction snapping into place with the same satisfying feedback. This was not the mushy, silent travel of a rubber membrane, the standard for every handheld that had come before. This was the sound of a microswitch, a component lifted from the control panels of industrial machinery and the joysticks of SNK’s own arcade behemoths, now miniaturized and sealed inside a device meant for a coat pocket. In that moment, the engineering argument of the pocket acquired a new, uncompromising dialect. The WonderSwan, released months earlier, had refined the art of subtraction.

This was an unabashed declaration of addition. The Neo Geo Pocket made a specific, bold Form Factor Covenant: it promised arcade-precise control anywhere you went, a covenant written not in pixels or processor speed, but in the physical language of switches and springs. Among a small, fervent cohort of players and critics, the device was immediately recognized as a masterpiece of tactile design. The reception was one of awed respect. Reviewers marveled at the ‘clicky stick,’ noting how it transformed playing a portable fighting game from an act of approximation into one of precision. The machine felt substantial, its shell made of a harder, more metallic-feeling plastic than the toy-like Game Boy. Its screen, while still a reflective monochrome LCD, was sharp.

For those who valued the physical interface above all else, the Neo Geo Pocket represented an apotheosis. It was the handheld that gamers who cared about feel had always wanted. Yet this brilliant reception existed within a vacuum.

By the holiday season of 1998, the same season the Game Boy Color launched with a library of hundreds of compatible games, the Neo Geo Pocket was struggling to find shelf space outside of specialty stores. Its commercial failure was swift and stark. It sold, but not in the numbers that moved markets or dictated retail strategies. The verdict from the wider world was a quiet, indifferent shrug. The machine that perfected the sensation of play was, in the grand economic theater of handheld gaming, stillborn.

To understand why SNK would build such a device, and why that device was doomed, one must open it up, tracing the lineage of its components back to their origins. The company, Shin Nihon Kikaku, was not a console manufacturer in the Nintendo or Sega mold. Its identity was forged in the arcades. Since the early 1980s, SNK had been producing some of the most technically impressive and brutally difficult coin-operated games in Japan. Its hardware platform, the Neo Geo Multi Video System (MVS), was legendary.

For a price, arcade operators could buy a cabinet that housed a motherboard capable of running multiple game cartridges, each offering graphics and sound that dwarfed what home consoles could achieve. The home version, the Neo Geo AES, was a prohibitively expensive luxury item, a status symbol for the hardcore. The culture of SNK was one of premium performance for a dedicated audience. It was a culture of the arcade stick: large, weighted bases housing Japanese-style microswitched levers that clicked with every directional input, the preferred tool for mastering games like Fatal Fury and Samurai Shodown.

When SNK decided to enter the handheld market in the late 1990s, it was not attempting to beat Nintendo at its own game. It was attempting to translate its core philosophy into a portable form. The team, led by veteran engineer Eiji Yamanouchi, started with the interface. The goal was to replicate the precise control of an arcade stick. Rubber-membrane d-pads, the industry standard, were unacceptable; they lacked the tactile feedback and the rapid-fire potential of a microswitch.

The engineering challenge was one of radical miniaturization. A standard arcade microswitch was far too large. The solution was a custom-designed, scaled-down microswitch assembly built directly into a miniature thumbstick. The joystick itself did not physically tilt far; its movement was primarily about actuating one of four tiny switches arranged in a cross. This was the source of the click. Every input was a digital event, a clean break in an electrical circuit, allowing for rapid, unambiguous directional commands. The shell was designed to be rigid to prevent flex, ensuring the internal mechanism remained perfectly aligned. The battery compartment was modest, holding two AA cells, because the power draw of the microswitches and the simple 8-bit processor was low. Every decision flowed from the central mandate: control fidelity. This was the ‘inner works’ of the device, a triumph of focused engineering.

The Neo Geo Pocket was, in essence, a delivery system for its joystick. This purity of vision, however, collided with a business reality that was already fracturing. SNK in 1998 was a company living on borrowed time and reputation.

Its finances were a precarious house of cards built on arcade revenue and the modest sales of its luxury home hardware. The development of the Neo Geo Pocket, and its color successor a year later, required significant capital. The company was overextended, investing in new arcade hardware and pinball machines while trying to fund a foray into the competitive handheld market.

The device launched with a scant software library. SNK’s own titles, ports of its fighting games like The Last Blade and King of Fighters R-1, showcased the joystick’s brilliance but appealed to a niche audience. Third-party support was minimal and hesitant. While a deal with Sega brought a respectable version of Sonic the Hedgehog, and a partnership with Capcom yielded a port of Rockman (Mega Man), the overall catalog remained anemic.

The Neo Geo Pocket Color, released in 1999 to correct the original’s monochrome screen, faced the same problem. Its library grew to include a handful of genuine classics, like the innovative strategy game SNK vs. Capcom: Card Fighter’s Clash, but it never approached a critical mass.

The cost of the hardware, while not exorbitant, was higher than a Game Boy Color. SNK was asking consumers to pay a premium for superior feel in a device that offered far fewer things to feel superior about.

The consequences of this collision were catastrophic for SNK and instructive for the history of handhelds. The company’s financial implosion began in 2000. Burdened by debt from multiple ventures and insufficient revenue from its pocket console, SNK filed for bankruptcy in October of that year. Its assets, including the prized Neo Geo brand and intellectual property, were sold off. The corporate entity that had created the microswitched joystick effectively ceased to exist. Production of the Neo Geo Pocket Color sputtered and halted. For the device’s devoted users, it became a beautiful relic, a closed ecosystem of perhaps forty-odd excellent games, forever frozen in time. Its failure was not a result of poor engineering, but of a fatal mismatch between a company’s insular, craft-focused philosophy and the market’s demand for an expansive, affordable software ecosystem.

The dominant factor was indeed the brute-force economics of platform lock-in. Nintendo’s Game Boy line, by 2000, was not just a piece of hardware; it was a continent. Developers flocked to it because its audience was guaranteed. Consumers bought it because its games were abundant. The Game Boy Color, backward-compatible with a decade’s worth of software, represented an economy of scale and a wall of content that a premium specialist like SNK could not hope to penetrate, let alone scale. This economic reality traced its own lineage back through Nintendo’s corporate history. By the late 1970s, Nintendo was struggling financially. Two key events in Nintendo’s history occurred in 1979: its American subsidiary was opened in New York City, and a new department focused on arcade game development was created. That pivot, driven by necessity, ingrained a deep, institutional caution regarding risk.

The subsequent success of the Game & Watch line, created by Yokoi from the technology used in portable calculators, and the Famicom/NES taught Nintendo that control over the platform, through cartridge licensing and strict developer agreements, was more valuable than any single hardware innovation.

This was the foundational logic SNK ignored. SNK approached the handheld as a hardware company, seeking to win on the merit of its engineering. Nintendo approached it as a platform sovereign, where hardware was merely the gate to a taxed kingdom. The Neo Geo Pocket’s microswitches were a better solution to a problem of control, but Nintendo had long ago defined the problem differently.

For Nintendo, the primary problem was never precise control in isolation; it was building a device cheap and robust enough to sell tens of millions of units, creating a market so large that software would flood in, making the device indispensable. The Game Boy’s rubber d-pad was not chosen because it was superior, but because it was good enough, reliable, and inexpensive to manufacture at colossal scale. SNK’s covenant was for the connoisseur. Nintendo’s was for the crowd.

The microswitch itself, a humble component of brushed metal and spring tension, was a direct descendant of industrial logic. In factories and aircraft cockpits, such switches were valued for their durability and unambiguous state—they were either open or closed, on or off, with no ambiguous middle ground. This binary certainty was what SNK’s arcade designers craved. In the frantic, frame-perfect world of a King of Fighters match, a player needed to know, instantly and through their fingers, that a command had been registered.

The company’s engineers, therefore, were not merely shrinking a joystick; they were transplanting an ethos. The act of miniaturization required rethinking the switch’s internal architecture, balancing the need for a satisfying tactile bump against the risk of fatiguing a player’s thumb over long sessions. The resulting mechanism was a marvel of condensed intent, a physical manifesto declaring that portability should not mean compromise.

Yet this manifesto was written in a language the mass market had never learned to read.

This insular focus on control fidelity created a paradox for software development. Designing a game for the Neo Geo Pocket Color meant designing for the click. Titles like SNK Gals’ Fighters and Fatal Fury: First Contact were engineered around precise quarter-circle motions and rapid directional taps, their play mechanics leveraging the hardware’s unique responsiveness.

This created an exceptional experience for the initiated but erected a subtle barrier for the casual player. The very precision that delighted enthusiasts could feel unforgiving to someone accustomed to the more forgiving, analog-like glide of a rubber membrane.

Furthermore, the commitment to this specialized interface may have inadvertently narrowed SNK’s own vision for its software library. The company’s development resources, already stretched thin, flowed disproportionately toward perfecting ports of its arcade fighters—genres that showcased the joystick—while potentially overlooking opportunities in puzzle, adventure, or role-playing games that might have broadened the system’s appeal. The hardware dictated a certain type of software, which in turn attracted a certain type of player, creating a virtuous cycle for a niche but a vicious one for market share.

The financial pressures on SNK were not abstract forces but daily realities that shaped every decision. While Nintendo could amortize the cost of Game Boy hardware development across years of sales and a vast licensing revenue stream, SNK had no such cushion. Each cartridge sold had to directly recoup its production cost and contribute to funding the next game, under a constant shadow of debt. This precariousness affected everything from marketing budgets to retail partnerships.

Where Nintendo could blanket television networks with advertisements and secure prime endcap displays at toy stores, SNK’s outreach was often limited to gaming magazines and specialty shop shelves. The company’s reputation as an arcade powerhouse did not automatically translate into leverage with big-box retailers like Toys “R” Us, who prioritized shelf space for products with proven, high-velocity sales. Thus, even when a curious consumer might have sought out the Neo Geo Pocket Color, they often simply could not find it in the channels where most handheld purchases were impulsively made.

The contrast with Nintendo’s institutional strategy was rooted in decades of divergent evolution. Nintendo’s near-bankruptcy in the late 1970s had seared into its corporate DNA a profound aversion to unchecked hardware risk. Its salvation through the Game & Watch and later the Famicom established a template: create affordable, durable hardware as a vessel for software sales, and maintain ironclad control over that software ecosystem to ensure quality and profitability. This generated a fortress mentality where market share was the primary defense against oblivion.

SNK’s path was different. Its identity was forged in the high-stakes, hit-driven world of arcades, where attracting coins with technological spectacle and deep gameplay was the immediate goal. This culture celebrated heroic engineering and catered to dedicated patrons. When SNK moved into the home with the AES and later the handheld market, it brought this artisan’s mindset with it—the belief that if you built a superior machine for the discerning user, an audience would materialize. It was a philosophy of quality as its own justification, one that tragically underestimated the platform economics Nintendo had mastered.

In this light, the Game Boy Advance’s launch was less a competitive blow than a historical correction.

The final, unanswerable challenge arrived in March 2001. The Game Boy Advance launched in Japan. It was not a specialist tool. It was a mainstream juggernaut. Its 32-bit processor delivered visuals that approached the Super Nintendo, its library was announced as a who’s-who of Japanese and Western developers, and its price point was aggressively mass-market. Its d-pad was a conventional, silent rubber membrane affair.

Against this onslaught, the remaining stock of Neo Geo Pocket Color units on shelves looked like what they were: artifacts of a parallel track that had reached a dead end. The GBA did not beat the NGPC on the metric of tactile precision. It rendered the metric irrelevant.

It redefined the terms of the argument away from elite craftsmanship and toward accessible power and volume. The field was cleared. The verdict on the Neo Geo Pocket Color’s failure was a market-wide lesson that a handheld built on a covenant of pure control, without the software empire to sustain it, could not survive.

This verdict, delivered not by critics but by cash registers, left Nintendo’s next monolithic advance unopposed. In the warehouses and shipping containers, the last production runs of SNK’s masterpiece sat packed in boxes, their microswitches silent. Their fate signaled the closing of a particular frontier in the pocket wars. Its existence made the coming attempt at addition, at a handheld that felt like a precision instrument, not just inevitable, but a necessary counter-experiment in the ongoing argument of the pocket. The following pressure would not emerge from a rival’s alternative vision of what a handheld should be, but from within the very architecture of Nintendo’s own triumph, as millions of players tilted their new Game Boy Advances toward any available light source, squinting at a screen that had won every battle except the one against the sun.
