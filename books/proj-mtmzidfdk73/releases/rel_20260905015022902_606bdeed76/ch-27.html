The Unfinished Machine (2024-2025)（27）

By the summer of 2024, the global topography of handheld gaming resembled a delta in flood season. A single, once-powerful channel—the hybrid doctrine—had fanned out into countless distributaries, each carving its own path through the landscape of consumer demand. This was not a planned diversification but a fracturing, a structural failure of consensus.

The monolithic market that had coalesced around the Nintendo Switch by 2021, a realm of software-driven harmony, now appeared as a historical interlude. By 2024, the unity was gone.

In its place was a crowded bazaar of forms and philosophies, a tangible sprawl of plastic, silicon, and glass where every stall offered a different solution to the same old problem. The central engineering argument of the pocket had not been resolved. It had proliferated.

To descend from that aerial view to the concrete reality of a retailer’s shelf or a digital storefront page in early 2024 was to witness a taxonomy in disarray. Devices were sorted by brand and price, but the underlying categories were philosophical.

A sleek, angular black slab boasting an AMD Ryzen processor sat next to a small, pastel-colored rectangle that precisely echoed the dimensions of a 1989 Game Boy. A unit with a brilliant seven-inch, 120Hz display was marketed beside another with a deliberately modest, four-inch screen with visible grid lines. Price points formed a wide spectrum, from under one hundred dollars for simple emulation boxes to well over a thousand for handheld PCs with specifications rivaling laptops.

This marketplace no longer directed consumers toward a dominant standard. It presented them with a field of fragments, each a fully realized answer to the enduring question of sacrifice, each valid and none preeminent.

The success of the hybrid model and the subsequent legitimization of the PC-derived handheld had not settled the debate. They had demonstrated that multiple, contradictory answers could coexist, demolishing the assumption that a single trade-off hierarchy could ever again claim to be definitive. The Switch, having shipped over 150 million units worldwide by December 2024, had proven the hybrid concept’s commercial dominance, but its very success had shattered the old orthodoxy.

The most technologically aggressive vector of this fragmentation originated not from traditional console architects, but from the computing industry.

Companies like Asus and Lenovo, leveraging their expertise in gaming laptops and partnerships with semiconductor designers, entered the arena with a proposition that inverted decades of handheld orthodoxy. Their machines, the Asus ROG Ally and the Lenovo Legion Go, were conceived from a single, uncompromising premise: to deliver a portable experience functionally equivalent to a mid-range gaming PC.

The trade-offs required by this goal were severe and were not hidden. To manage the thermal output of a chip like the Ryzen Z1 Extreme and to power a vibrant 1080p, high-refresh-rate display, these devices needed substantial internal cooling assemblies and large-capacity batteries. The Ally weighed over six hundred grams; the Legion Go, with its detachable controllers, approached eight hundred.

True pocketability, in any traditional sense, was an impossibility. A carrying case was a mandatory accessory, not an optional one.

Battery life under load could be measured in tens of minutes, not in the tens of hours that had once defined the category.

This was the console-first philosophy pushed to its logical extreme, a machine that fundamentally rephrased the book’s core question: what do you give up to make a console fit in your hands?

The answer was the pocket itself. This design was not an engineering failure but a deliberate and calculated market position. It assumed a user whose primary value was unfettered access to a vast, pre-existing library—the Steam catalog—and for whom seamless, all-day portability was a secondary concern. It assumed that player would be proximate to a power outlet, or willing to incorporate a substantial external battery pack into their kit.

The Legion Go’s detachable controllers even offered a superficial nod to the Switch’s modularity, but the device’s core identity was raw, translatable performance.

These machines made no attempt to build a new, bespoke software ecosystem. Their value was as a conduit, a window. They willingly conceded the traditional handheld’s foundational virtues of endurance and self-containment to win on the battlefield of graphical fidelity and total library size.

Their very existence was a declarative statement that the Switch’s and the Steam Deck’s compromises were just that—compromises—and that a measurable segment of the market would pay a premium to mitigate them, even if the cost was tethering the device closer to a backpack than to a jeans pocket.

Simultaneously, from the precise opposite direction, came a resurgence of nostalgia given exacting physical form. While the power-first handhelds gazed relentlessly toward the next graphical benchmark, companies like Analogue and Anbernic looked deliberately, meticulously backward. Their products fetishized specific moments frozen in the object-history of gaming.

The Analogue Pocket stood as the archetype. It was not a generic emulation machine; it was a forensic re-creation of the Game Boy form factor, executed in modern materials and centered on a custom screen technology designed to mimic the visual characteristics of an original liquid crystal display while eliminating its flaws. It accepted physical cartridges.

Its purpose was not to play thousands of games from dozens of systems through software emulation, but to play a specific library of games in a specific, authentic manner. It answered the question of what to give up by giving up everything except the original experience. It sacrificed breadth, modern online connectivity, and often affordability for a kind of tactile and visual purity. Anbernic’s broader array of devices performed a similar archival function for other legacy systems, from the Sega Game Gear to the PlayStation Portable. These were handhelds as time capsules, their engineering trade-offs focused not on maximizing performance or battery life, but on the accurate preservation of a particular feel and function from a specific past. This retro vector was a direct material reaction to the software-driven, service-oriented future that had consolidated in the previous decade.

As game libraries became increasingly digital and ephemeral—subject to the shuttering of online stores, the revocation of licenses, or the inevitable end of server support—the physical cartridge and a dedicated machine to play it on acquired a new aura of permanence and legitimacy.

The retro handheld was an argument against planned obsolescence. Its primary trade-off was irrelevance to the contemporary software market; it could not play the latest annual Call of Duty or receive a Fortnite update. In return, it offered a fixed point, a machine whose library and functionality were complete and knowable at the moment of purchase. It was the antithesis of the perpetually updating, network-dependent device.

This market segment thrived precisely because the mainstream had moved so decisively toward the ephemeral and the cloud-based. The pocket, therefore, was contested not only by competing visions of the future, but by meticulously crafted, tangible visions of the past.

Between these poles of raw, forward-looking power and curated, backward-looking nostalgia, the original pocket-sized computer—the smartphone—continued its own, parallel and internally fragmented evolution.

The mobile gaming landscape of 2024 was itself a layered study in divergent approaches. On one tier sat the free-to-play titles engineered for touchscreens, their core loops and economies built around brief sessions and microtransactions. On another tier operated subscription services like Netflix Games and Apple Arcade, which offered curated suites of premium, often original experiences for a monthly fee, attempting to construct a console-like library within the phone’s ecosystem.

On a third tier were the efforts to retrofit the phone with handheld legitimacy through hardware peripherals. Controllers from companies like Backbone and Razer, such as the Kishi, clipped onto smartphones, transforming them into facsimiles of a Switch or Steam Deck, their screens becoming the display for games streamed from cloud services like Xbox Game Pass Ultimate or PlayStation Plus Premium.

This approach proposed the ultimate trade-off: you gave up dedicated hardware entirely. The pocket was not for a gaming machine; the pocket was the gaming machine, provided you attached the correct accessories and maintained the necessary subscriptions. This mobile vector challenged the very definition of a handheld gaming device.

Was it a purpose-built object, or merely a contingent confluence of software and add-ons? The smartphone’s ubiquity and relentless hardware refresh cycle gave it a perpetual, inherent advantage in display technology and processing power over any dedicated device.

Its fatal compromise was control. Touchscreen interfaces were fundamentally inadequate for vast genres of traditional games, and the accessory solution was always a compromise—an additional item to carry for your primary device. Furthermore, the phone’s primary identity as a tool for communication and social connection meant gaming was forever competing for focus, storage, and battery life.

The mobile space demonstrated that the core engineering argument could be sidestepped entirely by leveraging an existing, universal platform, but in doing so, it often diluted the dedicated gaming experience into something secondary, something interstitial. It conquered the pocket by refusing to define a dedicated space for play within it, absorbing gaming as one function among many.

The causal mechanism for this explosion of simultaneous, contradictory answers was the definitive dissolution of a unified market paradigm.

The Nintendo Switch’s dominance from 2017 to 2021 had proven the commercial viability of the hybrid concept, but in doing so, it had also created a vast, installed base with a predictable set of expectations about software and form. The Steam Deck’s success shortly thereafter proved there was a substantial, hungry market for a different kind of portable performance, one tied to an open, pre-existing platform rather than a walled garden.

These twin pillars did not establish a new, singular orthodoxy; they shattered the old one. They demonstrated empirically that the term “handheld” could legitimately encompass two radically different hardware philosophies and that both could find robust commercial success.

This validation opened the floodgates. Without a single, overwhelming standard to compete against—like the Game Boy in the 1990s or the Nintendo DS in the 2000s—companies and niche manufacturers were liberated to pursue highly specific interpretations.

The historically high barriers to entry for creating a new, competitive software ecosystem remained, but the rise of the PC as a portable library and the enduring, monetizable appeal of retro game collections lowered them significantly. A manufacturer no longer needed to court and secure third-party developers to build a compelling product. It could leverage the entirety of Steam, or it could leverage the powerful allure of nostalgia.

This period of profound fragmentation and unresolved competition was, therefore, the logical terminus of the pocket wars’ historical arc.

The central engineering argument—what do you give up to make a machine that fits in a pocket—exploded into a dozen simultaneous, contradictory answers precisely because the definitions of both “machine” and “pocket” had themselves undergone a conceptual explosion. The pocket was a backpack compartment for the Asus ROG Ally player, a carefully preserved time capsule for the Analogue Pocket owner, and a smartphone holster for the mobile gamer. The machine was a scaled-down desktop computer, a faithful material replica, and a multipurpose glass slab running a subscription app.

The market could sustain these parallel truths because each served a distinct user’s hierarchy of needs, a different weighting of priorities: raw power versus true portability, nostalgia for a specific past versus demand for the contemporary, sheer convenience versus tangible commitment.

The landscape was one of vibrant, chaotic pluralism, but it was not one of stable equilibrium. The sheer diversity of viable options placed immense, novel pressure on the very concept of a mainstream handheld device.

Nintendo, observing this splintered field from Kyoto, faced its full complexity as it finalized its next move.

The successor to the Switch, released on June 5, 2025, entered a market that was structurally unrecognizable from the one its predecessor had first confronted in 2017. That earlier machine had faced a dormant handheld sector and two failing home consoles. The Nintendo Switch 2, which would sell more than 3.5 million units in its first week, arrived into an arena crowded with competent, aggressive alternatives representing the entire spectrum of possible trade-offs. Its hybrid doctrine was no longer a novel synthesis; it was one established option among many.

Its primary challenge was no longer to prove a concept, but to defend an existing territory in a war that had become multilateral.

The concrete consequence of this fragmentation was a market that could no longer be commanded, only segmented. The monopoly power of the late 2010s had not, as some feared, produced stagnation.

Instead, it had created the conditions for a fertile chaos, proving that the handheld space was large enough to support multiple winning formulas.

The unfinished machines of 2024 and 2025 were not prototypes awaiting a final, unifying vision. They were finished statements of divergent faiths, each complete in its own logic, each catering to a different congregation of players. Their simultaneous existence and commercial viability became the new, enduring condition of the craft.

The core engineering dilemma was therefore more alive and contested than it had been at any point since the early battles between the Game Boy and the Game Gear. There was no heir apparent to the throne, only a collection of claimants, each ruling a small but sustainable domain.

The next movement in the pocket wars would not be a search for a new, unifying answer, but a prolonged test of whether any single philosophy could ever again consolidate the field, or if the pocket’s future was destined to remain a permanent, unresolved state of pluralistic conflict.
