Chapter 30

The Argument That Never Ends (2024–Present)

From a vantage point that saw not individual warehouses but the flow of data itself, the global marketplace in 2024 presented as a vast and simultaneous inventory of resolved arguments. Supply-chain algorithms and digital storefronts rendered obsolete the notion of a single, seasonally dominant handheld. A query for “portable game console” returned a mosaic of competing truths.

The Nintendo Switch appeared, its blue and red Joy-Cons a universal glyph for hybrid play. Adjacent to it, the Steam Deck’s black chassis and twin trackpads signaled a different allegiance. Scrolling further revealed the brushed aluminum of an Analogue Pocket, promising fidelity to the past, and the stark, canary-yellow cube of a Playdate with its side-mounted crank. Their prices formed a rough ladder, but their underlying philosophies were incommensurate, each a complete and internally consistent answer to a question that had once been fought over in binary terms.

This was no longer a shelf but a spectrum. The core engineering dispute—what one must surrender to craft a machine for the pocket—had not been answered. It had atomized.

To descend from this aerial view of simultaneous availability was to witness a consumer’s quiet paralysis, the cursor hovering not from a lack of options but from an overabundance of coherent, contradictory covenants.

This pluralism was the direct outcome of the settled condition left by the preceding era of consolidation. The rule established was that supremacy belonged to the platform that could cultivate the deepest software garden. By December 2024, the Nintendo Switch, having shipped over 150 million units, stood as a monument to that rule’s effectiveness. Its persistence was the central, gravitational fact of the landscape.

The Switch Lite and the OLED model were variations on a proven theme, refinements of a covenant that traded some raw power and effortless pocketability for seamless transition between domestic contexts and access to an exclusive, vast ecosystem. The mainstream was now a settled country.

Its dominance, however, did not suppress alternative doctrines. Instead, it defined the prohibitive cost of a direct assault.

To challenge the Switch on its own terms required a software library of comparable depth, a barrier so high it deflected competitive energy outward, into the fertile periphery. The result was not a new war for the center, but a permanent and simultaneous fragmentation around it.

One flank of this fragmentation championed raw power and platform openness, a philosophy that approached the pocket not as a constraint to be honored but as a challenge to be overwhelmed. The standard-bearer was the Steam Deck, released by Valve in 2022, and its proliferating cohort of devices from Ayaneo, GPD, and OneXPlayer. These were not handheld consoles in a traditional sense but portable personal computers. Their resolution of the portability trilemma was explicit: they sacrificed compact form, battery life, and often cost to deliver desktop-grade performance in a graspable frame. A Steam Deck might last three hours under the load of a modern title, its fan audibly expelling the heat generated by its accelerated processor. It was bulkier than any mainstream handheld since the Atari Lynx.

Its covenant was the antithesis of a walled garden; it granted access to a user’s entire Steam library and, by virtue of its PC architecture, the freedom to install any software, from competing storefronts to emulators. This was the “console-first” ideal pushed to its logical extreme—parity not with a specific home console, but with a user’s own gaming PC. The cost was absolute.

These devices did not fit in a pocket; they demanded a carrying case, a frequent connection to power, and a mindset that treated battery life as a managed resource. Valve’s hardware team had not invented a new platform so much as they had successfully repackaged an existing one, deliberately re-opening the distinction between handheld and console that the Switch had sought to dissolve. Their commercial viability proved a sustainable market existed for users who prioritized computational power and software openness above all other concerns.

A parallel line of devices moved in the opposite direction, towards radical focus and nostalgic purity. Here, the sacrifice was not power but contemporary relevance.

The Analogue Pocket, with its milled aluminum shell and precise glass screen, rejected software emulation. It employed field-programmable gate array hardware to mimic the original circuitry of the Game Boy, Game Gear, and Neo Geo Pocket. It offered a flawless, lag-free recreation of decades-old games, but it played only those games. Its covenant was one of preservation and reverence, treating old cartridges as enduring artifacts rather than obsolete media. It asked the user to forsake the new for a perfect experience of the old.

Alongside it, a host of dedicated retro handhelds from companies like Anbernic and Miyoo, often running open-source Linux software, provided tiny, inexpensive portals to emulated libraries of the 8-bit and 16-bit eras. They resolved the trilemma by operating entirely outside contemporary performance demands, achieving remarkable battery life and genuine pocketability by inhabiting a technological past. Their existence was a direct consequence of democratized technology; the chips inside were cheap and commodified, the software was frequently open-source, and global online marketplaces handled their distribution. They answered not “what can we fit in a pocket?”

but “which past do you wish to carry?”
At the furthest edge of this spectrum lay the curiosities, devices that proposed a pocket machine could be the vehicle for a single, defining idea. The Playdate was the starkest example. This small, yellow handheld with a black-and-white screen featured a mechanical crank on its side. It was a device built around a gimmick, pursued with such earnest and creative commitment that it constituted a niche. Its seasonal library of games was designed exclusively for its unique interface. Its covenant was one of curated surprise and tactile novelty. It sacrificed everything—color, broad third-party support, mainstream appeal—for the sake of a singular design statement. It demonstrated that in a fragmented market, a niche could be an artistic premise as much as a business model.

The simultaneous coexistence of the mainstream hybrid, the power-hungry PC portable, the retro-focused purist, and the experimental artifact represented the ultimate legacy and judgment of the engineering argument that had defined the genre for over three decades. The argument had not been won.

It had simply exhausted the possibility of a singular arena. The historical cycle of conflict and correction—the Game Boy’s battery life against the Game Gear’s color, the GBA’s absent backlight against the SP’s clamshell solution—had once moved toward a temporary consensus before the next challenger arose. That process had now accelerated into a state of permanent simultaneity. Every prior correction was now a coexisting product line.

The causal mechanism was the comprehensive democratization of technology and distribution. The barriers to creating a dedicated gaming handheld had collapsed. System-on-a-chip designs were commodities. Small-scale manufacturing and global logistics were accessible to firms of any size. Digital storefronts and crowdfunding platforms provided direct routes to a global audience.

Consequently, a hardware philosophy no longer needed to defeat its rivals to survive; it merely needed to find and sustain its own congregation of adherents. Within this enduring pluralism, the human figures of the past could be assessed not as prophets of a single true path, but as architects of a debate that had outgrown any individual vision.

Gunpei Yokoi’s “lateral thinking with withered technology” lived on in the Playdate’s constrained charm and the efficient nostalgia of a retro handheld. Ken Kutaragi’s relentless drive for power and multimedia convergence found its heir in the cooling fans of a Steam Deck. Satoru Iwata’s software-centric platform strategy remained the bedrock of the Switch’s continued reign. Their doctrines were not obsolete; they were all valid, each materially manifest in a different quadrant of the contemporary landscape.

They had not been fighting to determine the one correct answer. They had been contributing distinct tools, constraints, and philosophies to a collective, global workshop. The history of handheld gaming revealed itself not as a linear march toward a definitive portable form, but as the gradual accumulation of viable, parallel alternatives.

The most compelling counter-explanation to this reading remained brute-force economics: that platform ecosystems and lock-in, not philosophical design conflicts, determined success. This was indisputably true for dominance at the scale of the Switch. Its library, its developer relationships, and its brand loyalty were the formidable walls protecting its kingdom.

Yet the economic reality of 2024 cut in two directions. The same globalized, democratized supply chains that allowed Nintendo to manufacture tens of millions of Switches also enabled a small team to produce tens of thousands of Analogue Pockets or Ayaneo devices. Platform lock-in was a moat for the giant, but the ocean of global commerce had risen so high that countless smaller islands could thrive above the waterline. The market could now sustain multiple, parallel definitions of economic viability. A device selling a few hundred thousand units was not a failure if its costs were calibrated to that scale.

In a crowded field, design philosophy had become the primary product differentiator. The economics of monolithic scale had fractured into the economics of sustainable niches. This fragmentation was not a static equilibrium but a state of perpetual, low-intensity revelation. Each new device that reached the market constituted a fresh hypothesis about player desire, a materialized answer to the eternal question.

This democratized landscape, however, was not born in a vacuum. It was the direct, material consequence of a technological plateau that had transformed high-performance components into affordable commodities. The system-on-a-chip (SoC) architectures that powered smartphones and tablets had become so powerful, efficient, and—crucially—so readily available through global semiconductor supply chains that they offered a ready-made foundation. A company like Ayaneo or Anbernic did not need to design a processor from scratch; it could select from a menu of proven, mass-produced AMD or ARM chipsets and build a chassis around them. This commoditization of core technology lowered the engineering barrier from a monumental R&D undertaking to a sophisticated integration challenge.

The result was a Cambrian explosion of form factors, each built upon a similar technological substrate but expressing a radically different interpretation of its potential. The same basic silicon that could be tuned for the silent, fanless efficiency of a retro emulation device could, with a more aggressive thermal solution, be pushed to deliver PC-grade performance in a handheld shell. The argument had shifted from what was possible to what was prioritized.

This priority was now expressed through a device’s covenant—the implicit promise it made to its user about the nature of the experience.

The Steam Deck’s covenant was one of uncompromised access and ownership. It treated the player as a power user, granting administrative privileges over the hardware itself. This stood in stark contrast to the Nintendo Switch’s covenant of curated convenience and exclusive worlds, a promise that had been honed over decades of first-party software mastery.

The Analogue Pocket’s covenant was one of faithful stewardship, treating gaming history as a tangible heritage. Each covenant created its own gravitational field, attracting a community defined by shared values as much as by shared software libraries. Online forums and subreddits for each device type became not merely tech support hubs, but ideological echo chambers where the virtues of one philosophy were passionately defended against the perceived compromises of others. The consumer’s paralysis was not merely a choice between devices, but between identities—between being a tinkerer, a nostalgist, a mainstream adventurer, or an avant-garde experimentalist.

Within this ecosystem, the legacy of past innovators became not a blueprint to follow, but a lexicon to borrow from. The Playdate’s radical focus on a single, novel interface channeled the spirit of Gunpei Yokoi’s “lateral thinking,” applying withered technology—in this case, a monochrome screen and a simple crank—to evoke new forms of play rather than to cut costs. The relentless iteration on the PC handheld form factor, with each new model boasting marginal gains in thermal performance or screen quality, echoed Ken Kutaragi’s relentless pursuit of technical supremacy, albeit divorced from the need to anchor an entire proprietary platform.

Satoru Iwata’s software-centric ethos, which viewed hardware as a vessel designed solely to deliver compelling games, found its purest modern expression not only in the Switch’s first-party titles but in the way niche devices like the Playdate or the Analogue Pocket were launched with meticulously curated software experiences or perfect compatibility. Their philosophies were no longer corporate mandates but available tools in a shared kit.

The economic underpinning of this pluralism was a redefinition of viability itself. For decades, the handheld business was a winner-take-most arena where selling tens of millions of units was the only metric of survival. The new landscape operated on a logic of scalable sustainability. A successful product could be one that moved 100, 000 units directly to a passionate audience via a crowdfunding campaign, with production costs calibrated to that precise scale. Digital storefronts like Steam and Itch. io provided global distribution for software without the need for physical cartridges or costly retail partnerships, further lowering the barriers for niche hardware. This allowed companies to serve audiences that were infinitesimal by Nintendo’s standards but were large enough to form a viable business. The market did not consolidate because the cost of entry had fallen below the revenue potential of these micro-audiences.

Some, like the legion of PC handhelds, competed fiercely on nearly identical specifications—screen refresh rates, processor tiers, RAM allocations—within the same broad philosophical church.

Others sought to define new territory entirely. The landscape was now surveyed not by a handful of corporate generals, but by a scattered cohort of designers, engineers, and entrepreneurs, all reading the same history and drawing divergent conclusions. The argument had escaped the conference rooms of Kyoto, Redmond, and Tokyo and permeated the global workshop.

Tom Kalinske, the former Sega of America CEO, had once described the genesis of the 32X as a stopgap measure born from a disagreement between regional offices on timing, a compromise that attempted to bridge a strategic rift. The contemporary landscape was the final form of that kind of disagreement, institutionalized and made permanent, not as a compromise but as a diaspora of uncompromising positions. The final verdict on the endless nature of the argument was thus embedded in the marketplace’s very structure. There would be no final pocket. The cycle would not conclude.

The pressure to choose between power, portability, and price would forever be resolved in a dozen different ways, each resulting in a device that was, for its intended audience, the correct answer. The handheld would persist as a uniquely personal object, a companion shaped by individual preference rather than by industry decree. The wars of the pocket had not concluded with a peace treaty, but with a proliferation of sovereign states, each minting its own currency of play. The cursor hovering on the screen, suspended between thumbnails that each contained a world, was the inheritor of every past conflict. It held the power to validate, with a simple click, one philosophy over all the others, a act of choice that would be repeated, without finality, for as long as there were pockets to fill.
