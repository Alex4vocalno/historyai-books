Chapter 22

The Constant in a Falling Apple

The most unsettling moment in the measurement of gravity arrived not with a failed experiment, but with a perfect one. For over a century, the great project had been to find the number—the single, universal constant that governed the attraction between all masses. By the dawn of the twentieth century, that number had been cornered. In laboratory after laboratory, using balances of exquisite delicacy, physicists could measure the gravitational pull between lead spheres with a precision that would have stunned Newton.

They had captured the force, quantified it, and written it into the cornerstone of cosmic law. And this very success produced the first major crisis of modern gravity. The crisis was this: they now had an exquisitely precise number for which they had no explanation whatsoever. The constant was measured, but it was not understood. It was a fact in search of a theory, an arbitrary knob on the universe’s machinery whose position could be read with staggering accuracy, but whose reason for being there was a complete mystery.

This was a profound shift in the nature of physical inquiry. Newton’s law of universal gravitation had been a triumph of unification. It declared that the force pulling an apple to the ground was the same force holding the moon in its orbit, all governed by a simple mathematical relationship. The gravitational constant, G, was the linchpin of that relationship. It was the fixed number that translated the masses of objects and the distance between them into a specific, predictable force. To measure G was to complete the Newtonian edifice, to move from a qualitative principle to a quantitative, predictive science.

Henry Cavendish’s 1797 experiment with the torsion balance had been the first great step. By measuring the tiny twist in a wire caused by the gravitational attraction between lead balls, he effectively “weighed the Earth,” determining its density and, by implication, the strength of gravity’s grip. His work was refined throughout the nineteenth century. The number was pinned down, digit by painstaking digit. Yet this empirical completeness masked a conceptual poverty.

Physics could tell you that gravity had a certain strength, but it could not tell you why it had that strength and not another. The constant G simply appeared in Newton’s equation as a given. It was not derived from any deeper principle about the nature of space, time, or matter. It was an input, not an output. This stood in stark contrast to other fundamental constants that were emerging at the time. Consider the speed of light, c. By the late nineteenth century, this was not merely a measured velocity of electromagnetic waves.

It fell out naturally from the elegant theoretical framework of James Clerk Maxwell’s equations for electricity and magnetism. The speed of light was woven into the very fabric of those equations; it was a consequence of the relationship between the permittivity and permeability of the vacuum. An option for deriving c that does not directly depend on a measurement of the propagation of electromagnetic waves is to use the relation between c and the vacuum permittivity ε₀ and vacuum permeability μ₀ established by Maxwell’s theory: c² = 1/(ε₀μ₀).

The constant c had a theoretical home. It made sense within a larger story. The gravitational constant G had no such home. It was a lonely number. You could not derive it from the properties of empty space or from some more fundamental symmetry. You could only find it by going into a laboratory, hanging up some lead balls, and measuring their whisper-soft attraction. Its value—approximately 6.

674 × 10⁻¹¹ cubic meters per kilogram per second squared—was a brute fact of the universe. This realization, dawning around the turn of the twentieth century, marked a crucial turn. Gravity, the force humanity knew best from daily life, was revealing itself as the force understood least at its foundation. The Newtonian “mask”—the intuitive picture of a force acting instantly across distance—had enabled phenomenal predictive power. It had guided spacecraft and mapped galaxies.

But the mask was now hiding something: the utter arbitrariness of gravity’s strength. The mask worked so well that for centuries no one thought to ask what lay behind its fixed, numerical expression. Success had created a constraint.

The very act of perfecting the measurement of G exposed the question the theory was never built to answer. This crisis of the constant intersected with a deeper revolution in physics. While experimenters were refining the value of G, theorists like Einstein were dismantling the Newtonian stage upon which it acted.

Special relativity, published in 1905, unified space and time into a single spacetime and established the speed of light as a universal speed limit. This new framework immediately clashed with Newton’s gravity, which implied instantaneous action at a distance—a concept incompatible with relativity’s finite cosmic speed limit. Einstein spent the next decade wrestling gravity into a new form.

His general theory of relativity, completed in 1915, replaced the idea of a force with the geometry of spacetime. Mass and energy told spacetime how to curve; curved spacetime told objects how to move. “Falling” was simply following the straightest possible path through a warped geometry. In this breathtaking new vision, what became of Newton’s constant G? It was absorbed, but not explained.

It reappeared in Einstein’s field equations, still as a fundamental coupling constant. It still set the scale of gravity’s effect. In Newton’s equation, G told you how strong the force was for given masses. In Einstein’s equation, it told you how much curvature a given amount of mass-energy would produce. Its role had been geometrized, but its origin remained just as opaque. General relativity provided a dazzling new mechanism for gravity—the bending of spacetime—but it did not derive the strength of the bending from first principles.

The arbitrary knob was still there; it had just been relocated to a more fundamental control panel. This presented a peculiar and persistent problem. A theory that purports to be fundamental should, in principle, allow you to calculate the values of its constants. They should emerge from the theory’s logic, not be inserted by hand based on experiment. The fact that G had to be measured in a lab and plugged into Einstein’s equations meant that general relativity, for all its profundity, was not a complete, self-contained theory of gravity.

It was a theory of how gravity behaves, given its strength. The “why” of that strength was left outside, in the realm of empirical fact. This is the foundational loop hinted at earlier: testing a fundamental theory often requires assuming an older one is approximately true. To measure G with a torsion balance, you assume Newton’s laws of motion and his inverse-square law are roughly correct to interpret the twist of the wire. You use the old theory to calibrate the tool that tests the new framework which supersedes it.

The constant slithers through this loop, its value confirmed ever more precisely, its meaning receding ever further from view. The mystery of G’s value is not a minor technicality. It is a direct pointer to the limits of our understanding. Why is gravity so incredibly weak? This is the constant’s most startling feature. To feel its weakness, consider that the entire mass of the Earth, concentrated beneath your feet, holds you to the ground. Yet you can overcome that planetary pull with the minor effort of jumping up.

A small fridge magnet can defeat the gravitational pull of the entire planet, lifting a paperclip against Earth’s tug. The magnet’s electromagnetic force is astronomically stronger than gravity’s grip. Translated into the constant, this means G is a vanishingly small number. If it were even slightly larger, the universe would have collapsed upon itself instantly after the Big Bang. If it were slightly smaller, stars and galaxies could never have coalesced from the primordial gas. Our existence seems to balance on a knife-edge set by this number.

Yet we have no theory that tells us why the knife-edge is where it is. This arbitrariness is what makes G a driver for new physics. It is an unanswered question that refuses to go away. Throughout the twentieth century, as general relativity was confirmed again and again—by the bending of starlight, the shift of Mercury’s orbit, the slowing of clocks in gravity—the question of G’s origin persisted. It became the quiet, unresolved bass note beneath the fanfare of success.

The constant was the piece of the Newtonian world that Einstein’s revolution had not revolutionized. It was a relic, a parameter that future theories would have to explain. That future collision is where the geometry mask of general relativity begins to fray at the edges. The mask presents gravity as curvature. But what if the source of that curvature is not just matter and energy as we see them, but something hidden in the vacuum of space itself? This brings us to the most dramatic confrontation between measurement and theory: the cosmological constant.

When Einstein first formulated his equations, he found they described a dynamic universe that should either expand or contract. Believing, like most of his contemporaries, in a static universe, he introduced an extra term—the cosmological constant, denoted by the Greek letter lambda (Λ)—to act as a repulsive force that would perfectly balance the attractive pull of gravity, holding the cosmos in equilibrium. He later called this his “greatest blunder” after the discovery of the expanding universe made it seem unnecessary.

But lambda has made a spectacular comeback. Modern observations show the universe’s expansion is not slowing down but accelerating. The best explanation for this acceleration is a form of energy inherent to empty space—dark energy. Mathematically, this behaves exactly like Einstein’s cosmological constant. It is a constant energy density of the vacuum. And here, the problem of constants reaches a fever pitch. Quantum field theory, our tremendously successful framework for describing particles and forces, suggests that the vacuum should be seething with a tremendous amount of energy arising from fleeting quantum fluctuations. This is not speculative; it is a standard calculation.

But when you calculate how much this vacuum energy should curve spacetime—when you plug it into Einstein’s equations as a source for lambda—you get a number that is wrong by a factor of 10¹²⁰. That is a 1 followed by 120 zeros. It is the worst mismatch between theory and observation in the history of science. This is not a small error. It is a chasm.

The cosmological constant problem reveals that the “geometry mask” of general relativity might be hiding the true source of cosmic dynamics in the vacuum itself. The theory beautifully describes how spacetime curves in response to energy.

But when we use our best theory for energy—quantum theory—to calculate what should be there, we get an answer that makes no sense. The predicted curvature from quantum vacuum energy would have torn the universe apart instantaneously. The fact that we observe a universe that is, in comparison, gently and slowly accelerating means that the actual value of lambda is unimaginably fine-tuned to almost, but not quite, cancel out this monstrous quantum prediction.

We are back to a problem of constants: why is lambda so small? Or, equivalently, why is the gravitational constant G so small that it makes gravity weak enough for this delicate cancellation to even be possible? The two constants, G and lambda, are now intertwined in the greatest puzzle of fundamental physics. Their values are not explained by general relativity or by the Standard Model of particle physics.

They are inputs. And their precise, seemingly orchestrated values suggest they are clues to a deeper theory that unifies quantum mechanics and gravity—a theory of quantum gravity. In such a theory, constants like G would not be arbitrary. They might be determined by the geometry of tiny, curled-up extra dimensions, or emerge from the dynamics of fundamental strings, or be the outcome of a vast landscape of possible universes. We do not yet know.

But the relentless, frustrating precision of our measurements—from Cavendish’s torsion balance to the satellite maps of the cosmic microwave background—has boxed the problem in with exquisite clarity. We know the numbers with fantastic accuracy. Their mystery is now unavoidable. The history of gravity is often told as a story of cumulative progress, where each model is rendered obsolete by a more accurate one. There is truth in this, but it is not the whole truth. It is not a simple, asymptotic march toward a final answer. It is a story of successive masks.

Each new mask—Aristotle’s tendencies, Newton’s force, Einstein’s geometry—grants a new and powerful way of seeing, of predicting, of controlling. But each mask also hides new and more fundamental questions. The Newtonian mask hid the arbitrariness of G behind its predictive triumph. The geometric mask of general relativity hides the vacuum energy crisis behind its elegant description of orbits and black holes. The masks are not lies; they are tools. And like all tools, they are defined by what they allow you to do and what they prevent you from seeing.

The moment a tool works perfectly is often the moment you must look for the next one, because its very perfection illuminates the contours of its limitations. Thus, the constant in the falling apple, once a symbol of universal law, became a symbol of fundamental mystery. It is a measured fact that refuses to be mere fact. It sits at the boundary between the known and the unknown, a precise numerical monument to all the physics we have yet to invent.

The pressure it creates is not vague or philosophical. It is concrete. Every ultra-precise measurement of G, every observation of an accelerating galaxy, tightens the vise. It forces the question: what kind of theory can explain not just how gravity works, but why it works with this specific, fragile strength? The answer will require not just a new equation, but a new way of seeing—a new mask that will, inevitably, reveal its own hidden frontiers. This leaves physics in a position of tense expectation.

The unexplained constant is more than a gap; it is an active instability in our understanding. Gravity’s precise strength remains an arbitrary input, a keystone borrowed from a older architecture and lodged into a new one. A theory that cannot derive its own constants is, in a deep sense, incomplete. It is a recipe that requires you to bring your own most crucial ingredient. The next movement in the story, therefore, cannot be another refinement of measurement or another confirmation of geometry.

The pressure of the arbitrary knob demands a theory that can turn it—a theory that can tell us why, when we measure the pull between two lead spheres in a quiet room, the universe always gives the same, inexplicable answer.
