Chapter 9

The Director’s Hour of Reckoning

The telegram from Philadelphia General Hospital arrived without ceremony on the morning of October 1, 1918, no different in its physical form from the routine communications that crossed Wilmer Krusen’s desk each day. The paper bore the hospital superintendent’s signature and a timestamp of 8:47 a.m. Its message, preserved in the hospital’s correspondence file, reported that influenza admissions had more than doubled in forty-eight hours. The hospital’s thousand beds were occupied to capacity. Medical staff were falling ill at a rate that would, within the week, incapacitate nearly a quarter of the physicians on duty. The telegram ended with a request for instructions and assistance that its sender already knew could not be answered from existing resources.

Krusen read the message in his office on the fourth floor of the Municipal Building, where marble corridors designed to convey permanence now channeled clerks carrying papers between departments that had not yet adjusted their rhythms to emergency. The director was fifty-three years old, a physician trained in an era when public health meant quarantine stations and sanitary inspection, not mass casualty events. His appointment book, examined later by investigators, showed the notation he had made the previous evening: a visit to Pennsylvania Hospital scheduled for 9:00 a.m. On October 2. The notation suggested a man still operating within categories of normal administrative practice, still believing that direct observation of clinical conditions could substitute for systemic response.

Why this telegram, and why now? The Bureau of Health had received alarming communications before. Throughout the final week of September, individual physicians had reported unusual clusters of severe respiratory illness. The Navy Yard’s medical officer had documented the spread among sailors with precision that should have triggered broader alarm. The mortality returns for the last week of September, compiled from death certificates filed with the city registrar, showed an uptick that exceeded seasonal variation. Yet Krusen had permitted the Liberty Loan parade to proceed on September 28, a day chosen for the launch of the Fourth Liberty Loan offering $6.9 billion in bonds at 4.25 percent. He had stood among the dignitaries on the reviewing platform as 200, 000 Philadelphians pressed into the downtown streets, had accepted the evening’s newspaper coverage that described the event as “a great day in Philadelphia” and “the first premonition of victory.”

The difference on October 1 was quantitative and therefore qualitative. Earlier reports had described threatening possibilities. The Philadelphia General Hospital telegram described an institutional rupture. A hospital of that size, with its century-old mission to serve the city’s indigent sick, did not send emergency appeals without cause. The superintendent had calculated the ratio of incoming patients to available staff and found it approaching collapse. The telegram represented not medical opinion but arithmetic.

Krusen’s response, reconstructed from the fragmentary minutes of Bureau meetings and his subsequent correspondence with Mayor Thomas B. Smith, proceeded through stages that revealed the constraints upon his authority. First, he requested updated mortality figures from the city registrar, a delay that consumed several hours while clerks compiled certificates filed through September 30. The returns showed 118 deaths attributed to influenza and pneumonia in the twenty-four hours following the parade. The number was already higher than any daily count in the city’s recorded history for those causes, and it represented only the first harvest of infections contracted during the mass gathering.

Second, Krusen consulted with his medical inspectors, the field staff who investigated reported cases and enforced isolation orders. Their oral reports, not preserved in written form, confirmed that the epidemic had spread beyond the Navy Yard and its immediate surroundings into residential neighborhoods across the city. The inspectors described households where multiple members had fallen ill simultaneously, where the absence of healthy adults had left children untended, where the standard protocol of home isolation had become meaningless because no healthy caregiver remained.

Third, and only after these confirmations, Krusen requested an appointment with the mayor. The meeting occurred at 4:00 p.m. On October 1, in Smith’s office on the second floor of City Hall. The mayor, sixty-two years old and serving his second term, had built his political career on the efficient administration of a city whose population had grown by nearly a quarter in the preceding decade. His support for the Liberty Loan parade had been enthusiastic; the city’s quota of $259 million in war bonds required visible demonstrations of civic commitment. The surviving record of their conversation consists of a brief notation in the mayor’s appointment book and a longer memorandum Krusen prepared the following morning for his own files.

Krusen presented the mortality figures and the hospital superintendent’s appeal. He noted that the Visiting Nurse Association, a private organization that provided home care to the poor, had reported its staff overwhelmed by requests for assistance. The association’s director had informed Krusen that her nurses were making choices about which patients to visit based on likelihood of survival rather than order of request. The moral collapse that precedes institutional collapse had begun: triage protocols abandoned in favor of improvised selection.

Smith’s response balanced political calculation against the evidence before him. The mayor had supported the parade; reversing that support now would require acknowledging error. Yet the numbers Krusen presented were not subject to interpretation. The two men agreed that the situation required formal action, but they disagreed, according to Krusen’s memorandum, about the scope and timing of that action. Smith favored targeted measures: additional hospital capacity, perhaps, and public warnings about hygiene. Krusen argued for broader intervention, though his memorandum suggests he stopped short of recommending complete closure of public gathering places. The meeting ended with a decision to reconvene on October 2 with representatives from the hospitals and the Visiting Nurse Association, and with instructions to Krusen to prepare draft orders for the mayor’s review.

The transition the chapter must trace ran from wartime publicist to crisis manager, from the man who had declared the parade safe to the man who would, within forty-eight hours, close the city’s schools and churches. The transformation was reluctant because it required renouncing the framework of normalcy that had governed Krusen’s previous decisions. That framework had not been irrational. It reflected the genuine priorities of a nation at war, in which the maintenance of civilian morale and the fulfillment of financial obligations to the federal government ranked alongside, and sometimes above, the control of infectious disease. Krusen’s earlier optimism had been structurally induced, not personally foolish.

The night of October 1 brought no respite. Hospital admissions continued at the accelerated pace the superintendent had reported. Physicians working emergency shifts described patients who arrived with mild symptoms and progressed, within hours, to cyanosis and respiratory failure. The clinical course of the disease, which would later be analyzed in medical journals, compressed the ordinary timeline of influenza pneumonia into a single day or night. The speed of deterioration meant that hospital admission was often followed almost immediately by death, creating a psychological shock among medical staff that impaired their capacity for systematic response.

At 9:00 a.m. On October 2, Krusen kept his appointment at Pennsylvania Hospital. The visit was characteristic of his method: direct observation before formal decision. The hospital, founded in 1751 and occupying a city block at Eighth and Spruce Streets, was older than the nation and had survived previous epidemics through adaptations that its administrators now attempted to replicate. Krusen toured the medical wards, spoke with the attending physicians, and examined the emergency arrangements for receiving additional patients. The hospital’s president, Dr. J.M. Anders, provided figures that confirmed the pattern at Philadelphia General: admissions doubling every two days, staff falling ill at rates that would soon force the closure of entire departments.

The visit lasted two hours. Krusen returned to the Municipal Building with notes that he incorporated into a revised memorandum for the mayor. The memorandum, preserved in the city archives, marked a shift in rhetorical register. Where his previous communications had emphasized control and confidence, this document acknowledged an emergency of unprecedented character and recommended immediate and comprehensive measures to prevent the further spread of infection. The specific measures were listed in order of escalating severity: mandatory reporting of all cases, prohibition of public funerals, closure of schools, closure of theaters and places of amusement, and finally such other restrictions upon public assembly as the situation might require.

Why this progression? The ordering reflected Krusen’s assessment of political feasibility as much as medical necessity. Schools could be closed without immediate economic consequence; theaters and amusements affected commercial interests that would resist. The other restrictions remained undefined, a placeholder for measures that Krusen was not yet prepared to name. The memorandum also proposed the establishment of emergency hospitals in buildings not ordinarily used for medical care, a recognition that the existing institutional capacity would soon be exhausted.

The second meeting with Mayor Smith occurred at 3:00 p.m. On October 2. It included, by Krusen’s request, representatives from the city’s major hospitals and from the Visiting Nurse Association. The hospital superintendents presented unified testimony: their institutions were approaching the limit of safe operation, and without immediate reduction in new admissions, they would be forced to turn away patients regardless of medical need. The visiting nurse director described a situation in the residential districts that paralleled the institutional crisis. Her staff of 126 nurses had made 1, 847 home visits in the preceding week, an increase of 340 percent over the weekly average. She reported that twenty-three nurses had themselves fallen ill, and that the remaining staff were working sixteen-hour days with no prospect of relief.

The presence of these witnesses transformed the conversation. Where Krusen had presented data, they presented human consequence. The mayor, whose political sensitivity had been shaped by decades of ward-level politics, could not dismiss testimony from institutions and individuals with established standing in the community. The meeting concluded with Smith’s authorization to proceed with the closure orders, though the specific text would require further refinement and the effective date remained under discussion.

The decisive turn came when administrative process, however delayed, began to move faster than the epidemic’s acceleration. The seventy-two hours from October 1 to October 3 represented the lag time between the recognition of crisis and the implementation of response. The lag was not unique to Philadelphia. Similar intervals have been documented in other American cities during the same weeks, as health officials struggled to overcome the inertia of normal governance. What distinguished Philadelphia was the magnitude of the gathering that had occurred during the lag, the 200, 000 citizens exposed to infection at a moment when the epidemic’s potential was already visible to informed observers.

While Krusen and Smith negotiated the terms of official response, a different form of inquiry was proceeding in the laboratory of the Henry Phipps Institute at the University of Pennsylvania. Dr. Paul Lewis, thirty-four years old and recently returned from military service, had begun the scientific investigation that would run parallel to, and eventually be overwhelmed by, the clinical catastrophe. Lewis was a specialist in pathology and bacteriology, trained at the University of Wisconsin and at the Rockefeller Institute, who had spent the preceding year studying respiratory infections at Camp Pike in Arkansas. His return to Philadelphia in late September placed him at the center of the epidemic’s scientific investigation.

On October 1 and 2, Lewis conducted the autopsies that would provide the first definitive evidence of the disease’s pathology. Working in the Phipps Institute’s laboratory, he examined tissue samples from victims who had died at Philadelphia General Hospital and at the Navy Yard. His findings, published later in the Journal of the American Medical Association, described a form of pneumonia that differed from the bacterial pneumonias ordinarily associated with influenza. The lungs showed extensive hemorrhage and edema; microscopic examination revealed an inflammatory process that destroyed alveolar tissue with unprecedented speed. Lewis’s work established that the epidemic was not simply an unusually severe outbreak of familiar disease but a distinct clinical entity, though the viral agent responsible would not be identified for another fifteen years.

The scientific track and the administrative track proceeded on different timelines with different standards of evidence. Lewis could not issue a closure order; Krusen could not culture a pathogen. The parallel inquiries created a characteristic tension of public health emergencies, in which the need for immediate action conflicts with the requirements of certain knowledge. Krusen was aware of Lewis’s work—the two men met on October 2, according to Lewis’s laboratory notes—but the meeting produced no change in administrative policy. The pathology of the disease, however precisely described, did not alter the available interventions. The closure orders would be the same regardless of whether the causative agent was bacterial, viral, or unknown.

The drafting of the closure orders occupied the evening of October 2 and the morning of October 3. Krusen worked with the city’s law department to ensure that the language would survive legal challenge, and with the mayor’s office to coordinate the public announcement. The orders as finally issued were comprehensive in scope but precise in detail. Schools were to close immediately and remain closed until further notice. Churches were prohibited from holding services, though private devotions were not restricted. Theaters, moving picture houses, dance halls, and other places of public amusement were ordered closed. Public funerals were banned; private burials were limited to immediate family. Saloons and pool rooms, whose closure had been debated, were permitted to remain open with restrictions on crowding—a concession to political pressure that would later be criticized as a fatal compromise.

The orders were published in the morning newspapers of October 3 and posted in public buildings throughout the city. The Philadelphia Inquirer carried the full text on its front page, beneath a headline that described the measures as precautionary and emphasized their temporary character. The newspaper’s editorial voice, which had celebrated the Liberty Loan parade five days earlier, now supported the closures as necessary for the public welfare. The shift in editorial position was not acknowledged as such; the newspaper treated the epidemic as a new development rather than a consequence of decisions already made.

The implementation of the orders revealed the gap between administrative intention and institutional capacity. School principals received telephoned instructions to dismiss their students at noon on October 3; many had already observed significant absenteeism, as parents kept children home from fear of infection. Theater managers, informed by police officers carrying printed notices, protested the loss of revenue but complied without legal challenge. Church leaders, consulted through informal channels before the public announcement, raised constitutional objections that were overridden by the mayor’s insistence on emergency authority.

The most immediate effect of the orders was symbolic rather than epidemiological. They announced that the city had entered a condition of official crisis, that the framework of normal civic life had been suspended. This announcement came, by the mortality returns for October 3, after the epidemic had already infected tens of thousands of Philadelphians. The closures could not prevent cases that were already incubating; they could only reduce the rate of new exposure. The measure of their success or failure would become visible only in the mortality figures for the following weeks, when the city would experience the deadliest period of its history.

The published closure order and its stark list of shuttered institutions created a new reality: a city officially in crisis, with thousands already beyond help.
