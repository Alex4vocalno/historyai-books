Chapter 18

The Silence in the Telegraph Office

Seen from above as November arrived—across the whole map—the machinery of Philadelphia did not stop; it merely changed its rhythm, like a factory that has burned through its raw stock and now waits for new material to arrive, the belts still turning, the gears catching on air. The epidemic’s violent peak had passed. Its systems of care and burial remained compromised. Its population had diminished by more than twelve thousand dead. Yet the city continued to function, not through recovery but through the momentum of necessity, the accumulated weight of obligations that death could not suspend indefinitely.

In the main telegraph office on Chestnut Street, the afternoon shift of October 28 began with an observation that no operator could have articulated precisely but that each felt in the changed resistance of the keys beneath their fingers. The frantic traffic of mid-October—WIRE COFFINS IMMEDIATELY, PHYSICIANS URGENTLY NEEDED, HOSPITAL FULL SEND ELSEWHERE—had thinned to something else. The messages still came, but they had grown shorter, more formal, as if the senders had exhausted their supply of adjectives along with their supply of hope. A death notice to a family in Pittsburgh. A business inquiry to a supplier in New York: WHEN RESUME SHIPMENTS STOP. A wire from the Navy Yard to the Surgeon General’s office in Washington, its code unbreakable to the civilian operators but its length—twenty words where two hundred had been standard—speaking its own language of depletion.

Twenty-four hours after the Liberty Loan parade had ended, 118 Philadelphians had been described as coming down with “a mysterious, deadly influenza.” Now, a month later, the wires that had carried urgent requests for assistance carried only routine mortality reports, their urgency faded to administrative habit.

The operators worked in a long room with high windows that looked onto the street, where the afternoon light fell in dusty rectangles across their instruments. They were skilled readers of pattern, trained to hear meaning in the interval between clicks, and what they heard now was absence. The urgent staccato of crisis had given way to a subdued, procedural silence. They transmitted the messages that arrived. They waited for messages that did not come.

Four miles north, at the potter’s field in the northeast section of the city, a shift of seminarians from St. Charles Borromeo Seminary had taken up their shovels at dawn.

They were not the same young men who had begun this work two weeks before; the original volunteers had fallen sick or exhausted themselves, and new hands had arrived to replace them, inheriting both the labor and the strange intimacy with death that it required. They dug in trenches that ran in parallel lines across the field, each grave marked by a wooden stake with a number painted in black.

The work had lost its panic. They knew by now how deep to dig, how to angle the spade to catch the clay, how to rest without quite stopping. A priest moved among them with a prayer book, but the seminarians had learned that the prayer was often finished before the grave was ready, and they continued their work in a silence that was itself a kind of devotion.

The contrast between these two centers—one of communication, one of burial—measured the city’s condition. The telegraph office processed information; the potter’s field processed bodies. Both continued to function, but the relationship between them had shifted. In mid-October, the wires had driven the digging: each urgent message had meant another corpse to be received. Now the digging continued on its own momentum, while the wires carried news of a different kind. The epidemic had not ended. It had merely entered a new phase, one in which the living began to reckon with what the dead had left behind.

Wilmer Krusen appeared at the Bureau of Health on the morning of October 29 looking, according to those who saw him, like a man who had not slept in a bed for several days. The observation was accurate. The Health Director had been sleeping in his office since the second week of October, rising to visit hospitals and cemeteries, to meet with the Mayor’s emergency committee, to sign the orders that closed theaters and churches and saloons. He had not seen his family in weeks. His collar was loose at the neck, and his eyes had the fixed, slightly glazed quality of someone who has looked too long at figures that refuse to improve.

The meeting that morning was informal, convened in his office rather than the boardroom, attended by three assistant health officers and a representative from the Mayor’s office. Krusen spread the mortality returns on his desk. The numbers for the week ending October 26 showed 1, 782 deaths from influenza and pneumonia—still catastrophic by any standard, but down from the 4, 597 of the week before, and the 2, 681 of the partial week that had included the worst days. The curve was descending. The question was what to do with this information.

The representative from the Mayor’s office spoke first. He did not need to complete his thought. The streetcar system had been operating on a reduced schedule since October 5, carrying only essential workers and those with special permits. The Mayor’s office had been receiving complaints from business owners, from factory managers, from citizens who found themselves walking miles to reach their employment. The war production quotas hung over every discussion. Philadelphia was still expected to meet its obligations.

Krusen touched the mortality sheet with his finger, not pointing at anything in particular. Deaths remained three times the normal rate, he noted. Three times.

The representative acknowledged this. But the trend was favorable, he said. The direction mattered.

The Health Director looked at his assistants. They had been with him through the worst of it, through the nights when the phone rang continuously with reports of hospitals at capacity, of nurses collapsing at their stations, of bodies that could not be removed from homes because no one was available to remove them. They had signed requisitions for coffins that never arrived, for embalmers who had died, for gravediggers who had quit in fear. They had watched Krusen refuse to cancel the Liberty Loan parade on September 28, had watched him maintain in public that there was no cause for alarm while the Navy Yard filled with sick sailors. They knew what favorable trends meant to him now.

Krusen stated his position. Reopening would not be discussed until the daily count fell below one hundred.

The representative from the Mayor’s office made a note. He did not argue. He had his instruction for the day, which was to press for a date, any date, on which the city might resume its ordinary commerce. He would return tomorrow with the same request, and the day after. The pressure would build not through confrontation but through repetition, through the accumulated weight of business necessity against the cautious judgment of a man who had already misjudged once.

In the telegraph office, the afternoon of October 29 brought a message that the senior operator read twice before transcribing. It was addressed to the Philadelphia Chamber of Commerce from a manufacturer in Trenton, and its text was simple: RESUME PRODUCTION WHEN QUERY REPLY PAID. The manufacturer wanted to know when his Philadelphia customers would be operating again. He was willing to pay for the answer. The operator filed it with the commercial traffic and thought, without quite formulating the thought, that the city was becoming a question that other places asked about.

The seminarians finished their trench at four in the afternoon, having prepared thirty graves. The priest recorded the numbers in his book. Thirty graves meant space for thirty bodies, though they had learned to dig some deeper than others, to accommodate the occasional double burial of family members who had died within hours of each other. The stakes were numbered consecutively from 1, 847 to 1, 876. The previous day’s stakes had reached 1, 846. The count was precise because the city required it, though the city had also required that the stakes be removed after burial, to prevent the field from becoming a visible monument to its own failure. The seminarians would dig tomorrow, and the day after. The supply of dead had slowed but not stopped.

That evening, Krusen walked from the Bureau of Health to his temporary lodgings at the Bellevue-Stratford Hotel, a route that took him past the Academy of the Fine Arts. He did not look up at the building, though he passed within twenty feet of its entrance. Inside, the faculty had been meeting to discuss the students who would not return.

Among them was Claud Joseph Warlow, born in Williamstown, Pennsylvania, on March 31, 1888, who had died on October 6, 1918. Warlow had been one of the Academy’s most promising pupils. His chalk drawings on the blackboard at school had been so accomplished that they were allowed to remain for months as examples to other children. After his father’s death around 1906, he had postponed his ambition to enter the Academy, supporting himself through sign-painting and sketching in oils in his spare time. He had finally enrolled, had finally begun the formal study that his talent deserved, and had died at thirty, one of the thousands whose names would not appear in any official accounting of the epidemic’s cost because the city lacked the administrative capacity to record them all.

Krusen did not know Warlow’s name. He knew only the aggregate figures, and the weight of his own decisions in producing them. He walked on to the hotel, where he would sleep for a few hours before returning to the Bureau of Health to face the same questions, the same pressures, the same slow decline of a catastrophe that refused to declare itself finished.

October 30 brought a change in the weather, a cold rain that fell steadily from morning through evening and turned the clay at the potter’s field to something approaching liquid. The seminarians worked in oilskins, their shovels heavy with mud, their progress slowed to half the usual rate. The priest suggested that they suspend operations, but the city had specified a quota, and the quota was tied to the number of bodies awaiting burial at the morgue. They continued, digging graves that filled with water as fast as they could be excavated, bailing with buckets when the depth reached two feet, working in a rhythm that had become mechanical, detached from any sense of urgency or meaning.

In the telegraph office, the rain affected the lines. Messages arrived with static, with dropped words, with the kind of interference that operators called “atmospherics” and that seemed, to some of them, to match the content of what they were transmitting. A wire from Washington announced that the Fourth Liberty Loan, which had opened on October 24, had already reached its subscription quota in several districts. The loan consisted of twenty-year bonds at 4¼ percent, dated October 24, 1918, maturing October 15, 1938, not convertible into future issues. The operators transmitted this information without emotion, but they noted its timing. The nation was still at war, still raising money, still looking toward a victory that the epidemic had made harder to imagine.

A message from Chicago arrived in the late afternoon, routed through Philadelphia to New York. The Chicago operator reported that the city had experienced an explosive growth in cases, spreading faster than the winter epidemic of 1919 though with fewer deaths. New epidemic-related measures were being renewed. Health authorities recommended masks. The Philadelphia operator copied the message and filed it for the attention of the Bureau of Health, though he suspected that Krusen already knew. The influenza was not a local event. It was a national one, a global one, moving through cities in waves that no single board of health could control.

Krusen received the Chicago report at six in the evening, after the close of business hours. He read it standing at his desk, still in his overcoat, rainwater dripping from the brim of his hat. The comparison to winter 1919 confused him for a moment—he had lost track of dates, of the distinction between what had happened and what was predicted to happen—and then he understood. The operator in Chicago had made an error, or the report was garbled, or time itself had become unstable in the presence of so much death. He set the message aside and turned to the day’s mortality figures. Ninety-seven deaths. Below one hundred for the first time since October 7.

He sat down. The chair creaked. He had established a criterion, and the criterion had been met. The Mayor’s representative would arrive in the morning with the same question, and this time Krusen would have to answer it differently. The streetcars, the theaters, the saloons, the ordinary commerce of a city at war—they would have to resume, not because it was safe but because the alternative was a paralysis that the city’s economy could not sustain. He understood this. He had understood it from the beginning. What he had not understood, what he was only now beginning to grasp, was that the decision to reopen would not mark the end of the epidemic but merely its transformation into something else, something slower and more insidious, a presence that would linger in the lungs of survivors and the memories of a city that had watched its systems fail.

The seminarians finished their shift in darkness, the last graves illuminated by lanterns that the priest had procured from somewhere. They had dug eighteen graves in ten hours, short of the quota but acceptable given the conditions. The numbers went into the priest’s book: 1, 877 through 1, 894. They walked back to the seminary in a group, too tired to speak, their boots heavy with clay, their bodies aching in ways that would become familiar over the following days. They would return tomorrow, and the day after, until the city told them to stop.

Krusen did not sleep that night. He worked at his desk, drafting the order that would reopen the streetcars on November 1, limiting their capacity, requiring masks, specifying the hours of operation. The language was precise, bureaucratic, designed to create the appearance of control. He wrote it and rewrote it, searching for some formulation that would acknowledge the risk without paralyzing the city, that would allow commerce to resume without declaring victory over a disease that had already killed more Philadelphians than any single event since the yellow fever epidemic of 1793.

In the telegraph office, the night shift processed the messages that accumulated while the city slept. A death notice to Albany. A shipping inquiry to Norfolk. A coded communication from the Navy Yard to Surgeon General Rupert Blue’s office in Washington, its content unreadable to the civilian operators but its length—fifteen words—speaking of a situation that had stabilized without resolving. The operators worked in the yellow light of their desk lamps, their fingers moving across the keys in patterns that had become automatic, that required no conscious attention. They were transmitters, not interpreters. The meaning of what they sent was not their responsibility.

Dawn came on October 31 with fog that reduced visibility to fifty feet and delayed the morning streetcar service that still operated on the reduced schedule. Krusen walked to the Bureau of Health through streets that seemed emptied of their usual density, as if the population had not yet returned from the places where it had scattered to escape the infection. He passed a newspaper vendor with headlines about the Fourth Liberty Loan, about the advance of Allied forces in France, about the imminent end of a war that had shaped every decision of the past four years. The epidemic appeared on page three, below the fold: INFLUENZA DEATHS DECLINE. The phrase was accurate and misleading in equal measure. The deaths had declined from catastrophic to merely terrible. The city was not healthy. It was exhausted.

The Mayor’s representative arrived at nine, earlier than usual, as if he had been waiting for this meeting. Krusen handed him the draft order without speaking. The representative read it, his lips moving slightly, and then looked up with an expression that might have been gratitude or might have been relief at a burden transferred.

The theaters, the representative asked. Krusen gave the date: November fourth, if the numbers held.

And the churches?

The same.

The representative made notes. He did not ask about the saloons, which had been operating illegally throughout the closure period, their doors open to anyone who knew to knock. Some things did not require official permission. The city had maintained its rituals of prohibition even as it failed to maintain its rituals of burial, and this contradiction no longer seemed remarkable to anyone involved.

In the telegraph office, the morning of October 31 brought a message that the senior operator set aside for special handling. It was from the Surgeon General’s office, addressed to all health officers, and it announced that the epidemic had entered a new phase. The language was careful, hedged with qualifications, but its core was clear: the worst was past. The operators transmitted the message to Chicago, to St. Louis, to San Francisco, to the network of cities that had been connected by wire since the first cases appeared in September. Each operator added it to the file of official communications, the record of a federal authority that had observed the catastrophe without preventing it and now declared its conclusion without having controlled it.

The seminarians did not receive this news. They were digging in mud that had frozen overnight, their shovels ringing against the thin crust of ice that formed on the surface of the clay. The priest had brought coffee in a thermos, and they drank it in turns, holding the cup with gloved hands, watching their breath rise in the cold air. They had reached stake number 1, 923. The count continued, precise and relentless, a measure of work that would not be finished when the epidemic ended because the epidemic had no clear ending, only a gradual dissipation into the ordinary causes of death that the city had always known.

Krusen signed the order at noon on October 31, 1918. The streetcars would resume full service on November 1. The theaters would reopen on November 4, with capacity restricted and masks required. The churches would follow on the same date, with services shortened and ventilation emphasized. The saloons, technically, would remain closed, though everyone knew this prohibition would not be enforced. The order was printed in the afternoon editions of the newspapers, alongside the continuing mortality reports that showed ninety-one deaths for October 31—still triple the normal rate, still evidence of a disease that had not departed but merely relented.

In the telegraph office, the afternoon shift noted the changed character of the traffic. The inquiries about coffins and physicians had almost ceased. In their place came business communications, arrangements for shipments, negotiations about contracts that had been suspended for three weeks. The city was resuming its connections to the larger world, reestablishing the flows of commerce that had been interrupted by the closure orders. The operators transmitted these messages without comment, but they felt the shift in their fingers, the return to a rhythm that predated the emergency, that belonged to the ordinary functioning of a city at war.

The seminarians finished their day’s work at four, having dug twenty-two graves. The priest recorded the numbers and led them in a brief prayer. They walked back through streets that were already changing, that showed more movement, more purpose, than they had seen in weeks. A streetcar passed, its windows filled with faces that looked out at the city with expressions that might have been hope or might have been simple exhaustion. The seminarians did not ride. They walked, as they had walked every day, their boots leaving tracks in the mud that would be erased by the next shift of weather.

Krusen remained at his desk until evening, reviewing the plans for the reopening, anticipating the questions that would arise when the theaters filled and the streetcars carried their restricted loads. He had not slept more than four hours in any night since October 7. He had not seen his family. He had not attended a funeral for any of the twelve thousand dead, though he had signed the orders that sent many of them to the potter’s field, that authorized the trench graves, that transformed the city’s procedures for death into something improvised and temporary.

He understood now that his reputation would not survive this episode, that his decision to allow the Liberty Loan parade would be remembered and judged, that his caution in the weeks after would be seen as too little, too late. This understanding did not change his actions. He continued to work, to sign orders, to meet with representatives from the Mayor’s office and the federal government, because the machinery of public health required operation even when its operator had lost confidence in his own judgment.

The telegraph office closed at midnight on October 31, its operators leaving through a door that opened onto Chestnut Street, where a few late walkers moved through the fog that had returned with evening. The last messages transmitted had been routine: a shipping confirmation to Boston, a death notice to Baltimore, a coded communication from the Navy Yard that the night operator had not attempted to decipher. The silence that settled over the instruments was not absolute—there was always traffic, always some connection to maintain—but it was a different silence than the frantic urgency of mid-October, a silence that spoke of transition rather than crisis, of exhaustion rather than panic.

The first tentative steps toward reopening had been taken. The city stood poised between trauma and the imperative to resume its place in a nation still at war, its systems of communication and burial still functioning, its Health Director still at his desk, its seminarians still digging in the frozen mud of the potter’s field, stake number 1, 924 waiting for the body that would claim it.
