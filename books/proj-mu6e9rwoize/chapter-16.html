Chapter 16

The Excruciating Descent

By 1912, the Beardmore Glacier had exacted its toll in crevasses and cold. Now the Ross Ice Shelf presented a different proposition: not the vertical drama of icefalls, but the horizontal monotony of snow and distance. The surface was better here. The sledge runners slid without the grinding protest of blue ice. Men could haul without probing with alpenstocks, without rope work, without the constant negotiation of broken ground. They had entered this new terrain with something like relief.

On 19 February 1912, Scott recorded the mileage with satisfaction they could still afford. They had reached Mount Hooper Depot. The entry noted 6.5 miles for the day. This was not the 10 or 12 miles of the early return, but it counted as progress. The depot represented food, fuel, the proof that others had traveled this route and lived. The men pitched their tent, posted their flag, settled into routines that had become automatic after three months on the plateau and glacier.

That night the snow began.

Scott described it in the journal he kept with pencil stubs that grew shorter each week. “Dense, windless snowfall.” The phrase sits in the entry for 20 February without elaboration. No wind. That was the significant absence. The Antarctic storms that expedition narratives had made famous came with violence. Wind announced itself. Wind built drifts, sculpted snow, created the hard-packed surface that made sledging possible or the soft powder that made it torture.

This snow fell without wind. It accumulated. It smothered.

The distinction shaped what the men could do. A blizzard with wind has rules. You wait it out. You secure the tent, check the guy ropes, endure the noise and shaking canvas until the barometer turns. Then you dig out, survey the drift, adjust course, move on. The delay is finite. The windless snowfall offers no such clarity. It continues. It fills the hollows where you might place your feet. It loads the sledge until the runners sink. It covers the tracks you laid yesterday, the cairns you built to mark your route, the very surface you must travel. And it does all this silently, without drama, without the narrative satisfaction of a storm defeated.

Bowers’s diary for the same period records the phenomenon with his characteristic precision. He noted the depth of the fall, the condition of the surface, the temperature. His entries grew shorter as the days passed. On 21 February: “Still snowing.” On 22 February: “Snow continues.” The redundancy is itself a record. There was nothing else to report. They did not travel. They lay in their bags, listened to the whisper of flakes on canvas, watched the tent walls press inward under accumulating weight. They melted snow for water, cooked meals on the primus, checked frostbitten extremities. The cold was not extreme—temperatures in the minus twenties Fahrenheit—but the humidity of falling snow penetrated everything. Damp replaced dry cold as the enemy.

The rations they consumed during these three days were not replaced. This was the arithmetic that governs every entry without stating itself. Each man required approximately 4, 500 calories per day for hauling work. The rations Scott had calculated provided less than this even at full measure. Short rations imposed by supply constraints at the final depot before the pole had reduced this further. Now, immobilized by weather, they consumed without producing. The miles they had hoped to make up dissolved into falling snow.

Scott’s journal entries from this period show a voice adjusting to constraint. On 20 February, he wrote of hoping to get away “if possible.” By 22 February, the conditional had hardened: “Impossible to move.” The progression merits attention. Hope, then qualification, then surrender to circumstance. This was not the Scott of the early expedition, the commander who had pushed ponies past their limit, who had insisted on motor sledges despite their failures, who maintained schedule against advice. That Scott had operated in a world where will could modify outcome. The Scott of Mount Hooper Depot recognized different physics. The snow fell. The sledge sank. The men lacked strength to break trail through powder that reached their knees.

The party’s composition had changed since the pole. Edgar Evans was dead, his body left in snow below the Beardmore. The four who remained—Scott, Wilson, Bowers, and Oates—had adapted routines to reduced numbers. Sledge loads were redistributed. The tent, designed for five, now accommodated four with slight luxury of space. Watches through the night, cooking rotations, medical inspections: all continued with one less voice, one less pair of hands. Evans’s death had removed the immediate crisis of his deterioration, but it had not altered the fundamental equation of the return. They were still behind schedule. They were still consuming more energy than rations replaced. The weather that trapped them at Mount Hooper attacked not their numbers but their rate.

Oates’s feet had begun to trouble him seriously on the glacier descent. Frostbite sustained in the crevasse fall earlier in the journey had never fully healed. Now, confined to the tent for three days, damage progressed in damp cold. Wilson, serving as medical officer, inspected the feet daily. His journal records appearance without prognosis: the discoloration, the loss of sensation, the swelling that made boots difficult to remove and impossible to replace with comfort. Oates said little. The silence was characteristic. The cavalry officer who had managed the expedition’s ponies, who had argued with Scott about their care, who had sustained physical demands longer than Evans, had no vocabulary for complaint. He recorded his own condition in terse military phrases. “Feet rather bad,” he wrote in his own diary, when he wrote at all.

The snow stopped on 23 February. Total accumulation was approximately two feet—enough to transform the surface, not enough to constitute disaster in itself. The disaster was the time. Three days of full rations consumed. Three days of potential marching lost. The distance to the next depot, to the safety margin that One Ton Depot represented, remained fixed while their capacity to cover it diminished.

They broke camp with efficiency of long practice. The tent came down, was folded, loaded. The sledge was excavated from its drift, runners cleared, trace harnesses distributed. Scott recorded the start with usual notation of time and condition. The surface was “very soft.” The sledge sank. The men sank. Each step required lifting feet weighted with clinging snow, placing them in holes that collapsed, pulling the trace with resistance that glacier ice had not prepared them for. They covered 3.5 miles that day.

The contrast with their expectation measures their predicament. They had entered the Ross Ice Shelf anticipating speed. The descent from the plateau, the hard surface of coastal ice, the assistance of gravity and improved conditions: these had figured in Scott’s planning as recovery phase, the period when accumulated delay could be erased. The windless snowfall converted anticipation into its opposite. Every mile now cost more than on the glacier. The surface that should have helped them had become another form of resistance.

Bowers’s diary for 24 February records the continuation: “Surface very soft. Short march.” The entry is typical of his method—fact without interpretation, numbers speaking for themselves. They made 5 miles. The following day, 4.5 miles. The day after, with surface slightly improved by wind that had begun to pack new snow, 6.5 miles. This was the range now: not the 10 or 12 of the early return, not even the consistent 8 that Scott had calculated as necessary, but 4, 5, 6 miles of desperate labor.

The food deficit accumulated with each short march. Pemmican ration, already reduced, was now consumed at rate that guaranteed exhaustion before next depot. Wilson, who had charge of medical stores, began to distribute supplementary oil intended for emergency use. The oil extended cooking, allowed more hot water, more warmth in the tent. It did not add calories. The men’s bodies continued to consume themselves, muscle tissue breaking down to fuel hauling, fat reserves depleted long since.

Oates’s condition became the focus of unspoken attention. His feet, when Wilson examined them in the tent each evening, showed progression of freezing injury that polar medical literature had documented but not cured. Toes had lost all color. Sensation had retreated to the ankle, then above. Swelling prevented proper circulation. Walking on these feet produced damage that continued after walking stopped. Oates knew this. He had seen similar cases in the cavalry, men who had ridden in wet cold, whose boots had frozen on their feet. The treatment was rest, warmth, time. None were available. The march continued because the alternative—remaining in place, consuming rations, waiting for improvement that would not come—was worse.

Scott’s journal entries from this period show a commander calculating with reduced resources. On 26 February, he noted distance to next depot: 67 miles. At current rate, this represented 10 to 12 days of marching. Food on the sledge would last, with continued short rations, for approximately that period. The margin had disappeared. The safety that depots were meant to provide—extra food, fuel, psychological certainty of supply—had been consumed by the delay at Mount Hooper and the slow marching that followed.

The weather continued variable. Days of windless calm alternated with days of drifting snow that forced partial halts. Temperature, recorded in both Scott’s and Bowers’s journals, remained in minus twenties and thirties Fahrenheit—cold enough to freeze exposed skin, not cold enough to stop travel entirely. Wind chill, when wind blew, drove effective temperature lower. The men learned to read sky through tent fabric, to distinguish quality of silence that meant continued snowfall from silence that meant clearing.

By 28 February, they had reached position approximately 40 miles from next depot. Surface had improved marginally, allowing marches of 7 and 8 miles. This was still below necessary rate, but it represented progress. Scott’s journal shows a voice attempting optimism: “Things looking up,” he wrote, though context suggests he knew the optimism was provisional. The food deficit was now structural. Even if they maintained 8 miles per day, even if weather held, they would reach depot with nothing in reserve. The final 150 miles to One Ton Depot, the largest supply cache and effective point of safety, would have to be covered with whatever strength remained after next resupply.

Physical condition of the party, recorded in Wilson’s medical notes and implied in journal entries, had reached a threshold. The men were not merely tired. They were depleted in ways that affected judgment, coordination, the small motor skills required for camp work. Primus lighting, normally automatic, now required multiple attempts. Tent pitching, sledge loading, navigation with theodolite: all took longer, demanded more concentration, produced more errors. Bowers, who had maintained most consistent performance, showed strain in his handwriting, letters growing larger, less controlled. Wilson, whose medical responsibilities added to his load, slept less, observed more, said little.

Oates’s feet had reached stage that Wilson recorded without prognosis. Tissue damage was extensive. Walking on them produced trauma that could not heal. Not walking on them was impossible. The choice this implied—between group’s progress and Oates’s preservation—had not yet become explicit. The group continued to march because alternative was collective death. Oates continued to march because alternative was abandonment, and because his own code permitted no other course.

On 29 February, they made 6 miles. Surface had softened again, wind dropping, snow beginning to drift in old pattern. Scott’s journal entry for the day includes rare direct statement of concern regarding Oates’s feet. The concern was not merely humanitarian. A man who cannot walk at full pace slows the sledge. A man who requires assistance reduces hauling strength by more than his own contribution. The mathematics of polar travel are relentless: four men hauling at 75% efficiency produce less than three men hauling at full capacity. Oates was not yet at 75%, but the trajectory was clear.

The depot they reached on 1 March—named for its location relative to Mount Hooper, marked by single flag and buried cache—contained food and fuel laid by support party in January. Relief of finding it, of loading fresh supplies onto sledge that had grown light with consumption, was recorded in all three journals. Scott noted date, quantity, improved spirits of party. Bowers recorded exact weights, distribution, condition of cache. Wilson inspected medical supplies, extra oil, supplementary rations included against just this contingency.

The loading took time they could not spare. Repacking, redistribution, consumption of hot meal from depot stores: these were necessary procedures that consumed hours of daylight. They did not travel that day. The following morning, 2 March, they set out with full sledges and intention of making up lost ground.

The surface defeated them. Snow that had fallen during their approach to depot had not consolidated. Sledge runners sank. Men sank. They covered 5 miles. The day after, 4 miles. Food they had loaded, calculated for specific rate of progress, was now being consumed at rate that guaranteed exhaustion before next resupply. The deficit that had been structural became critical.

Scott’s journal from this period shows a commander confronting limits of his planning. Depots had been laid at distances calculated for specific pace: 10 to 12 miles per day on return, with moderate loads, in reasonable weather. Actual pace—4 to 6 miles, with heavy loads, in conditions varying from difficult to impossible—converted depots from waystations of security into markers of insufficiency. Each depot reached represented survival to that point. Each departure represented new crisis of calculation, new assessment of whether remaining food would last to next.

The weather continued its pattern of variable obstruction. Days of windless snowfall alternated with days of drifting snow that forced partial or complete halts. Temperature remained in range that permitted travel but punished it, cold penetrating reduced insulation of clothing that had not been properly dried in weeks. Men’s bodies, consuming themselves for fuel, produced less internal heat. The margin between sustainable effort and collapse narrowed.

By 5 March, they had covered approximately 30 miles from depot. Next supply point lay 50 miles ahead. At current rate, this represented 8 to 10 days of marching. Food on sledge, calculated for that period at short rations, provided no margin for further delay. Condition of party, recorded in journals through filter of exhaustion and hope, had reached point where daily march represented limit of physical capacity.

Oates’s feet had become the determining variable. Wilson’s medical notes, preserved in records found with the bodies, describe progression from frostbite to gangrene. Tissue damage, compounded by continued use, had advanced beyond possibility of recovery under field conditions. Oates knew this. His diary entries, sparse throughout, ceased entirely in this period. His contribution to party’s work—hauling, camp work, shared labor of survival—continued, but at cost visible to all.

The march of 6 March covered 4 miles. Surface was “very bad,” in Scott’s description—soft snow, no wind to harden it, sledge sinking to its crossbars. The following day, 3.5 miles. The day after, with surface slightly improved, 5 miles. The pattern was established: not collapse of single catastrophic day, but grinding reduction of capacity, conversion of effort into distance at rate that food supply could not sustain.

Scott’s journal for 8 March includes calculation that earlier entries had avoided. They were 50 miles from next depot. They had food for 7 days at short rations. The weather must improve or they could not make it. The “must” is voice of commander who has exhausted resources of adaptation. The weather did not improve. The following day brought more windless snowfall, the pattern that had trapped them at Mount Hooper repeated at more critical point in their journey.

They were forced to camp. Snow fell for two days. Consumption of rations continued. Distance to next depot remained fixed at 50 miles. Men’s strength, already depleted, suffered further erosion of immobility. Cold of tent, damp with condensation and breath, penetrated more deeply than cold of movement. The waiting, which early expedition had learned to endure as temporary condition, had become permanent in prospect.

When they resumed on 11 March, surface was worse than before. Accumulated snowfall had transformed ice shelf into landscape of drifts and hollows without pattern or predictability. Sledge runners sank. Men broke trail through snow that reached their thighs. They covered 3 miles. The following day, with Oates’s feet now requiring assistance for most difficult sections, 2.5 miles.

The arithmetic had become inescapable. At 2.5 miles per day, 50 miles to next depot represented 20 days of marching. Food would last 7. The deficit was not manageable by any adjustment of ration or pace. The only variable remaining was condition of the men, and that variable was deteriorating.

Scott’s journal from this period shows a voice stripped of command assumptions that had governed expedition. Entries are shorter, more factual, less concerned with morale or interpretation. On 13 March: “Oates very badly.” On 14 March: “Only 4 miles.” On 15 March: “Surface terrible.” The deterioration is recorded without drama because drama had become routine. Each day brought same choices, same insufficient outcomes, same consumption of resources without corresponding progress.

The party that departed Mount Hooper Depot on 23 February had carried calculated food deficit. Three days of windless snowfall had converted that deficit into crisis. Slow marching that followed had made crisis fatal. Now, in second week of March, with Oates unable to walk unassisted for more than few miles, with next depot still 40 miles distant, with food on sledge measured in days rather than weeks, the party faced consequence that earlier delays had made inevitable.

They continued to march because alternative was immediate death. They continued to haul because sledge carried food that prolonged existence. They continued to calculate because calculation was only resource remaining. Distance to One Ton Depot, the largest supply cache, the point where relief parties might be encountered, remained approximately 100 miles. At current rate, this represented more than month of marching. Food would not last. Men’s bodies would not last. Weather, which had been immediate agent of immobilization at Mount Hooper, continued its variable obstruction, neither permitting full progress nor stopping them entirely.

Oates’s condition had reached point where his contribution to hauling was negative. He required assistance to break trail, to negotiate drifts, to maintain pace. The assistance reduced speed of other three, increased their expenditure of energy, accelerated their own deterioration. The choice this implied—between group’s survival and Oates’s inclusion—had not yet been made explicit. The group continued to function as four because alternative had not been formulated, because code that governed their conduct permitted no other arrangement, because hope of improvement, however faint, persisted against evidence.

On 16 March, they covered 3 miles. Surface was “impossible,” in Scott’s description. Oates’s feet, when Wilson examined them in tent, showed black discoloration of advanced gangrene. The smell, which medical literature recorded but journals did not mention, would have filled confined space. The pain, which Oates had managed through earlier stages, had become continuous, unresponsive to limited analgesics in Wilson’s kit.

The following day, 17 March, they made only 2 miles. Oates could not walk without support. The support slowed party to pace that guaranteed exhaustion of food before distance. The calculation that Scott had avoided, that expedition’s planning had not contemplated, had become unavoidable. The party departed their camp that morning with Oates still among them, still hauling when he could, still supported when he could not. The march ended early, tent pitched in drifting snow, men settled into bags with knowledge that following day would bring same impossible choices.

They had left Mount Hooper Depot with a food deficit measured in days. Now, eleven days later, the deficit was measured in lives. The next depot remained beyond reach. The one after that—One Ton, with its larger cache, its possibility of encounter with relief—had receded from prospect into abstraction. What remained was concrete: four men in a tent, three of whom could still walk, one of whom could not, and food enough for none of them to complete the journey they had calculated in the planning rooms of London and the winter quarters of Cape Evans. The mathematics of survival had overtaken the mathematics of exploration.
