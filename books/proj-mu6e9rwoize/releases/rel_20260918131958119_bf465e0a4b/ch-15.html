Chapter 15

The First Death on the Return

They broke camp on 19 January 1912. The Norwegian tent remained standing at the Pole. Its flag snapped in the wind. By 4 February, the transformation was complete.

Wilson opened his diary that morning. He recorded unsteady gait and mental torpor. Three pages earlier in the same leather-bound volume, Scott had called Petty Officer Edgar Evans the strongest of the party. The contrast required no underlining.

The deflated party had begun the 862-mile return journey on 19 January 1912 with forced brightness. Scott wrote his fears directly. “I’m afraid the return journey is going to be dreadfully tiring and monotonous,” he admitted on the day they turned north. They had reached the Pole in fifty-five days from the foot of the Beardmore Glacier. They needed to match that pace descending. They carried less food than they had brought south. The weather had already begun to turn against them.

At Cape Evans, fifteen hundred miles north, the storekeeper checked his ledger. He recorded issues of oil and pemmican for the supporting parties. Those teams had turned back at latitude eighty-seven degrees thirty-two minutes south. They had returned to the hut by mid-January. Apsley Cherry-Garrard sat at the table now. He stared at the empty bunks. He knew only that Scott was overdue.

Evans fell hard on 30 January. His hand struck ice during the descent from the plateau. Frostbite set in within hours. The hand swelled inside his woolen mitten. He could not close his fingers around his ski stick. The stick slipped repeatedly during hauls.

Snow-blindness followed immediately. He walked with both mittened hands extended forward. He felt for crevasses he could no longer see. His pace slowed to a stagger. The others shortened their harness straps to keep him in contact. They could not leave him behind.

Wilson recognized the symptoms of advanced scurvy. The disease had begun weeks earlier on the polar plateau. Fresh food had run out entirely there. The men ate only pemmican, biscuit, and tea. Vitamin C deficiency degrades connective tissue systematically. Capillaries weaken throughout the body. Old wounds reopen without trauma.

Wilson examined Evans’s gums on 4 February. They were spongy and swollen around every tooth. His mental confusion deepened daily. On 10 February he asked if they were going back to the Pole. He did not recognize their direction. His nervous system was failing.

Scott recorded these developments differently. He wrote that Evans had nearly broken down in brain. He described physical failure using mechanical metaphors. He had used identical language for the failed motor sledges at Cape Evans. Now he applied it to a man. The habit could not obscure what the diary recorded.

Simpson’s meteorological charts arrived at Cape Evans daily. They showed a pattern of deterioration on the Beardmore. Temperatures ranged between minus twenty and minus forty degrees Fahrenheit during early February. Wind speeds increased steadily. Clear days gave way to whiteout conditions.

Sledge runners sank into soft drifts instead of gliding over hard snow. Hauling effort multiplied with every mile. Scott tracked the correlation in his own journal. On 31 January he noted very bad light all day. On 1 February he wrote that the surface was very bad indeed. On 3 February he recorded thick weather and heavy snow.

The party had covered thirteen nautical miles on 20 January. By 4 February they managed barely seven. The gap between required and achieved pace widened each day. No relay party waited on the glacier below them. The supporting teams had deposited their loads and returned north. Cherry-Garrard had watched Scott’s team diminish toward the southern horizon on 3 January. Five figures on ski grew smaller until they merged with the snow surface.

The isolation was absolute at eighty-five degrees south. The depot system offered their only mitigation. Lower Glacier Depot lay ahead at latitude eighty-five degrees five minutes. It held additional pemmican and biscuit laid in December. Reaching it was essential.

They arrived there on 7 February after eleven days of retreat from the Pole. They were still eight hundred miles from Cape Evans. The depot allowed full rations temporarily. Speed would have saved food supplies. Their reduced speed consumed more than calculated.

The three conditions operated as interconnected failures rather than separate misfortunes. Bad weather prevented rapid travel that might have outrun scurvy’s progression. Scurvy weakened muscles needed for hauling through soft snow. Evans’s damaged hand prevented him from contributing to sledge work.

His snow-blindness required another man to guide him constantly. His mental torpor slowed every decision at camp. The party redistributed his load across four remaining harnesses. They shortened marching hours to accommodate his pace. These adaptations preserved group cohesion. They accelerated food consumption dangerously.

Wilson’s medical observations grew more specific as Evans declined. He noted that Evans’s hand had become very bad indeed. He recorded frequent falling episodes and complaints of dizziness. He observed confusion regarding dates and locations. These indicated neurological involvement beyond peripheral symptoms.

Evans fell again on the afternoon of 16 February near the foot of the Beardmore Glacier. This time he did not rise from the snow. The party halted at four o’clock in heavy drift. They unhitched from the sledge traces. They walked back two hundred yards to his position.

He remained conscious but unable to stand upright. His legs would not support his weight. They made camp around his recumbent body inside their shared tent. Wilson lit the primus stove for warmth. Evans took no food that evening. His breathing grew shallow and irregular after dark.

He died at half past twelve on the morning of 17 February 1912. His death occurred without regaining consciousness after he collapsed into sleep. The location was near the foot of the Beardmore Glacier, approximately seventy-nine degrees fourteen minutes south latitude. Scott noted the time precisely in his journal. Wilson recorded the clinical sequence of symptoms leading to death. Bowers wrote simply that Evans died tonight.

The body lay inside the tent through which four men must continue passing. The ground outside was solid ice to unknown depth. Digging a grave would require hours of labor by exhausted men. Their tools were inadequate to frozen ground.

They built a cairn over him instead. They used his own skis and ski stick as vertical markers. They left him on the glacier surface covered by snow blocks. The makeshift memorial stood visible against the white expanse for any subsequent party.

They redistributed his personal effects immediately after breakfast. His watch and personal diary became cargo on the remaining sledge. His reindeer-skin sleeping bag remained with the party for continued use. His allocated pemmican and biscuit rations went into common stock.

These transactions took less than one hour to complete. By mid-morning on 17 February only four harnesses remained where five had been arranged for two weeks prior. Each surviving man now pulled proportionally more weight.

Scott attempted retrospective explanation in his journal entry for that day. He wrote that he feared Evans had not been as strong as they previously imagined. He attributed the decline to hand damage from a fall seventeen days prior. He suggested this injury had doubtless affected circulation detrimentally. He avoided acknowledging the systemic scurvy evident to his medical officer.

Wilson made no such attempt at explanation or comfort. His entry for 17 February recorded simply that all hands were showing signs of scurvy simultaneously. He noted swollen gums and old scars reopening on multiple bodies. He examined his own mouth tissue in the dim tent light that evening. He found identical spongy deterioration there too.

Fresh meat remained eight hundred miles north at Cape Evans. Vegetables and fruit existed only in memory there at latitude seventy-nine degrees south. The treatment was unavailable. The diagnosis was irreversible by any means carried on the sledge.

The meteorological record showed temporary clearing on 17 February. Temperatures rose to minus twenty degrees Fahrenheit. Wind dropped to light airs from the southeast direction. Barometric pressure rose steadily throughout morning hours.

Four men harnessed themselves to the sledge after breaking camp. They marched into these relatively favorable conditions. They covered eight statute miles that day according to Bowers’s navigation notes. This distance exceeded their recent daily averages significantly. They could not sustain such exertion going forward.

The mathematics of survival had inverted completely now. Five men traveling fast could survive on allocated rations. Four men traveling slowly required more total food per mile covered. Speed saved supplies previously. Their circumstances now prevented speed entirely.

They reached the eighty-degree depot on 21 February after four days of hard marching from Evans’s death site. This cache held four weeks of food for a reduced party plus fuel for cooking. It represented their largest remaining resupply point before the Barrier itself.

Inside the cairn they found a note from Lieutenant Evans’s supporting party dated early January. It wished them good luck and reported all well at that earlier date. The optimism of that message seemed to belong to a different expedition now. It predated knowledge of Amundsen’s victory and their own slow defeat.

Oates’s feet began showing serious trouble immediately after leaving this depot. Bowers’s eyes failed intermittently from renewed snow-blindness attacks. Scott’s frostbitten feet refused healing despite rest periods allowed at depots. Wilson noted scurvy advancing visibly in every man’s symptoms now.

The supporting parties that had turned back at eighty-seven degrees thirty-two minutes south had faced their own calculus of risk during January. Lieutenant Evans’s team of four men, with Lashly and Crean hauling alongside, had deposited their final depot load on 26 January and begun the long retreat to Cape Evans. They traveled faster than the polar party precisely because they traveled lighter, with smaller loads and full physical capacity among all four members. Yet even this relative advantage did not prevent serious strain. Lashly’s ankles had swollen alarmingly from the constant strain of man-hauling, and Crean had developed frostbite in his left foot that required careful monitoring during the final weeks of their return. These injuries, sustained by men in comparatively good condition, offered an unspoken index of what the polar party must endure with one member already compromised and four others gradually following the same path of physical deterioration.

Cherry-Garrard’s vigil at Cape Evans during late January and early February assumed a particular quality of helpless precision. The supporting parties returned with specific intelligence: Scott had been making good time toward the Pole, the depots were found without difficulty, the weather had held surprisingly fair for the season. This information created a false horizon of expectation. The storekeeper’s ledger recorded steady consumption of supplies by the returned men, but also the careful preservation of allocated rations for the polar party’s eventual return.

Cherry-Garrard helped organize these reserves according to the predetermined depot schedule, calculating dates and distances with the abstract certainty of mathematics that could not account for Evans’s hand injury, for the scurvy already advancing in Wilson’s diagnostic notes, for the weather system building across the Beardmore. His diary entries during this period alternate between practical administrative details and sudden, unguarded sentences about the empty bunks and the silence from the south.

The meteorological records compiled by Simpson at Cape Evans reveal a pattern that the polar party could not fully perceive from their position within it. The high-pressure systems that had favored their outward journey collapsed during late January, replaced by a persistent low-pressure trough that funneled maritime air masses across the Ross Ice Shelf and up the glacier. This was not merely unfortunate weather but a climatic regime shift characteristic of the Antarctic autumn, arriving several weeks earlier than the expedition’s planning had anticipated. Simpson’s instruments recorded barometric pressure falling steadily from 29.50 inches on 28 January to 28.90 inches by 3 February, a gradient that translated directly into the strengthening winds Scott described in his journal. The temperature differential between the plateau and the coast intensified accordingly, generating the katabatic wind conditions that converted manageable travel days into exhausting battles for progress.

The medical specifics of Evans’s condition, as Wilson recorded them, deserve careful attention because they illuminate the interaction of multiple failure modes that characterized this phase of the expedition. The hand injury of 30 January, initially diagnosed as frostbite with possible fracture, became a portal for systemic infection as the tissue necrosis advanced. Wilson’s examination on 4 February noted both the swollen gum tissue characteristic of scurvy and a foul odor from the damaged hand suggesting anaerobic bacterial colonization. In the absence of antiseptic supplies beyond basic carbolic solution, Wilson could clean the wound but could not prevent the spread of toxins through Evans’s compromised circulatory system. The mental confusion that Scott attributed to “brain” failure likely represented combined effects: hypoxia from the high-altitude plateau, toxin absorption from infected tissue, and the cerebral hemorrhages that advanced scurvy can produce as capillary integrity fails throughout the body.

The decision-making environment within the polar party during this period operated under constraints that subsequent criticism has sometimes failed to appreciate. Scott’s leadership, often examined through the lens of his eventual fate, here faced immediate practical challenges without clear precedent in polar travel.

The option of leaving Evans behind at a depot, while theoretically preserving the progress of the fitter men, would have constituted an immediate death sentence for the abandoned man and likely a psychological rupture from which the remaining party could not recover. The alternative—slowing pace, redistributing loads, accepting the accelerated consumption of supplies—represented a collective choice that all four surviving members affirmed through their continued actions.

Wilson’s medical authority and Bowers’s navigational expertise gave them standing to propose alternatives, yet their diaries record no serious consideration of separation. The ethos of the expedition, forged through three years of shared shipboard life and two seasons of depot-laying sledge journeys, permitted no other resolution.

The specific geography of the Lower Glacier Depot, reached on 7 February, conditioned the options available to the party in ways that have received insufficient attention. Located at the junction where the Beardmore’s descending ice stream began to flatten toward the Ross Ice Shelf, this depot represented the last point where the party could have established a substantial camp with reasonable access to hunting opportunities.

Emperor penguin colonies existed on the sea ice some miles to the east, and seal holes occurred along the pressure ridges where glacier met shelf. Scott had established this depot in December with full knowledge of these resources, anticipating that return parties might need to supplement rations through local foraging.

Yet in February, with Evans unable to travel independently and the remaining men already showing early scurvy symptoms, the energy required to establish a hunting camp exceeded their available capacity. They rested one day, consumed full rations, and pressed northward—choosing momentum over recuperation because the logistics of stationary survival had become impossible.

The redistribution of Evans’s load after his death on 17 February followed patterns established during the preceding two weeks but with permanent structural implications. Where previously his harness had remained attached to the sledge with slack taken up by the others, now his physical absence required complete reorganization of the remaining traces.

Bowers, the shortest man in the party, assumed the forward position where his navigational observations could be made without stopping the sledge. Oates and Scott took the middle positions, with Wilson—still the fittest man despite his own advancing scurvy—pulling the rear trace where the weight fell heaviest.

This arrangement, maintained for the subsequent three weeks until Oates’s final incapacitation, distributed the additional load according to remaining physical capacity rather than rank or original role. The efficiency loss was measurable: Bowers’s navigation records show their pace dropping from 1.5 miles per hour on 17 February to barely one mile per hour by 21 February, as the new configuration required more frequent rests and more elaborate coordination at crevassed sections.

The note found at the eighty-degree depot, written by Lieutenant Evans’s party in early January, carried an emotional weight that Scott’s journal acknowledges only indirectly. The message reported “all well” and wished the polar party “good luck” in terms appropriate to the expedition’s original optimistic framing. For the four men reading it in late February, this document represented a communication across an unbridgeable temporal divide—between the expedition as planned and the expedition as experienced, between the confident departure from Cape Evans and the diminished return. Scott copied the note’s contents into his journal without editorial comment, a reticence that suggests the impossibility of adequate response. The depot itself, with its carefully calculated four weeks of supplies, offered material reassurance that the planning had been sound even as the execution encountered irreversible setbacks.

The immediate acceleration in daily mileage after Evans’s death, noted in Bowers’s records and confirmed by Scott’s journal, represented a physiological rather than psychological phenomenon. The removal of Evans’s variable pace—his frequent falls, his need for guidance, his extended periods of confusion at camp—allowed the remaining four men to establish a more consistent rhythm of march. They covered eight statute miles on 17 February and maintained similar distances for three subsequent days before deteriorating conditions and their own physical decline forced reduction.

This temporary improvement illustrates the cruel mathematics of polar travel: the party had become more efficient precisely because it had become smaller, yet this efficiency purchased at the cost of a man’s life could not be sustained against the progressive debilitation of the survivors. By 21 February, when they reached the eighty-degree depot, Oates’s feet had developed the frostbite lesions that would not heal, and Bowers’s snow-blindness had returned with sufficient severity to require him to navigate by touch and verbal direction from Scott.

Each suffered different specific manifestations of the same underlying nutritional collapse. Their bodies consumed muscle tissue to maintain core temperatures against wind chill factors below minus sixty degrees effective temperature. Each lost strength required for hauling precisely when hauling requirements increased due to reduced numbers.

With Evans dead, the remaining four men pressed on with diminished manpower and supplies. The expedition had transformed into a pure survival march without scientific objectives remaining achievable. They left the eighty-degree depot on 24 February facing overcast skies and falling snow. They entered the Ross Ice Shelf’s flat white expanse where wind replaced crevasses as their primary antagonist.
