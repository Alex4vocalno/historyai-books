Chapter 20

The Search Party Sets Forth

The diaries are finished. What remains are objects in snow.

On 4 November 1912, four men stood at One Ton Depot and read its contents like a ledger of absences. Edward Leicester Atkinson, the senior naval officer now commanding at Cape Evans, directed his party’s examination with the method of a surgeon annotating a corpse. The depot sat complete. The cases of biscuit they found stacked in pyramids. The oil tins stood in rows, full, their brass caps unturned.

The pony meat, frozen hard as stone, lay where it had been cached eleven months before. The snow had drifted over some of it, but the inventory was intact. Atkinson wrote down what they saw. Charles Seymour Wright, the physicist, checked the cases against his memory of the depot-laying journey. Apsley Cherry-Garrard, whose eyes still showed the strain of the winter journey to Cape Crozier, walked the perimeter of the cache without speaking. Patrick Keohane, the Irish petty officer, began digging out a sledge they had left there.

The temperature was minus 15 Fahrenheit. The sky was clear. The Barrier stretched away southward in a single plane of white, unmarked by any dark speck that might be a tent.

They had traveled from Cape Evans in eight days, following the flagged route that Scott’s party had marked for their own return. The search party’s daily marches averaged fifteen miles. Their loads were heavy but not desperate: four weeks’ provisions, full sleeping gear, a spare tent. They had no dogs. Atkinson had decided against them, citing their exhaustion from the winter’s work and the difficulty of feeding them from the depots. The men would man-haul, as Scott’s party had man-hauled, but with this difference—they were fit, well-fed, and traveling in spring conditions with the sun circling overhead. The same system, functioning now as it was designed to function, revealed by contrast how it had failed eight months before.

Back in March, Scott’s party had reached this same depot in a different universe of circumstance. They arrived on 16 March 1912, having descended the Beardmore Glacier with Edgar Evans already dead, with Lawrence Oates’s feet turning black, with their rations calculated to the ounce and the ounce miscounted. The pony meat they found here had been cached without the fat that would have made it nourishing. The oil they found was short by several tins, a deficit that would cost them heat, then mobility, then life.

They had left this depot on 20 March with eleven days’ food for a march they estimated at ten days to the next cache at Corner Camp. The weather held them. The blizzard began on 20 March and did not lift. They died eleven miles south of where Atkinson now stood, in a tent they could not strike, with fuel and food exhausted, still 148 miles from Cape Evans.

Atkinson knew these facts in outline. Cherry-Garrard had returned from his March attempt to meet Scott with a partial account: the empty tent at One Ton, the waiting, the final return alone. The winter had passed in enforced ignorance.

The remaining expedition members at Cape Evans waited through the winter, continuing their scientific work—meteorological observations, biological collections, the routine of maintaining a station in isolation. Atkinson, as the senior officer, had carried the burden of decision.

In the spring he faced a choice: whether to direct first efforts toward the rescue of Victor Campbell’s Northern Party, marooned somewhere along the Victoria Land coast, or to establish if possible the fate of Scott’s polar party.

A meeting of the whole group decided that they should first search for signs of Scott. Practicality governed. Campbell’s men, if living, would be in immediate want. Scott’s party, if they had survived the winter, would have made their way to some depot. The search for them could wait until the relief of Campbell was accomplished.

This decision, made in late October, carried its own weight. Atkinson led the relief expedition to Evans Coves, found Campbell’s party alive after a winter of ice-cave survival, and brought them back to Cape Evans on 7 November. Only then could the search for Scott begin in earnest. The delay was eleven days. Whether those days would have altered the outcome—whether Scott’s party might have been found living in some intermediate camp—belongs to the arithmetic of regret. The search party that departed on 29 October 1912 traveled in the certainty that they would find only remains.

Atkinson’s method was systematic. Trained at the Royal Naval Hospital at Haslar before he joined the expedition, he approached the search as a diagnostic procedure. Each depot must be examined for signs of passage. Each flag must be noted, its condition assessed. The snow surface must be scanned for tracks, for discarded objects, for any disturbance that might indicate where five men had passed or halted. The party moved in a rhythm of march and measurement: rise at 6 a. m., breakfast on pemmican and biscuit, strike the tent, load the sledge, haul until 1 p. m., lunch for an hour, haul until 6 p. m., camp, prepare the evening meal, sleep. The temperatures ranged from minus 10 to minus 25 Fahrenheit. The wind was moderate. By Antarctic standards, the conditions were favorable.

They found the first evidence on 12 November, eight miles south of One Ton Depot. A cairn of snow blocks, built with the expedition’s standard technique, rose from the flat surface. Beside it lay a single object: a sledge, abandoned, its runners pointing south. The search party approached it with the caution of men who have found what they were seeking and now must learn what it means. The sledge was one of the Norwegian-pattern models they had used for depot-laying, not the larger sledge that would have carried the polar party’s main loads. This suggested a support party, a turning-back, a relay of some kind. But whose?

Wright examined the sacking that covered the load. The snow had penetrated it, freezing the contents into a solid mass. They chipped away the ice. Inside lay a collection of personal effects: a camera, geological specimens, a pair of ski boots, a bag of spare clothing. Men who expected to return did not abandon such things. The camera was particularly telling. Scott had carried photographic equipment to the Pole, intending to document the achievement. Its presence here, eight miles south of One Ton, indicated that someone had turned back before reaching that critical depot, leaving behind what they could not haul.

Atkinson recorded the find in his notebook. The position: latitude 79° 29’ S. The date of discovery: 12 November 1912. The objects: listed in detail. Then the party continued south, following the faint trace of ski tracks that showed through the winter’s snow accumulation. The tracks were old, partially drifted over, but still readable to trained eyes. They led toward the point where, according to Cherry-Garrard’s account and their own calculations, the final camp must lie.

The psychological burden of this march differed fundamentally from any other journey these men had made. They were not exploring unknown terrain. They were retracing a route whose every milestone now carried retrospective meaning. Each depot they passed—Safety Camp, Corner Camp, the Bluff—was a station in someone else’s catastrophe. The flags that Scott’s party had planted to guide their return now guided the searchers toward their discovery. The system of markers, designed for navigation, had become a system of mementos.

Cherry-Garrard’s particular position in this party added a layer of complexity. He had been here before, in the darkness of March, waiting in a tent at One Ton Depot for men who never came. He had heard, or thought he heard, distant sounds that might have been voices, might have been wind. He had turned back on 10 March 1912, leaving the depot stocked with food and fuel that Scott’s party would never reach. Now he returned in daylight, in company, with the same landscape offering different evidence. The experience of polar travel is always in part the experience of one’s own mind in isolation; Cherry-Garrard’s mind contained memories that the others could only imagine.

The search party’s composition reflected Atkinson’s assessment of requirements. He needed men who knew the route, who could interpret snow conditions, who possessed the physical resilience for sustained man-hauling. Wright had been on the geological expedition to the west and understood sledge travel. Keohane was an experienced seaman with demonstrated capacity for cold-weather work. Cherry-Garrard, despite his physical limitations—his eyesight damaged by the winter journey, his constitution never robust—possessed knowledge that no one else could supply. Atkinson himself, at thirty-two, was the oldest of the group and the only one with medical training. They were, by the standards of the expedition, a balanced team.

Their daily routine emphasized the contrast between their circumstances and those of the men they sought. They ate full rations: one pound of pemmican per man per day, twelve ounces of biscuit, two ounces of butter, sugar, tea, cocoa. They burned oil without calculation, keeping their tent warm enough for comfort. They slept in bags that were dry and thick. At One Ton Depot they had found the supplies Cherry-Garrard had left, still intact, and they drew on these to supplement their own stores. The abundance that had mocked Scott’s need now sustained his investigators.

The weather held. For nine days they marched south from Cape Evans, encountering nothing worse than surface drift and occasional fog. The Barrier’s surface was in spring condition: hard enough for good sledge travel, not so soft as to impede progress. They made their camps on schedule, recorded their positions, maintained their instruments. Wright took magnetic observations when conditions permitted. Atkinson collected meteorological data. The expedition’s scientific purposes continued even in this mission of recovery.

On 10 November they reached One Ton Depot and spent a full day examining it. The depot’s completeness was, for Atkinson, a piece of evidence requiring documentation. He measured the remaining supplies, calculated what Scott’s party would have found, assessed what difference the full complement might have made. The oil deficit was confirmed: several tins short of the planned cache. The pony meat was there, but without the supplementary fat stores that would have made it nutritionally adequate. Small failures in the logistical chain, the kind that accumulate without appearing critical until they intersect with other failures—weather, health, navigation error—to produce catastrophe.

They left One Ton Depot on 11 November, traveling light. The sledge carried only essential gear; they would return to collect the depot’s surplus on the way north. The ski tracks they followed became fresher, less drifted, more distinct. The final march took them across a surface that Scott’s party had traversed in desperation, in the last hours of usable strength, with Oates already gone to his death in the snow and the remaining four men knowing they would not survive. The searchers read this landscape backward, finding in its emptiness the record of final effort.

The cairn appeared at 11 a. m. On 12 November. It stood eleven miles south of One Ton Depot, positioned on a slight rise that would have offered some protection from the prevailing wind. The snow around it was disturbed, not by recent activity but by the structural collapse of objects beneath the winter’s accumulation. Atkinson approached it with Wright beside him. Keohane held the sledge. Cherry-Garrard stood apart, his damaged eyes straining to resolve what the others could already see.

Five men had not built this cairn. It was too small, too hastily constructed. Someone had raised it in haste, with frozen hands, in conditions that permitted no delay. The snow blocks were irregular, the structure unstable. It marked a place where something had stopped.

They found the tent pole first: a bamboo shaft protruding through the drift, its flag long torn away. Then the ridge of the tent itself, collapsed under snow weight, the green fabric darkened by exposure. The shape beneath it was wrong for a single object. It suggested volume, mass, the compressed geometry of human forms.

Atkinson directed the excavation. They worked with the deliberation that the situation required, though each man felt the pressure of what they were about to confirm. The snow was hard-packed, frozen into layers that recorded the winter’s storms. They used ice-axes and bare hands. The tent emerged gradually: its walls, its entrance, the snow banked against its lee side. The door was closed from within.

The interior, when they opened it, was a tableau of final arrangement. Three bodies lay in the sleeping bags, positioned for maximum warmth in a space that had grown progressively colder. Scott was in the center, his bag open at the top, his right arm extended across the body beside him. Wilson lay to his left, partially turned toward Scott, his bag closed to the neck. Bowers was on the right, curled in a posture that suggested the final stages of hypothermia. Oates’s bag was there, empty, his place marked by the arrangement of the others. Evans was not present; he had died earlier, on the descent of the Beardmore, and his body lay somewhere up-glacier, unfound and unfindable.

The objects in the tent told the story of the final days. The sledge-meter, stopped at 895 statute miles from Cape Evans, recorded the distance they had traveled to reach the Pole and return. The diary on Scott’s chest, opened to the entry of 29 March 1912, gave the date of his last written record. The letters, stacked on the fabric of the tent wall, addressed to wives, mothers, friends, the public, established the distribution of final thoughts. The meteorological log, maintained by Wilson to the end, showed temperatures of minus 30 and minus 40 Fahrenheit in the days preceding death. The photograph of Kathleen Scott, frozen into the pages of her husband’s diary, preserved a face that would outlive the watching.

Atkinson’s medical examination confirmed what the arrangement suggested. The bodies were frozen solid, preserved by temperatures that had prevented decomposition. There was no evidence of violence, no indication that the end had come other than by the gradual failure of vital functions in extreme cold. Scurvy had weakened them; starvation had completed the work. The precise sequence—who died first, who last—could not be determined with certainty, though Scott’s position and the diary record suggested he had survived the others by hours or perhaps a day.

The search party worked through the afternoon, documenting what they found. Wright photographed the tent’s interior, the first photographic record of polar death. Atkinson collected the papers, the diaries, the meteorological records, securing them against further damage. Keohane and Cherry-Garrard excavated the sledge that lay buried beside the tent, finding it loaded with geological specimens—thirty-five pounds of Beacon Sandstone from the plateau’s edge, carried through the final desperate march because the expedition’s scientific purposes had survived even the recognition that carrying them was fatal.

They camped that night eleven miles from One Ton Depot, in the same location where five men had died. The tent they pitched was the same model, from the same supply, erected with the same technique. The difference was everything: their health, their provisions, their certainty of return. They burned oil from the depots that had been too far for Scott to reach. They ate food that had been cached for men who could not come to get it. The system that had failed once, catastrophically, functioned now with mechanical precision, delivering them safety in the same coordinates where it had delivered others death.

In the morning they began the work of burial. The bodies could not be moved to Cape Evans; the distance was too great, the conditions too demanding. They would be interred where they lay, in a cairn built from the snow blocks that surrounded the tent. The search party would take what was portable—the records, the photographs, the personal effects—and leave what was not. The tent itself would become the shroud, its green fabric folded over the bodies it had sheltered through the final hours.

Atkinson read the burial service from the Book of Common Prayer, the words drifting across the empty Barrier. The party stood at attention, their breath visible in the cold air. Then they built the cairn, block by block, raising it to a height that would protect the grave from drifting snow. They placed a cross at its summit, inscribed with the names and dates: Scott, Wilson, Bowers, Oates, Evans. The inscription recorded that they had died on the return from the Pole, that they had given their lives for their country, that they had left a record of heroism that would inspire future generations. The formulas of public commemoration, necessary and insufficient.

The search party turned north on 13 November, leaving the cairn behind them. They traveled faster now, unburdened by the anxiety of discovery, propelled by the urgency of carrying their news to the world. The depots they passed—One Ton, Corner Camp, Safety Camp—read differently in this direction: not as stations of hope denied, but as waypoints in a journey completed. They reached Cape Evans on 26 November, having been absent twenty-eight days.

What they brought back was not rescue but verification. The polar party was dead. The causes of death were multiple, cumulative, systemic: the failure of transport, the miscalculation of rations, the inadequacy of depot-laying, the unpredictability of weather, the physical deterioration of men pushed beyond sustainable limits. No single decision had killed them; no single decision could have saved them. The expedition’s design, with its interdependent choices about ponies, dogs, motors, man-hauling, had created a structure that could survive only in favorable conditions. The conditions of March 1912 had not been favorable.

Atkinson wrote his report in the days following return. He detailed the search party’s route, its findings, its methods of documentation and burial. He appended the recovered diaries, the meteorological records, the personal letters. He noted the condition of the depots, the quantities of supplies found, the implications for future expedition planning. The report was factual, comprehensive, restrained. It did not assign blame, though it established the facts from which blame might be inferred.

Cherry-Garrard wrote also, in the journal he had kept through the search. His account differed in tone: more personal, more speculative, more burdened by the particular knowledge of his own earlier journey to One Ton Depot. He recorded his conversations with Atkinson, his observations of Wright’s scientific exactitude, his own emotional responses to the discovery. These writings would become, years later, the basis for his book The Worst Journey in the World, the definitive personal account of the expedition’s catastrophe.

The search party has sighted a cairn eleven miles south of One Ton Depot; what lies beside it is now the only question.
