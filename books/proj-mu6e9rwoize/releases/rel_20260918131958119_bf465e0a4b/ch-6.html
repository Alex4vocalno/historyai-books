Chapter 6

The Winter Quarters of the Mind

Inside the Cape Evans hut, months before the depot ledger closed, the acetylene plant hissed in its annex, its sound cutting through the pervasive cold that seeped through gaps in the felt insulation. The last light had vanished on 23 April 1911, leaving the prefabricated structure stranded at the edge of a frozen sea in a world without sun. Twenty-five men, the shore party, moved between the ice foot outside and the crowded interior, where stores were stacked to the ceiling in the central room. A wall of packing cases, ordered by Scott to maintain naval discipline, divided the officers’ quarters from the men’s sleeping area on the east side. The division was complete save for the shared space where they ate, and where, on certain evenings, one of them would stand and speak.

On 15 May 1911, Edward Wilson opened his portfolio of sketches. The hut was warm enough for men to sit without their outer coats, though they kept them within reach. Wilson had arranged his zoological drawings in sequence: the skuas at Cape Adare, the Weddell seal embryos he had collected during the Discovery expedition, the penguin studies from the previous summer. He held each sheet to the acetylene light. The men sat on packing cases or on their bunks, some with notebooks, others with their hands in their pockets.

Wilson spoke about the embryology of Emperor penguins. He had proposed this study in the Zoology section of the Discovery Expedition’s Scientific Reports. Now he was preparing to attempt it in the winter darkness, a journey to Cape Crozier that would require three men to sledge across thirty miles of sea ice in temperatures that could reach minus sixty degrees Fahrenheit. He did not emphasize the difficulty.

He described instead what they might learn: how the penguin developed inside the egg, what adaptations allowed it to survive in cold that killed other species, whether the anatomy confirmed or contradicted the evolutionary theories then circulating in the journals that reached them months late, if at all. The sketches passed from hand to hand. The men studied the careful pencil work, the annotations in Wilson’s small hand. Someone asked about the coloring. Wilson explained that Ponting would photograph the specimens, that Meares had offered to tint some of the lantern slides for future lectures. The acetylene hissed. The wind moved against the hut walls.

When Wilson finished, Scott thanked him and proposed the next lecture for the following week. The men dispersed to their sleeping bags. The hut settled into the sounds of breathing and the occasional cough. Outside, the dark was complete.

This was the first of the winter lectures. They continued through May, June, July, and into August, when the sun returned. The subjects were recorded in diaries and in the South Polar Times, the expedition newspaper that Cherry-Garrard edited with Shackleton’s nephew from materials contributed by the entire party. Bowers lectured on sledging rations and the mathematics of load distribution. Taylor spoke about the geology of the western mountains. Atkinson described the parasites he had found in seal intestines. Scott himself gave at least one address on the history of Antarctic exploration, tracing the narrative from Cook through Ross to his own first expedition and Shackleton’s.

The lectures served multiple purposes. They maintained the structure of naval routine in an environment that had removed most of its ordinary supports. They allowed men who had been selected for physical endurance to demonstrate intellectual capacities that the expedition’s public profile would later emphasize. And they filled the hours between the evening meal and sleep, hours when the absence of daylight could press on men who had no task requiring their full attention.

The scientific work continued in parallel. The meteorological station operated twenty-four hours a day, with observations recorded every four hours on standardized forms that would eventually reach the British Meteorological Office. The instruments were housed in a screen on a post outside the hut: thermometers dry and wet, barometer, anemometer, the equipment for measuring humidity and wind velocity. Wilson made his sketches in the laboratory corner, working from specimens preserved in formaldehyde or from memory of observations made during the daylight months. He prepared for the winter journey to Cape Crozier by studying the Emperor penguin embryos he had already collected, by consulting his own earlier notes, by calculating the minimum equipment that would allow three men to survive six weeks in the darkness with no possibility of rescue if the sea ice broke or the weather closed in. The calculations were precise. The margin for error was not large.

The South Polar Times appeared at irregular intervals, its production occupying the men on days when outdoor work was impossible. Cherry-Garrard set the type by hand in the small printing press they had brought south. The paper was rationed. The content included parodies, poems, mock advertisements, accounts of depot journeys written in the style of official reports, and contributions from men who had never before attempted literary composition. The newspaper served as a collective project that required cooperation across the rank divisions maintained elsewhere in the hut. An entry in Bowers’s diary for June 1911 noted that the South Polar Times was going well, without elaboration. The surviving copies show the range of the men’s preoccupations: jokes about the food, laments about the cold, serious accounts of scientific work, and—always present but rarely explicit—the awareness of Amundsen somewhere to the east.

The Norwegian’s presence shaped the winter more directly than the surviving documents acknowledge. In Melbourne, six months earlier, Scott had received a cable from Christiania, now Oslo, where Roald Amundsen had composed a message of studied neutrality: he was proceeding south. The document reached Scott on 12 or 13 October 1910, depending on which account one follows. The Terra Nova had sailed for New Zealand before Scott could alter his plans.

Now, in the winter darkness, Amundsen was established at the Bay of Whales, on the Ross Ice Shelf at a point roughly four hundred miles east of Cape Evans. The distance was too great for direct communication. The British party knew of his presence through inference and through the reports of the Terra Nova’s master, who had sighted the Fram in February during the coastal survey that preceded the landing at Cape Evans. Scott had considered a landing at the Bay of Whales himself, had rejected it because the ice conditions were unstable and because his plans required a base closer to the route up the Beardmore Glacier. Amundsen’s choice gave him a shorter distance to the Pole—sixty miles shorter by the calculations Scott’s men made in the hut—but required him to find a new route through the mountains that guarded the plateau.

The race existed as a fact the men could not discuss directly because no official announcement had been made. Scott’s orders from the Admiralty and the Royal Geographical Society specified the conquest of the Pole as the expedition’s primary objective. His public appeal had stated this explicitly. The main objective was to reach the South Pole and to secure for the British Empire the honour of this achievement. There were other objectives, both scientific and geographical. Wilson insisted that the scientific work would make the bagging of the Pole merely an item in the results.

But the bagging mattered. It mattered to the governments that had contributed funds, to the subscribers who had answered the public appeal, to the naval officers who understood exploration in terms of priority and claim. Amundsen’s cable had transformed a planned achievement into a competition. The winter lectures, the newspaper, the meticulous meteorological observations—all acquired a secondary meaning as preparation for the test that would come with the return of the sun.

The private record shows the pressure more clearly than the public one. Scott’s diary, maintained with the regularity his naval training demanded, recorded the daily weather, the progress of scientific work, the health of the men and animals. It also recorded, increasingly as the winter advanced, his reflections on leadership and on the nature of the task before him.

On 5 June 1911, he noted that the winter was passing pleasantly, a formula that appears in several entries. On 21 June, mid-winter day, he described the celebrations: the decorated tree, the gifts, the speeches, the milk punch that Clissold the cook had prepared. The entry for that day ran to several pages, unusual for Scott, suggesting an effort to commemorate what he recognized as a turning point. After mid-winter, the days would lengthen. The preparations for the southern journey would accelerate. The abstract goal would become concrete in the loading of sledges, the killing of ponies to lay depots, the final selection of the party that would make the attempt.

Lawrence Oates’s diary, preserved in fragments, shows a different pattern. The cavalry officer who had joined the expedition to manage the ponies recorded his doubts about their condition with increasing directness. The animals had suffered during the voyage south. They had recovered somewhat in the relative shelter of Cape Evans, but the winter confinement in the snow wall enclosure had weakened them. Oates noted their weight loss, their susceptibility to cold, the difficulty of maintaining their condition on the ration of compressed fodder the expedition had brought.

His entries for June and July 1911 contain repeated references to the ponies that suggest a mind returning obsessively to a problem without solution. The ponies were essential to Scott’s plan for the depot-laying journey that would precede the polar attempt. They could carry more weight than dogs over the initial stages of the route. They would be killed at successive depots to provide fresh meat for the returning parties. But they required snow surfaces firm enough to support their weight, temperatures moderate enough to prevent frostbite in their unprotected hooves and lungs, and a rate of travel that would not exhaust them before they reached their designated endpoints. Oates’s diary suggests he recognized that these conditions would not obtain, that the plan assumed a cooperation from weather and ice that the Antarctic environment was unlikely to provide.

The contrast between Scott’s public confidence and Oates’s private doubt defines the psychological structure of the winter. The hut was too small for secrets. The men knew the ponies were failing. They knew the motor sledges had already proved unreliable during the brief trial run in April, before the sun set. They knew the depots laid during that trial were insufficiently marked, their locations recorded with a precision that assumed landmarks visible only in daylight. The information circulated in conversations during the working day, in the shared sleeping quarters, in the silence of men lying in their bags listening to the wind.

But the official narrative, maintained in the lectures and the newspaper and Scott’s diary, emphasized progress and preparation. Between what was known and what was said ran a particular kind of tension, a collective holding of knowledge that could not be acknowledged without threatening the morale the expedition required.

Wilson’s diary, more extensive than Scott’s and more candid in its scientific observations, provides the bridge between public and private. His entries record the daily meteorological observations with the precision required by the forms: temperature, barometric pressure, wind direction and force, cloud cover, precipitation, visibility. They also record his own state of mind with a reticence that was itself informative.

On 23 April 1911, the day the sun set, he noted simply that the sun was gone. In the weeks that followed, he described his work on the penguin embryos, his conversations with Cherry-Garrard and Bowers about the winter journey, his reading of the geological reports from the western party. The entries grow longer in June, as the darkness reached its maximum and the psychological demands of the environment intensified.

He recorded dreams, unusual for him, in which he was walking in England. He noted the difficulty of maintaining concentration on detailed work when the absence of daylight disrupted his sense of time. And he described the physical symptoms of the winter: the dryness of the air that cracked skin and lips, the static electricity that sparked from every metal surface, the persistent cough that affected several of the men and that he suspected was related to the acetylene lighting.

The winter journey to Cape Crozier, which Wilson proposed and Scott approved, emerged from this context as a project that served scientific and psychological purposes simultaneously. The Emperor penguins bred in the darkness of the Antarctic winter, incubating their eggs on their feet in colonies on the sea ice. No previous expedition had observed this process directly. Wilson believed that the embryology of these birds might provide evidence about the evolutionary history of flightless birds, possibly connecting them to reptilian ancestors. The scientific value was genuine. But the journey also gave three men—Wilson, Bowers, and Cherry-Garrard—a defined task during the period of maximum darkness, a reason to endure the conditions that pressed on everyone. Scott’s approval of the plan, recorded in his diary on 20 May 1911, noted that the right men had gone to attempt it, a formulation that acknowledged the risk while asserting confidence in the outcome.

The preparation for the winter journey occupied June and early July. The three men tested their equipment on short trips from the hut, establishing the minimum clothing that would allow survival, the maximum load the sledge could carry, the techniques for camping in darkness and extreme cold. They would travel with two sledges, one tied behind the other, carrying sleeping bags, camping equipment, six weeks’ provisions, and the scientific gear for preserving specimens. The total weight was 253 pounds per man, a figure Cherry-Garrard recorded with the precision that characterized all his later accounts. They would follow the coast of Ross Island to Cape Crozier, then descend to the sea ice where the penguin colony was located. The return would follow the same route, unless conditions forced a change. If they failed to return by their scheduled date, there was no possibility of rescue. The hut would assume they were dead.

The winter journey departed on 27 June 1911, in the darkest period of the year. Scott recorded the departure in his diary with a formality that masked his anxiety. This winter travel was a new and bold venture. The three men were visible for a few minutes as they pulled their sledges away from the hut, then absorbed into the darkness that extended from the ice foot to the mountains and beyond. The hut returned to its routines. The meteorological observations continued. The lectures resumed. Oates continued his notes on the declining condition of the ponies. Scott continued his preparations for the southern journey that would begin when the sun returned. The absence of the three men created a space in the hut’s life that was filled, as such absences always were, with work and with the effort not to imagine what might be happening beyond the reach of light.

Wilson’s diary of the winter journey, preserved despite the conditions of its composition, describes an experience at the outer limit of human endurance. The temperature reached minus 77 degrees Fahrenheit. The darkness was complete except for the aurora and the occasional appearance of the moon. The sea ice cracked and shifted, requiring constant attention to the route. The tent, a modified version of the standard expedition model, proved inadequate to the wind at Cape Crozier, forcing the men to shelter behind a snow wall they built and rebuilt as the storms eroded it.

They collected three Emperor penguin eggs, the scientific objective of the journey, at the cost of frostbite, exhaustion, and what Wilson later described as a temporary loss of the will to survive.

They returned to Cape Evans on 1 August 1911, thirty-five days after their departure, having traveled approximately three hundred miles. All three survived. The eggs, carefully preserved in the venesta case, would eventually reach the Natural History Museum in London, where they would be examined and found to contain embryos too degraded to provide the evidence Wilson had sought.

The return of the winter journey party coincided with the first signs of the sun’s return. On 4 August 1911, a faint glow appeared on the northern horizon. By mid-August, the sun was above the horizon for several hours each day. The light transformed the environment, making visible the changes that darkness had concealed. The sea ice had shifted. The pressure ridges had grown. The snow surfaces that the ponies would be required to cross were softer than Oates had feared, the result of wind redistribution during the winter storms. The men who had remained at the hut emerged into the light with the pallor and tentative movements of convalescents. The three who had been to Cape Crozier moved more slowly still, their frostbite healing, their bodies recovering from the weight loss that the journey had imposed.

The final weeks of winter were occupied with preparation for the southern journey. Scott announced the composition of the parties that would lay the depots, the dates of their departure, the routes they would follow. The motor sledges, repaired after their April failure, were tested again and found still unreliable. The ponies were harnessed and exercised, their condition confirming Oates’s doubts without altering the plan that required them. The dogs, managed by Meares and the Russian dog driver Dimitri, were in better shape, but Scott’s strategy assigned them a secondary role, supporting rather than leading the advance. The depots laid in April were relocated and remarked, their positions verified against the original survey. The supplies were checked against the lists, the pemmican and biscuit and oil that would sustain the parties on the ice shelf and the plateau beyond.

The psychological transformation that the winter had accomplished became visible in these final preparations. The men who had lectured on their specialties, who had edited the newspaper, who had maintained the meteorological station with naval precision, were now focused on a single objective. The scientific work continued—Wilson prepared his specimens for the journey, Taylor organized the geological collections, the meteorological observations were maintained without interruption—but it occupied a secondary place in the collective consciousness. The race with Amundsen, never acknowledged in official documents, shaped every decision about timing and route. The knowledge that the Norwegian was already on the ice shelf, possibly already ascending his chosen route through the mountains, created an urgency that compressed the preparation schedule and eliminated the margin for error that a more deliberate approach might have preserved.

Scott’s diary for the final days of winter recorded this compression without acknowledging its source. On 24 August 1911, he noted that the sun was now making itself felt. On 1 September, he wrote that preparations for the southern journey were well advanced. The entries are brief, functional, dominated by lists of tasks completed and tasks remaining. The reflective tone that had characterized the midwinter entries has disappeared, replaced by a focus on immediate execution. The leader who had organized lectures and approved newspaper publication is now entirely occupied with the logistics of sledging: the weights, the rations, the selection of men and animals, the coordination of multiple parties that will depart at intervals to lay the depots that the polar party will require for its return.

The men who would form that polar party were not yet finally selected. Scott had indicated that he would lead it, and his choice of companions would be determined by their performance during the depot-laying journeys. Wilson was certain, by virtue of his scientific role and his proven capacity for endurance. Bowers, returned from Cape Crozier with his energy apparently undiminished, was likely. Oates’s position was complicated by his responsibility for the ponies, which would require him to accompany the initial stages of the southern journey regardless of his ultimate destination. Evans and Cherry-Garrard were possibilities, their selection dependent on factors that Scott did not disclose. The uncertainty was itself a form of pressure, requiring men to demonstrate their value in circumstances where the criteria for judgment were not fully specified.

The sun returned to Cape Evans with the ceremonial completeness that the winter had made unimaginable. On 26 August 1911, the full disk was visible above the horizon for the first time since April. The men observed it from the ice foot or from the veranda of the hut, some with smoked glass, others with the naked eye against the snow glare that would soon become an occupational hazard. The light revealed the environment in its full extent: the frozen sea, the volcanic slopes of Ross Island, the distant mountains of the mainland, the route south that would lead across the ice shelf to the Beardmore Glacier and the plateau beyond.

The view was magnificent and forbidding. The men who saw it had been changed by the winter. They had endured darkness and cold and the psychological strain of isolation. They had developed a collective identity that emphasized stoicism, duty, and the pursuit of knowledge as its own justification. And they had, in ways that none of them fully recognized, committed themselves to a conception of exploration that valued character over adaptation, perseverance over prudence, the maintenance of effort over the recognition of limits.

The winter had been an active forge. It had shaped the expedition’s psychology through the daily routines of scientific work, the collective projects of lectures and newspaper production, the private struggles recorded in diaries that would not be read until after the outcome was known. The men who emerged into the August light were harder than they had been in April, more certain of their purposes, more resistant to doubt. This hardness was their strength and their vulnerability. It would carry them across the ice shelf and up the glacier and onto the plateau where the Pole waited. It would also constrain their choices in circumstances where flexibility might have preserved their lives. The sun illuminated men who had been psychologically conditioned for an ordeal defined by character, not merely by geography. The conditioning was complete. The ordeal was about to begin.
