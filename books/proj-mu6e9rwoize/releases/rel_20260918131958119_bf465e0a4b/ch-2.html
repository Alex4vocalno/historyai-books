Chapter 2

The Rival’s Telegram

The coal smoke from the Melbourne docks still clung to the deck of the Terra Nova when the envelope reached Scott. He was in his cabin, or perhaps at the hotel on Collins Street where he had taken rooms to escape the ship’s cramped quarters—the sources differ on the precise location, agreeing only on the date, October 12 or 13, 1910, and on the document itself. It was a cable form, the kind generated by electrical impulses through undersea cables that spanned the British Empire, carrying commerce and command across latitudes that ships still measured in weeks. The message had originated in Christiania, now Oslo, where Roald Amundsen had composed it with the same care he applied to navigation: sufficient information to create obligation, insufficient to create clarity.

Scott read it twice. His journal, maintained with the regularity of a naval officer who understood that command decisions required documentary justification, records the fact of reception and the quality of his response. The telegram stated: “Beg leave to inform you Fram proceeding Antarctic. Amundsen.” Nothing more. No destination within that continent. No objective named. The Norwegian’s ship was southbound, but whether toward the Ross Sea, the Weddell Sea, or some coastal survey of secondary importance remained deliberately obscured. Amundsen had spent the northern summer preparing what the world understood as an Arctic expedition, a drift across the polar sea to challenge the disputed claims of Cook and Peary. His equipment, his personnel, his public statements had all been oriented northward. The telegram announced a change in theater without explaining the abandonment of purpose.

The Terra Nova lay at anchor in Port Phillip Bay, where the water ran brown with river silt and the late Australian spring carried the smell of eucalyptus through the rigging. The ship had arrived from Durban, where Scott had rejoined her after his fundraising passage on a faster liner. Now she was coaling, watering, taking on fresh meat for the dogs. The ship’s log for October 12 records these operations with the impersonality of maritime routine: 125 tons of coal, customs clearance, mail departure. The private crisis occasioned by the telegram appears only in Scott’s journal and in subsequent recollections, where the contrast between public procedure and private deliberation becomes visible.

Scott called his officers. Lieutenant Edward Evans, the second-in-command, was present. Victor Campbell, the navigator, attended. Edward Wilson, chief of scientific staff and the expedition’s moral center, read the message in silence. The composition of this council reflected the expedition’s structure: naval command, technical expertise, scientific purpose. Each represented a constituency that the telegram affected differently. Evans, ambitious and competitive, would see opportunity in confrontation. Campbell, methodical, would calculate distances and dates. Wilson, who had joined for the study of emperor penguin embryology at Cape Crozier, would measure the threat to research against the claims of national prestige.

The discussion that followed is not recorded verbatim. Scott’s journal summarizes rather than transcribes, noting the division of opinion without attributing specific positions to named individuals. Some officers argued for acceleration: press south immediately, establish the base at Cape Evans or Cape Royds without the planned interval in New Zealand, begin depot-laying in the earliest possible season. Others urged adherence to the original schedule, pointing out that Amundsen’s intentions remained unknown and that haste risked the very thoroughness that distinguished this expedition from mere adventure. The sources permit us to reconstruct these positions from subsequent decisions and from the characters of the men involved, but we cannot recover the words spoken in that cabin or hotel room where the Australian spring pressed against windows glazed for English winters.

Scott’s decision, recorded in his journal, was to maintain the planned course. The Terra Nova would proceed to Lyttelton, New Zealand, take on final stores according to the original manifest, and sail for the Ross Sea in November or December as scheduled. The telegram would be answered with silence or with formal acknowledgment that revealed nothing of Scott’s own intentions. The expedition would not become a race unless Amundsen forced the issue by declaring his destination.

This resolution has accumulated contradictory judgments. Later historians have praised Scott’s steadiness, his refusal to abandon scientific method for competitive panic. Others have condemned it as fatal complacency, a misreading that allowed Amundsen to establish a head start never to be overcome. The evidence sustains both interpretations because the evidence is incomplete. What is documented is the decision itself and its immediate consequences: the ship’s log resuming its routine, the coaling continuing, the dogs exercising on the dock where they attracted crowds of curious Melburnians.

The photographer Herbert Ponting set up his equipment to record this scene. His surviving images show the Terra Nova at anchor, the dogs in their traces, the officers in uniforms ill-suited to the Australian heat. The pictures preserve a party still confident, still publicly committed to the scientific objectives that had been announced at departure. The competitive shadow that had entered with the telegram is not visible in these exposures. It existed only in documents and conversations, in the journal entries that Scott composed each evening, in the calculations that Evans made regarding distances to the pole and the time required to establish supply depots.

The telegram’s obscurity was its power. Amundsen had composed it with full knowledge of its effect. He had spent years preparing for the Arctic, only to have that objective rendered meaningless by the disputed claims of Cook and Peary. His letter to Fridtjof Nansen, written during this period and later preserved, explains the necessity that drove him south: the North Pole claims had dealt a “death blow” to his original plans, forcing him to seek “forgiveness” for this change of theater while hoping that his achievements would nevertheless satisfy his supporters. This private explanation never accompanied the public telegram. Scott received only the announcement of movement, not the rationale for abandonment.

The asymmetry was deliberate and consequential. Amundsen had kept his revised objective secret even from his own crew until the moment of revelation at Madeira, when the Fram had already turned southward. His hundred Greenland hounds, his Arctic clothing, his skis and sledges designed for cold—all had been acquired for the north, then redirected without public explanation. The secrecy extended to his precise destination within the Antarctic. The telegram notified Scott of competition without defining its terms, creating a space of uncertainty that would compress over the following weeks into certainty of confrontation, but on ground chosen by the Norwegian.

Scott’s expedition had been designed for different values. The three years of planning that preceded the Terra Nova’s departure from Cardiff had emphasized comprehensive scientific investigation. Twenty-six specialists had been assembled: geologists, biologists, meteorologists, physicists, a staff larger than any previous Antarctic expedition. The motor sledges, untested in polar conditions, represented a gamble on technological progress. The Manchurian ponies represented a gamble on animal transport that Scott preferred to the dogs he imperfectly understood. The whole apparatus was calculated for methodical advance: depot-laying in the first season, the southern journey in the second, a return governed by the calendar of Antarctic summer rather than by competitive urgency.

Amundsen’s telegram struck at the center of this calculation. It introduced a variable that the design had not anticipated: a rival timeline. The methodical approach became, in competitive conditions, a liability. Yet the competitive conditions were not fully established. The Norwegian’s destination remained unknown. The South Pole was one possibility among several. The magnetic pole, the exploration of King Edward VII Land, the survey of unknown coastlines—any of these might attract a competitor without necessarily threatening Scott’s primary objective. The telegram’s vagueness forced preparation for maximum competition while publicly maintaining original purpose, a dual posture that consumed energy and distorted decision-making.

The immediate practical effects manifested in small adjustments. Scott authorized the purchase of additional dog food in New Zealand, supplementing supplies already loaded. He reviewed the depot-laying plan with Evans, calculating whether earlier parties could establish supply points farther south than originally intended. He considered, and rejected, the possibility of landing a preliminary party to begin work before the main body arrived. Each option carried risks that the original schedule had been designed to avoid. Early depot-laying required early access to the Ross Ice Shelf, which might not be navigable. Additional dog food consumed weight that could have been allocated to scientific equipment. A preliminary party would divide forces before base establishment, violating principles of concentration that Scott had learned in his first Antarctic command.

The rejection of haste was consistent with Scott’s character and training. He was a naval officer, accustomed to plans approved by committees and executed with due regard for contingency. His first expedition, the Discovery voyage of 1901–1904, had been marked by thoroughness rather than daring. His leadership style emphasized consultation and documentation. The journal he maintained was not merely personal record but formal account, written with awareness that Admiralty officials, the Royal Geographical Society, future historians might read it. The telegram from Amundsen entered this documentary stream as administrative problem, not as provocation to improvisation.

Yet the administrative response itself imposed costs. The expedition’s scientific staff had been selected for specialist skills, not for capacity in competitive sledging. Wilson, in particular, had joined for the emperor penguin study that would require the winter journey to Cape Crozier—a purpose that the pole, contested or uncontested, could not advance. The telegram threatened to subordinate all such purposes to a single symbolic destination, devaluing the knowledge production that had justified the expedition’s expense and complexity.

Wilson’s reaction to the news is not recorded in detail. His diary for October 1910, preserved in the Scott Polar Research Institute, shows attention fixed on practical work: the condition of the dogs, the organization of scientific stores, the health of the men. He was, by temperament and training, a physician who accepted discomfort as price of observation. The competitive implications of Amundsen’s movement may have concerned him less than their effect on Scott’s equilibrium. Wilson had known Scott since the Discovery expedition. He had observed his commander’s sensitivity to criticism, his need for recognition, his tendency toward depression when progress seemed slow. The telegram arrived when Scott already carried the burden of fundraising, of public expectation, of the ship’s mechanical and biological uncertainties. Wilson’s role, as he understood it, was to sustain morale while protecting the scientific program from competitive distortion.

This protective effort would prove insufficient. The telegram’s effect was cumulative rather than immediate. It established a frame of reference that would govern subsequent decisions, from the loading at Lyttelton to the composition of the final polar party. Each choice that followed would be made in Amundsen’s unacknowledged shadow. The shadow was not visible to the journalists who interviewed Scott about his scientific objectives, or to the crowds who gathered to see the strange ship with its cargo of animals and machines. But it altered the expedition’s internal weather, the atmosphere of its deliberations, the criteria by which success and failure would eventually be measured.

The asymmetry of information extended to method as well as destination. Amundsen had committed entirely to dogs, learned from the Netsilik Inuit during his Arctic years, acquired a transport monopoly efficient and proven. Scott’s mixed system—motors, ponies, dogs—was inherently slower, requiring maintenance of multiple supply chains, multiple veterinary regimens, multiple failure modes. The telegram revealed Amundsen’s route but not his method. Scott could infer from general knowledge that the Norwegian was experienced, disciplined, prepared for cold. He could not know that his rival had optimized for speed while he himself had optimized for comprehensiveness. The uncertainty prevented abandonment of the mixed approach, yet the mixed approach could not be accelerated into competitiveness without accepting risks that its design had been intended to eliminate.

The geographical coordinates of Port Phillip Bay placed Scott at a critical distance from the information networks that might have clarified Amundsen’s intentions. Melbourne was no longer the imperial outpost it had been during the gold rushes, yet it remained a node of colonial administration where telegraphic communication arrived with frustrating delays and partial coverage. The cable from Christiania had traveled through London, through the relay stations of the Eastern Telegraph Company, accumulating in its transmission the very obscurity that Amundsen had planted at its origin. Scott could not cable directly to Norway for clarification; he could not consult with the Royal Geographical Society without weeks of delay. The expedition was already in motion, committed to a southern trajectory that would carry it beyond easy consultation with any authority that might interpret the Norwegian’s silence.

This isolation was structural rather than incidental. Antarctic expeditions of this era operated as sovereign enterprises once they cleared their final port, their commanders invested with powers of decision that would not be reviewed until return or disaster. Scott understood this condition from his Discovery experience, when winter quarters at Hut Point had placed his party beyond effective communication with the outside world for two consecutive years. The telegram arrived at the precise moment when this isolation was about to become absolute, when the Terra Nova would pass beyond the reach of cables and newspapers into the seasonal rhythm of the southern ice. Amundsen had chosen his timing with navigational precision, ensuring that his message would reach Scott when verification was impossible and response was constrained by imminent departure.

The commercial context of the Melbourne stop compounded these pressures. The expedition’s finances, never secure, required visible progress to satisfy subscribers and the British government alike. Scott had spent the northern summer on fundraising passage, leaving his ship and officers to proceed without him, a division of attention that the telegram now rendered potentially costly. Every day in port consumed coal and provisions; every delay in New Zealand would compress the available season for depot-laying. The expedition’s commercial sponsors—Messrs. William Beardmore and Company, the Daily Mail, the various scientific societies—had invested in a timeline that the Norwegian’s intervention threatened to disrupt. Scott’s decision to maintain schedule was therefore not merely strategic but fiduciary, a protection of commitments already made to institutions that would not look kindly on competitive improvisation.

The physical condition of the expedition’s transport animals reinforced the case for deliberation over haste. The dogs, acquired hastily in Siberia and inadequately quarantined, had already suffered mortality on the passage from Cardiff. The ponies, purchased in Manchuria by the Russian expert Anton Omelchenko, were unfamiliar to their handlers and untested in shipboard conditions. To accelerate the schedule would be to demand performance from biological systems already under stress, to risk the very foundation of the expedition’s mobility before it reached the ice. Scott’s journal entries from this period show attention to veterinary detail that later critics would dismiss as excessive caution but that contemporary practice recognized as necessary prudence. The telegram’s demand for speed collided with the biological reality of animal adaptation, a collision that would recur throughout the expedition’s subsequent history.

The officers’ council that considered the telegram operated within these multiple constraints: informational, financial, biological, temporal. Their deliberations, however unrecorded in verbatim form, can be reconstructed from the subsequent distribution of responsibilities. Evans was assigned to review the depot-laying calculations, a task that acknowledged his navigational expertise while channeling his competitive energy into planning rather than advocacy. Campbell received responsibility for the ship’s routing to Lyttelton, ensuring that navigational decisions remained separated from strategic speculation. Wilson, characteristically, was asked to assess the medical implications of any accelerated schedule, a role that preserved his scientific focus while contributing to the collective decision. This division of labor suggests a commander seeking to contain the telegram’s disruptive potential through institutional routine, distributing its pressure across functional specialties rather than concentrating it in executive deliberation.

The documentary record of this period reveals a characteristic pattern in Scott’s command style: the transformation of crisis into paperwork. Where Amundsen operated through secrecy and sudden announcement, Scott responded to uncertainty through elaboration of plan. The additional dog food authorized for New Zealand purchase required requisition forms, budget adjustments, correspondence with the expedition’s business manager. The review of depot-laying possibilities generated memoranda, draft schedules, alternative scenarios that were filed rather than implemented. This administrative thickening served multiple purposes. It provided employment for anxious energy. It created audit trails that would justify subsequent decisions. It preserved the appearance of methodical progress against the disorientation of competitive surprise. Whether it advanced the expedition’s practical readiness remains debatable; that it advanced Scott’s psychological equilibrium seems probable from the journal’s increasingly formal tone in the days following the telegram’s receipt.

The contrast with Amundsen’s documented methods during this same period illuminates the structural difference between the two enterprises. While Scott’s officers debated in Melbourne harbor, the Norwegian was proceeding south with a crew that had learned of their destination only at Madeira, too committed to withdraw, too isolated to dissent. His ship carried no scientific staff whose purposes required protection from competitive distortion. His dogs, his equipment, his entire logistical system had been optimized for a single objective that his crew now shared by necessity if not by prior choice.

The Terra Nova sailed from Melbourne on October 17, 1910. The log records the time of weighing anchor, the pilot’s departure, the course set for New Zealand. The telegram had been received, discussed, filed. The ship carried its news southward, toward the next port, the next season, the ice that waited beyond the horizon. Scott wrote in his journal of the need for steady nerves and methodical preparation. He did not write of race. The word was not yet spoken, the concept not yet admitted. But the structure of competition was already in place, a grid of urgency and comparison that would constrain every subsequent movement.

In Lyttelton, the final preparations would proceed according to plan modified by anxiety. The dogs would be supplemented. The ponies would be inspected. The motors would be crated for the passage through the Southern Ocean. The men would write their last letters home, uncertain whether they were participants in a scientific expedition or competitors in an undeclared contest. The telegram had made both conditions simultaneously true, without providing the clarity that would allow choice between them. The expedition sailed on, now shadowed by a rival timeline, its original methodical logic under strain.
