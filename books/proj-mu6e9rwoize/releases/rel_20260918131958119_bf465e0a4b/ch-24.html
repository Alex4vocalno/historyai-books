Chapter 24

The Last Sledges of the Season

Seen from above, the whole machinery of empire had swallowed the news. Telegrams had traveled through undersea cables, each relay adding delay and distortion, until the Admiralty in London possessed the bare facts. Now the survivors at Cape Evans waited not for rescue but for instruction, for the acknowledgment that their work was finished. The wireless masts stood against the sky, but no answering signal came; the Terra Nova was somewhere in the southern ocean, her hold perhaps carrying supplies for a second winter that no one wished to spend. In that interval, the men faced a season that would not wait: five dead, a tent on the ice, a race lost before it was known to be run.

From above, the Ross Ice Shelf would have appeared as it always had: a vast white plain extending toward an invisible horizon, its surface marked by the geometry of human intention. The depots lay at regular intervals, each positioned by theodolite and sextant, each marked by a flag or black banner that had faded from wind and sun. These piles of snow-covered stores had been placed with precision and hope in the summer of 1911–1912, when the Polar Party marched south and supporting parties turned back at successive latitudes. The mathematics had seemed sound: distance divided by consumption, weight distributed across stages, a pathway built of pemmican and oil that would carry men to the Pole and back again.

Now that system stood empty of its purpose. The northernmost cache, One Ton Depot at 79°29’S, had been reached by no one living.

What remained—food, fuel, equipment, the material investment of two years’ labor—still possessed value in a world where such resources were measured against debt and obligation. To leave them would compound failure with waste.

The Antarctic summer of 1912–1913 was brief and already fading. Ice that had softened enough to allow sledging would soon harden into a surface too rough for travel, then break apart into pressure ridges and crevasses no party could cross. The men at Cape Evans understood this calendar. They had lived by it for two years, had watched the sun dip below the horizon and return, had learned to read the subtle signs of seasonal change in a landscape that appeared static. They knew that active operations must conclude by mid-January if they were to escape before the Terra Nova’s expected arrival. The window for movement measured weeks, not months.

The sledging season opened with small parties moving out from Cape Evans, not toward discovery but toward retrieval. Tryggve Gran, the young Norwegian who had accompanied the search party that found Scott’s tent in November, led one such journey. His diary records the departure on December 5, 1912: three men, two sledges, a load of empty bags and broken-down tents. Their destination was One Ton Depot, the cache placed by Teddy Evans’s party in February 1911 to sustain the Polar Party on their return march. The depot had been untouched when the search party passed it in November, its flag still flying, its contents undisturbed by the men who had died eleven miles to the south. Now Gran’s party would salvage what they could carry.

The journey south followed a track already marked by tragedy. Gran had been there before, had stood at the tent where Scott, Wilson, and Bowers lay dead, had helped read the final diary entries and recover the documents that would become the expedition’s official record. To retrace those miles with empty sledges, then load them with the supplies that might have saved the Polar Party had they reached them, imposed a particular weight on the work.

The ski tracks from November were still visible in places, frozen into the snow surface as permanent as any engraving. The men did not speak of what they saw.

The depot ledger, when they reached it, recorded stores placed by Evans’s party: oil by the gallon, biscuit and pemmican by the pound, tobacco and chocolate in tins that had waited through two winters. Gran’s men loaded what their sledges could hold—fuel above all, the critical resource that had run out for the Polar Party—and turned north.

Why this labor, when the dead could not be helped and the living might simply wait for the ship? The question answers itself through the structure of the expedition itself.

The British Antarctic Expedition was a private venture financed by public contribution and a government grant, with further backing from the Admiralty, which released experienced seamen to the expedition, and from the Royal Geographical Society. It operated within an economy of obligation that transcended individual survival.

Every tin of fuel recovered was a tin that need not be purchased and shipped from England. Every pound of food was a reduction in the debt the expedition owed its creditors, the subscribers who had paid for a share in imperial achievement. The men who performed this labor understood that their own return depended on the same system of depots and caches that had failed the Polar Party.

To salvage those stores was to preserve their own lifeline, to maintain the infrastructure of polar travel against future need.

The contrast with the depot-laying journeys of 1911–1912 could not have been starker. Then, the expedition had moved outward from Cape Evans in a surge of organized optimism. Scott had divided his men into supporting parties, each with specific turning points, each depositing supplies for the stages ahead.

The motor sledges had been tried and abandoned within days, their engines failing in the cold. The ponies had struggled and died, their flesh added to the depot stores in a grim economy of conversion. But the system itself—the mathematics of distance, weight, and consumption—had seemed sound. The men had worked with the knowledge that they were building a pathway to the Pole and back, that each mile of sledge haul was an investment in ultimate success.

Now the same mathematics governed a retreat. The depots that had been placed to enable advance were being stripped to enable withdrawal, their contents flowing backward toward the base in a reversal of the original intention.

Throughout December and early January, small parties moved across the ice shelf and up the Beardmore Glacier, gathering what remained of the expedition’s investment. The work was methodical, unsentimental, governed by naval discipline and the practical necessity of accounting. At Cape Evans, the hut that Scott’s party had erected in 1911 served as the base of operations. Scott’s Hut, a building located on the north shore of Cape Evans on Ross Island, stood as a wooden structure insulated with seaweed and lined with stores that had been designed for a scientific campaign of indefinite duration. Now it became a site of inventory and packing, its interior crowded with crates and specimens, its tables covered with the paperwork of dissolution.

Edward Atkinson, as senior naval officer, directed this work. He had been left in command when Scott marched south, had organized the search parties in the spring, had composed the official report of the disaster that would be transmitted through the wireless and carried by the Terra Nova to New Zealand. Now he presided over the expedition’s practical closure. His authority was administrative rather than charismatic; he was a surgeon by training, accustomed to the management of bodies rather than the inspiration of men. But he understood that the work of completion mattered as much as the work of discovery. The expedition had committed to science, to geography, to the advancement of knowledge. Those commitments survived the death of the leader and four of his companions. To abandon them would be to render the deaths meaningless in a ledger that measured achievement against cost.

The meteorological records—daily observations of temperature, wind, barometric pressure, cloud cover—were collated and secured. Two years of continuous observation represented one of the longest such runs in Antarctic history, a dataset that would outlast any individual narrative of heroism or failure. The geological specimens collected on the southern journey, hauled at enormous cost across the ice shelf and up the glacier, were crated for shipment: rocks from the polar plateau, the Transantarctic Mountains, the volcanic formations of Ross Island. The photographic negatives, the zoological samples, the magnetic observations: all were processed, labeled, and stored against the return of the ship. This was the work of institutional survival, the transformation of experience into evidence that could be circulated through the networks of science and empire.

The sledging parties faced conditions that varied with the season and the terrain. On the ice shelf, the surface was generally level but the temperature unpredictable. A day of relative warmth, with the thermometer rising toward zero, could be followed by a blizzard that pinned the men in their tents for forty-eight hours. The wind came from the south, from the interior of the continent, carrying snow that reduced visibility to a few yards and filled the tracks behind them as they traveled. The parties learned to read the sky, to recognize the dark band on the southern horizon that preceded a blow. They had no wireless communication with Cape Evans. Once beyond the range of visual signal, they were entirely self-sufficient, dependent on the same equipment and rations that had failed the Polar Party. The irony was not lost on them, but it was not spoken.

The Beardmore Glacier presented different challenges. The route up the glacier to the polar plateau had been pioneered by Shackleton in 1908 and followed by Scott in 1911. It was known to be crevasse-ridden, with hidden fractures in the ice that could swallow a sledge and its team without warning. The search party in November had found the remains of the Polar Party’s ascent: abandoned equipment, the bones of ponies, the marks of camps where men had rested before the final push to the plateau. Now the salvage parties moved through this landscape of prior failure, gathering what could be recovered. They found sleeping bags, cookers, spare clothing, scientific instruments—objects that had been carried at enormous labor and discarded when the men could no longer haul them. Some of this material was worth retrieving. Some was left where it lay, too damaged or too heavy to justify the effort of transport.

The question of what to save and what to abandon was not merely practical. It was a judgment on value, on what mattered from the expedition’s investment in a situation where not everything could be preserved. The scientific records were non-negotiable; they represented the expedition’s official purpose, the justification for its funding and its place in the imperial project. The personal effects of the dead—diaries, letters, photographs—had already been recovered by the search party and secured at Cape Evans. What remained was the infrastructure of polar travel: sledges, tents, cooking gear, the mechanical equipment of survival. Some of this would be shipped back to England for museum display, transformed from working tools to relics of sacrifice. Some would be left to freeze into the ice, future archaeological evidence for a later age of exploration that would judge their methods against its own.

Gran’s party returned from One Ton Depot on December 12, having covered approximately 270 miles in eight days. Their sledges were loaded with fuel and food that would now sustain the remaining men through the final months of waiting. The depot itself was not emptied; the salvage parties took only what they could use or carry, leaving the remainder to the ice. In later years, explorers would find these caches, recognize the markings, understand the system that had placed them. The depots became archaeological sites, evidence of a particular way of organizing polar travel that had reached its limit in the winter of 1912.

Other parties worked the western route, toward Castle Rock and the Ferrar Glacier, recovering stores laid for the geological expedition that had operated in the dry valleys. This had been the work of a scientific party conducted in parallel with the polar attempt, equally dependent on the depot system, equally exposed to the risks of weather and distance. Those men had survived, had returned to Cape Evans with their specimens and their data, their depots now redundant and available for salvage. The pattern repeated across the expedition’s operational area: resources deployed for multiple objectives, now being gathered back into a single point of departure.

The tempo of these operations accelerated as January advanced. The Terra Nova was expected in McMurdo Sound in late January or early February, bringing supplies for a second winter or, more probably, orders for evacuation. The ship’s arrival would close the window for sledging. Whatever remained on the ice when she sailed would be abandoned for at least a year, possibly forever, as the ice shifted and the crevasses opened and closed in their slow geological rhythm. Atkinson drove the parties hard, knowing that the expedition’s accounts would be scrutinized in London, that every pound of salvaged material would count in the reckoning of cost against achievement, that the Admiralty and the subscribers would ask what had been saved from the investment they had made.

The men who performed this work were not the same men who had set out in 1910. Death and departure had reduced the original complement of thirty-three. Tryggve Gran would return to Norway, his expertise in skiing no longer required in a country that had already reached the Pole and claimed the priority that Scott had sought. Some of the naval ratings would transfer to other ships, other stations, their Antarctic service noted in records that would follow them through their careers. The scientific staff—meteorologists, biologists, geologists—would carry their data back to the institutions that had sponsored them, their individual contributions to be extracted and published in papers that might or might not acknowledge the cost of their collection. The expedition as a living community was dissolving, its members dispersing into the larger structures of navy, academy, empire.

The final sledging parties returned to Cape Evans in mid-January 1913. Their sledges were unloaded, the salvaged stores inventoried and added to the hut’s already crowded interior. The meteorological records were complete, the geological collections crated, the photographic archive assembled. With the active operations concluded, the remaining work was preparation for departure. The hut was secured against the weather, its stores arranged for possible future use, its scientific instruments packed or protected where they stood. The dogs, those that had survived, were housed and fed against the ship’s arrival. The ponies were long dead; their bones lay scattered across the barrier, markers of the transport method that had failed. The motor sledges, abandoned in the first season, were rusting where they had stalled, monuments to technological optimism defeated by temperature and terrain.

The Terra Nova reached McMurdo Sound on February 18, 1913. Her arrival was not the triumphant return of a successful expedition but the practical closure of a failed one. The transfer of men, materials, and records took ten days, conducted in conditions of cold and wind that made the work miserable and dangerous. Atkinson supervised the loading, ensuring that the scientific legacy was preserved, that the official records were secure, that the personal effects of the dead were handled with appropriate care. The ship sailed for New Zealand on February 22, carrying twenty-nine survivors and the accumulated evidence of what had happened.

What remained on the ice was silence. The hut at Cape Evans stood empty, its door secured, its windows shuttered against the weather that would slowly dismantle it. The depots on the barrier, stripped of their most valuable contents, still marked the route south with their flags and banners, a system of signs without travelers to read them. The tent where Scott, Wilson, and Bowers had died remained standing, the snow slowly accumulating around its walls until it would be buried and preserved in the ice. The cross erected over the site, built from skis and bamboo poles by the search party, would last for decades before succumbing to the same process. The landscape itself was unchanged, indifferent to the human drama that had played across its surface, waiting for the next arrivals who would find these traces and wonder at the system that had placed them there.

With the last sledges returned and Cape Evans secured, Antarctica is left silent; all that remains is to carry home what was saved.
