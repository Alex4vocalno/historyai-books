Chapter 5

The First Depot Lies Unmarked

The cold deepened. The darkness extended. What remained of the expedition’s machinery stood ready for a spring that would test what it could and could not do. But first, the autumn had to finish its work.

On 23 April 1911, three men hauled a sledge through snow that gave like wet sand beneath their boots. Robert Falcon Scott led, with Edward Wilson beside him and Henry Bowers trailing the trace. They had left Cape Evans three days before, bound south across the Barrier with a load that the expedition’s planners had calculated for motor transport. The motors had failed in January, frozen into silence.

Now flesh took their place. The aim of this first season’s depot-laying, formalized in Scott’s orders, was to place a series of depots on the Barrier from its edge—Safety Camp—down to 80°S, for use on the polar journey the following spring. The final depot would be the largest, and would be known as One Ton Depot. The work was to be carried out by 12 men, the eight fittest ponies, and two dog teams; ice conditions had prevented the use of the motor sledges.

The temperature that morning, recorded in Wilson’s diary, stood at minus 22 degrees Fahrenheit. By noon it had dropped to minus 31. The men wore windproof outer clothing of Burberry gabardine, woolen underlayers, and finnesko boots of untanned sealskin. Their breath crystallized on the fur trim of their hoods. Each step sank six inches into snow that should have carried them. The sledge runners, designed for hard ice, dragged through the softening surface like plows through loam.

Scott kept the pace he had learned on the Discovery expedition a decade before: twenty miles a day on good ice, less when the surface broke down. They made four miles by lunch. The sledge weighed 670 pounds. Three men pulling together generated, in these conditions, barely enough traction to keep it moving.

Wilson wrote that night in his tent, his pencil stiff with cold. He recorded the surface as terrible. The word appears in his diary for 23 April without elaboration. He was a man who measured his words, a physician trained to observe without embellishment. If he called the surface terrible, it had exhausted his vocabulary of restraint.

Bowers, younger and more exuberant, recorded the same day with characteristic precision. He noted the thermometer at minus 31 degrees, the very soft surface, the distance of only 4.1 miles. He added the ponies behind them, seven animals under Lawrence Oates’s charge, making slower progress still. The dogs, handled by Cecil Meares and Dimitri Gerof, had fared better in the morning but now struggled in the same yielding snow.

The expedition had divided its transport into three systems, each with its own logic and its own vulnerabilities. The motors, revolutionary and untested, had already removed themselves from the equation. The ponies, Oates’s responsibility, were supposed to carry loads to the foot of the Beardmore Glacier before being shot for meat. The dogs, Meares’s domain, would run shuttle trips between depots, their speed compensating for their smaller capacity. And then there was this fourth system, improvised on the spot: men hauling sledges by hand, a method Scott had hoped to avoid but now found himself commanding.

The purpose of this journey was to lay the first of the supply depots that would sustain the polar march the following spring. The plan, formalized in Scott’s orders before departure, called for a chain of depots reaching to 80 degrees south. Each depot would contain food, fuel, and equipment cached against the return journey. The first and most critical of these, the depot that would stand nearest to the pole itself, was to be placed at 80 degrees precisely, or as close to it as the autumn conditions allowed.

They were nowhere near 80 degrees. On 23 April they stood at approximately 79 degrees 29 minutes south, still forty miles short of the target. The ponies had begun to fail. Oates, riding with the rearmost group, watched his animals sink to their bellies in snow that offered no purchase for their hooves. He had warned of this in his reports to Scott. The ponies, purchased in Manchuria and shipped through tropical heat to Antarctic cold, lacked the conditioning for deep snow. Their small feet punched through the crust where the broad paws of dogs spread the weight.

Scott made his decision on 24 April. The entry in his journal, written in the evening after consultation with Wilson and Bowers, records the reasoning with the flatness of a man already calculating how to explain retreat. The ponies were done, he wrote. They could not reach 80 degrees with them. They must deposit here and make the best of it.

The journey had started on 27 January, according to Apsley Cherry-Garrard, “in a state of hurry bordering on panic.” Progress had been slower than expected, and the ponies’ performance was adversely affected because Oates was opposed to using Norwegian snowshoes and had left them behind at Cape Evans. On 4 February, the party had established Corner Camp, 40 miles from Hut Point, when a blizzard struck. Now, in late April, the ponies were finished.

The place they chose lay on a slight rise in the Barrier surface, visible against the horizon but offering no natural landmark. The depot consisted of one ton of supplies: bags of biscuit, tins of pemmican, cans of oil, and bales of fodder for the returning animals. The weight was recorded in the depot ledger maintained by Bowers, who noted each item with his usual exactitude. The location was marked in the navigation log at 79 degrees 29 minutes south, 11 miles short of the intended position.

They did not build a cairn. The snow was too soft to hold one. They did not erect a flag on a pole, as would be standard practice for a major depot. The wind, blowing steadily from the south at twenty knots, would have shredded any fabric they raised. Instead they placed a single bamboo pole with a black flag, weighted at the base with a sledge packed with snow. The flag was visible for perhaps half a mile in clear weather, invisible in drift.

Bowers photographed the depot before departure. The image survives as a low mound in an undifferentiated white expanse. The bamboo pole leans slightly. The black flag hangs limp. Behind it, the pony party appears as small dark figures against the snow. The horizon shows no feature, no break in the curve of the Barrier. The photograph gives no sense of scale. A man standing beside the depot would appear the same size as the depot itself, or smaller.

They named it One Ton Depot, after the weight of supplies it contained. The name was practical, descriptive, temporary. It would become permanent through repetition in the expedition’s records, then in the search party’s reports, finally in the histories. One Ton Depot: a name that marks a shortfall, a compromise, a place where intention yielded to circumstance.

The return journey began on 25 April. The temperature had risen slightly, to minus 18 degrees, but the surface remained soft. The ponies, relieved of their loads, moved more freely, though two of them—Jehu and Chinaman—showed signs of collapse that Oates recognized. He had spent twelve years in cavalry regiments. He knew when an animal was finished.

Scott walked with the leading party, Wilson and Bowers beside him. They had abandoned the man-hauled sledge, distributing its load among the pony sledges and the dog teams. The dogs, Meares reported, were in good condition despite the journey out. Their pace had been faster, their rests more frequent. The difference between dog and pony performance, noted in separate diary entries by Scott and Oates, would become a point of contention in the expedition’s post-mortem assessments. For now it was simply observed, filed, not acted upon. Oates had been accepted onto the expedition mainly on the strength of his experience with horses and, to a lesser extent, his ability to make a financial contribution of £1, 000 towards the expedition. His role was to look after the ponies Scott intended to use for sledge hauling during this depot-laying stage and the first half of the trip to the South Pole.

They reached Cape Evans on 29 April, having covered the return distance in four days against the six days outward. The wind had been at their backs. The surface, frozen harder in the colder nights, had carried them better. The ponies stumbled into the stable Oates had built, two of them dying in the following week from exhaustion and cold-damaged feet. The dogs were unhitched and fed their ration of seal meat. The men walked into the hut, into warmth, into the long Antarctic night that would last until August.

Scott wrote his official report of the depot journey on 1 May, sitting at the table in the hut’s central room. The report, preserved in the expedition’s archives, summarizes the outcome with naval brevity. The depot was established at latitude 79 degrees 29 minutes south, containing one ton of supplies. The intention to proceed to 80 degrees was abandoned due to condition of ponies and state of surface. The passive construction distributes responsibility across conditions rather than decisions. The report does not mention the unmarked state of the depot, the lack of a proper cairn, the single bamboo pole that might or might not survive the winter’s blizzards.

Wilson, writing his scientific notes in the same room, added a personal observation that did not appear in any official document. The depot was not well placed, he wrote. They should have pushed on, or marked it better, or both. The sentence stands alone in his diary, without following explanation. Whether he shared this thought with Scott, whether it influenced any subsequent planning, the record does not say.

The winter settled over Cape Evans. The sun disappeared in May and would not return until August. Inside the hut, the twenty-five men of the expedition established routines of scientific work, maintenance, and waiting. The meteorological station recorded temperatures that dropped to minus 58 degrees in July. The magnetic observatory tracked the shifting poles. The biological laboratory preserved specimens collected during the summer. The stores were inventoried, the sledges repaired, the plans for the spring refined.

Through this waiting, the depot lay out on the Barrier, eleven miles short of its intended position, marked by a single pole that might be buried in drift, might be blown over, might be invisible against the white horizon when the polar party needed it most. The men in the hut knew this. They had walked away from it. The knowledge sat among them, largely unspoken, one of many compromises that the expedition had already accumulated.

In June, Wilson led the winter journey to Cape Crozier, a separate narrative of endurance that tested different limits. In July, the sun returned and the temperatures began their slow rise toward the spring. In August, the first sledging parties set out to extend the depot chain, to place additional supplies at 81, 82, 83 degrees south, building toward the pole in increments that would make the final march possible.

Each of these later depots was marked more carefully than the first. The lesson of One Ton Depot, if lesson it was, had been partially absorbed. Cairns were built, flags raised on multiple poles, locations verified by sextant and recorded in duplicate. The expedition’s logistics, observed through the depot ledgers, show increasing care as the spring advanced. But the first depot remained where it was, unimproved, its position fixed by the decisions of April.

The polar journey began on 1 November 1911, with sixteen men, two motor sledges, ten ponies, and two dog teams leaving Cape Evans for the south. The motors failed within days, as they had before. The ponies were shot at successive depots, their meat cached for the return. The dogs turned back at the foot of the Beardmore Glacier, their work done. By early January 1912, five men remained: Scott, Wilson, Oates, Bowers, and Edgar Evans.

They reached the pole on 17 January, finding Amundsen’s tent and flag already there. The return journey, documented in Scott’s final journals, became a chronicle of diminishing strength and deteriorating conditions. Evans died on 17 February, collapsing at the foot of the Beardmore. Oates walked out of the tent on 16 March, accepting death to spare his companions the burden of his failing body. Scott, Wilson, and Bowers continued, reaching One Ton Depot on or about 10 March, according to the last dated entry in Scott’s journal.

They found the depot. The single bamboo pole, the black flag, the supplies that had waited through the winter — all were there. The pemmican was edible. The oil, critical for melting snow and cooking, had largely evaporated through faulty cans — a manufacturing defect, not a planning failure, but fatal nonetheless. The depot gave them enough to continue, not enough to survive the storms that pinned them in their final camp, eleven miles from the next depot south, the one they had never built.

The search party found their bodies in November 1912. The report, written by Edward Atkinson, describes the scene with the same restraint that Wilson had brought to his diary. The tent was standing, the three bodies inside. Scott’s arm was across Wilson. Bowers lay apart, his position suggesting he had moved after the others died. The depot pole was visible from the tent, perhaps two hundred yards away. The supplies it contained would have sustained them through the storm, had they been able to travel that distance, had the storm allowed travel, had any of the variables broken differently.

The report notes the location of the depot, confirming the latitude recorded in 1911: 79 degrees 29 minutes south. The search party added a cairn and a cross, marking what the original party had not. The depot, renamed in the histories by its function rather than its weight, became the pivot point of the expedition’s tragedy: the place where survival became possible but not probable, where the margin proved too narrow, where the decisions of one April returned to claim their consequence.

The man-hauling party’s experience on 23 April exposed a fundamental miscalculation in the expedition’s transport planning that would reverberate through subsequent decisions. Scott had based his calculations on Discovery expedition experience, when sledging parties had encountered hard, wind-polished snow surfaces that allowed efficient travel. The Barrier in late autumn presented something entirely different: a surface softened by the season’s last warmth and then frozen unevenly, creating a crust that held neither man nor beast reliably. The sledge runners, twelve inches wide and designed for optimal glide on hard ice, sank deep enough to create drag equivalent to hauling uphill on level ground. Wilson’s medical observation, recorded in his diary that evening, noted the peculiar quality of the exhaustion: not the clean fatigue of muscular exertion but a grinding depletion from continuous low-grade resistance, each step requiring recovery of sunken boots before the next could begin.

The contrast between transport systems became more pronounced as the depot-laying party progressed. Meares’s dog teams, running on broad paws that distributed weight across the snow surface, maintained better speed through the soft sections, though even they faltered when the crust broke entirely. The dogs’ performance suggested what might have been possible with different planning—more dog teams, fewer ponies, perhaps no man-hauling at all—but this observation, noted in multiple diaries, remained unconnected to any revision of spring plans. The expedition’s institutional inertia, the weight of equipment already purchased and shipped, the commitments made to sponsors and the Royal Geographical Society, all pressed against any fundamental reassessment. Scott’s decision to continue with the three-system approach, despite its evident failures on this first test, reflected not stubbornness alone but the practical constraints of an expedition already in motion, its resources fixed, its timeline determined by the Antarctic seasons.

Oates’s position with the ponies grew increasingly isolated as the journey progressed. His military background in the 6th Inniskilling Dragoons had prepared him for the management of horseflesh in difficult conditions, but nothing in his experience matched the combination of deep soft snow and extreme cold that confronted his animals on the Barrier. The ponies’ small hooves, selected for the hard ground of Manchuria where they had been purchased, punched through the snow crust to the softer layer beneath, exhausting the animals with each step as they struggled to extract their legs.

Oates had argued before departure for more extensive conditioning, for snow-shoes adapted for pony use, for recognition that these animals were not the Arctic-bred Siberian ponies that Nansen had employed successfully. These arguments, recorded in his letters and in expedition correspondence, had been overridden by considerations of cost, availability, and schedule. Now, watching Jehu and Chinaman founder in snow that rose to their bellies, he confronted the consequences of decisions made months before and thousands of miles away.

The depot’s inadequacy came down to small technical failures compounding under polar conditions. The decision not to build a cairn, recorded without elaboration in Bowers’s notes, derived from the snow’s physical properties: too soft to hold shaped blocks, too deep to reach underlying ice that might have provided anchorage. The single bamboo pole, standard expedition equipment, stood eight feet high when planted, but the soft snow required burial of two feet for stability, reducing visible height to six feet. The black flag, chosen for visibility against snow, hung limp in the steady southerly wind that characterized Barrier weather; its dark fabric absorbed enough solar radiation to melt the snow immediately around the pole’s base, creating a shallow depression that gradually undermined stability. These were not oversights but adaptations to conditions, each reasonable in isolation, together producing a marker inadequate to its purpose.

The navigation that established the depot’s position introduced further uncertainty. Bowers’s observation, recorded in the navigation log, gave the latitude as 79 degrees 29 minutes south, but the conditions of observation—cold-stiffened fingers, trembling instruments, the difficulty of obtaining a clear horizon in flat light—affected precision. Scott’s later references to the depot location vary slightly, suggesting that the precise position remained somewhat uncertain even to those who had placed it. This uncertainty, unremarked in contemporary accounts, would prove significant when the returning polar party searched for the depot in conditions of poor visibility and extreme exhaustion, their capacity for precise navigation degraded by cold and hunger.

The psychological dimension of the retreat decision, recorded in the diaries with characteristic British restraint, nonetheless reveals the pressure upon expedition leadership. Scott’s journal entry for 24 April, written after the decision to deposit short, shows a man managing his own disappointment while maintaining authority before his subordinates. The phrase “make the best of it,” repeated in Wilson’s and Bowers’s accounts of the same conversation, suggests a determination to extract value from failure rather than acknowledge it fully. This pattern—tactical retreat presented as strategic adjustment, partial failure incorporated into future planning without fundamental revision—would characterize subsequent expedition decisions.

In the hut at Cape Evans, after the search party’s return, the storekeeper made his final entries in the depot ledger. He wrote the dates of deposit and recovery, the weights remaining, the locations verified. The arithmetic was simple. One ton had gone out. Less than half came back, consumed by the polar party, abandoned in the final camp, lost to evaporation and time. The ledger closed with a balance that measured more than supplies.

Scott’s Hut still stands on the north shore of Cape Evans on Ross Island in Antarctica. The British Antarctic Expedition of 1910–1913 — the Terra Nova Expedition — erected it in 1911 under Robert Falcon Scott’s command. Inside remain the table where Scott wrote his reports, the bunks where the men slept through the winter darkness, the stores arranged in their original positions. The depot ledgers are there too, in a box marked “Supplies.” The handwriting is clear, the figures exact, and every entry still carries its consequence forward.
