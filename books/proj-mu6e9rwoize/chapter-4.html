Chapter 4

The Machinery of Hope

The base camp stood complete on its volcanic promontory. Its supplies had been sorted and stored. Its vulnerabilities lay unpacked alongside its equipment, visible to any eye that knew how to look. The men had been trained to a method that had never been tested against the cold that was coming.

On 4 January 1911, the Terra Nova had rounded Cape Crozier and found its anchorage in McMurdo Sound. Now, three weeks later, the hut at Cape Evans was weatherproofed. Edward Wilson had arranged its stores according to his system. The men were quartered in the naval discipline that Scott insisted upon. The scientific instruments stood on their benches. The darkroom was ready. The ponies were picketed on the volcanic scoria. The dogs were chained to their posts. The motor sledges rested under canvas shelters where Bernard Day and his mechanics worked with the absorbed patience of men who believe that machinery responds to attention. The hut, erected on the north shore of Cape Evans, was a statement of permanence, chosen specifically because Scott had rejected reoccupying his old, colder base at Hut Point, 20 km to the south, hoping this location would keep the ship free of ice.

The machinery did not respond.

On 23 January 1911, Day started the first motor sledge on the sea ice of McMurdo Sound. The engine caught, ran for several minutes, then stalled. The temperature was recorded at 15 degrees Fahrenheit, well above the figures that would come, but low enough to thicken the oil and stiffen the metal.

Day tried again. The engine coughed, turned over, died. The mechanics checked the carburetor, the fuel lines, the spark. They worked for two hours. The sledge moved a quarter of a mile across the ice, then stopped again. Day made notes in the motor log. The entries tracked a sequence of starts and stalls across the morning. The pattern was established.

The machines had been built to specifications drawn up in England, tested in English winters, shipped through tropical seas, and now asked to function in a cold that was not yet at its worst. The expedition had spent nearly seven times as much on these motors as on the dogs and ponies combined. The investment was already demanding its return.

Scott watched the tests from the ice edge. His journals from this period show a man managing contradictory evidence. He noted the mechanical failures without alarm, recording Day’s optimism about modifications that would be made. He noted, too, the performance of the dogs, which Cecil Meares had brought across the ice from Hut Point, and the condition of the ponies, which Lawrence Oates was assessing with a professional eye that found little to praise.

The three transport systems were not alternatives. They were components of a single plan, each assigned its stage of the journey south. The motors would carry the heaviest loads across the barrier in the early season, when their engines could still function. The ponies would follow, establishing depots as far as the Beardmore Glacier. The dogs would make the final push to the pole and back, their speed and endurance reserved for the highest latitude. The plan required each system to perform its function, in sequence, without failure.

Day’s stalled engine on 23 January was a small thing in itself. It was also the first visible crack in an interdependent structure.

The hut at Cape Evans was the physical expression of Scott’s method. Wilson had designed its interior with the precision of a man who understood that winter quarters must sustain both work and morale. The scientific area occupied the western end, with benches for meteorology, biology, and the photographic darkroom that Herbert Ponting would use to process his plates. The living quarters ran along the eastern wall, sleeping bags arranged in tiers, a stove at the center, a gramophone for the evenings. The stores were stacked in bins labeled with their contents and quantities: pemmican, biscuit, butter, tea, cocoa, sugar, salt. The storekeeper maintained a ledger in which every item issued was recorded with its date and recipient. The system was naval, methodical, complete.

It assumed that the men who used it would be the men who had packed it, that the quantities calculated in London would match the appetites of men working in cold, that the plan drawn up in advance would survive contact with the place where it was to be executed.

Between 23 January and the end of March, Day conducted eleven recorded tests of the motor sledges. The results followed a pattern that the mechanics learned to expect but not to solve.

At temperatures above 20 degrees Fahrenheit, the engines would start and run for useful periods. Below that threshold, the starting became difficult, the running intermittent, the stalls more frequent. Day modified the carburetors, changed the fuel mixture, wrapped the engines in insulating blankets. Each modification produced a temporary improvement, followed by reversion to failure. The mechanics worked in the open, on the ice, with tools that froze to their hands and parts that cracked when dropped. They were skilled men, trained in the new technology of internal combustion, believers in the future that machinery represented. Their log records their persistence without recording their doubt.

On 14 February, Day noted that an engine ran well for 45 minutes, covered 3 miles, then stalled on the return when the temperature dropped to 18°F. On 3 March, he recorded that he was unable to start the engine at 8°F, wrapped it in insulation, tried again at noon, achieved a brief start, and stalled again. The cold was not yet the cold of winter. The motors were already failing.

Oates observed these trials with the silence that had made him unpopular on the voyage out. The testimonies of crew members, recorded later by Belgrave Ninnis, describe a man who did not increase his popularity with the fellows. Oates did not perform friendliness. He had been recruited for his knowledge of horses, and he applied that knowledge with the detached professionalism of a cavalry officer who has seen animals used badly and expects to see it again.

The ponies had been a late addition to the expedition’s planning, procured in Manchuria and Siberia by Meares, selected for hardiness and size, shipped in conditions that Oates could only guess at. They arrived at Cape Evans in poor condition, their coats not yet grown for the climate, their digestions troubled by the change of feed. Oates set to work with the method he had learned in India and South Africa: gradual conditioning, careful rationing, close observation of each animal’s capacity.

His diary from February and March records a developing pessimism that he did not share with Scott in direct terms. On 12 February, he wrote that the ponies were not doing well, that several were showing signs of weakness, that the cold affected them more than he had expected. On 28 February, he noted sore eyes, coughs, loss of condition, and observed that these were not the animals he would have chosen. The entries are brief, functional, the prose of a man who records facts without embellishment. But the facts accumulated.

Of the nineteen ponies that had been landed, two died in the first month. Others showed symptoms that Oates recognized: the respiratory infections that spread in confined quarters, the digestive failures that followed overwork in cold, the general decline that he had seen in horses pushed beyond their adaptation. He worked to save them, exercising them on the ice, adjusting their rations, treating their ailments with the limited veterinary supplies available. The effort was constant. The results were insufficient. The ponies were not bred for this. The plan required them to perform as if they were.

Meares and the dogs occupied a different operational world. Where Day worked with machinery that failed in ways he could diagnose but not repair, and Oates with animals that declined in ways he could observe but not reverse, Meares managed a transport system that functioned according to its design. The dogs had been selected in Siberia, where Meares had traveled in late 1910 to procure them.

He had chosen for the qualities that polar experience had validated: coat density, foot toughness, willingness to work in harness. At Cape Evans, he established a routine that differed markedly from the naval discipline of the hut. The dogs were chained in lines, fed on seal meat that the men hunted from the ice edge, exercised in teams that Meares trained for the specific tasks of sledge hauling.

His diary records efficiency, progress, satisfaction. On 15 February, he noted that the dogs were in excellent condition, that they had covered 10 miles with full load, that temperature was not a factor. On 8 March, he recorded that the team was working well together and ready for longer journeys.

The contrast was not lost on the men who observed all three systems. Cherry-Garrard, recording his impressions in the expedition’s second winter, would note the different spirit that surrounded each transport method: the mechanics’ frustrated ingenuity, the horseman’s guarded pessimism, the dog driver’s quiet confidence.

But in January and March of 1911, these observations were not yet judgments. They were simply the atmosphere of a base camp that was settling into its routines, each specialty absorbed in its own challenges, the larger plan still intact enough to seem viable. Scott moved among these worlds with the energy of a commander who believes that attention can overcome obstacle. He visited the motor shelter, the pony lines, the dog teams. He consulted Day, Oates, Meares. He recorded their reports in his journal, adding his own assessments. The motors would improve. The ponies would recover. The dogs would do their part. The plan would hold.

The geological expedition of January to March 1911 tested this confidence in partial ways. A party led by Griffith Taylor traveled west from Cape Evans to explore the coastal region between the McMurdo Dry Valleys and the Koettlitz Glacier. They used ponies for transport, and the ponies failed. Three of the animals collapsed on the return journey, unable to carry their loads, forcing the men to man-haul the sledges across ice and scoria. The party returned to Cape Evans on 14 March, having accomplished its scientific objectives but having lost animals that the larger plan could not spare. Taylor reported to Scott in the hut, detailing the work done, the specimens collected, the geological formations mapped. He reported, too, the failure of the transport. Scott listened, recorded, planned. The loss was noted. The plan was not revised.

The expedition had inherited this method from its predecessors, and Scott had refined it in his own experience. The Discovery expedition of 1901–1904 had taught him that Antarctic exploration was a matter of persistence, of character, of the will to continue when bodies and equipment failed.

The terrible journey of 1902–1903, when Scott, Wilson, and Shackleton had man-hauled to 82°17’S and returned with Shackleton broken by scurvy, had established the template: men could achieve what machines and animals could not, if their endurance was sufficient. The new expedition had been designed to extend this achievement through the addition of mechanical and animal transport, not to replace it. The motors, the ponies, the dogs were force multipliers, not substitutes.

At the critical moment, when the glacier rose and the altitude increased, the men would haul. This was the British way, the method that Scott believed in and that his nation expected. The failures of January and March were setbacks, not verdicts. The plan would hold.

The winter journey to Cape Crozier, undertaken by Wilson, Bowers, and Cherry-Garrard in June and July 1911, lay in the future when this chapter closes. But its preparation was already underway in the routines of the base camp.

Wilson had conceived the journey as a scientific necessity: the collection of Emperor penguin eggs from the rookery at Cape Crozier, to test his theory about the embryology of these most primitive birds. The plan required travel in the Antarctic winter, in darkness and cold that no previous expedition had attempted. Scott approved it, with the qualification that the right men must go.

Wilson chose Bowers for his strength and Cherry-Garrard for his willingness. They began their preparation in the months of mechanical failure and animal decline, training on the ice, testing equipment, building the physical and mental resources that the journey would demand. The winter journey would become, in Cherry-Garrard’s later account, the touchstone of the expedition’s character: the hardest journey ever made. Its preparation was continuous with the work of the base camp, the same men, the same methods, the same faith that sufficient effort could overcome any obstacle.

Scott’s Hut, as it would be called in later years, was the material container of all this activity. Erected in January 1911 on the north shore of Cape Evans, it was a structure designed for permanence in a place that offered none. Its walls were of wood, insulated with felt and seaweed, anchored to volcanic rock that would move with the frost. Its roof was of canvas and wood, pitched to shed the snow that would accumulate. Inside, the temperature could be raised to 50 degrees Fahrenheit by the stove, a tropical warmth against the cold outside. The hut was a statement: that British organization could establish a foothold in the most hostile environment on earth, that the expedition would not merely survive but work, that the winter would be a season of preparation rather than retreat.

The statement was true in its terms. The hut functioned. The men lived, worked, prepared. The terms were not the whole truth.

By late March 1911, the motors had been tested, modified, tested again, and finally stored against the winter. Day’s last recorded trial, on 24 March, produced the familiar result: the engine started with difficulty, ran 20 minutes, stalled at 12°F. The mechanics wrapped the machines in their covers and turned to other tasks, maintenance work, scientific assistance, the general labor of the base. The ponies, reduced by death and weakness to a core of animals that Oates considered marginally viable, were brought into shelter for the winter months, fed on the fodder that had been landed at such cost, exercised when weather permitted. The dogs, alone among the transport systems, continued their training through the darkening days, Meares taking them out on runs that extended their range and tested their endurance. The three operational cultures settled into their winter routines, each with its own understanding of what the coming season would require.

Scott’s journals from this period show a commander managing complexity without acknowledging uncertainty. He recorded the mechanical failures as engineering problems, soluble with time and ingenuity. He recorded the pony losses as regrettable but acceptable, within the margin that planning allowed. He recorded the dog performance as satisfactory, confirming his confidence in Meares. He did not record, because he did not perceive, the structural fragility that these separate records disclosed. The plan required the motors to function in temperatures where they had already failed. It required the ponies to recover condition that they had already lost. It required the dogs to perform at distances that they had not yet attempted. Each requirement was individual, plausible, defensible. The combination was not.

The Antarctic winter began to close in. In April, the sun set for the last time, not to rise again until August. The temperature dropped, the wind rose, the base camp contracted into the light and warmth of the hut. The men settled to their winter work: scientific observations, equipment maintenance, physical training, the education of those who would lead the sledging parties in the coming spring. The motors waited under their covers. The ponies waited in their shelter. The dogs waited on their chains. The plan waited with them, frozen in place, its assumptions untested, its interdependencies unexamined.

The expedition had committed itself to a sequence of transport methods, each depending on the one before, each with its own vulnerabilities, none with demonstrated capacity for the task assigned. The commitment was made. The test was deferred. The cold deepened, the darkness extended, and the machinery of hope stood ready for a spring that would prove what it could and could not do.
