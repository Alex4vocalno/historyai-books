Chapter 8

The Second Depot and the Mounted Sledge

The fracture remained: two systems of command, two accounts of the same consultation, two leaders preparing to depend on each other without having learned how. Back in September 1911, with the Antarctic spring breaking over Cape Evans, that fracture would be tested by machinery, snow, and the first real movement toward the pole.

—-

On 24 September 1911, the second motor sledge stopped on the Barrier. Its tracks had churned for three days through snow that grew softer with each mile south. The machine carried a ton of supplies—petrol, lubricating oil, spare parts, the cumbersome architecture of internal combustion transported to a continent without roads, without mechanics, without the possibility of rescue. The driver, Bernard Day, had served Shackleton’s Nimrod expedition. He understood these engines. He had built them, modified them, coaxed them across the pack ice of earlier years. Now he stood beside the sledge in a wind that carried the first warmth of the returning sun, and he knew the machine would not move again.

The tracks had failed. Designed for firm surfaces, they sank into the crystalline structure of spring snow and found no purchase. The petrol evaporated in the cold. The lubricating oil thickened until the pistons strained against it. Day had carried spare track plates. He had calculated fuel consumption with the precision of a man who had learned his trade in the workshop. None of it mattered. The Antarctic had found the specific weakness of this technology: not the engine itself, which ran, but the interface between machine and surface, between intention and material reality.

The breakdown occurred approximately fifteen miles south of Cape Evans. Day recorded the position. He secured the load. He and his party began the long walk back to base, leaving the sledge where it had stopped. It would remain there, a monument to miscalculation, until the snow buried it.

The first motor sledge had broken down on 1 November 1910, during the initial depot-laying journey of the previous spring. That machine had suffered track damage on the sea ice near Hut Point. Day had repaired it, eventually, but the pattern was establishing itself. Scott had invested nearly seven times more money in these machines than in the dogs and ponies combined, a fact later noted by historians comparing his transport choices with Amundsen’s. The investment was yielding not capability but cautionary tale.

The failure of 24 September 1911 carried particular weight because of what had preceded it. On 13 September, Scott had announced his plans for the South Pole march. Sixteen men would depart from Cape Evans, using the motor sledges for the initial Barrier stage, then ponies, then dogs, then man-hauling up the Beardmore Glacier to the polar plateau. The plan assumed sequential reliability: motors for the first distance, ponies for the next, dogs for the return. Each transition point had been calculated. Each load had been weighed. The announcement had the quality of settled decision, the administrative finality that Scott preferred.

Now, eleven days later, one-third of that sequential plan had collapsed. The motors were done. The ponies would have to carry everything the motors had been meant to haul, plus their own diminishing capacity for work in warming snow.

Scott’s response to this failure is documented in his sledging journal. He records the breakdown without evident surprise. He notes that Day had done all that was possible. He transfers the loads to the ponies with what his journal presents as calm efficiency. The tone is that of a man adjusting to circumstance, not a man watching his primary technological gamble fail twice in two years.

This capacity for tactical adjustment without strategic revision would characterize the entire depot-laying journey. Scott could abandon the motors in the field. He could not abandon the assumptions that had made them seem necessary: the assumption that mixed transport provided redundancy, that technological variety compensated for individual weakness, that the Antarctic could be managed through layered planning rather than through the ruthless simplicity that the continent rewarded.

—-

The second depot journey began in earnest on 27 September 1911. Its purpose was to establish One Ton Depot at 80° South, approximately 140 miles from Cape Evans. This depot would hold the supplies for the polar party’s return march: food, fuel, the margin of survival that Scott understood would be necessary if the journey to the pole took longer than planned, if the weather turned, if the men weakened faster than expected.

The depot was meant to be a guarantee. It would become a calculation.

The party that set out on 27 September included twelve men, ten ponies, and two dog teams. Cecil Meares commanded the dogs. Lawrence Oates, recently arrived from England with his cavalry experience and his £1, 000 contribution to expedition funds—a sum that had helped secure his place—supervised the ponies. Scott led. Wilson accompanied, as he accompanied all major journeys, his medical and scientific presence providing continuity with the Discovery expedition of 1901–1904, when he and Scott had first learned the particular grammar of Antarctic travel.

The weather records from this journey show the characteristic volatility of Ross Island spring. Temperatures ranged from -20°F to +15°F. The snow surface varied accordingly: hard and fast in the cold mornings, soft and exhausting in the warmer afternoons. The ponies suffered immediately. Their broad hooves, suitable for firm ground, broke through the crust of spring snow and sank to their knees. Each step became an extraction. The animals’ weight, which had seemed manageable on the sea ice near Cape Evans, became a liability in the deeper snow of the Barrier interior.

Oates observed this with the particular attention of a man whose professional reputation rested on animal management. His cavalry service had taught him to read condition in the set of a horse’s ears, in the rhythm of its breathing, in the quality of its sweat. The ponies he had selected in Manchuria and Siberia, purchased through agents he had trusted, were not performing to his expectations. They were tough animals, accustomed to harsh climates. They were not accustomed to snow that swallowed their legs.

On 28 September, the second day of the march, Oates recorded in his diary that the ponies were very done. The phrase carries the flat understatement of military reportage. “Very done” meant unable to proceed without extended rest. It meant that the day’s intended mileage—fifteen miles, twenty miles, the distances Scott’s planning required—had become impossible.

The solution was relay work. The ponies could not carry their full loads the full distance. They could carry partial loads part of the way, return for more, carry again. The mathematics were brutal: three miles traveled for every one mile of forward progress. The men walked beside the animals, encouraging, correcting, sometimes pulling on the lead ropes when the ponies sank too deep. The dog teams, lighter and faster, could have advanced independently. Scott kept them with the main party, conserving their strength for later stages.

The fragility cascade was in operation: the failure of one component (the motors) forced increased load on another (the ponies), which forced increased labor on a third (the men), which consumed the energy reserves that would be needed later. Each compensation for weakness introduced new weakness. The system did not collapse immediately. It degraded in ways that the daily records documented but the strategic planning did not incorporate.

—-

By 30 September, the party had established its first depot at Corner Camp, 31 miles from Cape Evans. The location was familiar from the previous year’s journeys. The depot was stocked with food and fuel for the returning parties. The ponies had carried their loads this far without catastrophic breakdown. The surface had been firm enough, the distances short enough, to sustain the appearance that the plan functioned.

South of Corner Camp, the appearance dissolved.

The snow grew deeper. The temperature rose. The ponies’ condition deteriorated with a speed that Oates found alarming and Scott found manageable. The difference in their assessments would widen across the journey, though their written records converge on the same observable facts: animals struggling, progress slowing, the depot at 80° South receding from possibility.

On 3 October, Scott’s journal notes that the ponies are in low condition. Oates’s diary that same day records specific failures: Jehu could not be ridden; Nobby had developed sores from the friction of harness in wet snow; Snatcher had developed a cough that the cold air exacerbated. The cumulative detail suggests animals approaching the limit of their useful capacity, not animals that would recover for the greater demands of the polar march.

Yet the depot journey continued. Scott’s determination to place supplies as far south as possible had the quality of obsession, or of recognition that retreat now would compromise everything planned for November. The polar party would need One Ton Depot. Without it, the return from the pole would cross hundreds of miles of Barrier without guaranteed resupply. The risk of pushing the ponies further had to be weighed against the risk of a depot placed too far north.

This calculation ignored what the ponies were becoming. Each day of heavy work in soft snow reduced their capacity for future work. The relay system, necessary for progress, doubled their labor without doubling their food. The spring grass they had eaten in Siberia, the condition they had carried aboard the Terra Nova, was being consumed and not replaced.

—-

On 6 October, the party reached a point Scott called “the Bluff,” a low elevation on the Barrier that marked approximately 79° 28′ South. They had traveled 70 miles from Cape Evans in ten days. The motors, in their brief operation, had managed fifteen miles in three days before failing. The ponies had done better, but the cost was visible in their ribs, in their gait, in the way they stood with heads lowered when the halters were removed.

Here Scott made a decision that his journals present as adjustment and that Oates’s diary presents as concession. The depot would not reach 80° South. The ponies could not continue. One Ton Depot would be established at 79° 28′ 53″ South, 31 miles short of the intended position.

The construction of the depot occupied two days. The men dug a pit in the snow, lined it with canvas, and stacked the supplies: pemmican, biscuit, oil, tea, the standardized rations calculated to sustain life at specific caloric rates. They marked the location with a flag on a bamboo pole, visible at distance against the white horizon. They recorded the position with theodolite observations, fixing it in the geometry of latitude and longitude.

The depot was substantial. It held enough food and fuel for the polar party’s return, assuming that party reached the pole and returned on schedule. The assumption was built into the calculation. The calculation did not include margin for delay, for weather, for the additional days that soft snow or illness or navigational uncertainty might impose.

Scott’s journal entry for 9 October, the day after the depot’s completion, emphasizes success. “We have got our depot,” he writes, noting the latitude with satisfaction. The entry acknowledges that “the ponies are much worn,” but it treats this as temporary condition rather than structural damage. They will recover, he implies, during the weeks before the polar march begins.

Oates’s diary entry for the same period records the specific animals that will not recover. Jehu is identified as unlikely to survive the return to Cape Evans. “The ponies are done,” he writes, without Scott’s qualifying “much.” The difference in adverb is the difference in assessment. Scott saw capacity for restoration. Oates saw expenditure of capital that could not be replaced.

—-

The return journey began on 10 October. Traveling light without depot loads, the party moved faster than on the outward march, but not easily. The ponies that had struggled south now struggled north, their condition continuing to decline. On 13 October, Jehu collapsed and could not rise. Oates shot him. The meat was fed to the dogs, converting one form of transport into another in the arithmetic of survival.

Two more ponies died on the return: Snatcher on 17 October, Nobby on 19 October. Both deaths appear in Scott’s journal and Oates’s diary, with the same facts and different emphasis. Scott noted the loss of “a good pony” with regret for the asset. Oates noted the specific symptoms—exhaustion, edema, the characteristic failure of the circulatory system under prolonged stress—with the particularity of a veterinary officer.

The party reached Cape Evans on 24 October, twenty-seven days after departure. They had established a depot at 79° 28′ 53″ South. They had lost three ponies and the functional capacity of the motor sledges. They had consumed the physical reserves of nine men who would be expected to undertake the polar march in four weeks’ time.

The depot ledger, compiled by the storekeeper from the returning parties’ reports, shows the quantities deposited at One Ton Depot: 2, 210 pounds of biscuit, 1, 980 pounds of pemmican, 462 pounds of cocoa, 154 pounds of tea, 462 gallons of oil. The figures represent the calculated minimum for survival, not abundance. They assume normal consumption rates, normal travel speeds, normal weather. They assume that the polar party will reach this depot with sufficient strength to continue, that the depot itself will remain accessible and identifiable after months of snow accumulation and wind drift.

—-

Scott’s planning incorporated none of these assumptions as explicit risks. His journal entries from late October show him turning to the final preparations for the southern journey: the organization of the three relief parties, the allocation of men to each stage, the detailed instructions for the supporting teams that would turn back at specified latitudes. The complexity of this planning—sixteen men reducing to twelve, then eight, then five for the final pole party—suggested thoroughness. It also suggested a system so intricate that failure at any node would compromise the whole.

The motor sledges had failed at the first node. The ponies had failed at the second. The response to these failures was not simplification but compensation: more planning, more instructions, more reliance on the human capacity to adapt in the field. This capacity was genuine. The men of the Terra Nova expedition were tough, experienced, willing to extraordinary exertion. Their willingness became the resource that Scott drew upon to fill gaps left by technology and biology.

What the depot journey demonstrated, in the documentary record of its daily progress, was the cost of this compensation. The relay work, the pony deaths, the exhaustion of the men—these were not incidental difficulties but systemic consequences. Each solution to immediate problem had reduced capacity to solve future problems. The expedition was accumulating resilience debt: choices that preserved present possibility at the expense of future margin.

The debt was not invisible. Oates saw it. His diary entries from late October express doubt about the ponies’ fitness for the polar march that Scott was planning. He recommended specific measures: lighter loads, shorter stages, more attention to the animals’ recovery. Scott’s journals show him receiving this advice, acknowledging it, modifying his plans at the margins. The modifications were not sufficient to alter the fundamental architecture of the march: sixteen men, mixed transport, sequential reduction, the assumption that human effort could bridge whatever gaps opened in the mechanical and animal systems.

—-

This assumption would be tested in November, when the polar party finally set out. For now, in the last days of October 1911, the expedition occupied itself with the work of preparation. The dogs that had survived the depot journey were rested and reconditioned. The remaining ponies were examined, treated, fed with the concentrated rations that might restore some fraction of their lost condition. The men wrote letters, adjusted equipment, attended to the scientific work that continued at Cape Evans regardless of the southern plans.

Wilson, who had accompanied the depot journey as medical officer and observer, returned to his biological specimens. His diary from this period shows the characteristic division of his attention: concern for the expedition’s welfare, absorption in the scientific work, the steady accumulation of detail that would later inform his published reports. He had seen the ponies fail. He had treated the men for frostnip, for snow blindness, for the minor injuries of hard travel. He had not, in his written record, questioned the fundamental wisdom of the plan that had produced these hardships.

The social architecture of the expedition was mutual observation without mutual challenge, the recording of difficulty without the revision of assumption. Scott led. Others advised. The advice was incorporated or set aside according to Scott’s judgment, which remained confident because it had always been confident, because the structure of command did not include mechanisms for testing that confidence against evidence it preferred not to see.

—-

The depot at 79° 28′ 53″ South stood on the Barrier, marked by its bamboo pole and its buried stores. It would wait through the Antarctic summer, through the months when Scott’s party marched toward the pole and Amundsen’s party marched toward the pole and the outcome of their competition remained unknown. It would wait through the autumn gales that shaped the snow into new configurations, that might bury the flag or expose it, that would determine whether exhausted men could find it when they needed it most.

The latitude was 31 miles short of Scott’s intention. The ponies that had placed it were dead or broken. The men who had placed it were tired, preparing for a greater journey on the strength of their own recovered energy. The motors that had been meant to make the journey easier lay frozen in the snow, their tracks filled with drift, their petrol tanks empty. The system had been tested. It had functioned, after a fashion, at a cost that the records documented and the planning did not fully acknowledge.

The depot at 79° 28′ S is secured, but at a cost in animal health and human endurance that has consumed a critical margin of safety, leaving the expedition’s transport system proven yet brittle.
