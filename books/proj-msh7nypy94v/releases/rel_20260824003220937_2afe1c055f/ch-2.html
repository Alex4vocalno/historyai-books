Chapter 2

Selecting the Company (Spring–Summer 1914)

The man waiting at 4 New Burlington Street stood out from the others on the pavement, though he did not intend to. Frank Worsley was a New Zealand sailor, born in Akaroa in 1872, experienced in the Merchant Navy and holding a commission in the Royal Navy Reserve.

He had come to London on a different matter entirely, but the previous night, drifting into sleep, he had experienced a peculiar waking dream of navigating a ship through phantom icebergs that loomed not in some polar bay but down the very length of Burlington Street itself. He took it as a sign, though of what he could not say.

The next day, passing the address, he saw the posted notice for the Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition and walked in. The encounter that followed lasted only minutes. Ernest Shackleton, amidst the controlled chaos of his expedition headquarters, asked him a few questions about his sea experience and his views. Then he offered him the captaincy of the Endurance. There was no protracted review of service logs, no committee deliberation.

It was a decision born of instinct, a swift recognition of something in the man’s bearing—an optimistic resilience, a nautical certainty—that Shackleton trusted more than any line on a curriculum vitae. Before the ice could test the men, Shackleton tested his own judgment in assembling them. In that brief exchange, the first critical piece of a human architecture was locked into place. The shopfront at 4 New Burlington Street had become a vortex of ambition. Letters and telegrams piled high on every surface, outnumbering the ship’s plans and provisioning lists that shared the desk.

This was the physical manifestation of the public response to Shackleton’s audacious plan. In the wake of Captain Robert Falcon Scott’s tragic polar failure two years earlier, a potent mixture of national feeling and personal yearning sought an outlet. Britain’s imperial prestige, bruised by the loss of the race to the Pole, craved a redemptive triumph. Countless men craved glory, or escape from the ordinary, or simply a share in a deed that would etch their names into history.

The applications represented a cross-section of Edwardian endeavour: decorated Royal Navy officers, university scientists, clerks, farmers, soldiers, and boys barely out of school. To process this deluge using the conventional metrics of the age—service records, academic citations, letters of introduction from notable patrons—would have followed the institutional script.

But Shackleton was not filling positions in a civil service. He was drafting a constitution for a micro-society destined for extreme stress. His unspoken doctrine, refined through bitter experience on previous expeditions, was becoming operational in those cluttered London rooms: technical credentials were necessary but utterly insufficient.

The paramount qualification was character—specifically, the character to endure prolonged monotony and acute fear without fracturing the group’s cohesion. Cheerfulness and mutual tolerance were not social luxuries; in the environment he anticipated, they would become the most vital equipment, more crucial than any sextant or sledge when every physical tool failed. His method was intensely personal and often appeared eccentric to outsiders. He conducted the interviews himself, probing for temperament with questions that bypassed the expected professional interrogation.

The case of Reginald James, a young physicist from Cambridge, illustrates the principle. James, a specialist whose mind worked in the abstractions of magnetism and meteorology, might have prepared for a rigorous examination of his scientific proposals.

Instead, Shackleton reportedly asked him if he could sing. The question was neither a joke nor a diversion. It was a diagnostic. In the close, darkened quarters of a ship locked in ice for months on end, with morale a tangible, dwindling resource, the ability to contribute to the communal life—to join a chorus, to share a laugh, to momentarily dispel the gathering gloom—was a survival skill as critical as any ability to calibrate an instrument. The query tested for a willingness to participate, for a personality that could integrate rather than isolate.

James’s answer, and more importantly his agreeable, even-tempered demeanor in response, passed the test. He was hired not solely for his intellect, but for his assessed value as a compatible member of a sealed community.

The decision prioritized the cohesion of the whole over the stellar, perhaps idiosyncratic, performance of a single part. This philosophy of selection represented a conscious departure from the institutional norms that had shaped British polar exploration. The Royal Navy’s approach, exemplified in Scott’s expeditions, was an extension of its own hierarchical structure: formal rank, technical proficiency, and strict, unquestioning discipline were the paramount virtues. It was a system designed for the execution of orders in the chaotic theater of war or the precise manoeuvres of a fleet. Shackleton, though a product of the Merchant Navy and steeped in naval tradition, was assembling a different kind of organization.

It would need naval discipline in its day-to-day operations on a working ship, but its social contract would be that of a private, almost familial, venture. He was not merely hiring a set of functions—a navigator, a geologist, a cook. He was curating a community that would have to be self-sustaining, self-governing, and self-correcting under pressure for which no standard procedure existed.

This demanded a deliberate balancing of roles and, more subtly, of personalities. The selection of Frank Worsley as captain epitomized this balance. The role demanded a master mariner of exceptional skill, capable of handling a barquentine-rigged vessel through the treacherous, ice-choked waters of the Weddell Sea. The Royal Navy offered a deep bench of such men.

Yet Shackleton’s swift offer to Worsley, after a conversation that resembled a meeting of minds more than a formal interview, was a statement of principle. He trusted his instinctual read of a man’s spirit over the impersonal validation of a service record.

He sensed in Worsley not only nautical competence but an adaptable optimism, a quality that would later prove decisive when the New Zealander, against all navigational odds, guided the tiny James Caird across eight hundred miles of the storm-racked Southern Ocean. It was an appointment that valued resilient character as the foundation upon which technical skill must rest. For the other critical, load-bearing positions in this nascent society, Shackleton employed a different but complementary strategy: he returned to known quantities.

This calculated assembly extended to roles where technical skill had to be weighed against potential social friction. The carpenter, Henry McNish, exemplified this calculus. “Chippy” McNish was a brilliant craftsman, his skills with wood and tools beyond dispute and absolutely critical for a wooden ship heading into ice. He was also famously irascible, stubborn, and possessed of a sharp, skeptical independence that could, and would, curdle into overt insubordination under the right conditions of stress. Shackleton hired him anyway. The decision was a cold managerial assessment of value versus risk.

He judged that in a crisis—specifically, the kind of crisis where the improvising of boats or sledges from wreckage might mean the difference between life and death—McNish’s peerless skill would outweigh the friction of his difficult personality. It was a gamble that accepted a known and contained problem in exchange for a potentially life-saving capability. Shackleton would rely on his own authority and the insulating, moderating pressure of the group he was shaping to manage the man.

Every appointment was filtered through this dual lens of utility and social effect. Even the choice of a cook was strategic.

The cook controlled a primary daily routine—the preparation and distribution of food—a function ripe with the potential for grievance. In the confined, dark, and monotonous world of an Antarctic winter, a surly or unfair cook could become a focal point for discontent, a catalyst for fractures in group solidarity. Equally, a cook with a steady hand and an equitable temperament could perform a vital, if unheralded, service to morale.

The man selected, Charles Green, would later prove this point under the most extreme duress, his quiet diligence in apportioning shrinking rations becoming a pillar of routine and a symbol of fairness. The process was as much defined by exclusion as inclusion. Brilliant but dour candidates, men of high qualification but whose temperament radiated a corrosive pessimism or a prickly individualism incompatible with close-quarters life, were turned away. The now-legendary advertisement that supposedly ran in newspapers—“Men wanted for hazardous journey. Low wages, bitter cold, long months of complete darkness, constant danger, safe return doubtful. Honour and recognition in case of success.”

—whether it ever actually appeared in print is less important than the fact that it perfectly captured the spirit of his recruitment. It was a psychological filter. It discouraged the romantic dilettante and attracted a certain type of realist, or at least the sort of man who respected blunt honesty over glamorous deception. The men who presented themselves after such a stark summons were already partially self-selected for a degree of hardiness and clear-eyed commitment. They were applying for the ordeal, not just the glory. By late July 1914, the human assembly was nearly complete. Twenty-eight men had been chosen, a mosaic of backgrounds and functions: officers and scientists, sailors and tradesmen, drawn from Britain, the Dominions, and beyond. What unified them was not a common past, but a common future, chosen for them by Shackleton’s singular judgment.

He had balanced the ensemble with a conductor’s care: the steady reliability of Wild and Crean, the skilled pugnacity of Worsley and McNish, the equable temperaments of James and other scientists like Leonard Hussey—the man whose banjo, saved from the wreck against all odds, would later provide a thin, vital thread of melody through despair. He had included the brute strength and saltwater practicality of trawlermen for the forecastle, men like John Vincent and William Stephenson, whose world was one of action, not reflection. The final list was more than a crew roster; it was a designed human system.

Its theoretical strength lay in its calculated diversity and intended unity. The optimistic would, in theory, buoy the pessimistic. The stoic would steady the volatile. The skilled craftsman would compensate for the inevitable failures of equipment. The scientist hauling on a sledge harness would learn a respect for the seaman’s knot, and the sailor listening to a lecture on the aurora would find his world enlarged.

The institutional context against which Shackleton operated was one of profound, if unspoken, transition. The era of the gentleman-explorer, funded by private patronage and often characterized by a certain amateurish élan, was giving way to an age of professionalized, scientifically-driven endeavor. The Royal Geographical Society, the gatekeeper of British exploration, increasingly emphasized meticulous planning, peer-reviewed objectives, and the collection of data that served imperial and scientific interests.

Shackleton’s expedition, for all its grand imperial branding, was fundamentally a private venture, financed through a patchwork of loans and donations—including £10, 000 from Scottish industrialist Sir James Caird and a generous sum from tobacco heiress Janet Stancomb-Wills—and his own relentless salesmanship.

This financial and organizational independence granted him a crucial liberty: he was answerable to no selection committee, no naval board, no society sub-committee that might prioritize a candidate’s pedigree or academic connections over his perceived fortitude. The clutter of 4 New Burlington Street was not just physical; it was the tangible disorder of a process freed from institutional bureaucracy, where one man’s intuition was the sole governing principle.

To understand the radical nature of this approach, one must consider the shadow of the recent past that hung over the entire enterprise: the catastrophe of Scott’s Terra Nova expedition. While publicly revered as martyrs to science and empire, the story of Scott’s polar party’s demise had, within exploration circles, sparked a fierce, if discreet, debate about leadership, preparation, and group dynamics. The official inquiries and published narratives focused on weather, scurvy, and misfortune.

But a substratum of opinion questioned the social architecture of the venture—the rigid class distinctions between officers and men, the sometimes fraught relationships within the final party, the psychological weight of a command style that could be aloof and introspective. Shackleton, having been a part of Scott’s Discovery expedition and having led his own Nimrod venture to within 97 miles of the Pole, was a keen student of these unspoken lessons. His recruitment was, in part, a conscious corrective.

He was not assembling a naval wardroom and a lower deck to be transported to the ice; he was building a single, integrated unit where a scientist would be expected to haul alongside a sailor, and where an officer’s authority would derive as much from demonstrated endurance and concern for his men as from the braid on his sleeve.

This philosophy extended beyond the selection of individuals to the careful engineering of the group’s social ecosystem. Shackleton understood that homogeneity could be as dangerous as discord; a crew composed entirely of stoic, silent types might lack the vital spark of humor, while one made only of boisterous optimists might crumble when confronted with genuine despair. His curation sought a dynamic equilibrium. The inclusion of Leonard Hussey, the young anthropologist and meteorologist, is a case in point. Hussey was not the most physically formidable applicant, nor was he a seasoned polar hand.

But he possessed a buoyant, musical temperament and brought with him his banjo—an instrument Shackleton specifically instructed him to bring. This was not a frivolous indulgence. In the calculus of survival, Hussey and his banjo were assigned a tactical role: to serve as a machine for the generation of morale, a tool for collective catharsis and temporal cohesion during the long polar night. His selection was an investment in psychological infrastructure.

Similarly, the choice of the photographer, Frank Hurley, was a gamble on artistic temperament in a utilitarian environment. Hurley was a brilliant and driven technician, but he was also a perfectionist with a volatile streak, capable of staggering bravery in pursuit of an image but also of petulance.

Shackleton, recognizing the immense propaganda and historical value of a visual record, accepted the potential for friction. He banked on the channeling of Hurley’s obsessive energy into his work, and on the fact that the stunning results of that work would, in themselves, become a source of pride and unity for the whole crew. Each such decision added a layer of complexity to the group’s dynamic. The forecastle hands, men like the tough, experienced trawlermen, provided a ballast of saltwater practicality and physical power.

Their world was one of action, not theory; they would be the muscle for the sledging, the grunt work on the ice. Their inclusion guaranteed a core of uncomplaining labor, but Shackleton also knew that left in isolation, such a group could develop a sullen separateness. Hence, the deliberate blending. Officers like Lionel Greenstreet, the First Officer, and Thomas Orde-Lees, the storekeeper and motor expert, though from different backgrounds, were expected to bridge these worlds.

Orde-Lees himself was a fascinating, borderline case in Shackleton’s selection. A Royal Marine with a reputation for mechanical genius and boundless energy, he was also a noted eccentric, a fitness fanatic, and a man whose self-absorption could grate on others. He was, in many ways, the antithesis of the self-effacing team player Shackleton claimed to seek. Yet his skills were unique and potentially invaluable, particularly regarding the expedition’s novel motor sledges. Shackleton’s acceptance of Orde-Lees was a testament to the commander’s confidence in his own ability to manage difficult personalities and to harness their talents for the common good. It was a gamble that acknowledged no system could be composed solely of easy men; the key was to ensure that the difficult ones brought indispensable value and that their quirks could be contained within the broader, healthier social fabric.

The process was not merely additive; it was also subtractive. The legend of the stark advertisement serves as a metaphor for a rigorous, if informal, psychological vetting that turned many away. The financial reality underpinning this entire selection process remained a constant, gnawing pressure. The expedition was a monumentally expensive gamble, funded not by the deep coffers of the state but through Shackleton’s tireless fundraising, a patchwork of loans, public subscriptions, and donations from wealthy patrons beguiled by his vision. This precarious financial footing imposed its own, invisible filter on recruitment.

Unlike a Royal Navy venture, Shackleton could not offer the security of a guaranteed salary or a pensionable career. The men who signed on did so for a share of potential honor and a modest wage, accepting a tangible risk to their future prospects. This very insecurity, however, forged a different kind of bond. They were not servants of an institution but stakeholders in a singular, personal endeavor.

Their commitment was to the man and the mission, a dynamic Shackleton cultivated deliberately. It fostered a sense of shared destiny that bureaucratic, state-funded expeditions often lacked. The clutter of unpaid bills and promotional prospectuses in the office at 4 New Burlington Street was the backdrop against which these life-and-death judgments were made, a reminder that the social vessel he was constructing would need to be buoyant enough to stay afloat amidst financial as well as physical storms.

Shackleton’s method, for all its focus on the intangible qualities of character, did not occur in a historical vacuum. The late Edwardian era was one of profound fascination with human capability and endurance, reflected in popular literature, the nascent field of psychology, and a growing public appetite for tales of heroism that tested the limits of the spirit. His intuitive vetting—the search for the optimistic, the resilient, the tolerant—echoed, albeit in an unscientific form, contemporary inquiries into the components of effective leadership and group psychology. He was operating as a pragmatic ethnographer of his own potential team, studying nuances of speech, reaction to odd questions, and social demeanor with the same intensity a scientist might bring to a laboratory specimen.

His office became an arena for a kind of applied human chemistry, where he mixed and tested combinations, anticipating reactions under future extremes. This process, though deeply personal, was systematic in its own right. He maintained mental ledgers of compatibility, noting how a candidate’s energy might complement or clash with someone already chosen, building not just a list of names but an interactive web of relationships.

The selection of the sailors for the forecastle followed this same principle of calculated integration. Men like Timothy McCarthy, a jovial Irish seaman with immense strength, and Walter How, a steady and reliable able seaman, were chosen not only for their proven nautical skills but for their reputations as good “shipmates.”

In the closed world of a merchant vessel, this term carried profound meaning; it denoted a man who pulled his weight without complaint, who shared his tobacco, who could be relied upon in a crisis without histrionics. Shackleton, through his networks and discreet inquiries, sought these men out. They would form the reliable, uncomplaining backbone of the watch-keeping and heavy labor, their predictable solidity providing a stable platform upon which the more specialized talents of the scientists and officers could function.

Yet, even here, he avoided homogeneity. By including a spread of ages, nationalities (English, Irish, Scottish, a New Zealander like Worsley), and even temperaments within the deck crew, he prevented the formation of a monolithic, and potentially oppositional, forecastle culture. The social ecosystem was designed to be cross-pollinating.

Nowhere was the tension between professional credential and personal character more acute than in the case of Frank Hurley, the Australian photographer. Hurley arrived with a formidable portfolio of work from Douglas Mawson’s recent Australasian Antarctic Expedition, a body of evidence that demonstrated not just technical mastery but a terrifying physical bravery in obtaining images. His credentials were impeccable.

Yet Hurley was also known to be fiercely independent, stubborn, and possessed of a volatile temper—traits that had caused friction on previous ventures. Shackleton faced a clear-cut dilemma: reject the finest pictorial chronicler of the age to preserve perfect harmony, or incorporate a brilliant but potentially disruptive element. His decision to hire Hurley was a masterclass in risk management. It acknowledged that the expedition’s legacy, its very claim on history and future funding, would be irrevocably shaped by its visual record.

He judged the incendiary value of Hurley’s work to be worth the potential for sparks from his personality. Crucially, this decision was not made in isolation. It was made with the knowledge that he had already secured the calming, stabilizing presence of men like Frank Wild and Tom Crean, whose immense social gravity could absorb and dampen perturbations from others. Hurley’s artistic temperament was a force he intended to channel, not suppress.

This intricate balancing act extended to the very edges of the expedition’s purpose. The scientific staff, while essential for lending academic legitimacy to the venture, were selected with the same dual criteria. Alexander Macklin, the young Scottish surgeon, was chosen not merely for his medical degree but for his calm, analytical demeanor and his physical robustness—a doctor who would need to be a sledger and a companion as much as a clinician. Similarly, James Wordie, the geologist from Cambridge, impressed with his intellectual rigor but was also noted for his quiet steadiness and lack of pretension.

Their scientific expertise granted them a place on the roster; their assessed character granted them a place in Shackleton’s envisioned community. They were to be working members of the whole, not a cloistered academic contingent. This insistence on integration was a direct rebuttal to the sometimes fraught relationships between “scientifics” and “sailors” that had marked other expeditions, where class and education could create unbridgeable divides on the ice.

As the final names were inked onto the articles of agreement, the gathering threat of a different cataclysm began to shadow the venture. In that summer of 1914, the geopolitical tensions in Europe simmered and then boiled over. The assassination in Sarajevo in late June and the subsequent cascade of mobilizations created a competing call to duty. For some of the men, particularly those holding Royal Navy Reserve commissions like Worsley, there was a palpable tension between their pledge to a polar expedition and their potential obligation to King and Country.

Shackleton, sensing the existential threat this posed to his carefully assembled company, moved swiftly. He offered each man the chance to withdraw for naval service, assuring them there would be no stain on their honor. Crucially, he then secured from the Admiralty—leveraging his connections and the expedition’s imperial branding—an official dispensation for the entire crew. This bureaucratic maneuver, often overlooked, was a final, critical act of selection preservation.

It was an organization built to absorb shock, to distribute strain, to bend without breaking. In these spring and summer months of 1914, Shackleton had performed his first and perhaps most foundational act of survival leadership. He had constructed a vessel of men that he hoped would prove as resilient as the wooden vessel that would carry them south.

But the design remained a theory, a blueprint drawn in the calm of London offices. Its mechanisms of mutual support, its capacity to withstand the corrosive effects of fear, boredom, and gnawing uncertainty, were entirely unproven. The men themselves, while confident in their selection and stirred by the adventure, were largely ignorant of the specific, grinding pressures for which they had been chosen.

They knew the expedition’s grand, public objective—the continent-spanning march—but not the gruelling, static, psychologically draining ordeal that would inexorably precede it. Their bonds were professional, casual, or yet unmade. The real work of forging a society lay ahead, in shared labor and shared danger. The Endurance, now fully crewed and provisioned, lay at her berth.

The last stores were being stowed. The company was selected, a human system designed for endurance, now awaiting its first real test beyond the calm of the English Channel. The society Shackleton had built was verbatim, untried. It was about to be shipped into a world where the only constants would be cold and change, where the plans so carefully laid in London would meet an adversary that could not be outfought, only outlasted. The waiting was over. The testing began.
