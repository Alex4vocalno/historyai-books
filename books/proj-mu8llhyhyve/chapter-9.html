Chapter 9

The Vote for Patience

Washington had left the Temple of Virtue. The door closed behind him. The room that had been silent now filled with the sound of men finding their seats, clearing their throats, shifting their weight on the hard benches. The spectacles had gone. The letter had gone. The general had gone. What remained was the business of turning feeling into resolution.

Horatio Gates stood at the front of the room. He had called this meeting to order, and now he would have to see it through. The man who had presided over the proceedings so far—the man before whom Washington had appeared uninvited, taking the floor and the room—now faced a different task. He had to guide these officers toward something they could sign.

The atmosphere had shifted. Washington’s appearance had drained the room of its revolutionary heat. The anonymous addresses that had summoned them here, the fiery words that had spoken of desertion and contempt, now seemed to belong to a different moment. The officers had come to this building ready to act. They left it ready to vote.

Gates called for order. The murmur subsided. He reminded the officers that they had been assembled to consider the anonymous addresses and to determine what course the army should take in response to Congress’s failure to pay them. The question before them was not whether their grievances were just—they were—but what form their redress should take.

Major John Armstrong sat among the officers. He had written those anonymous addresses. He is generally acknowledged as the author of the two anonymous letters directed at the officers in the camp. The first, titled ‘An Address to the Officers’ and dated March 10, 1783, had called for a meeting to discuss back pay and other grievances with Congress and form a plan of action. He had urged them to despise the insolence of Congress and to prepare for something other than patience. His words had circulated through the camp for five days. They had found readers. They had found believers. They had brought these men to this room. Now Armstrong watched as the officers prepared to reject the very course he had urged upon them.

The proceedings turned to resolutions. This was how the army did its business. A meeting was called. A chair was chosen. Resolutions were proposed, debated, amended, and voted upon. The process itself was a kind of discipline—a way of channeling emotion into the orderly channels of institutional action. The officers knew these forms. They had used them before.

The first resolution came from General Henry Knox. Washington’s trusted artillery chief had not authored the anonymous addresses. He had not called for mutiny. But he understood the grievance as well as any man in the room. His resolution acknowledged the officers’ unshaken confidence in the justice of Congress and their disposition to support the authority of the United States. On its face, it was a statement of loyalty.

But loyalty, in this room, was not simple. The resolution also declared that the officers would continue to persevere in their earnest desires for a settlement of their just accounts. They would wait. They would trust. But they expected to be paid. The language was careful. It affirmed while it demanded. It supported while it reminded.

The officers debated. Some wanted stronger language. The anonymous addresses had spoken to their anger—why should the resolutions speak only of patience? Others recognized that Washington’s intervention had changed what was possible. The general had counseled patience. He had asked them not to open the floodgates of civil discord. He had asked them to trust Congress. If they now passed resolutions that threatened or denounced, they would be rejecting the man who had led them through eight years of war.

The vote came. The resolution passed. The officers had affirmed their loyalty while restating their demands. They had rejected the anonymous call for direct action while insisting that Congress fulfill its obligations. This was not surrender. It was strategic recalibration—a channeling of threatened force into a constitutional petition.

A second resolution followed. This one addressed the anonymous addresses directly. The officers declared that they viewed the addresses with abhorrence. They rejected the sentiments they contained. They reaffirmed their unchangeable attachment to the cause of their country. The language was unambiguous. The addresses had called for action. The officers answered with abhorrence.

Armstrong watched. The words he had written—the arguments he had crafted—were now formally repudiated by the very men he had sought to mobilize. The conspiracy had failed. The threat of military force against Congress had been defused. Washington’s appearance had turned the room, and the officers’ vote had confirmed the turn.

But the vote was not the end. The officers had to communicate their decision. They had to send word to Congress. They had to make their loyalty a matter of record while preserving their grievance as a matter of right. A committee was appointed to draft a memorial—a formal address to Congress that would convey the officers’ sentiments and their demands.

The committee included Knox. It included other senior officers. It did not include Armstrong. The man who had written the anonymous addresses would not write the official memorial. His role in the conspiracy was not yet public knowledge, but his influence had ended. The officers had chosen a different path, and they would choose different authors to describe it.

Gates remained in the chair. The man who had presided over the meeting that Washington had entered uninvited now presided over the meeting that ratified Washington’s appeal. It was a curious position. Gates had allowed the anonymous addresses to circulate. He had permitted the meeting to proceed. He had done nothing to stop the momentum toward confrontation. Now he watched as that momentum was turned aside.

The proceedings in the Temple of Virtue represented a transformation. The emotional climax of Washington’s speech had become institutional action. The heated room had cooled into deliberate, documented resolution. The officers had walked in ready to act. They walked out having voted to wait.

This was not merely a change in mood. It was a change in the nature of the threat. The nationalist politicians in Philadelphia—Robert Morris, Gouverneur Morris, Alexander Hamilton—had hoped to use the army’s anger to force Congress toward a federal taxing power. They had wanted a crisis. They had wanted the threat of military force to compel political change. The anonymous addresses had been designed to create that threat.

The threat had materialized. The officers had gathered. They had been ready to act. But Washington’s intervention had redirected their anger toward constitutional channels. The vote for patience was not a defeat for the officers’ cause—it was a transformation of that cause into something less dangerous and more durable. The officers would petition. They would memorialize. They would wait. But they would not march.

The nationalist gambit had succeeded in generating pressure. Congress now faced a united officer corps with legitimate grievances and the moral authority of Washington’s endorsement. The pressure was real. But the gambit had failed in its ultimate aim. There would be no military coup. No march on Philadelphia. No forced establishment of a federal taxing power through the threat of army violence.

Washington had turned the room. The officers had voted. The conspiracy had ended as a military threat. But it had transformed into something else—a political lever that would operate through different means.

The meeting in the Temple of Virtue had begun with an anonymous summons. It ended with a formal vote. The distance between those two moments measured the distance between conspiracy and constitution. The officers had been called to action by an unknown hand. They had been redirected by a known face. They had come ready to force change. They left having voted to petition for it.

The resolutions they passed would travel to Congress. The memorial they authorized would follow. The words they chose would be read in Philadelphia by the same politicians who had hoped for a different outcome. The nationalist faction had wanted the army’s anger. They had gotten the army’s petition. The difference mattered.

In the days after the meeting, the camp at Newburgh settled into an uneasy calm. The officers had voted for patience, but patience was not forgiveness. They had affirmed their loyalty, but loyalty was not surrender. They had rejected the anonymous addresses, but they had not rejected their grievances. The army still had not been paid. The war still hung in the balance. Peace had been signed in Paris, but peace had not yet arrived in America.

Washington had bought time. The vote for patience was a vote to wait—to give Congress one more chance to fulfill its obligations before the army dissolved. The officers had placed their trust in Washington, and Washington had placed his trust in the civilian government. That trust was now a kind of debt. Congress owed the army money. The army owed Congress patience. The ledger did not balance.

The proceedings in the Temple of Virtue revealed something about the nature of power in the young republic. Washington’s authority had been decisive, but it had been exercised through persuasion, not command. He had appeared uninvited. He had spoken without being called. He had asked rather than ordered. And the officers had responded. They had voted to wait because they chose to wait—not because they were forced to wait.

This was the power of spectacle. Washington had turned the room not by threatening his officers but by appearing before them—by speaking to them as men who had served together, by reminding them of what they had built, by asking them to trust the cause for which they had fought. The spectacles he put on to read his letter had humanized him. The letter itself had shown that he shared their frustrations. The combination had worked. The officers had voted to follow his counsel.

But the vote for patience was also a performance—a demonstration of the officers’ capacity for self-restraint. They had shown that they could be trusted with the power they possessed. An unpaid army could choose constitution over coup. The republic’s defenders would not become its destroyers. This was the performance that the vote enacted.

The performance served a purpose. The officers knew that their restraint would be noted in Philadelphia. Congress would hear of their vote. Their willingness to wait would strengthen their claim to be paid. The vote for patience was not merely an act of loyalty—it was an act of leverage. The officers were trading the threat of force for the moral authority of restraint.

This was the calculated politics beneath the emotional surface. Washington’s speech had moved them. But the vote they passed was also a strategic choice. A path had opened that combined loyalty with demand. They had taken it. The anonymous addresses had offered a different path—one that combined defiance with threat. They had rejected it. The choice was deliberate.

The committee appointed to draft the memorial now had work to do. They would have to find words that expressed the officers’ grievances while affirming their loyalty. They would have to convey the depth of their frustration while reassuring Congress of their patience. They would have to make clear that the vote for patience was not a vote for passivity. The army still expected to be paid.

The meeting in the Temple of Virtue had lasted only a few hours. The consequences would last far longer. The vote for patience had ended the immediate crisis. But it had also established a precedent. The army had threatened. The army had been turned. The army had voted to wait. The pattern would be remembered.

Washington understood what had happened. He had turned the room, but he had not resolved the underlying problem. The officers still had not been paid. Congress still owed them years of back wages. The peace, when it finally arrived, would bring demobilization—and demobilization would bring its own crisis. The vote for patience had bought time. It had not bought resolution.

The nationalist politicians in Philadelphia also understood. The conspiracy they had nurtured had failed in its immediate aim. But it had succeeded in demonstrating the army’s power. The officers had gathered. They had been ready to act. Only Washington’s intervention had prevented something worse. The threat had been real. The memory of that threat would linger.

In this sense, the vote for patience was not an ending but a transformation. The army’s anger had been channeled into constitutional forms. But the anger remained. The grievance remained. The unpaid wages remained. The vote had turned the conspiracy’s energy into a petition. The petition would now travel to Congress. What Congress did with it would determine what came next.

The officers filed out of the Temple of Virtue. The building that had held their heated emotions now held their recorded votes. The room that had witnessed Washington’s spectacles now witnessed their signatures. The space that had been the stage for conspiracy became the site of constitutional action.

Gates remained for a moment after the others had gone. He had presided over the meeting that Washington had entered. He had watched as the room turned. He had seen the officers vote to reject the addresses that his own aide had written. Armstrong’s authorship was not yet public, but Gates knew. He had allowed the addresses to circulate. He had permitted the meeting to proceed. Now he presided over its conclusion.

The role Gates played that day would be remembered differently by different observers. Some would see him as a dupe—unaware of Armstrong’s authorship, helpless to stop the momentum the addresses had created. Others would see him as a conspirator—willing to let the army’s anger build, hoping to benefit from the pressure it created. The truth was more complicated. Gates had not written the addresses. He had not stopped them. He had presided over the meeting that they provoked. He had watched Washington take the floor. He had guided the officers toward their vote. His role was ambiguous—neither fully complicit nor fully innocent.

The vote for patience resolved the immediate crisis. But it left questions unanswered. The officers had voted to wait. How long would they wait? They had voted to trust Congress. What if Congress did not honor that trust? They had rejected the anonymous addresses. What would happen to the man who had written them?

Armstrong’s role would eventually become known. The addresses had been anonymous, but anonymity could not last forever. The man who had urged the officers to action would face the consequences of his words. But that reckoning lay in the future. For now, the officers had voted. The crisis had passed. The army would wait.

The proceedings in the Temple of Virtue demonstrated something essential about the young republic. The civilian government had been threatened by its own army. The threat had been real. The officers had gathered, they had been angry, and they had been ready to act. But the threat had been turned—not by force, but by persuasion. Washington had asked them to wait. They had voted to wait. The constitution had held.

This was not inevitable. Other armies in other times had made different choices. The temptation to use military force to compel political change was real. The grievance was just. The power was present. But the officers chose differently. They chose to trust the process, to honor their commander, to wait for Congress to act.

The vote for patience was a vote for the republic. It was also a vote for the officers’ own interests. A march on Philadelphia would destroy what they had built. Military force against the civilian government would betray the cause for which they had fought. Their honor depended on restraint. They voted to preserve that honor.

But they also voted to preserve their claim. The resolutions they passed affirmed their loyalty while restating their demand. They would wait. They would trust. But they expected to be paid. The vote for patience was not a release of Congress’s obligation—it was a renewal of that obligation under more favorable terms. The officers had shown their restraint. Now Congress would have to show its gratitude.

The committee set to work on the memorial. The words would have to be chosen carefully. The officers’ grievances had to be stated clearly. Their loyalty had to be affirmed unequivocally. Their patience had to be presented as a choice, not a surrender. The memorial would be the formal record of what had happened in the Temple of Virtue—the transformation of conspiracy into constitution.

Washington waited for the results. He had turned the room, but he knew that the turning was only the beginning. The vote for patience had bought time. The memorial would buy more. But eventually, the army would have to be paid. Congress would have to act. The debt would have to be settled.

The nationalist politicians in Philadelphia also waited. They had hoped for a crisis that would force a federal taxing power. They had gotten a crisis. They had not gotten the force. The army had voted to petition rather than to march. The threat had been turned. But the pressure remained.

The vote for patience was, in this sense, the managed outcome that the nationalists had hoped for—a demonstration of the army’s power that stopped short of actual violence. The conspiracy had been controlled. The explosion had been channeled. The officers had voted to wait, but their vote had shown what they could have done. The threat had been real enough to frighten Congress. The restraint had been real enough to reassure them. The combination was potent.

This was the debt theater in action. The officers’ vote for patience was an act of faith in a debt that might never be paid. They agreed to wait for money that Congress did not have, from a government that could not tax, in a republic that could not compel. Their patience was a performance—a demonstration of loyalty that created its own kind of obligation. Congress would now have to recognize that obligation. The officers had shown their trust. Congress would have to show its gratitude.

The meeting in the Temple of Virtue ended. The officers returned to their huts. The camp at Newburgh settled into an uneasy quiet. The vote had been taken. The resolutions had been passed. The committee had been appointed. The memorial would be drafted.

But the underlying facts had not changed. The army still had not been paid. The officers still had grievances. Congress still owed them years of back wages. The peace still had not arrived. The war still had not ended. The vote for patience had transformed the crisis, but it had not resolved it.

Washington understood this better than anyone. He had turned the room. He had guided the officers toward a constitutional resolution. He had prevented the conspiracy from becoming a coup. But he had not solved the problem of the unpaid army. That problem would remain until Congress found the will and the means to pay what it owed.

The vote for patience was a beginning, not an end. It marked the transformation of military threat into political pressure. It demonstrated the power of Washington’s authority and the officers’ restraint. It showed that the republic could survive even the gravest threats to its existence. But it also showed how fragile that survival was. The army had voted to wait. What would happen when patience ran out?

The committee worked through the night. They drafted the memorial that would convey the officers’ sentiments to Congress. They chose their words carefully. They affirmed loyalty while stating grievance. They expressed patience while demanding payment. They rejected the anonymous addresses while honoring the army’s cause.

The memorial would be the formal record of what had happened in the Temple of Virtue. It would tell Congress that the officers had gathered, that they had considered the anonymous addresses, and that they had voted to reject them. It would tell Congress that the officers affirmed their loyalty to the civilian government. It would also tell Congress that the officers expected to be paid.

The vote for patience had transformed the conspiracy into a petition. The threat of military force had been channeled into constitutional action. The officers had shown that they could be trusted with power. Now Congress would have to show that it could be trusted with trust.

A powerful, unified petition from the officer corps was now en route to Congress in Philadelphia.
