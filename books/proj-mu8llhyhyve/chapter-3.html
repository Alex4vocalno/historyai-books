Chapter 3

The Anonymous Addresses

A lone officer, his uniform coat unbuttoned against the damp chill, paced the muddy track between rows of silent tents.

On the morning of March 10, an unsigned letter began circulating in the army camp at Newburgh. Later acknowledged to be written by Major John Armstrong, Jr., aide to General Gates, the letter decried the army’s condition and the lack of Congressional support. It called upon the officers to assemble and deliberate on their grievances. The paper passed from hand to hand through the winter quarters, its phrases burning with controlled fury. Officers read it in their cramped quarters, the cold seeping through the log walls, and recognized in its words an articulation of what they had whispered for months.

The document itself was remarkable for its rhetorical precision. Titled An Address to the Officers of the Army, it began with a rehearsal of the army’s sufferings—six years of service without pay, promises deferred, certificates that depreciated to pennies on the dollar. But it quickly moved from grievance to proposition. The address summoned the officers to a meeting on March 11, to consult upon the present crisis and determine on some line of conduct. The meeting was unauthorized. It circumvented the chain of command. It proposed, in effect, that the officer corps constitute itself as a political body independent of its general.

What made the address incendiary was the precision of its implications. The officers had long grumbled about their treatment. Congress had promised half-pay for life in 1780, then commuted it to five years’ full pay in 1782, then failed to fund either. The states had refused to contribute their quotas under the Articles of Confederation. The army had been fed on promises and paper. But grievance was one thing; organized insubordination was another. The address transformed private resentment into public threat.

The text urged the officers to act before news of a peace treaty arrived. If peace came, the army would be disbanded, the officers scattered to their homes, and their leverage evaporated. If peace arrives, the address warned, the army will be disbanded, and you will then be a body of unpaid creditors, dispersed through the continent, without the means of collecting your dues. But if they acted while still under arms, while still concentrated and organized, they might compel Congress to meet their demands. The logic was unmistakable. It was the logic of mutiny dressed in the language of petition.

The address ended with a call to assembly. The officers were to meet the following day at the public building known as the Temple of Virtue. They were to come prepared to adopt such measures as shall appear best calculated to obtain justice. The phrase was vague enough to permit multiple interpretations, but the context made its meaning clear. The officers were being summoned to plan coercion.

That the address appeared when it did was no accident. For weeks, nationalist politicians in Philadelphia had watched the army’s distress with the eye of craftsmen assessing a tool. Robert Morris, the Superintendent of Finance, had suspended pay to the troops in January, pleading insolvency. His correspondents in the army reported growing unrest. Alexander Hamilton, who had served as Washington’s aide before winning election to Congress, wrote to his former commander with advice: the army’s discontent might be useful. The claims of the army, urged with moderation but with firmness, Hamilton suggested, may operate on those weak and wavering minds which have been too much attached to state prejudices to adopt a just and enlarged system of national government. The threat of force, in other words, might frighten Congress into granting the impost—the federal taxing power that nationalists had sought for years.

Hamilton was not alone in this calculation. Gouverneur Morris, Robert Morris’s assistant and a sharp advocate for a stronger national government, had written in similar terms. The army’s desperation was a political resource. If properly directed, it might break the deadlock that had paralyzed the Confederation government. The question was not whether to use the army’s anger but how to channel it without losing control.

The anonymous addresses that appeared at Newburgh in early March 1783 were the tangible link between this political design in Philadelphia and the military unrest in the Hudson Valley. They transformed abstract nationalist strategy into concrete provocation. Whoever wrote them understood both the officers’ grievances and the political opportunity those grievances presented. The addresses weaponized the army’s desperation to advance a constitutional agenda.

Who, then, was the author?

Major John Armstrong, Jr., is generally acknowledged as the penman of the two anonymous letters. Armstrong was a Pennsylvanian, born in Carlisle in 1758, the son of a general who had distinguished himself in the French and Indian War. The younger Armstrong had served with distinction as well, carrying the wounded General Mercer from the field at Princeton and later joining the staff of General Horatio Gates. By 1783, he was Gates’s aide-de-camp, stationed at Newburgh, and privy to his commander’s frustrations and ambitions.

Gates himself occupies an ambiguous position in the affair. He was Washington’s senior major general, a man of considerable military experience and no small vanity. He had commanded the American forces at Saratoga, where his victory over Burgoyne had made him briefly the most celebrated general in America. Washington had sent him reinforcements for that campaign, but Gates had neglected to mention Washington’s contribution in his official report. The omission had not been forgotten. Gates’s admirers in Congress had once pushed him as a potential replacement for Washington, a notion that came to nothing but left its residue of rivalry.

Whether Gates encouraged, condoned, or merely tolerated Armstrong’s authorship remains unclear. He never publicly acknowledged the addresses as the work of his aide, nor did he condemn them when they appeared. His silence was itself a statement. In the army’s hierarchy, an aide’s actions reflected on his general. Gates’s failure to suppress the addresses or discipline their author suggests at least complicity, perhaps more. The addresses served his interests as well as Armstrong’s. If Washington could not secure the army’s pay, perhaps a demonstration of officer discontent might create space for a rival to emerge.

The first address appeared on March 10. The second followed on March 12, after Washington had intervened. Together, these documents constituted a coordinated assault on the chain of command. They proposed that the officers meet without authorization, deliberate without their general, and decide upon actions that might include refusing to disband at peace, marching on Congress, or withdrawing the army’s protection from the country. Each option carried the same implication: the military would compel the civilian government to meet its demands.

The rhetoric of the addresses was carefully calibrated. They did not call for outright mutiny. They spoke instead of justice and honor, of moderation and firmness. They appealed to the officers’ sense of themselves as patriots who had been betrayed by ingratitude. Can you then consent to be the only sufferers by this revolution, the first address asked, and, retiring from the field, grow old in poverty, wretchedness and contempt? The question was not merely rhetorical. It was an incitement.

The addresses also contained a darker suggestion. If peace came before the officers secured their pay, they should refuse to lay down their arms. If peace arrives, the first address warned, and you then disband without securing your just dues, you will become the scorn of mankind. The alternative was implicit: remain under arms, refuse to disband, and compel Congress to act. This was military coercion by another name.

The second address, which appeared on March 12, was more explicit. It acknowledged Washington’s response to the first address—his general orders denouncing the unauthorized meeting—and pressed forward regardless. The officers should meet, the second address insisted, but they should do so on their own terms, not Washington’s. It questioned whether the commander-in-chief truly understood their plight. If he does not, the address implied, then you must act for yourselves. The phrase stopped just short of sedition.

What gave the addresses their peculiar power was their combination of familiar grievance and novel proposal. The officers had complained for years about their pay. Petitions had been sent to Congress, memorials composed, empty promises endured. But an unauthorized meeting had never been summoned. Actions placing them in direct opposition to their commander had never been proposed. The addresses crossed a line that had not previously been crossed. They made explicit what had previously remained implicit: the army might refuse to obey.

The mechanism of anonymous agitation was itself significant. By circulating without attribution, the addresses permitted every officer to project onto them his own hopes and fears. They could be read as the work of a single malcontent or as the voice of a movement. They could be dismissed or embraced, ignored or acted upon. Their anonymity made them a test. Any officer who endorsed them revealed his disposition; any officer who denounced them declared his loyalty. The addresses forced a choice.

That choice was not merely military; it was political. The officers’ grievances were real, but the addresses transformed those grievances into a weapon aimed at the Confederation’s constitutional structure. The nationalists in Philadelphia wanted a federal taxing power. The army’s pay depended on such a power. By pushing the officers toward confrontation, the addresses advanced the nationalist agenda. The army’s desperation was being weaponized to force a political solution that Congress had repeatedly rejected.

This was crisis engineering. The addresses did not create the army’s distress; they exploited it. They did not invent the officers’ anger; they directed it. They transformed diffuse grievance into focused threat, private complaint into public ultimatum. And they did so in language calculated to appeal to men of honor who might otherwise shrink from sedition. The addresses framed coercion as self-defense, mutiny as justice.

The officers who read the addresses on that cold March morning faced a decision. Obedience to their general meant ignoring the summons. Attendance at the unauthorized meeting risked their careers. Endorsement of the addresses meant declaring themselves in opposition to Washington. Or they could wait, watch, and see which way the wind blew.

Washington’s response came swiftly. On the morning of March 11, his general orders denounced the anonymous address as disorderly and irregular. He forbade the officers from attending the unauthorized meeting. But he also announced a meeting of his own, to be held on March 15. This meeting, he said, would be conducted properly, with proper notice and proper authority. He asked the officers to come and hear a report from a committee he had appointed to press their claims with Congress.

Washington had bought four days. The question was whether four days would be enough.

The second address, appearing on March 12, tested whether Washington’s intervention had worked. It urged the officers to reject Washington’s proposed meeting and attend the unauthorized assembly instead. It questioned whether Washington truly represented their interests or had been co-opted by Congress. It suggested that the general had become too moderate, too willing to compromise, too distant from the suffering of his men. The address was careful not to attack Washington directly. It praised his past service while implying that his present conduct was wanting. We have loved him, the address said of Washington. We have followed him. But has he not been too long the advocate of a government that has deceived us?

The question was whether the officers would follow where the addresses led. The army’s mood was volatile. Grievance had accumulated over years of neglect. The promises made in 1780 and 1781 and 1782 had all been broken. The certificates that represented their pay had depreciated to a fraction of their face value. Many officers had sold their certificates to speculators for whatever they could get, taking pennies on the dollar rather than wait for a Congress that might never pay. Those who held their certificates watched their value decline with each passing month. Those who had sold them regretted the price. All of them felt cheated.

The addresses gave shape to this formless resentment. A plan was offered, a meeting proposed, a course of action suggested. They promised that the officers were not alone, that others shared their frustration, that something could be done. The appeal was powerful. Even officers who distrusted the anonymous authorship might find themselves drawn to the message.

The question of authorship mattered, but it was not the only question. Armstrong may have written the addresses, but Armstrong did not act alone. He was Gates’s aide. His actions reflected on his general. Gates’s silence suggested approval, or at least toleration. And beyond Gates, there were the nationalists in Philadelphia—Hamilton, the Morrises, and others—who had been corresponding with officers and politicians about the army’s utility in pressing for a federal impost. The addresses did not emerge from a vacuum. They emerged from a network of discontent and design.

The chain of causation ran from Philadelphia to Newburgh, from nationalist strategy to military provocation. Hamilton had written to Washington suggesting that the army’s claims be urged with moderation but with firmness. Robert Morris had suspended pay, creating the very distress that made the officers desperate. Gouverneur Morris had corresponded with officers about the political uses of military grievance. The addresses were the logical product of these machinations. They translated political calculation into incendiary text.

But translation is not the same as control. The nationalists might have encouraged the army’s discontent, but they could not dictate how that discontent would express itself. The addresses went further than Hamilton’s letters had suggested. They called not merely for petition but for unauthorized assembly, not merely for firmness but for potential defiance. The gap between Philadelphia’s design and Newburgh’s execution revealed the danger of crisis engineering. Once ignited, a fire burns where it will.

The officers were now caught between two authorities. On one side stood Washington, their commander-in-chief, who had led them through eight years of war. A meeting on March 15 had been called. The anonymous addresses had been denounced. They had been reminded of their duty and their oath. On the other side stood the anonymous agitators, who called them to an unauthorized assembly, who questioned Washington’s judgment, who urged them to act before peace deprived them of leverage. The choice was not merely between obedience and insubordination. It was between two visions of the army’s role in the emerging republic.

Washington’s authority rested on eight years of service. He had shared the army’s hardships, endured its winters, led it through defeat and victory. The officers knew him. They had served under him. Many loved him. But Washington also represented the civilian government that had failed to pay them. Patience had been urged, moderation counseled, trust in Congress’s good intentions requested. Those urgings had produced nothing. The officers might love Washington and still doubt his strategy.

The anonymous addresses offered an alternative. They proposed that the officers act for themselves, that they seize the initiative, that they refuse to wait for a Congress that had already proved itself unwilling or unable to pay. The addresses did not name Gates as an alternative to Washington, but Gates’s position as the senior major general after Washington made him the natural focal point for discontent. If Washington could not or would not secure the army’s pay, perhaps Gates might.

The rivalry between Washington and Gates was an open secret in the army. Gates’s supporters in Congress had pushed him as a potential replacement for Washington during the dark days of 1777 and 1778, when the army had suffered at Brandywine and Germantown and Valley Forge. The effort had failed, but Gates’s ambitions had not disappeared. He remained a figure around whom opposition might coalesce. The addresses did not mention him, but their appearance among officers stationed near his headquarters suggested his influence.

The mechanism of anonymous agitation depended on plausible deniability. Armstrong could write the addresses, Gates could tolerate them, and neither would be publicly responsible. If the addresses succeeded in provoking a confrontation, the nationalists would have their crisis. If they failed, the authors could remain hidden. Anonymity protected the conspirators while exposing the army’s divisions.

The four days between March 11 and March 15 became a contest of wills. Washington had called the officers to a meeting on the 15th. The anonymous addresses had called them to a meeting on the 11th. The question was which summons the officers would obey. Washington’s general orders had forbidden the unauthorized assembly, but orders alone could not suppress the discontent that the addresses had articulated.

The camp at Newburgh waited. Officers discussed the addresses in their quarters, debated their meaning, argued over their proper response. Some denounced them as sedition. Others defended them as necessary provocation. Most occupied an uncertain middle ground, resentful of Congress, loyal to Washington, unsure what to do.

The addresses had done their work. Whispered grievances had been transformed into printed provocation, private complaint into public summons. A choice had been forced upon the officers. And the stage had been set for confrontation.

On March 15, the officers would assemble at the Temple of Virtue. Washington would address them. The question was whether his words would be enough to turn them from the course that the anonymous addresses had set.

The nationalists in Philadelphia watched and waited. Conditions for crisis had been helped into existence. Correspondence with officers had been maintained, the expression of grievance encouraged, a federal impost pressed that would fund the army’s pay. They had hoped that the army’s distress would force Congress to act. Now they would see whether the crisis they had encouraged could be controlled.

The addresses themselves were the crucial spark. They made the conspiracy overt. They moved it from whispered grievances to planned assembly. They called the officers to action and set the terms of their deliberation. Whether Armstrong wrote them alone or as part of a coordinated effort, whether Gates encouraged them or merely permitted them, whether the nationalists in Philadelphia directed them or merely exploited them—the addresses transformed political theory into imminent threat.

The army had not been paid for years. It waited at Newburgh for a peace already signed in Europe but not yet ratified. Its officers had been worked on by nationalists in Congress who wanted their anger to force a federal taxing power. Now two anonymous addresses called them to a meeting that could end in a march on Philadelphia.

Washington would take the floor uninvited on March 15. A letter would be read. Spectacles would be put on. And the room would turn.

But on the morning of March 10, none of that had yet happened. There was only the paper, passed from hand to hand, its phrases burning with controlled fury. The officers read it in their quarters, recognized their own grievances in its words, and began to consider what they might do.

The anonymous addresses had arrived.
