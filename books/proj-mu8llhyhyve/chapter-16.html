Chapter 16

The Hamilton-Morris Correspondence Unmasked

The memorandum Robert Morris submitted to Congress on April 30, 1783, presented its financial reckoning in the clear columns of a merchant’s ledger. It recorded an empty treasury, millions owed to the army, and public debt certificates trading at a fraction of their value. The document noted the unratified impost, blocked by a state’s refusal. This public record showed a government unable to meet its obligations. It did not show the private correspondence that had helped create the crisis now demanding resolution.

The spring of 1783 had brought an uneasy calm to the Hudson Highlands. Washington’s intervention at the Temple of Virtue on March 15 had defused the immediate threat of mutiny. The officers had dispersed. The anonymous addresses that called them to that meeting had been repudiated. But the conditions that produced those addresses—the unpaid wages, the worthless certificates, the sense that Congress had forgotten its army—remained unchanged. Morris’s report made that clear enough. What the report did not say was that certain men in Philadelphia had understood those conditions not as a problem to be solved but as a pressure to be applied.

The letters between Robert Morris and Alexander Hamilton, written in the weeks before and after the Newburgh meeting, reveal a coordination that the public record concealed. Hamilton, the young nationalist congressman from New York, had been corresponding with Morris, the Superintendent of Finance, about the army’s grievances and their political utility. Their exchange shows that the crisis at Newburgh was not merely a spontaneous eruption of soldierly anger, though it was certainly that as well. It was also a lever, deliberately pulled by men who believed that only a crisis could force the states to surrender the revenue power they hoarded.

Hamilton had written to Washington on February 13, 1783, at a moment when rumors of peace were reaching Philadelphia but the army’s pay remained unresolved. His letter carried a warning about the officers’ growing unrest. But it also carried a suggestion: that the claims of the army might be employed to compel the states toward a national funding system. Washington rejected the suggestion. He would not introduce the army as a political instrument. But Hamilton’s idea did not die. It found other channels.

The correspondence between Hamilton and Morris that spring reveals how that idea evolved. Morris, as Superintendent of Finance, had been attempting for two years to secure a federal impost—a duty on imports that would give Congress an independent revenue stream. The Articles of Confederation required unanimous consent for such measures, and Rhode Island had refused. Without that revenue, Morris could not pay the army. Without the army’s pressure, he could not force Rhode Island. The logic was circular, and the circle had to be broken.

In March 1783, feeling embittered over their lack of payment from Congress and secretly manipulated by a faction of nationalist politicians in Philadelphia (usually given as Robert Morris, Gouverneur Morris, and Alexander Hamilton), senior officers of the army encamped at the nearby New Windsor Cantonment found themselves reading anonymous addresses that called them to action. The addresses, now known to be the work of Major John Armstrong Jr., an aide-de-camp to General Horatio Gates, articulated what many officers felt. Years of service without compensation. Promises made and not kept. Paper certificates that could not buy provisions. The first address, titled “An Address to the Officers” and dated March 10, 1783, called for a meeting to discuss back pay and other grievances with the Congress and form a plan of action. The addresses asked whether this was to be their reward.

The first address circulated in early March. It spoke of the officers’ patience and their limits. It suggested that if Congress would not pay, the army might need to take matters into its own hands. A second address followed, calling for a meeting on March 15 to consider what action to take. The language was inflammatory. The implication was clear. This would effectively be a military mutiny against the civilian government, that could rapidly devolve into a military coup d’état.

Washington had not been consulted. When he learned of the planned meeting, he recognized the danger immediately. He also recognized that he could not simply forbid it without driving the discontent underground. He requested that the officers meet instead on March 15 at the Temple of Virtue, a large meeting hall at Newburgh, and that they hear what he had to say. The officers agreed. They gathered as summoned, expecting to hear from their commander.

What happened next has become part of the founding mythology. Washington entered the hall uninvited. He spoke to the officers of their duty, their honor, the legacy they would leave if they turned their swords against the government they had fought to create. Then he pulled a letter from his coat, a communication from a congressman that he said he wished to read to them. He began to read, stopped, and pulled from his pocket a pair of spectacles. He put them on, remarking to the assembled officers that he had grown gray in their service and now found himself growing blind. The gesture—human, vulnerable, unexpected—broke the tension. The officers wept. The crisis passed.

The story is true, as far as it goes. Washington’s intervention was decisive. His decision to appear, to speak, to read, to put on his spectacles—all of it happened, and all of it mattered. But the story also conceals as much as it reveals. It presents the crisis as a spontaneous eruption of military discontent, met and mastered by a single act of leadership. It does not ask why the officers had been pushed to such a point, or who had done the pushing.

The Hamilton-Morris correspondence, examined after the fact, shows that the pushing was not accidental. Hamilton had written to Washington in February suggesting that the army’s discontent could be used as leverage. Washington had refused. But Morris, who controlled the nation’s finances, had his own reasons to want that leverage applied. His impost had been blocked. His treasury was empty. His credibility with foreign lenders was evaporating. He needed the army’s pressure to force Congress to act.

The letters between Hamilton and Morris do not contain a smoking gun—an explicit instruction to foment mutiny. What they contain is something more subtle: a shared understanding that the army’s anger was a useful political fact, and that its release might serve a purpose. Hamilton wrote to Morris describing the officers’ state of mind. Morris wrote back describing the financial impossibility of paying them. Each man understood what the other was saying. Neither wrote down the obvious conclusion.

Historian Richard Kohn notes that key details about the individuals and their motivations remain unclear. The historical record does not contain a memo that says “we created this crisis.” What it contains is something almost as damning: a series of letters in which two powerful men discuss the army’s grievances as a pressure point, coordinate their responses, and express satisfaction when that pressure produces movement in Congress.

The coordination between Morris and Hamilton operated on multiple levels. Hamilton was a congressman, positioned to influence legislative debate. Morris was the Superintendent of Finance, positioned to control what money moved and what did not. Together, they formed a nexus of financial and political power that could shape how the crisis was understood and what solutions were proposed.

Hamilton had been advocating for a stronger federal government since his days as Washington’s aide-de-camp. He had seen firsthand the dysfunction of the Continental Congress, its inability to compel the states to contribute men, money, or materiel. He believed that only a central government with real taxing power could meet the nation’s obligations and establish its credit. The army’s unpaid wages were, for Hamilton, both a moral failing and a political opportunity.

Morris shared Hamilton’s nationalist convictions. As Superintendent of Finance, he had attempted to create a national bank, consolidate the debt, and secure the impost. Each of these measures required the states to surrender some portion of their sovereignty. Each had been resisted. Morris had come to believe that only a crisis would break the deadlock.

The letters between them in the spring of 1783 reveal how they assessed the situation. Hamilton wrote of the officers’ “just claims” and their “impatience.” Morris wrote of the “impossibility” of payment without new revenue. Both men knew that the army’s anger was justified. Both men also knew that the army’s anger could be directed.

The anonymous addresses that appeared at Newburgh in March 1783 gave that anger direction. Armstrong, who authored them, was an aide to General Gates, a rival of Washington’s with his own reasons to resent the commander-in-chief. But Armstrong was also connected to the nationalist network. He would later become a favorite of the Constitution, serving in the new government the nationalists created. The addresses he wrote spoke the language of grievance, but they also spoke the language of constitutional crisis.

The first address asked the officers to consider whether they would accept “moderate measures” or demand “justice.” The second called for a meeting to decide. The language was ambiguous enough to permit multiple interpretations. A moderate reading would have the officers petition Congress for their pay. A radical reading would have them threaten Congress with force. The ambiguity was intentional. It allowed the officers to believe they were acting on their own behalf, while allowing the nationalists to claim they had not advocated mutiny.

Washington saw through the ambiguity. When he addressed the officers on March 15, he spoke of the “anonymous” nature of the addresses, the “insidious” attempt to “seduce” them from their duty. He did not name Armstrong. He did not name Hamilton or Morris. But he made clear that he understood the game being played. He would not permit the army to be used as an instrument of political pressure—not even for ends he might otherwise support.

Washington’s intervention succeeded. The officers passed resolutions affirming their loyalty to Congress and their confidence in Washington. The immediate crisis passed. But the underlying conflict did not. The army remained unpaid. The impost remained blocked. The nationalist project remained incomplete.

In the weeks after the Newburgh meeting, the correspondence between Hamilton and Morris turned to what had been achieved. They did not celebrate a catastrophe averted. They assessed a pressure applied. Congress, shaken by the near-mutiny, had begun to move. The impost was being debated again. Other revenue measures were being considered. The army’s anger had done what years of argument had not: it had forced Congress to confront the necessity of federal taxing power.

This was the calculation that the Morris-Hamilton letters reveal. The crisis at Newburgh was not, for them, a danger narrowly escaped. It was a demonstration of what could happen if Congress failed to act. The officers had been calmed this time. But the grievances remained. The next time, the outcome might be different.

The correspondence shows Morris and Hamilton coordinating their public statements and private efforts. Hamilton worked the floor of Congress, advocating for the impost and other nationalist measures. Morris worked the financial channels, making clear that without new revenue, payment was impossible. Each man reinforced the other’s message. The army’s anger was the proof that something had to change.

What the letters do not show is any concern for the officers themselves. The men who had served for years without pay, who held certificates worth fractions of their face value, who faced the prospect of returning home with nothing to show for their sacrifice—these men appear in the correspondence as a problem to be managed, not as people to be helped. Their genuine grievances are acknowledged, but only for their political utility.

This coldness is characteristic of the nationalist project. Hamilton and Morris believed they were building a nation. That project required sacrifices. If the officers’ anger could be used to achieve a larger end, so be it. The correspondence reveals a political calculus that treats human beings as instruments.

The distinction between the public crisis and private calculation becomes clear in the aftermath. Publicly, Congress expressed relief that Washington had restored order. Publicly, the officers expressed their continued loyalty. Privately, Morris and Hamilton assessed how close they had come to forcing the change they sought.

The army’s anger had been genuine. The officers’ grievances were real. No correspondence invented those facts. What the correspondence shows is that the nationalists understood those facts and positioned themselves to benefit from them. They did not create the army’s discontent. They did not need to. They simply ensured that the discontent would be felt where it mattered—in the halls of Congress, where the impost was being debated.

The mechanism of crisis engineering is visible in the letters. First, a genuine grievance exists. Second, that grievance is allowed to fester, because addressing it would require concessions the states are unwilling to make. Third, the grievance finds expression in action—a petition, a meeting, a threat. Fourth, that action is used to pressure the recalcitrant parties to change their position. The engineers do not create the grievance. They create the conditions under which the grievance becomes politically useful.

Morris controlled the financial conditions. Without the impost, he could not pay the army. He did not hide this fact. He made it public, in his reports to Congress and his correspondence with the states. But he also made it clear, in private, that the impasse served a purpose. If the army’s anger forced the states to act, the impost would pass. If the impost passed, the army could be paid. The sequence was logical, even inevitable, once one accepted the premise.

Hamilton provided the political channel. As a congressman, he could argue that the crisis proved the necessity of federal revenue. He could point to Newburgh and say: this is what happens when Congress lacks the power to meet its obligations. The argument was not wrong. But it was also not disinterested. Hamilton had been making the same argument for years. The crisis gave him new evidence.

The correspondence between them shows how they coordinated these messages. Hamilton would write to Morris describing the mood in Congress. Morris would write back describing the financial reality. Each man adjusted his approach based on what the other reported. The letters functioned as a private information network, allowing them to respond to events with a shared strategy.

This coordination did not require explicit conspiracy. Neither man needed to write “let us foment a mutiny.” They simply needed to understand that the army’s anger was useful, that its expression could be channeled, and that its suppression by Washington did not eliminate the underlying pressure. The crisis had passed, but the lesson remained. Congress had been frightened. The impost was being reconsidered. The nationalist project had advanced.

The human cost of this calculation fell on the officers themselves. They had been used. Their genuine anger, their real suffering, had been treated as a political lever. Washington had saved them from the worst consequences of their own actions. But he had not secured their pay. The certificates they held continued to trade at deep discounts. The promises made to them remained unfunded.

The correspondence between Morris and Hamilton shows no recognition of this cost. They wrote of the crisis as a political event, to be managed and exploited. The officers appear as a mass, not as individuals. Their sacrifices are acknowledged, but only as evidence of Congress’s failure, not as claims upon conscience.

This is the nature of power that the Newburgh conspiracy reveals. The officers had power of a sort—the power to disrupt, to threaten, to refuse. But their power was crude and easily channeled. Washington had turned it aside with a gesture. Morris and Hamilton had used it without risking anything themselves. The real power lay elsewhere: in the control of information, in the coordination of political strategy, in the ability to shape how a crisis was understood.

The Hamilton-Morris correspondence unmasked shows who held that power and how they used it. They were not evil men. They believed, with reason, that the nation needed a stronger central government. They believed, with reason, that the states’ refusal to fund Congress endangered everything the Revolution had achieved. They were willing to use the army’s anger to achieve those ends. The correspondence reveals the calculation, not the malice.

But the calculation had consequences. The officers learned that their service could be taken for granted. The soldiers learned that their sacrifices could be discounted. The certificates they held, traded at fifteen cents on the dollar, told a story about the value of promises made by governments that could not tax. The nationalists would point to that story as evidence of why taxing power was necessary. The officers would live with the consequences.

In the summer of 1783, the army began to disband. The preliminary peace treaty had been signed in April. The definitive treaty would come in September. The war was over. The officers went home, some with certificates in their pockets, some with nothing at all. The grievances that had brought them to the edge of mutiny remained unresolved.

Gates, whose aide had written the anonymous addresses, returned to Virginia in silence. His wife Elizabeth had died that summer. He retired to his estate, Traveller’s Rest, and took no public role in the debates that followed. Armstrong, the author of the addresses, would eventually find his way into the new government, his earlier radicalism forgiven or forgotten. Washington returned to Mount Vernon, expecting to retire from public life. Hamilton and Morris continued their work, building the financial system that would eventually replace the Articles of Confederation.

The correspondence between Hamilton and Morris remained private. It would not become public for years. But its effects were felt immediately. The crisis at Newburgh had demonstrated what could happen when Congress lacked the power to pay its debts. The nationalists used that demonstration to argue for change. The change they sought—a federal government with taxing authority—would come, but not before more crises, more failures, more demonstrations of the Articles’ inadequacy.

The letters show the mechanism of that change. They show how genuine grievances are transformed into political leverage. They show how private coordination shapes public outcomes. They show how the engineers of crisis operate in the space between what is said and what is understood.

The officers at Newburgh had believed they were acting on their own behalf. They were. But they were also acting, unknowingly, on behalf of men who saw their anger as useful. The correspondence reveals the dual nature of the conspiracy: one strand operating in the open, through anonymous addresses and officers’ meetings; the other operating in private, through letters that coordinated how the crisis would be interpreted and used.

Washington had seen the danger. His intervention had prevented the worst outcome. But even Washington could not undo the structural conditions that had produced the crisis. The army remained unpaid. The debt remained unfunded. The states remained sovereign. The nationalist lever had been pulled, but the machine had not yet moved.

The correspondence between Hamilton and Morris is a record of men who believed they knew how to move it. They understood power differently than Washington did. Washington believed in personal authority, in the force of character, in the willingness to stand before a room of angry men and ask them to trust him. Hamilton and Morris believed in structural change, in financial systems, in the alignment of incentives. They were willing to use Washington’s authority when it served their purposes. They were also willing to work around it when it did not.

The letters show the limits of each approach. Washington’s intervention had calmed the officers, but it had not solved the problem. Hamilton and Morris’s coordination had advanced their political project, but it had not secured the impost. Both approaches were necessary, and neither was sufficient. The crisis at Newburgh had ended, but the underlying conflict continued.

The officers went home. The correspondence continued. Congress debated. The states resisted. The certificates traded at deep discounts. The promise of payment remained, in every sense of the word, unfunded.

What the Hamilton-Morris letters reveal is not a secret plot so much as a shared understanding. These men saw the army’s anger as a resource. They did not create it, but they positioned themselves to use it. They coordinated their responses. They advanced their agenda. They accepted the consequences without accepting responsibility.

The officers who had gathered at the Temple of Virtue on March 15 did not know that their anger had been discussed in private letters. They did not know that Morris and Hamilton had assessed their grievances as useful pressure. They knew only that they had not been paid, that their certificates were worthless, that their government had failed them. Washington had asked them to trust that failure would be remedied. They had agreed to wait.

But waiting, they would learn, was not the same as receiving. The certificates they held would be traded and sold, passing into the hands of speculators who had never served a day in uniform. The men who purchased those certificates at fifteen cents on the dollar would eventually be paid in full. The men who had earned them would receive a fraction of what they were owed.

The Hamilton-Morris correspondence shows how such outcomes are engineered. Not through conspiracy in the shadows, but through the coordination of interests, the management of crises, the use of genuine grievances to advance predetermined ends. The engineers do not need to control every variable. They need only to position themselves where the pressure will be felt, and to ensure that the pressure is directed toward the outcome they desire.

The nationalist lever has been pulled, but the army’s pay remains unfunded, and Horatio Gates has returned to Virginia in silence.
