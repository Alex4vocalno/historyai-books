Chapter 14

Gates in the Shadows

On March 23, 1783, a memorandum was issued from the Continental Army’s headquarters at Newburgh. Produced as the encampment emptied and soldiers departed, it contained the formal language of military assignment, preceding the final returns and the closing of the quartermasters’ ledgers. The document said nothing about the crisis that had just passed. That silence was its first official sound.

The order was routine, assigning Major General Horatio Gates to command the troops remaining at Newburgh. It bore the familiar language of military bureaucracy: units accounted for, posts assigned, authority confirmed. Nowhere in its text did it mention the meeting that had taken place at Gates’s headquarters on the morning of March 15. Nowhere did it acknowledge the anonymous addresses that had circulated through the camp calling the officers to assemble. Nowhere did it refer to the fact that Gates himself had called that meeting to order, had sat in the chair while the temper of the room rose toward mutiny, had watched without protest until Washington walked through the door.

Gates received it at his headquarters, the same building where, days before, he had presided over the most dangerous gathering the Continental Army had ever seen. He read it, or perhaps he did not read it, and he signed the acknowledgment that a clerk prepared for him. The clerk noted the signature in the record. The record was filed. The war went on, which meant that army went on being unpaid, and the officers went on waiting for peace to become official, and Gates went on being a major general in the service of the United States.

The aftermath of Newburgh brought no trial, no inquiry, no court-martial. The conspiracy, if it was a conspiracy, had failed. Washington had entered the Temple of Virtue uninvited, had read a letter from a member of Congress, had put on his spectacles and asked his officers to forgive him for growing gray in their service. The room had wept. The crisis had passed. And now, in the weeks that followed, the question that should have demanded an answer received none. Who had written the addresses? Who had called the meeting? Who had stood by while the army was urged to threaten the civilian government with its swords?

The answers were known, or strongly suspected. Major John Armstrong Jr., Gates’s aide-de-camp, was generally acknowledged as the author of the two anonymous letters. The first, titled “An Address to the Officers” and circulated on March 10, 1783, called for a meeting to discuss back pay and other grievances with Congress and form a plan of action. The second, issued four days later after Washington canceled that meeting, urged the officers to distrust their commander-in-chief and take matters into their own hands. The language was careful, but the implication was clear: if the civilian government would not pay, the army might have to compel payment by force.

Armstrong had written the words. That much was understood within days of the addresses’ appearance. But Armstrong was Gates’s aide, stationed at Gates’s headquarters at Newburgh, writing with access to Gates’s papers and Gates’s confidence. The meeting on March 15 had been called to Gates’s quarters. Gates himself had convened it. When Washington arrived, uninvited and unexpected, Gates yielded the floor without resistance. He sat in silence while Washington spoke. He offered no defense, no explanation, no counter-argument. He simply watched as the room turned away from him and toward the man who had led them for eight years.

And then the meeting ended, and the army dispersed, and Gates was assigned to command what remained. No inquiry followed. No court-martial was convened. No officer was asked to explain his presence at the meeting or his role in circulating the addresses. The silence was a choice, not an accident.

Washington made that choice. In the days after March 15, he could have demanded an investigation. He could have ordered Armstrong arrested and questioned. He could have required Gates to account for his actions, to explain why the meeting had been held at his headquarters, why he had not reported the addresses to his commander-in-chief, why he had allowed himself to be the vehicle for what looked very much like an attempted mutiny. Washington had the authority. He had the evidence. He had the loyalty of the army, which had been demonstrated in the most dramatic fashion at the Temple of Virtue.

But Washington did nothing.

He wrote letters. To Congress, he warned that the army should not be used as an instrument of political pressure. To Hamilton, he declined to introduce the army as a tool for advancing nationalist aims. To his officers, he expressed gratitude for their restraint and continued service. But he did not write to Gates. He did not summon Armstrong for questioning. He did not convene a board of inquiry or refer the matter to a court-martial.

The reason for this inaction lay in the crisis itself. The Newburgh Conspiracy may have been orchestrated by prominent nationalists in Congress, including Alexander Hamilton and Robert Morris, to pressure the states to grant Congress a federal taxing power. Whether the conspiracy was a genuine military threat or a manufactured political crisis, its resolution required something other than punishment. Washington understood that a public reckoning would expose divisions within the officer corps, would reveal the extent of congressional involvement, and would force the army to air its grievances in a forum that could only deepen the wound.

Better to let the matter fade. Better to let Gates and Armstrong slip into obscurity, their influence diminished, their faction broken, their challenge to Washington’s authority ended without a formal charge. Better, in short, to neutralize the opposition than to prosecute it.

Gates remained at his post through the spring. His wife Elizabeth fell ill that summer. She had been a presence in his life for decades, a connection to the Virginia gentry that had helped him establish himself after years of service in the British Army. Now she lay dying at Traveller’s Rest, the estate Gates had built in the Shenandoah Valley. He applied for leave to visit her, and Washington granted it without comment. Gates traveled south, arriving too late. Elizabeth died in the summer of 1783. Gates buried her and remained at the estate, absent from the army during its final months.

Armstrong also left. He resigned his commission and returned to Pennsylvania, where he had family connections and property. Later, he would claim that he had written the addresses on his own initiative, without Gates’s knowledge or approval. He would insist that the letters were meant only to express the legitimate grievances of the officers, not to incite mutiny. He would spend the rest of his life defending his actions, or distancing himself from them, depending on his audience. But in the summer of 1783, he simply disappeared from the army’s rolls. No one asked him to stay. No one asked him to explain.

The silence extended to the official records. Washington’s general orders for the spring and summer of 1783 made no mention of the addresses, the meeting, or the suspected authors. The correspondence between headquarters and Congress avoided the subject. When officers wrote to one another, they sometimes referred obliquely to “the late unpleasantness” or “the business of March,” but they did not name names. They did not demand justice. They accepted, as Washington accepted, that the crisis had passed and that the best way to preserve the army’s unity was to let the matter die.

Washington’s silence was calculation, not forgiveness.

Washington had spent eight years building the Continental Army into a fighting force capable of defeating the British Empire. The logistics, the strategy, the politics—all of these he had managed. The jealousies of rival generals, the interference of Congress, the incompetence of supply officers, the desertions of enlisted men, the betrayals of trusted subordinates—through all of these he had navigated. He had learned that the army’s strength depended on its unity, and that unity depended on his ability to manage conflict without destroying the institution he led.

Gates had been a problem before. In the early years of the war, he had competed with Washington for command of the army. He had been the beneficiary of the Conway Cabal, the whispering campaign in 1777-78 that had sought to replace Washington with a more aggressive commander. He had presided over the victory at Saratoga, a success that had enhanced his reputation while Washington suffered through the winter at Valley Forge. The hero of the northern army, he was called, while Washington was the general who could not win a battle. The rivalry between them was old, deep, and unspoken.

But Gates had also suffered defeats. His conduct at the Battle of Camden in 1780 had been a disaster. He had fled the field, leaving his army to be destroyed. Congress had passed a resolution calling for a board of inquiry, the prelude to a court-martial, to look into his conduct. The board for Camden never convened. The war moved on. Gates was reassigned to a minor command, and then to Newburgh, where he waited with the rest of the army for peace to be signed.

Now he had presided over another crisis, one that threatened to destroy the civilian government that the army had fought to create. And again, he faced no formal consequences.

Washington could have destroyed him. A court-martial for Gates would have exposed his role in the March 15 meeting, would have forced him to explain his relationship with Armstrong, would have required him to defend himself against charges of mutiny or sedition. The evidence might not have been sufficient for a conviction—Gates had not written the addresses, had not spoken at the meeting, had not explicitly endorsed the threat of military force—but the trial itself would have ruined him. His reputation would have been shattered. His faction would have been eliminated. Washington’s authority would have been absolute.

But Washington chose another path. He chose silence.

The reason lay in power itself. Washington understood that his authority rested on more than rank. It rested on the loyalty of his officers, the respect of his soldiers, the confidence of Congress, and the trust of the American people. A public trial of a major general would have shaken that foundation. It would have revealed the depth of the army’s discontent, the extent of congressional manipulation, the fragility of the civilian-military relationship. It would have shown the world that the Continental Army, at the moment of its victory, had been on the verge of turning its guns on the government it served.

That truth was too dangerous to speak.

So Washington let it go unsaid. He allowed Gates to retire without disgrace. He allowed Armstrong to resign without being asked to account for his actions. He allowed the army to disperse without a public reckoning. By doing so, he preserved the unity of the officer corps, the credibility of the revolutionary government, and the myth of the Continental Army as a force that had fought for liberty rather than for pay.

Gates spent the summer at Traveller’s Rest. He did not return to active duty. In 1784, he formally retired from the army. He served as vice president of the Society of the Cincinnati, the organization of former Continental Army officers, and presided over its meetings when Washington could not attend. He remained a figure of some standing in Virginia society, a hero of Saratoga, a veteran of the war. But he never again commanded troops. He never again challenged Washington. He never again posed a threat to the chain of command.

Armstrong went on to a career in politics. He served in the Continental Congress, as a United States Senator from New York, and as Minister to France. He wrote a history of the war and defended his actions at Newburgh in memoirs and letters. He insisted that he had acted alone, that Gates had known nothing of the addresses until they were circulated, that the meeting had been called to restrain the officers rather than to incite them. The documents do not support his claims. The timing of the addresses, the location of the meeting, the presence of Gates in the chair—all of these facts point to a coordinated effort, or at least to Gates’s knowledge and acquiescence.

But knowledge is not the same as guilt. Gates may have known what Armstrong was writing. He may have approved it, or he may have failed to stop it. The surviving papers do not say. Washington chose silence, and silence left no record.

Private letters among the officers reveal that Gates’s reputation suffered. He was no longer trusted. His rivals whispered that he had been complicit in an attempt to subvert civilian authority. His allies distanced themselves. The faction that had gathered around him—the officers who had doubted Washington, who had resented his caution, who had believed that the war could be won more quickly by more aggressive action—scattered. Some retired. Some transferred to other posts. Some simply stopped writing to him.

The neutralization of Gates was complete. He had not been punished, but he had been rendered powerless. His base of support had evaporated. His standing in the army had been destroyed. His rivalry with Washington had ended, not in a dramatic confrontation, but in a quiet withdrawal. He was still a major general. He was still a hero of Saratoga. He was still a member of the Society of the Cincinnati. But he was no longer a threat.

Armstrong fared better, or at least he survived. His connection to Gates was known, but he was young, and his family had influence, and the new nation needed men of talent. He reinvented himself as a politician and a diplomat, leaving the army behind. He never faced formal charges. He simply moved on, as though the events of March 1783 had been a misunderstanding rather than a crisis.

The officers who had attended the meeting at Gates’s headquarters also escaped consequence. They had listened to the anonymous addresses. They had debated the merits of threatening Congress. They had considered the possibility of marching on Philadelphia. And then they had wept at Washington’s words, had voted to thank him for his service, had returned to their quarters to wait for the peace that was already signed in Paris. No one asked them to explain their presence. No one demanded to know what they had intended. No one held them accountable for what they might have done.

The army dispersed. The officers went home. The soldiers went home. The promises of pay, of pensions, of compensation for years of service—these promises remained unfulfilled. Congress had passed a compromise: instead of the half-pay for life that had been promised, the officers would receive five years of full pay in certificates. But the certificates were not money. They were paper, backed by a government that had no power to tax and no reliable source of revenue. They could be sold, at a discount, to speculators who would wait for them to mature. They could be held, in the hope that the government would someday honor them. But they could not be spent, not in the way that soldiers needed to spend, not for food and clothing and shelter and the tools to rebuild lives disrupted by eight years of war.

The grievances that had fueled the Newburgh Conspiracy did not disappear because Washington gave a good speech. They remained, embedded in the certificates that the soldiers carried home, embedded in the promises that the government had made and could not keep. The conspiracy had failed, but the conditions that created it—the unpaid army, the powerless Congress, the frustrated veterans—these conditions persisted.

What changed was the leadership. Washington had demonstrated that he could control the army. He could stand before his officers and ask them to trust him, and they would trust him. He could appeal to their honor, their sacrifice, their shared struggle, and they would respond. He could turn a potential mutiny into a moment of unity, and he could do it without firing a shot or arresting a single man.

This was power. Not the power to command armies in battle, though Washington had that too. This was the power to shape events through personal authority, to bend the will of men without forcing them, to resolve a crisis through presence rather than violence. Washington had built it over eight years of war, through countless small acts of leadership, through failures and successes, through the slow accumulation of trust.

And it was a power that depended on silence.

The silence around Gates, around Armstrong, around the events of March 1783, was the foundation of Washington’s authority. By not speaking, he allowed the crisis to fade. By not punishing, he allowed the army to remain united. By not investigating, he allowed the officers to return to their homes with their honor intact, if not their pay. He chose reconciliation over recrimination, unity over justice, silence over truth.

The choice had costs. The officers who had been willing to threaten Congress were never held accountable. The politicians who had encouraged the crisis—Hamilton, Morris, and their allies—were never forced to explain their role. The grievances of the soldiers were never fully addressed. The certificates they carried home would become a source of controversy, a burden on the new government, a reminder of promises unkept.

But the immediate crisis passed. The army did not march on Philadelphia. The civilian government was not overthrown. The Revolution did not devour its children, at least not this time.

Gates lived out his years at Traveller’s Rest. He married again, to a wealthy widow, and maintained his position in Virginia society. He corresponded with old friends and old rivals, including Washington. He never wrote about Newburgh, at least not in any document that survives. He never explained why he had called the meeting, what he had known, what he had intended. He took whatever answers he had to his grave.

Armstrong lived longer and wrote more. He defended himself in his memoirs, insisted on his innocence, blamed others for the crisis. But even he did not tell the full story, or perhaps he did not know it. The documents that survive—letters, orders, minutes of meetings—tell a partial story. They show that Armstrong wrote the addresses, that Gates hosted the meeting, that Washington intervened. They show that the crisis was real, that the threat was serious, that the outcome was uncertain until Washington walked through the door.

What they do not show is who knew what, who planned what, who intended what. The silence that Washington chose, the silence that preserved the army’s unity, also obscured the truth. The suspected architects of the conspiracy were neutralized, not punished. Their factions were broken, their influence eliminated, their challenge to Washington’s authority ended. But the questions remained.

Gates was the key. As long as he remained a major general, as long as he commanded respect among the officers, as long as he offered an alternative to Washington’s leadership, the army remained divided. The faction that gathered around him, the officers who doubted Washington, the men who believed that more aggressive action was needed—these were the ones who might have turned the conspiracy into a real threat. Without Gates, without his headquarters, without his presence in the chair on March 15, the addresses might have been ignored. With him, they were dangerous.

But after March 15, Gates was finished. Not disgraced, not punished, not driven from the service in shame. Simply finished. His influence gone, his faction scattered, his challenge to Washington ended. He remained a major general on paper, but he commanded nothing. He was a shadow of his former self, a hero of Saratoga who had presided over a mutiny and survived, but only as a survivor, not as a leader.

The summer of 1783 passed. The army continued to disperse. The peace was signed, officially, in September. The war was over. The Revolution had succeeded. And the men who had made it succeed—the officers who had led the troops, the politicians who had managed the Congress, the general who had commanded the army—these men now faced the question of what came next.

Washington returned to Mount Vernon, or planned to. He would be called back, eventually, to preside over the Constitutional Convention, to become the first President of the United States, to establish the precedents that would shape the new government. He would be remembered as the father of his country, the man who had won the war and then, uniquely among revolutionary generals, had stepped down rather than seizing power.

But that was later. In the summer of 1783, Washington was still the commander-in-chief of an army that was dissolving around him. He was still responsible for the men who had served under him, for the promises that had been made to them, for the peace that they had won. And he was still managing the aftermath of the crisis that had threatened to destroy everything he had built.

His management was silent. He did not speak of Gates, did not speak of Armstrong, did not speak of the addresses or the meeting or the threat. He let the matter fade, as he had let so many other matters fade, because he understood that silence was sometimes more powerful than speech.

The officers went home. The soldiers went home. The army that had camped at Newburgh, that had waited for peace, that had threatened mutiny, that had been calmed by Washington’s words—that army dispersed into the countryside, carrying its grievances and its certificates, its memories and its wounds. The suspected architects had been neutralized, not punished, leaving their grievances and those of all veterans as unfunded promises.
