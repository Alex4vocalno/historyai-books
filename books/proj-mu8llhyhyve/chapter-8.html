Chapter 8

Washington’s Spectacles

Washington stood at the back of the Temple of Virtue, a large rectangular hall with rough-hewn beams and a plank floor worn smooth by boots. The building, constructed for dances and public gatherings, now held nearly every general and field-grade officer in the Continental Army. They had been summoned by an anonymous address. Years without pay had embittered them; peace had been signed in Paris, and no one had told them what happened next. The question was whether those purposes could be reconciled, or whether the tension between them would produce something that no one had intended.

Washington had not been expected. The meeting had been called by and for the officers, under the presidency of General Horatio Gates. The anonymous addresses, the first titled ‘An Address to the Officers’ and dated March 10, 1783, and a second appearing two days later, made clear that the commander-in-chief was not to lead this assembly. The authors wanted the officers to act as a body independent of Washington’s authority. They wanted grievance to become action. They wanted an ultimatum to Congress: pay us, or we will not disband.

The room was full. Major John Armstrong Jr., aide to General Gates and the author of the addresses, sat near the front. He had drafted the words that brought these men together. He had calculated the effect. The first address had denounced Congress for failing in justice to the army. The second had called for this meeting, on this day, at this hour, to decide whether the army would reject those who had deceived them or dare to act on their own behalf. The language was deliberate. It echoed the rhetoric of 1776, of rights violated and tyrannies resisted. But the target was not George III. It was the Continental Congress.

Gates sat at the front, presiding. Since the war began, he had been Washington’s rival. He had commanded at Saratoga while Washington lost battle after battle. He had corresponded with members of Congress who doubted Washington’s abilities. Now he presided over a meeting that might decide the fate of the republic. The officers waited. What Gates would say remained unclear. What Armstrong had written was known only to a few. Who had arranged this moment, and why, was a question that hung in the air.

Washington entered through the rear door. The officers turned. He wore his blue and buff uniform, the same coat he had worn since 1775. Fifty-one years old, he had commanded this army for eight years. More battles lost than won. Retreated across New Jersey, frozen at Valley Forge, waited through years of defeat. He had also kept the army together. Held the line when Congress fled, when supplies failed, when the states refused to pay. Now he stood at the back of a room full of men who had followed him through all of it, and who had been brought there by an anonymous address that called his patience a weakness.

The room fell silent. Gates looked up. Washington walked forward. Permission to speak was not asked. Gates was not acknowledged as president of the meeting. The floor was simply taken, and the floor was his.

“Gentlemen,” he said. His voice was quiet. The room was still.

In the days since the first address appeared, Washington had learned things. The meeting was not spontaneous. The anonymous calls for action had been coordinated with political figures in Philadelphia. Men he trusted, Alexander Hamilton among them, had known what was being planned and had not warned him. The crisis was not simply a matter of unpaid soldiers reaching their breaking point. It was a crisis engineered to produce a result.

Nationalist politicians had shaped a conspiracy that spring. They wanted to use the army’s grievance to force Congress to adopt a federal taxing power. The anonymous addresses were the instrument. The meeting in the Temple of Virtue was the stage. The officers were the actors, and they did not know they had been cast.

Washington knew. A letter from Hamilton dated February 13 had warned him of the danger posed by an army without pay and had suggested that Washington might need to influence the officers toward a stronger government. The letter had been careful. What Hamilton wanted Washington to do was not stated directly. That he and Robert Morris and Gouverneur Morris were working to create a crisis that would force Congress to act was not admitted. Unrest was warned of, and readiness implied.

Now Washington stood before the officers. A letter was held in his hand. It was not from Hamilton. Congressman Joseph Jones of Virginia, a member of the committee appointed to address the army’s grievances, had written to Washington expressing sympathy for the officers and explaining the difficulties Congress faced. The letter was conciliatory. The justice of the army’s claims was acknowledged. Patience was asked.

Washington began to read. His voice was low. The room was silent. Jones’s words about the embarrassments that had delayed payment were read. The arduous task facing Congress was described. The need for cool, dispassionate consideration was urged.

The officers listened. Some shifted in their seats. The letter was not what they had come to hear. Action had brought them. The anonymous addresses had told them that patience had become a species of cowardice, that hope had become a delusion, that the time had come to suspect the man who would advise moderation. Now Washington stood before them reading a letter that asked for exactly that.

The text was struggled with. The letter was handwritten, and the light in the Temple was poor. Washington paused. The paper was looked down at. The officers were looked up at. Then a pocket was reached into, and a pair of spectacles drawn out.

The gesture was simple. It was also theater.

For years, Washington had worn spectacles. His officers had seen him use them in private, when reading dispatches or examining maps. But never in public had they seen him wear them. The commander-in-chief, the man who had held the army together through eight years of defeat and privation, had never shown this small weakness before a room full of subordinates.

The spectacles were put on. The officers were looked at. And then the words that turned the room were spoken.

“Gentlemen,” Washington said, “you must pardon me. I have grown gray in your service, and now find myself growing blind.”

The effect was immediate. The officers who had come to the Temple prepared to confront their commander found themselves confronting instead the evidence of his sacrifice. Gray had been grown. Blindness was coming. Eight years had been given to a cause that had nearly killed him, and he stood before them not as a general demanding obedience but as a man asking for understanding.

Suspicion had been called for by the anonymous addresses. Defiance had been called for. The officers had been told to suspect the man who would advise moderation. Now Washington stood before them, wearing spectacles he had never worn in public, asking them to pardon him for growing old in their service.

The room changed. The anger that had been building for weeks, the resentment that the anonymous addresses had shaped into a weapon, the frustration that had brought these men to the verge of mutiny, all of it shifted. The floor had been taken uninvited. A letter asking for patience had been read. And then something no political calculation could have predicted was done. His age was shown.

The gesture worked because it was true. Gray had been grown. Blindness was growing. Years of life had been given to a cause that had paid nothing but promises. The officers knew this. Beside him they had served. At Valley Forge they had seen him, when the army froze and starved and Congress did nothing. The line held when everything seemed lost had been seen. They had followed him because nothing was asked of them that he had not given himself.

Now they were asked to wait. Trust was asked for. The anonymous addresses that called for defiance were to be rejected, and the slow, uncertain process of republican government accepted instead.

Gates had been in the chair when the meeting began. The expectation had been that the officers would debate and perhaps vote on the ultimatum proposed by the anonymous addresses. The possibility had existed that the Continental Army would declare itself unwilling to disband until paid, that Philadelphia would be marched on, that Congress would be forced to act at the point of bayonets.

Washington had not been invited. He had come anyway. The floor had been taken. And with a single gesture, the simple act of putting on spectacles, the nature of the assembly had been changed.

Jones’s letter was finished. Brief remarks were spoken, advising the officers to reject the anonymous addresses, to trust Congress, to wait. The words were measured. The authors of the addresses were not attacked, though they were known. Gates was not denounced, though what he had allowed was understood. The political machinations behind the crisis were not revealed, though they were comprehended. Patience was simply asked for.

Then he left.

Washington exited the hall, leaving behind a room emotionally transformed but with its formal business unresolved. The officers sat in silence. Gates remained in the chair. Armstrong sat near the front, his addresses now useless. Action had been the purpose for which the meeting was called. Washington had turned it into something else.

Whether the army would defy Congress had been the question hanging over the Temple of Virtue. The anonymous addresses had framed that question as a matter of honor. Patience had been called cowardice, moderation betrayal, the time for action come. Washington had reframed the question. Loyalty—not to Congress, but to him—was what it had been made.

The distinction mattered. For eight years, the officers had followed Washington. Trust had been placed in him when Congress failed, when supplies did not come, when the states refused to contribute. He had been trusted because he had suffered with them. Now they were asked to trust him one more time. Not because Congress deserved trust, but because he did.

The power of Washington’s gesture lay in its simplicity. A thundering oration had not been delivered. Threats had not been made, nor cajolery. The political conspiracy behind the anonymous addresses had not been revealed. A letter had simply been stood before and read, the text struggled with, spectacles put on. The act was human. It was vulnerable. It was exactly what the anonymous addresses had not accounted for.

Everything had been calculated by the authors of the addresses. The timing, the moment when peace had been signed but not announced, when the army’s leverage was at its peak, had been calculated. The language, the echoes of 1776, the appeals to honor and justice, had been calculated. The target, not Washington, but Congress, with Washington cast as the moderate who could not be trusted, had been calculated. The outcome, a meeting that would produce an ultimatum, an ultimatum that would force Congress to act, a crisis that would create the federal taxing power they wanted, had been calculated.

The spectacles had not been calculated.

The gesture was not entirely spontaneous. Theater was understood by Washington. Since his youth, it had been understood, when the persona of the stoic commander was carefully cultivated. At Valley Forge it had been understood, when the troops were ridden among and their suffering shared. Throughout the war it had been understood, when appearance and silence were used to project authority. Now it was understood. More than a general reading a letter was needed by the officers. A man was needed to be seen.

The spectacles were real. The difficulty reading was real. The gray hair and failing eyesight were real. Eight years had been given to the cause. Visible aging had occurred. Health, fortune, and youth had been sacrificed. The gesture worked because it was true.

But it also worked because visibility was chosen. Never before had spectacles been worn in public. Weaknesses had been hidden, as commanders do. Now one was revealed, at the precise moment when revelation would have the greatest effect. The timing was not accidental. Expecting to speak, Washington had arrived at the Temple. Jones’s letter had been brought. Remarks had been prepared. And when the moment came, the spectacles that had been worn in private for years were reached for and drawn out.

What he wanted them to see was seen by the officers. Their commander, old before his time, struggling to read a letter that asked for patience, was seen. The cost of eight years of war was seen. Sacrifice was seen, and they were shamed.

The shame was the point. The officers’ pride had been appealed to by the anonymous addresses. Patience had been said to be beneath them, Congress said to have insulted them, honor said to demand action. Something deeper was appealed to by Washington. What they had already given was reminded to them. What he had given was reminded to them. And a little more was asked to be given.

A vote did not end the meeting. The anonymous addresses had been advised to be rejected and Congress trusted. A committee to prepare a report on their grievances had been asked to be appointed. Waiting had been asked for. After he left, what he suggested was done. A committee, General Henry Knox, General John Brooks, and Captain John F. Dearborn, was appointed to draft a response. The report was prepared by the committee as it retired. The meeting adjourned.

Gates remained in the chair, but the presidency had passed from him. The meeting he had presided over had become Washington’s meeting. The addresses Armstrong had written had become waste paper. The crisis that nationalist politicians had engineered had become a demonstration of Washington’s authority.

Into the cold March air the officers filed. Expecting confrontation, they had come. A lesson in power they left having received. Not the power of the anonymous addresses, with their appeals to honor and their calls for defiance. Not the power of the nationalist politicians who had engineered the crisis. But the power of a single individual who understood that authority comes not from position alone but from the willingness to reveal the human cost of holding it.

A gesture had turned the room. Simple, the gesture was. The calculation behind it was not. Knowing what he faced, he had arrived at the Temple. Anger was known to be in the officers. A direction had been given to that anger by the anonymous addresses. The army’s grievance was being used by political forces in Philadelphia to force a result they could not achieve through ordinary means. Mutiny could be the meeting’s end.

Something else was also known. Trust was placed in him by the officers. The one force stronger than grievance was known to be that trust. If they could be reminded of what they had shared—eight years of war, eight years of sacrifice, eight years of loyalty to him and to each other—the power of the anonymous addresses would be lost.

So he stood before them. A letter was read. The text was struggled with. Spectacles were put on. And pardon was asked for growing gray and blind in their service.

The room turned. The anger subsided. The crisis passed.

But the underlying problem remained. The officers had not been paid. No money was had by Congress. Contribution was refused by the states. No power to tax was possessed by the federal government under the Articles of Confederation. That was wanted to be changed by the nationalist politicians who had engineered the crisis. A federal taxing power that could support a standing army and pay the national debt was what they wanted. A stronger central government was wanted. The army’s grievance had been used to create pressure for that result.

The immediate crisis had been defused by Washington. Mutiny had been prevented. The principle of civilian control had been preserved. But the underlying problem had not been solved. Payment was still owed to the officers. Revenue was still lacking in the government. The federal taxing power was still wanted by the nationalists. The crisis had been delayed, not resolved.

In the days that followed, the report was drafted by the committee. Affirmation of loyalty to Congress and trust in Washington was made by the officers. The anonymous addresses and the call for defiance were rejected. Agreement was made to wait. But clear was made that infinite patience was not possessed. Action was asked of Congress. Payment was asked for.

Congress, facing the possibility that the army might refuse to disband, finally acted. A resolution granting the officers five years of full pay in lieu of the half-pay pensions they had been promised was passed. A compromise it was. What had originally been promised to the officers it was not. But something it was.

Everything they wanted had not been achieved by the nationalist politicians. Their federal taxing power had not been gotten—not yet. Later that would come, in a different crisis, with a different constitution. But something had been demonstrated. That the weakness of the central government could produce emergencies that threatened the republic had been shown. That the army’s grievance could be used as a political weapon had been shown. That crisis could be engineered had been shown.

Something else had been shown by Washington. That a single individual, armed with nothing but the moral authority of sacrifice, could stand against that engineered crisis and turn it aside had been demonstrated. That power could be exercised through humility as well as through force had been shown. That the officers would follow him even when following meant waiting had been proven.

Less than an hour the meeting in the Temple of Virtue lasted. Perhaps fifteen minutes Washington spoke. Seconds the gesture with the spectacles took. But everything was changed by those seconds.

Action had been called for by the anonymous addresses. Patience had been called for by Washington. Pride had been appealed to by the addresses. Loyalty had been appealed to by Washington. Defiance had been offered by the addresses. Trust had been offered by Washington. Washington was chosen.

Not because payment was promised by him were they chosen. Payment had not been promised. Not because trust had been earned by Congress were they chosen. Trust had not been earned. Because the right to ask had been earned by him were they chosen. Through eight years of shared suffering it had been earned. Through the gray in his hair and the failing of his eyes it had been earned. Through the simple act of standing before them and asking to be pardoned for growing old in their service it had been earned.

A result had been designed to be produced by the crisis engineered by nationalist politicians. A different one had been produced by Washington. The floor had been taken uninvited. A letter had been read. Spectacles had been put on. And he had walked out, leaving behind a room that no longer wanted to defy Congress, a room that no longer trusted the anonymous addresses, a room that had remembered why it had followed him in the first place.

The officers dispersed. Some returned to their huts. Some gathered in small groups to discuss what had happened. Some wrote letters home. The army would wait. Mutiny would not occur. Philadelphia would not be marched on. Washington would be trusted one more time.

But infinite was not the trust. Their patience had been given to Washington. Well used it was expected to be. Action was expected of Congress. Payment was expected. The promises made to them were expected to be kept.

If they were not, the anonymous addresses would return. The anger would return. The crisis would return. And not always would Washington be there to put on his spectacles and turn the room.

This was known. Time had been bought. The republic had been preserved from its most dangerous moment. But the underlying problem had not been solved. Its debts still could not be paid by the government. Contribution was still refused by the states. The army still waited.

Washington exited the hall. The officers remained. Gates remained. Armstrong remained. On paper the anonymous addresses remained, if not in influence. The crisis had passed, but its causes remained.

Action had been the purpose for which the meeting was called. A demonstration of restraint was what it had been turned into by Washington. But restraint was not resolution. Agreement had been made to wait. Waiting was not the same as being paid.

A crisis to force a result had been engineered by the nationalist politicians in Philadelphia. Failed, for now. But something about the power of crisis had been learned by them. They would remember.

Something had been learned by Washington too. Personal, not institutional, was his authority over the army—this he had learned. Him they followed, not Congress. Him they trusted, not the government. As long as he was there, they could be held back. But not always would he be there.

The spectacles went back into his pocket. The gray hair remained. The failing eyesight remained. The sacrifice remained. And the officers, filing out of the Temple of Virtue into the cold March afternoon, carried with them the memory of a commander who had asked them to pardon him for growing old in their service.

They would remember. They would wait. They would trust.

But paid they also expected to be.
