Chapter 7

The Meeting Called

The answers would come on March 15. The Temple of Virtue stood ready that morning, a forty-by-seventy-foot wooden hall the army had built with its own hands for sermons and dances, now repurposed for something neither. The raw timber walls had witnessed regimental balls and Sunday services. They were about to witness a reckoning.

Officers arrived in clusters, their breath visible in the March cold. They had walked from their huts along the frozen paths of the Newburgh encampment, past the snowbanks that had accumulated through the hardest winter of the war. Some came directly; others lingered outside in small groups, speaking in low tones before entering. The building could hold hundreds, and by the appointed hour, it would need to.

The summons had been explicit. On the morning of March 10, an unsigned letter had begun circulating in the army camp. Later acknowledged to be written by Major John Armstrong, Jr., aide to General Gates, it decried the army’s condition and the lack of Congressional support. It called them to assemble at eleven o’clock on this day to consider what measures they ought to take in response to the failure of Congress to honor its obligations. The language was careful, but the intent was not. This was not a petition. This was not a memorial. This was a meeting to discuss alternatives, and everyone who read the address understood what alternatives meant.

Major John Armstrong, Jr., aide-de-camp to General Horatio Gates, had written those words. He had written them at Gates’s quarters, with Gates’s knowledge, in the service of a design that extended well beyond the camp at Newburgh. The officers who filed into the Temple that morning did not know this, not with certainty. They knew only that they had not been paid in years, that their families suffered, that Congress had made promises it showed no sign of keeping, and that someone had finally said what many had whispered: patience had become humiliation.

The room filled. Colonel Walter Stewart arrived, immaculate as always, his uniform a rebuke to the threadbare coats around him. General Henry Knox entered, his massive frame drawing eyes, his expression unreadable. Brigadier generals and line officers, men who had wintered at Valley Forge and fought at Yorktown, took their places on the rough benches. They had come because the summons demanded it, and because they could not ignore it.

At the front of the room, Horatio Gates took the chair.

The symbolism was precise and devastating. Gates was Washington’s rival, a man whose ambitions had led him to intrigue against the commander-in-chief. He had presided over the Board of War during an earlier attempt to replace Washington with a general more pliable to civilian critics. He had failed then, but he had survived, and now he sat at the head of a meeting that Washington had not called, had not authorized, and had explicitly tried to prevent.

The previous four days had been a contest of wills. On March 10, the first anonymous address had appeared, nailed to a post and circulated hand to hand through the camp. Armstrong had written it with the urgency of a man who understood that the moment required action. The army, he declared, could no longer trust Congress. The time for petitions had passed. “Suspect the man who would advise to longer forbearance,” he had written, and the officers who read those words felt the heat of them.

Washington had responded within hours. On the morning of March 11, his general orders appeared, denouncing the anonymous address as disorderly and irregular. He had forbidden the meeting it called. But then he had done something unexpected. Instead of simply prohibiting the gathering, he had called one himself, postponing the rendezvous by four days to March 15. He had appointed General Gates to preside—a masterstroke that forced the conspirators to show their hand or retreat. And he had asked the officers to consider a report from a committee he would appoint, a report that would address their grievances properly.

It was a procedure, not a prohibition. Washington had turned a potential mutiny into a regulated meeting, with himself as the ultimate authority. But he could not control what happened inside the Temple of Virtue once the doors closed.

The nationalists in Philadelphia had been waiting for this. Robert Morris, the Superintendent of Finance, had been corresponding with Alexander Hamilton for months about the army’s pay. Hamilton, now a delegate to Congress, had written to Washington on February 13 with a warning that carried a deeper current. The army’s claims, he suggested, might be used to pressure the states into creating a national revenue system. The implication was clear: the threat of military unrest could force Congress to adopt the funding powers that Morris and Hamilton believed essential to the nation’s survival.

They had not created the army’s grievances. Those were real enough, the product of years of Congressional inaction and state refusal to fund the Continental Army. But they had shaped how those grievances might be expressed. The anonymous addresses circulating in Newburgh bore the marks of political design. They called for an ultimatum: if peace came and the army remained unpaid, the officers would refuse to disband. Some versions suggested they might march on Philadelphia. The language stopped just short of explicit threat, but the threat was there, waiting to be read.

This was crisis engineering at work. The nationalists needed the army’s anger, but they needed it channeled—brought to the brink and then pulled back once Congress capitulated. They needed a crisis, not a catastrophe. The meeting at the Temple of Virtue was their detonation point.

Gates called the assembly to order. The room quieted, but the tension did not dissipate. The officers had come to hear what would be proposed, and they expected proposals. Some hoped for moderation. Others hoped for action. A few understood that they were being used, that their desperation was being marshaled for purposes beyond their own redress, and they did not care. They had waited too long.

The army’s situation was genuinely dire. The quartermaster’s ledgers told the story in columns of unpaid figures. Officers had been promised half-pay pensions for seven years, a commitment Congress had made in 1780 to keep them in service. Enlisted men had been promised bounties and back wages. Most had received nothing. Many had sold their promissory notes to speculators at pennies on the dollar, taking what they could get because they could wait no longer. The notes passed from hand to hand, accumulating in the portfolios of financiers who had purchased them at a fraction of their face value. If Congress ever paid, the money would go to those who had bought the army’s paper, not to those who had earned it.

This was the grievance that the anonymous addresses exploited. Armstrong’s words spoke to men who had given years of their lives, who had bled and frozen and watched their comrades die, and who now faced the prospect of returning home without even the means to travel. The addresses offered them a way to be heard. But they also offered something more dangerous: the suggestion that they might take what they were owed, by force if necessary.

The second address, appearing on March 12, had been more explicit. It acknowledged Washington’s order postponing the meeting but insisted that the original call had not been disorderly—it had been necessary. It called on the officers to maintain their resolve, to remember that their cause was just, and to prepare for “an appeal to Heaven” if all else failed. The phrase was a euphemism for war, and everyone in the camp knew it.

Armstrong had written that second address as well, and he had done so knowing that Washington had forbidden the first. The defiance was calculated. The nationalists in Philadelphia—Morris and Hamilton and their allies—needed the army’s threat to be credible. Congress had to believe that the officers might actually march, that the Continental Army might turn its guns on the civilian government that had failed it. Only that belief would force the delegates to act.

But credibility required plausibility. The officers had to be angry enough, desperate enough, to make the threat real. And they had to be organized enough to act on it. This was the purpose of the meeting on March 15. It was political theater, staged for an audience in Philadelphia that needed to see the danger before it would fund the solution. Gates in the chair signaled that the army’s loyalty might be shifting. The presence of hundreds of officers signaled that the movement had numbers. The deliberations would signal that the threat was serious.

The room was arranged for drama. Gates sat at the front, his presence a reminder that Washington was not the only general the army respected—or feared. The officers faced him, their ranks and their grievances drawn up in rows. The Temple of Virtue, built for sermons, had become a stage for sedition.

But whose sedition? The officers believed they were acting on their own behalf, responding to years of neglect with the only leverage they had left. Armstrong knew that he was acting on behalf of a political faction that wanted to use the army’s desperation to force a national revenue. Gates knew that his presidency of the meeting placed him at the head of a potential alternative to Washington’s authority. And Washington, waiting in his quarters, knew that everything was at stake.

The commander-in-chief had not been idle. In the four days since his general orders, he had worked to turn opinion among the officers. His aides had circulated among the regiments, sounding out sentiment, identifying the hotheads and the moderates. He had met privately with key figures—Knox, Steuben, the generals whose voices carried weight. And he had prepared his own response, a statement he would deliver if the moment required it.

But the moment was not yet his. The meeting belonged to Gates, and Gates opened it with the business at hand.

The first speaker rose to address the assembly. The officers listened, some nodding, others scowling. The debate that followed was earnest and angry, a venting of grievances that had accumulated over years of broken promises and empty treasuries. Men spoke of their families, their debts, their honor. They spoke of Congress’s failure and the states’ refusal. They spoke of the injustice of returning home as beggars after winning a war.

The debate was real. The anger was real. The officers were not merely props in a Philadelphia drama, though they were that too. They were men who had given everything and received nothing, and they had come to the Temple of Virtue because they did not know where else to go.

But the structure of the meeting had been designed to channel that anger toward a specific end. The anonymous addresses had framed the question: not whether to act, but how. The second address had proposed an ultimatum. If peace came and the army remained unpaid, the officers would refuse to disband. They would remain under arms, a standing force that Congress would have to deal with. And if Congress refused to deal with them, they would deal with Congress.

This was the threat that Morris and Hamilton needed. It was not a mutiny—not yet. It was a lever. The army’s refusal to disband would give Congress a choice: fund the army and establish the revenue to do so, or face a military force with no civilian master. The nationalists believed that Congress would choose funding, that the states would be forced to cede taxing power, and that the resulting financial system would bind the nation together.

The officers in the Temple of Virtue did not see themselves as pawns. They saw themselves as men with legitimate claims and no other recourse. But their claims had been shaped by the addresses, and the addresses had been written by Armstrong, and Armstrong was Gates’s aide, and Gates was the man in the chair—and somewhere behind all of it were the financiers and politicians who stood to gain from the army’s threat.

The meeting proceeded. A committee was proposed to draft a report on the officers’ sentiments. The committee would consider the anonymous addresses and recommend a course of action. The language was procedural, but the implications were explosive. If the committee endorsed the ultimatum, the army would have a formal position. If the army had a formal position, it would have a negotiating stance. And if Congress refused to negotiate, the army would have its justification for action.

This was how crises were made. Not with a single dramatic moment, but with a series of steps, each one small enough to seem reasonable, each one leading toward a destination that no one would have chosen at the outset. The officers in the Temple of Virtue were choosing, step by step, to become something they had never intended to be: a threat to the civilian government they had fought to establish.

Washington understood this. He had seen it before, in earlier intrigues against his command, in the mutinies of the Pennsylvania and New Jersey lines, in the constant pressure of maintaining an army that could not be paid and would not be dismissed. He knew that armies in such circumstances became dangers to themselves and to everyone around them. He also knew that the danger could be managed, if the right hand guided it.

But his hand was not yet on the meeting. Gates held the gavel. The officers held the floor. And somewhere in Philadelphia, Robert Morris waited to see if the crisis he had helped create would produce the result he needed.

The nationalists had played a dangerous game. They had cultivated the army’s grievances without fully controlling the army’s response. They had relied on Armstrong’s addresses and Gates’s ambition, but they could not be certain what would happen when hundreds of armed men gathered in a room to discuss their options. The threat had to be credible, which meant it had to be real. But if it became too real—if the officers moved from threat to action—the result would be a catastrophe that no one could manage.

Hamilton had warned Washington of this danger in his February letter, but the warning had been double-edged. “The claims of the army, with the aid of its friends in Congress, might be improved as a powerful engine to obtain the great objects of the national interest,” he had written. The phrasing was characteristic: the army’s claims could be improved, its anger could be used. Hamilton was not simply alerting Washington to a threat; he was suggesting that the threat might serve a purpose.

Washington had read the letter carefully. He understood what Hamilton was proposing, and he understood the risk. The nationalists wanted to use the army’s desperation to force Congress to act, but they needed the army to remain under control. They needed a threat, not a rebellion. And they needed Washington to ensure the difference.

The commander-in-chief had not responded to Hamilton’s suggestion directly. He had simply prepared himself for what might come. The anonymous addresses had confirmed his fears. The meeting on March 15 would test his ability to manage a crisis that others had created.

In the Temple of Virtue, the debate continued. The officers spoke of honor and justice, of promises broken and debts unpaid. They spoke as men who had been pushed to the edge. But they also spoke as men who were being pushed by others, toward a destination that some of them could not see.

The morning wore on. The sun climbed over the Hudson, casting light through the windows of the Temple. The raw timber walls that had echoed with hymns now echoed with arguments. The building that had been raised for communal purposes had become the site of a potential schism.

Gates presided with the manner of a man who believed this moment might be his. He had served the Revolution, but he had never been Washington. He had won at Saratoga and lost at Camden, and in between he had intrigued against the commander-in-chief with a persistence that bordered on obsession. Now he sat at the head of a meeting that Washington had tried to prevent, a meeting that might redefine the army’s relationship to its commander and to Congress.

The officers around him were not all Gates’s men. Many remained loyal to Washington, even in their anger. They had come to the Temple because the summons demanded it, but they had not come to overthrow their general. They had come to be heard.

But being heard was not the same as being heeded. The committee that was forming would draft a report, and the report would recommend action, and the action would be presented as the will of the officers. Whether that will was genuine or manufactured, whether it reflected the army’s true sentiments or the designs of men who hoped to use it, would be difficult to determine. The meeting was producing a consensus, but consensus could be shaped by the questions that were asked and the men who asked them.

Washington watched from his quarters. He had not attended the meeting, but he had his informants. Aides and loyal officers slipped in and out of the Temple, bringing reports of what was said and who said it. The picture that emerged was mixed. The anger was real, but so was the hesitation. The officers wanted justice, but they did not want mutiny. They wanted to be paid, but they did not want to become the thing they had fought against.

The commander-in-chief prepared himself. He had a statement ready, a response to the crisis that would address the officers’ grievances while reasserting his authority. He would deliver it when the moment was right, at a meeting he would call, in a room he would control. But first he had to let the Temple of Virtue play out its purpose. He had to see what the officers would do when left to themselves.

The nationalists had lit a fuse. Now they waited to see if it would burn toward the powder or burn itself out. Washington waited too, but his purpose was different. He intended to extinguish the flame before it reached its destination.

The meeting at the Temple of Virtue was not a spontaneous outburst. It was a carefully staged event, designed to produce a specific effect on a specific audience. The audience was Congress, and the effect was fear. The officers who gathered on March 15 were the instruments of that fear, whether they knew it or not. Their anger was genuine, but it had been shaped and directed by men who hoped to use it for their own ends.

This was the nature of the crisis the nationalists had constructed. It had to be real enough to be credible, but controlled enough to be useful. They had cultivated the army’s grievances, amplified them through anonymous addresses, and now brought them to a head in a public meeting that would force Congress to pay attention. They had created a situation in which the civilian government would have to choose between funding the army and facing its wrath.

But the engineers could not control every variable. The officers in the Temple had their own wills, their own judgments, their own sense of what was right and what was possible. They might follow the script that Armstrong had written, or they might depart from it. They might embrace the ultimatum, or they might reject it. They might rally around Gates, or they might remember who had led them through the war.

The uncertainty was part of the design. A controlled threat required the possibility of uncontrolled consequences. Congress had to believe that the army might actually march, that the officers might actually turn their weapons on Philadelphia. The threat had to be real, which meant the outcome had to be uncertain.

But uncertainty cut both ways. If the officers moved too far, too fast, the crisis would become a catastrophe. The Revolution would end in military dictatorship, the Republic would die before it was born, and everything the officers had fought for would be lost. The nationalists understood this risk, but they believed they could manage it. They believed that Washington, when the moment came, would do what was necessary to bring the army back from the brink.

They were counting on him. Even as they created a crisis that threatened his authority, they depended on his authority to prevent the crisis from becoming a disaster. It was a dangerous calculation, and it placed Washington in a position he had not chosen. He would have to save the Republic from the very threat that his own allies had created.

The meeting in the Temple of Virtue stretched toward noon. The debates ran their course, the committee was named, the report was promised. The officers had gathered in anger, and they would leave with their anger intact. But they had not yet crossed the line. They had not yet committed to the ultimatum. They had not yet become mutineers.

Washington was waiting too. He had watched the meeting from a distance, and now he prepared to enter it directly. He would call a second meeting, his own meeting, where he would address the officers and offer them a different path. He would speak to their grievances and their honor, and he would ask them to trust him one more time.

But first, the meeting at the Temple had to conclude. Gates had to bring down the gavel. The officers had to return to their huts, carrying with them the weight of what they had heard and what they had not decided. The crisis had been staged, but the outcome remained unknown.

The nationalists had called the meeting for their own purposes. The officers had come for theirs. The question was whether those purposes could be reconciled, or whether the tension between them would produce something that no one had intended.

The morning ended. The meeting adjourned. The officers filed out of the Temple and back into the cold March air, their breath visible, their minds unsettled. They had gathered because an anonymous address had summoned them, and they had debated because their grievances demanded it. But they had not resolved anything. The crisis remained.

In his quarters, Washington received the reports and prepared his response. The engineers in Philadelphia waited for news. And in the camp at Newburgh, an army that had not been paid for years contemplated its options, unaware that the most important meeting had not yet occurred.
