Chapter 13

The Dispersal of the Army

A memorandum dated June 2, 1783, lay on the clerk’s desk at headquarters. It was the order for the army’s furlough, the first step toward its formal disbandment—the final answer to the officers who had waited at Newburgh.

But the paper said otherwise. Dated June 2, 1783, signed by George Washington as commander in chief, the furlough order lay on the clerk’s desk at headquarters. The language was crisp, the reach sweeping. Peace was treated as accomplished fact, though the definitive treaty would not be signed in Paris for months. Congress, the order explained, had resolved that a part of the army should be furloughed because the public finances made paying the arrears impracticable. The soldiers were told to go home.

The clerk copied the order into the regimental books. Name, rank, date of service. The quill scratched across the page. The machinery of dispersal had begun.

The half-pay compromise had passed Congress in late April. After years of refusing to honor the promise made in 1780—half-pay for life for officers who served until the war’s end—the legislators had finally acted. But they had not given the officers what they wanted. Instead of lifetime pensions, Congress commuted the obligation to five years of full pay, a lump sum to be discharged at the war’s conclusion. It was a bargain, not a gift. The officers had threatened collective action. Congress had offered partial satisfaction. The threat had worked, after a fashion, though not in the way the more radical nationalists had hoped.

The impost had passed as well, though in a weakened form. Rhode Island’s opposition had prevented the full measure from achieving the unanimous consent required under the Articles of Confederation. The national taxing power that Hamilton and Morris had sought—the power to levy duties without begging the states for contributions—remained incomplete. But enough had been achieved to allow both sides to claim victory. The nationalists had extracted a fiscal commitment from Congress. The officers had extracted a financial settlement from the government. The army had been used as leverage, and now the army would be sent away.

The certificates arrived at Newburgh in late May. They were not money. They were promises of money, issued by a government that had no money to give. The clerk watched as officers received them. Some examined the paper carefully, reading the fine print, calculating what five years of full pay would mean in certificates versus what it would have meant in hard currency. Others simply folded them and put them in their pockets, too tired to calculate. A few cursed.

The mathematics were simple and brutal. A certificate promising one hundred dollars might fetch forty on the open market, if the holder could find a speculator willing to bet that Congress would eventually redeem its paper at face value. Many officers needed cash immediately. Years had passed without pay. Farms had been neglected, families had scraped by on credit and charity, debts had accumulated. Certificates could not be eaten. They could not be planted. Creditors would not accept them. So the officers sold them, at whatever discount the market offered.

Speculators were waiting. They had been waiting for months, knowing the army would eventually be paid in paper. These buyers understood something that many soldiers did not: the certificates would become a form of political currency. Whoever held them would have an interest in strengthening the national government, because only a stronger central authority would be able to redeem them. The debt was not just a financial obligation. It was a mechanism for creating a constituency for federal power. This was the debt theater in its purest form—promises that bound men to a government that could not yet pay them.

Washington observed the process with a mixture of relief and foreboding. The general had written to Hamilton in April, declining to introduce the army as an instrument of political pressure. After the crisis ended, Washington expressed concern about using military discontent as leverage for fiscal legislation. He understood that the officers’ grievances were real, and he shared their frustration with Congress’s failures. But he also understood that the moment the army entered politics as an independent force, the Revolution would have failed. The military would have become a player in the very system it had fought to overthrow.

The dispersal proceeded regiment by regiment, company by company. The infantry went first, the line officers and the enlisted men who had borne the brunt of the fighting. Then the artillery, the engineers, the support services. The headquarters staff would remain until the end, whenever that came. Washington himself would stay until the British evacuated New York City, until the last British soldier left American soil, until the peace was formal and final.

The process mirrored the army’s own creation. In June 1775, the Continental Congress had created the Continental Army and appointed Washington its commander-in-chief. Now, eight years later, Congress ordered it disbanded.

Men left in waves. Some walked. Some rode horses they had brought from home or acquired during the war. Some crowded onto boats heading south along the Hudson, toward Albany and then overland to New England, or downriver toward New York and the roads that led to Pennsylvania, New Jersey, the South. Discharge papers, certificates, weapons in many cases, and memories—they carried what they could. They left behind the log huts they had built, the fortifications they had constructed, the graves of their comrades.

Triumph was absent from the mood. Celebration was muted at best. The war had been won, but victory felt hollow to men who had sacrificed years of their lives and received paper in return. The British Empire had been defeated. Independence had been secured. A new nation had been created. Now these soldiers were going home to farms that might be ruined, to families they might barely recognize, to debts they could not pay.

Letters written from the road reflected this ambivalence. One officer described passing through a village where inhabitants stared at the soldiers as if they were strangers, which in a sense they were. Another wrote of the strange sensation of walking past fields where he had once fought, now quiet and empty, the earthworks grown over with grass. A third simply noted the miles traveled and the money spent on food and lodging, his certificate already diminished by the discount at which he had sold it.

The events of March lingered in memory. The anonymous addresses had circulated, calling for a meeting to consider what action to take against Congress. Officers had gathered in the Temple of Virtue, uncertain of what would happen. Washington had entered uninvited, had asked to speak, had pulled from his pocket a letter from a member of Congress. He had struggled to read it, had reached for his spectacles, had remarked that he had grown gray in service and now found himself growing blind. The gesture had broken the tension. Officers had wept. The conspiracy had collapsed.

Now the army was leaving. Officers who had gathered in the Temple of Virtue were trudging along roads toward homes they had not seen in years. Soldiers who had listened to Washington’s speech were showing discharge papers to local officials, trying to prove they had served and were owed something. The institution that had terrified Congress with its potential for violence was scattering into irrelevance.

The nationalists had taken a calculated risk. Grievances in the army had been allowed, perhaps encouraged, to reach a boiling point. The threat of military action, it was hoped, would force the states to grant Congress the taxing power it needed. Whether the extent of the risk had been miscalculated, or whether the nationalists had calculated correctly and simply been lucky that Washington intervened before things went too far, the result remained ambiguous. The impost had passed in weakened form. The half-pay commutation had been approved. But the comprehensive fiscal reform that Hamilton and Morris sought remained incomplete.

This was the logic of the settlement. The officers had received enough to satisfy their immediate demands. Congress had granted enough to defuse the crisis. The nationalists had achieved enough to claim progress. And the army—the army that had made all of this possible by its mere presence, its mere potential for action—was being sent home. The instrument of coercion was being withdrawn.

The dispersal was anticlimactic. No ceremonies marked it, no parades, no grand farewells. Men simply left, in small groups and large ones, on foot and on horseback, by boat and by wagon. They melted back into the population from which they had emerged. Farmers again, or merchants, or craftsmen, or whatever they had been before the war. Certificates went with them, tucked into pockets or pouches, promises that would follow them for years.

Some of these certificates would be redeemed eventually, under the new Constitution, when Alexander Hamilton as Secretary of the Treasury made good on the revolutionary debt. Others would be lost, or destroyed, or forgotten. Many would be sold to speculators who would reap the profits of patience. Veterans would complain, for decades, about the treatment they had received. Politicians would argue, for decades, about how to handle the obligations the government had incurred. The certificates would become a permanent feature of the political landscape, a reminder that promises made without power are promises made in vain.

The process took weeks. By the end of June, most of the army was gone. Roads leading away from Newburgh were crowded with soldiers heading home. The encampment that had held thousands of men was nearly empty. Log huts stood vacant. The parade ground where Washington had addressed the officers on March 15 was silent. The Temple of Virtue was just a building again, a structure that would eventually be repurposed or torn down.

Washington remained. Duties had to be fulfilled. The British still occupied New York City, and until they evacuated, the war was not officially over. The commander in chief would stay until the end, whenever that came. But the army he had commanded was gone, or going, and the silence that fell over headquarters was heavy with meaning.

Letters flowed from the general’s quarters. Congress received reports on the progress of the furloughs. Governors of the states were urged to make provisions for returning soldiers. Former officers received recommendations and references for those seeking employment. Friends and colleagues heard Washington’s reflections on what had been accomplished and what remained undone.

In one of these letters, written in late June, Washington observed that the dispersal of the army marked the end of an era. The Continental Army had been the instrument of American independence. It had held together through years of defeat and privation. It had crossed the Delaware, survived Valley Forge, triumphed at Yorktown. Now it was dissolving, not in battle but in bureaucracy, not with a bang but with a whimper.

The contrast with the crisis of March was stark. Then, the army had been a powder keg. Now it was a stream of men on roads leading away from camp. Then, officers had gathered in mutinous anticipation. Now they walked alone or in pairs, thinking of home. Then, the threat of violence had hung in the air. Now the threat had been dispersed along with the men who might have carried it out.

This was the controlled conclusion of the crisis engineering that had brought the army to the brink. The emergency had been manufactured, or at least encouraged, to force a political solution. Now the emergency was being wound down with the same deliberation. The army had served its purpose. It had frightened Congress into action. It had given the nationalists leverage. Now it was being removed from the board, its pieces scattered, its power dissipated.

But the system remained the problem. That was what the dispersal revealed. Congress could not pay the army because Congress could not tax. Congress could not tax because the states would not let it. The states would not let it because they feared centralized power. They feared centralized power because they had just fought a war against a central government that had abused its authority. The logic was circular, and the circle could not be broken without something giving way.

The army had been a national institution. It had drawn men from all thirteen states, had fought under a single commander appointed by Congress, had been supplied—inadequately—through a national system of procurement. It had been the closest thing to a national force that the new country possessed. Now it was vanishing, dissolving back into the states from which it had come, leaving behind only the memory of what it had accomplished and the paper promises of what it was owed.

The clerk at headquarters finished his work. The last furlough orders had been copied, the last discharges recorded, the last regimental books closed. The army was gone. The camp was empty. The war was over. Over the next several months, much of the Continental Army was furloughed, although many of the rank and file realized it was effectively a disbandment. The army was formally disbanded in November 1783, leaving only a small force at West Point and several scattered frontier outposts.

Or rather, the war was over, but the peace had just begun. And the peace would bring its own challenges, its own conflicts, its own crises. The central government had no army, no revenue, no power to compel compliance. The states were sovereign, each pursuing its own interests, each suspicious of the others. The union was a name, not a reality.

Washington understood this better than anyone. He had watched the army disintegrate, had watched Congress fail, had watched the states bicker and stall. He had seen the system from the inside, and he knew its weaknesses. The dispersal of the army was not just a practical necessity. It was a revelation of the fundamental problem that the new nation faced.

Four years later, when the Constitutional Convention met in Philadelphia, one of the driving forces behind the new frame of government would be the memory of what had happened when promises were made without the power to keep them. The officers in Newburgh had waited. They had received their answer. It was not what they had asked for. But it was what the system could provide, and that was the point. The system was the problem. The compromise was the proof.

But that was in the future. In June 1783, the present was simpler and sadder. The army was going home. Men who had fought the war were leaving the field. The institution that had held them together was dissolving. Certificates in their pockets were worth a fraction of their face value, and the government that had issued them had no way to make them good.

The clerk gathered his papers. New employment would be needed. Perhaps a return to family, if they were still there. Perhaps one of the cities—New York or Philadelphia—where work as a scrivener or a clerk might be found. The skills learned at headquarters—keeping records, copying documents, maintaining accounts—would be useful somewhere. The war had taught things that peace would require.

Outside, the sun was setting over the Hudson. The camp stretched out before him, row after row of empty huts, silent and still. A few soldiers remained, the last of the last, waiting for orders or transportation. But most were gone. The army that had camped here, that had waited here, that had threatened here, was gone.

The road led away from Newburgh, toward the south and the east, toward the towns and farms and cities where soldiers would try to rebuild their lives. Hard-packed dirt, worn by years of use, rutted by wagons and horses and feet. It connected the camp to the world beyond. It was the path of dispersal.

The clerk began to walk. He did not look back.

The army has vanished as a national institution, leaving a vacuum of force and scattering veterans with paper promises across the states.
