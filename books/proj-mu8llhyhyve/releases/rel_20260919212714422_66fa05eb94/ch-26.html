Chapter 26

The Memory of Mutiny

The veteran with his worthless certificate found that the paper itself became a kind of memory. Not of promises kept, but of what had nearly happened instead. In the winter of 1787, Alexander Hamilton sat at a desk in New York, drafting essays that would argue for a new constitution. He wrote quickly, covering page after page with the dense argumentation that would become The Federalist. Military power and federal taxation lay at the center of his project. Four years earlier, he had written to Washington suggesting that the army’s grievances might be used to force Congress toward a funding system. Now he wrote as if that crisis had never existed. The Newburgh Conspiracy, a failed apparent threat by leaders of the Continental Army in March 1783, was now a memory to be curated, not cited.

Hamilton’s selective memory was not accidental. In Federalist No. 12, he argued that the federal government’s power to tax would provide revenue necessary for supporting civil government and paying public debts. He wrote of the Confederation Congress’s inability to raise funds through anything but requisitions that the states could ignore. He did not write of the officers at Newburgh who had been promised payment and received nothing. In Federalist No. 30, he returned to the theme: money, he argued, was the vital principle of the body politic, sustaining its life and motion, enabling it to perform its most essential functions. A body without money was a body without life. But Hamilton did not mention the body of armed men who had stood ready, in March 1783, to march on Philadelphia and take what they were owed.

The omission was strategic. Hamilton was arguing for a federal taxing power that nationalists had sought for years. The Newburgh crisis had been, for men like Hamilton and Robert Morris and Gouverneur Morris, a means to that end.

The threat of military unrest had been real enough, but its purpose had been to create political pressure that would force the states to cede revenue. Now, in 1787 and 1788, Hamilton needed to persuade his readers that federal taxation was the solution to disorder, not its cause. To acknowledge that nationalists had deliberately stoked military discontent would undermine the entire argument. So Hamilton wrote around the event. He wrote of the dangers of disunion, of the precarious peace that might follow if the states remained sovereign over their own purses. He wrote of the need for a standing army to defend against foreign threats, and of the danger that such an army might become the engine of despotism if not properly controlled.

But he did not write of the officers who had gathered in the Temple of Virtue, of the anonymous addresses that had circulated among them, or of the role that men like himself had played in bringing that moment to its crisis.

The curation of memory began almost immediately after Washington’s intervention on March 15, 1783. Washington himself understood that the event required careful handling. He had convened a court of inquiry to investigate the anonymous addresses, a procedural act that served multiple purposes: it demonstrated his commitment to civilian authority, it gave the officers a formal outlet for their grievances, and it created an official record that would emphasize his own role in defusing the crisis. The court produced documents. Those documents were preserved. Others were not.

Washington’s papers from this period reveal a pattern of selection. His letters to Congress in the weeks following March 15 emphasized the officers’ loyalty and their acceptance of his authority. He wrote to the president of Congress, Elias Boudinot, on March 18, describing how the officers had voted thanks to their commander in chief and had expressed confidence in Congress’s justice. The letter was accurate, as far as it went. But it did not capture the anger that still simmered beneath the surface, the sense among many officers that they had been managed rather than heard. Washington’s account emphasized the resolution. It did not dwell on the causes.

The anonymous addresses themselves were preserved, but their authorship remained officially obscure. John Armstrong, Jr., the aide-de-camp to General Horatio Gates who had written them, was never publicly named by Washington. The court of inquiry did not pursue him. Whether Washington knew Armstrong’s identity with certainty is unclear. Whether he chose not to know is a different question. The effect was the same: the addresses entered the historical record as the work of an anonymous hand, and Armstrong’s role remained a matter of speculation rather than proof. This too served a purpose. To name Armstrong would have been to name Gates, and to name Gates would have been to acknowledge that the conspiracy had support within the officer corps itself. Better to treat the addresses as the work of a single malcontent, a solitary voice that had been silenced by Washington’s intervention.

The memory of Newburgh, as it hardened into official narrative, became a story about Washington. The spectacles he had put on while reading his address became the moment’s defining image. Washington putting on his spectacles, remarking that he had grown gray and almost blind in service to his country, disarming his audience with a display of vulnerability. The story was retold. It appeared in early biographies, in commemorative orations, in the popular imagination. It was a story about a particular kind of power: the authority derived from a deliberate display of frailty, the credibility that came from showing that one had given everything and asked for nothing. The spectacles became a symbol. But symbols are curated. The story of the spectacles served to focus attention on Washington’s personal virtue and away from the structural conditions that had created the crisis.

The financial settlement that followed Newburgh further complicated the memory. Congress had, in the weeks after Washington’s intervention, approved a commutation of the officers’ pensions: five years of full pay instead of half-pay for life. This was not what the officers had been promised, but it was something. Robert Morris, the superintendent of finance, had pushed for the commutation as a way to reduce the long-term debt obligation. The officers, many of them desperate for immediate funds, accepted the compromise. They took their certificates and went home. The certificates traded at a fraction of their face value. Speculators bought them from veterans who needed cash. By 1790, when Hamilton’s Funding Act proposed to redeem the certificates at par, many of the original holders had sold them for pennies on the dollar.

The Funding Act was, in its way, the final act of the Newburgh drama. Hamilton proposed that the federal government assume the state debts and redeem all certificates at full face value. The effect was to enrich the speculators who had bought up the paper at depressed prices, while the original holders saw little benefit. Hamilton defended the policy on grounds of necessity: the government needed to establish its credit, and discrimination between original holders and current holders would be impractical. The argument was consistent with his broader vision of a strong federal government capable of managing a national debt. But it also meant that the officers who had gathered at Newburgh, who had threatened mutiny over their unpaid wages, received only a fraction of what they had been owed, while men who had never served bought their certificates at a discount and collected the full value.

The memory of this betrayal did not disappear. It circulated, but in subterranean forms. Veterans’ groups, meeting in taverns and private homes, exchanged their own accounts of what had happened. The anonymous addresses themselves were passed from hand to hand, read aloud in meetings of former officers who remembered March 1783 with a mixture of shame and resentment. Some of these men joined the Society of the Cincinnati, the organization of former Continental officers that some saw as a potential hereditary aristocracy. The Society became, for critics of the new order, a symbol of everything that was wrong with the settlement: an elite cadre of military men who had been bought off with commuted pensions and now threatened to become a permanent privileged class.

The political battles of the 1780s and 1790s kept the memory of Newburgh alive in other ways. When Daniel Shays and his followers rose in western Massachusetts in 1786 and 1787, demanding relief from debt and taxation, the echoes were unmistakable. Here were men, many of them veterans, who felt that the government had failed them. The suppression of Shays’ Rebellion became another argument for a stronger federal government, another reason to support the Constitutional Convention and its product. But for those who remembered Newburgh, the comparison cut both ways. The officers had been persuaded to stand down. The Shaysites had been crushed by force. The difference lay in the grievances themselves and in the status of the men who voiced them.

Hamilton’s Federalist essays did not mention Shays’ Rebellion by name, though they referred obliquely to the tempestuous waves through which the country had passed. The silence was characteristic. Hamilton was writing to persuade, and persuasion required selection. He did not need to remind his readers of the specific instances of unrest that had plagued the 1780s. He needed only to evoke the general sense that the Confederation had failed and that a stronger union was necessary. The Newburgh crisis, had it been widely known, would have illustrated his point perfectly: a military on the verge of mutiny because the government could not pay its debts. But it would also have raised uncomfortable questions about who had encouraged that mutiny and why. Better to let the specific memory fade into a general sense of the period’s dangers.

Gouverneur Morris, who had been one of the architects of the nationalist strategy in 1783, was equally reticent in his later accounts. In his speeches at the Constitutional Convention, Morris argued for a strong executive and a federal power to tax. He spoke of the need to check the unwise and the unsteady and to ensure that the government could command the means of paying its debts. He did not speak of the officers at Newburgh, though he had been among those who believed that their anger might be useful. Morris’s later memoirs and letters treated the Newburgh crisis as a moment of danger that had been averted by Washington’s leadership. The deeper story remained untold.

The result, by the time the new government was established in 1789, was a bifurcated memory. The official, nationalist memory of Newburgh emphasized Washington’s heroism: the commander in chief who had appeared uninvited before his officers, who had read them an address that appealed to their honor and their patience, who had put on his spectacles and reminded them of his own sacrifices. This memory appeared in commemorative speeches, in early biographies, in the public celebrations of Washington’s presidency. It reinforced the principle of civilian control over the military, the idea that the army’s proper role was to submit to the government’s authority even when that government failed to meet its obligations. And it reinforced the image of Washington as the selfless patriot who had no personal ambition, who had given everything to his country and asked for nothing in return.

But there was another memory, less official and less documented. It lived in the letters that former officers wrote to one another, in the conversations they had when they met, in the petitions they submitted to state legislatures and to Congress. This memory emphasized not Washington’s heroism but the government’s failure. It recalled the promises that had been made and broken. It recalled the certificates that had been issued and then sold for fractions of their value. It recalled the sense, among men who had risked their lives for independence, that their country had not fulfilled what it had started.

The two memories coexisted, but they did not compete on equal terms. The nationalist memory had the advantage of publicity. It was printed in newspapers, spoken in orations, taught to children. The other memory circulated privately, among men who were increasingly dispersed and increasingly marginalized. The veterans of the Continental Army were not, as a group, a powerful political constituency. Many of them were farmers or artisans or small merchants, struggling to rebuild lives that had been interrupted by years of service. They did not have the platform that Hamilton and Morris and their allies possessed. Their memory of Newburgh was a memory of grievance, and grievances that cannot be aired publicly tend to fester.

The spectacles story itself underwent its own process of curation. The earliest accounts of the March 15 meeting did not emphasize the gesture. Washington’s own report to Congress, written three days after the event, did not mention the spectacles at all. The detail appears to have entered the record through later recollections, some written years afterward, when the event had already begun to acquire its mythic dimensions. Whether Washington planned the gesture or improvised it, whether the officers’ reaction was as dramatic as later accounts suggested, is less important than what the story became. The spectacles became the symbol of Washington’s self-sacrifice, the visual proof that he had given his eyesight to his country. The story served to humanize Washington while also elevating him.

The political usefulness of this image should not be underestimated. In the 1790s, as the new federal government took shape, Washington’s reputation was its most important asset. The presidency was a new office, its powers undefined, its legitimacy untested. Washington’s presence in the office gave it credibility. His reputation for disinterested patriotism was essential to the new order’s claim to legitimacy. If Washington had been, as some critics suggested, just another politician seeking power, the whole project would have been undermined. The memory of Newburgh, carefully curated, reinforced the image of Washington as the leader who had saved the republic from military usurpation. The deeper story would have complicated that image beyond repair.

Hamilton, for his part, continued to benefit from the selective memory. As the first Secretary of the Treasury, he implemented the funding system he had long advocated. The assumption of state debts, the establishment of a national bank, the creation of a system of revenue—these were the policies he had sought since the early 1780s. The Newburgh crisis had been, in its way, a rehearsal for these achievements. The threat of military unrest had helped to create the political conditions in which a stronger federal government became thinkable. Hamilton did not need to remind anyone of that threat.

He needed only to point to the benefits of the new system: the restored credit, the stable currency, the government’s ability to meet its obligations. The veterans who had suffered from the old system’s failures were, for Hamilton, a necessary sacrifice. The speculators who had bought their certificates at a discount and now collected the full value were, in Hamilton’s view, the men who had taken the risk and deserved the reward.

The veterans themselves did not entirely accept this logic. In the years after 1790, petitions continued to arrive in Congress asking for additional compensation, for recognition of services rendered, for some acknowledgment that the original holders of the certificates had been treated unfairly. Some of these petitions were granted. Most were not. The government had, in its view, settled the debt. The certificates had been redeemed at par, as Hamilton had proposed. That the original holders had sold them for less was, from the government’s perspective, a matter of private transaction, not public responsibility.

The sense of grievance did not disappear. It became part of the political culture of the early republic, a counter-memory that ran beneath the official celebrations. The Society of the Cincinnati, despite its critics, kept the memory of the officers’ service alive. The veterans who gathered at their meetings exchanged stories of the war, of the hardships they had endured, of the promises they had received. They remembered Newburgh not as a moment when Washington had saved them from their own worst instincts, but as a moment when they had been on the verge of taking what they were owed—only to be persuaded to wait, and then to receive less than they had been promised.

The political divisions of the 1790s gave these memories new resonance. The Federalists, led by Hamilton and his allies, stood for a strong central government, a national bank, and a financial system that rewarded creditors and investors. The Democratic-Republicans, led by Thomas Jefferson and James Madison, positioned themselves as the defenders of the common man, the opponents of privilege, the critics of a system that seemed to enrich the few at the expense of the many. For the Democratic-Republicans, the story of the veterans’ certificates became a potent example of Federalist perfidy. The veterans themselves became symbols of the republican virtue that had been betrayed by financial manipulation.

Washington, in his retirement, watched these developments with concern. He had hoped that the new government would unite the country, that the divisions of the 1780s would give way to a common purpose. Instead, the divisions deepened. The Federalists and the Democratic-Republicans formed competing factions, each accusing the other of betraying the Revolution’s legacy. Washington himself tried to remain above the fray, but his sympathies were with the Federalists. He saw in the Democratic-Republicans a tendency toward disorder, a willingness to encourage popular discontent that reminded him of the dangers he had faced at Newburgh. The memory of March 1783, as he understood it, was a memory of how easily the army’s grievances could have turned into something darker. He had prevented that. He did not want to see it happen again.

But the memory that Washington carried was not the same as the memory that the veterans carried. For Washington, Newburgh was a crisis averted, a moment when his leadership had preserved the principle of civilian control. For many of the officers, Newburgh was a moment when their legitimate grievances had been deferred, and then partially denied. The commutation had given them five years of full pay instead of half-pay for life. The funding system had redeemed their certificates—but many of them had already sold those certificates, at a fraction of their value, to men who had never served. The sense of betrayal was not a matter of memory alone. It was a matter of material fact.

The documents tell the story. In the letters that former officers wrote to one another, in the petitions they submitted to Congress, in the accounts they left for their children, a different narrative emerges. They remembered the cold winters, the inadequate supplies, the promises that had been made and broken. They remembered the moment when they had been asked to trust their government one more time, and the moment when that trust had been rewarded with paper that they could not use. They remembered, too, the men who had encouraged them to demand their rights—and the men who had persuaded them to wait. The difference between those two groups was not always clear. Some of the same politicians who had warned of military unrest had also counseled patience. The line between provocation and resolution was blurrier than the official memory allowed.

By the end of the decade, the official narrative had largely prevailed. The Newburgh crisis was remembered as Washington’s finest hour before the presidency, a moment when he had saved the republic from its first great test. The deeper story remained largely untold. It was not entirely forgotten. It lived in the papers of the participants, in the letters that would eventually find their way into archives, in the memories that families passed down. But it was not the story that the new nation told about itself.

The new nation needed heroes, and Washington was the greatest of them. The story of his spectacles, his self-sacrifice, his disinterested leadership, served a purpose that went beyond historical accuracy. It established a model of republican virtue that subsequent generations could emulate. It taught that the proper response to grievance was patience, that the proper relationship between the military and the civilian government was submission, that the proper reward for service was the satisfaction of duty done. These were not unworthy lessons. But they were lessons that obscured as much as they revealed.

The officers who had gathered at Newburgh had been men of their time, shaped by the same revolutionary ideals that Washington embodied. They had believed that government existed to protect rights, that taxation required representation, that promises made to soldiers were obligations of honor. When they threatened to march on Philadelphia, they were not acting against the Revolution’s principles. They were acting on them. The government that had promised them pay and then failed to deliver was, in their view, a government that had betrayed its own foundations. Washington had persuaded them to stand down. But he had not resolved the underlying tension. He had deferred it.

The deferral was, in its way, the point. The Newburgh crisis had forced Congress to confront the consequences of its own weakness. It had pushed the states toward the realization that a stronger union was necessary. It had contributed to the political conditions that made the Constitutional Convention possible. The nationalists who had encouraged the crisis had achieved their goal, though not in the way they had originally intended. The threat of military unrest had not produced a federal taxing power in 1783. But it had produced a sense of crisis that lingered, that shaped the debates of the intervening years, that made the argument for a stronger government more persuasive. The Newburgh crisis was a controlled detonation. The explosion had been contained, but the shock waves had done their work.

The veterans who carried their certificates home, who sold them for what they could get, who tried to rebuild lives interrupted by years of service, were the human cost of that detonation. They had been the instrument of a political strategy they did not fully understand. They had been persuaded to trust, and their trust had been exploited. The paper they carried was evidence of that exploitation. It was also evidence of something else: the memory of a moment when they had been ready to act, when they had come close to taking what they were owed. That memory too persisted, even if it could not be spoken in the official commemorations.

The entrenched myth of Washington’s virtue, papering over the unresolved veteran debts that still demand a financial settlement.
