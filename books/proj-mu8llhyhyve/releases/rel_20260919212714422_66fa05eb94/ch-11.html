Chapter 11

The Debates in Philadelphia

The officers in Newburgh waited. In Philadelphia, a delegate rose to read what they had sent.

The Pennsylvania State House held its usual stillness, the afternoon light falling through tall windows onto the assembled delegates. The memorials from the Hudson Valley had arrived that morning, carried by express rider. Now they lay on the table before the secretary, who lifted the first page and began to read aloud.

The words were measured. The officers expressed their gratitude to Congress, their devotion to the republican cause, their confidence in the wisdom of the legislature. They asked only for what had been promised: the back pay owed to them, the commutation of half-pay pensions into a single lump sum, the settlement of accounts long overdue. The language was respectful. The tone was patient.

Every delegate in the room knew what lay behind those words. They knew about the anonymous addresses that had circulated in Newburgh three weeks earlier. They knew about the meeting called in the Temple of Virtue, the gathering that could have ended in a march on Philadelphia itself. They knew that Washington had appeared uninvited, had fumbled with a letter, had put on his spectacles and asked his officers to bear with him. The story had traveled fast. Washington had turned the room. He had shamed the officers into patience. He had contained the crisis.

But containment was not resolution. The memorials on the table proved that much. The officers had channeled their anger into petition. They had not abandoned their claims. Eight thousand unpaid soldiers waited sixty miles upriver, and every delegate understood what that meant. The army had been pushed back from the edge. It had not been pulled away from it.

The reading finished. The secretary set the papers down. The debate that followed would expose the fundamental weakness of the Confederation: its inability to raise revenue, its dependence on states that would not pay, its helplessness before obligations it could not meet.

Robert Morris had been waiting for exactly this moment.

The Superintendent of Finance had spent two years trying to keep the national government solvent. He had borrowed from France, from Holland, from private bankers scattered across Europe. He had issued his own notes, personal promises backed by his own credit, to feed and clothe an army that Congress could not pay. His credit was now exhausted. His notes traded at steep discounts. The army’s pay was six months in arrears, and some officers had received nothing for years.

Morris had written to the states repeatedly, pleading for the funds they had promised but never delivered. The responses had ranged from evasive to openly hostile. Georgia ignored his letters entirely. Rhode Island declared that it had already contributed its fair share. Pennsylvania, Morris’s own state, proved as reluctant as the rest. The Articles of Confederation gave Congress the power to request funds but no power to compel payment. The states had learned that they could refuse without consequence.

The memorials from Newburgh gave Morris something he had lacked: a deadline that Congress could not ignore. He did not waste it. On April 1, 1783, Morris submitted his report on public credit to Congress. The document laid out the numbers with brutal clarity. The foreign debt stood at nearly eight million dollars. The domestic debt exceeded thirty million. The army alone was owed back pay and pensions that would require millions more. Morris showed what every delegate already knew: the government had obligations it could not meet, promises it could not keep.

Then he made his argument. The only solution was a federal impost—a duty on imports that would give the national government an independent source of revenue. Without it, the government would default on its debts. Without it, the army would remain unpaid. Without it, the promises made to the soldiers who had won independence would prove hollow. The implication hung over the room without being spoken. An unpaid army was a dangerous army. Newburgh had proved that much.

Alexander Hamilton watched from his seat among the New York delegation.

He had arrived in Philadelphia in November 1782, just as the Newburgh crisis was beginning to take shape. The young delegate had served as Washington’s aide-de-camp for four years. He knew the army’s mood, its grievances, its simmering resentments. He also understood finance in ways that most delegates did not. Hamilton had been studying the problem of national credit since his days as a twenty-year-old artillery captain, writing letters to Robert Morris proposing banks and funding schemes.

The arrival on February 13 of rumors that a preliminary peace agreement had been reached in Paris heightened the sense of urgency among the nationalists. Hamilton had written to Washington that same day, warning him of the possibility of impending unrest among the officers. The letter had been careful, deliberate. Hamilton had urged Washington to use the army’s claims as leverage to pressure the states for a national funding plan. He wanted Washington to know what might be coming. He wanted to position himself as the general’s adviser on matters of finance and policy.

Now Hamilton watched his strategy unfold. The impost was not a new proposal. Congress had requested one in 1781, when the Articles of Confederation were still being debated. The Articles gave Congress the power to request funds from the states but no power to compel payment. The impost would change that. It would allow Congress to collect revenue directly, without begging the states for contributions that might never arrive. Twelve states had ratified the original impost. Only Rhode Island had refused, and its refusal had killed the measure.

Morris and Hamilton wanted to try again. The army’s memorials gave them the justification.

The debate that followed was bitter and prolonged. The nationalists argued from necessity. The army had to be paid. The debt had to be honored. The government had to have revenue. Morris made this argument in his report. Hamilton made it from the floor. The shadow of Newburgh hung over every speech.

The opposition saw that shadow differently. They saw a trap. The impost was a tax, but it was also a precedent. If Congress could collect revenue directly, it would no longer depend on the states. It would become a true national government, with its own power and its own interests. The delegates who opposed the impost feared consolidated power. They had fought a war against a distant parliament that claimed the right to tax them without their consent. Now Morris and Hamilton proposed creating a new power to tax, one that would sit in Philadelphia rather than London.

Rhode Island had refused the impost for exactly this reason. The state’s delegates had argued that the duty would bear hardest on commercial states, that it would benefit some at the expense of others, that it would create a permanent revenue stream that Congress could expand at will. Once the principle was established, where would it stop? What would prevent Congress from imposing new duties, new excises, new taxes on a people who had no direct representation in the national legislature?

Such fears were not abstract. They had driven the Revolution itself.

The debate stretched across days. Tempers flared. Morris and Hamilton pushed the impost as the only solution to the army’s grievances. The opposition pushed back, proposing alternatives that would keep the funding power in state hands. Let the states assume the debt, some suggested. Let each state pay its own soldiers. Let Congress request funds one more time, with the threat of the army as implicit pressure.

The nationalists rejected every alternative. State assumption would destroy national credit. It would leave some states struggling while others prospered. It would reward the states that had paid least and punish those that had contributed most. The debt was national. The payment should be national. And the only way to make it national was to give Congress the power to raise money directly.

The army’s memorials sat on the table through it all. They were moderate in tone but explosive in implication. Every delegate understood what they represented. The officers had channeled their anger into petition. But the anger had not disappeared. It waited, contained by Washington’s intervention, for Congress to act.

Washington’s influence now extended into the debate itself, though he remained in Newburgh. His speech in the Temple of Virtue had turned the room. The spectacles, the fumbling with the letter, the appeal to the officers’ patience—these had become legend within days. The story had traveled from Newburgh to Philadelphia, carried by letters and travelers and the sheer velocity of news in a nation at war’s end. Washington had saved the republic from its own army. He had reminded the officers of their duty. He had shamed them into submission.

The nationalists used that story even as they warned of what might happen if Congress failed to act. Washington had contained the crisis. But Washington could not solve it. Only Congress could do that. And Congress could solve it only by giving itself the power to pay the army. The argument was clever. It used Washington’s prestige to support the nationalist position while warning that even Washington’s prestige might not be enough next time. The general had quelled one mutiny. Could he quell another? Could he hold the army together indefinitely while Congress failed to meet its obligations? The officers had sent memorials this time. What would they send next?

Hamilton made this argument in letters and conversations outside the formal debates. The army’s claims were just. Congress had promised pay and pensions. The promises had to be honored. But honor required a funding mechanism, and the funding mechanism required the impost. The logic was tight. The pressure was real. The alternative—default, disorder, disintegration—was unthinkable.

The opposition was not convinced. They saw the pressure as manufactured. The army’s grievances were real enough. The officers had not been paid. The soldiers had suffered. But the crisis had been stage-managed. The anonymous addresses had called for a meeting that could have ended in a march on Philadelphia. Who had written those addresses? Who had organized the meeting? Who had ensured that Washington would be there to turn the room? The timing was too convenient. The nationalists wanted the impost. The army provided the threat. Washington provided the containment. And now Morris and Hamilton demanded the impost as the only way to prevent future threats.

It looked like a play. It looked like crisis engineering.

The opposition could not prove their suspicions. The authors of the anonymous addresses remained unknown, at least officially. Major John Armstrong’s role would not become public knowledge until later. But the pattern was clear. The army had been pushed to the edge. The nationalists had positioned themselves to catch the falling pieces. And now they were using the fear of military unrest to force through a measure that would transform the government.

The debate exposed the fundamental impasse at the heart of the Confederation. Congress could not pay the army without revenue. It could not raise revenue without the states. The states would not give revenue without being forced. And the only force available was the army, which could not be used without destroying the republic it had fought to create. The nationalists wanted to break this cycle by giving Congress independent taxing power. The opposition wanted to preserve the states’ sovereignty, even if that meant defaulting on the debt.

Both sides claimed the mantle of the Revolution. The nationalists argued that the Revolution had created a nation, and that nation needed a government capable of sustaining itself. The opposition argued that the Revolution had been fought against taxation without representation, and that giving Congress a taxing power would recreate the very evil the patriots had opposed. Both sides invoked the army’s sacrifice. Both sides claimed to honor the soldiers. Both sides accused the other of betraying the Revolution’s ideals.

The memorials from Newburgh became props in this theater. The nationalists pointed to their moderate tone as evidence that the officers remained loyal, that there was still time to act. The opposition pointed to their mere existence as evidence of the danger—that even loyal officers had been driven to petition by Congress’s failures. Each side found in the documents what it needed.

The debate continued through April. The weather warmed. Peace rumors circulated more confidently. The preliminary treaty had been signed in January. The final treaty would come later. But everyone knew the war was over. The British would evacuate. The army would disband. The question was what would happen in between.

Morris grew desperate. His credit was nearly gone. He had borrowed against expectations that the states would fund the government. They had not. He had issued notes that traded at steep discounts. He had promised payment that he could not deliver. The army’s pay was the most urgent obligation, but it was not the only one. The foreign debt needed service. The domestic creditors needed satisfaction. The whole structure of national credit rested on promises that Congress could not keep.

He pushed harder. In private letters, in conversations, in his public report, he made the stakes clear. The government would fail without the impost. The army would not be paid. The nation would default on its sacred debt. The rhetoric escalated. The pressure mounted.

Hamilton matched him. The young delegate had arrived in Congress with a reputation for brilliance and a hunger for influence. He knew the army’s mood. He understood finance in ways that most delegates did not. Together, they formed a phalanx. Morris provided the financial expertise. Hamilton provided the political argument. Both pointed to the same conclusion.

The opposition held. They had the votes to block the impost. They had Rhode Island’s example to cite. They had the fear of consolidated power to invoke. And they had the suspicion that the crisis had been manufactured to force their hand. They would not be stampeded into surrendering state sovereignty by an army that had already been contained.

The debate reached its climax in late April. A delegate rose to propose a compromise. Instead of a general impost, Congress would request a specific impost dedicated solely to paying the army. The revenue would be limited in time and purpose. Once the army was paid, the duty would expire. This would meet the immediate crisis without creating a permanent federal taxing power.

The nationalists saw the danger immediately. A limited impost might solve the army’s problem. But it would not solve the larger problem of national credit. It would not give Congress the revenue it needed for other obligations. It would not establish the principle of federal taxation. It would be a stopgap, not a solution. But it might pass. And it might be all they could get.

Hamilton argued against the compromise. He wanted the full impost. He wanted the precedent. He wanted the permanent revenue. But he also knew that the army needed to be paid. The compromise was inferior, but it was better than nothing. If the full impost could not pass, the limited impost might at least meet the immediate obligation.

Morris was more ambivalent. He needed revenue, any revenue. But he also needed the principle. The limited impost would pay the army once. It would not give him the ongoing income to manage the debt. It would not restore national credit. It would be a partial victory that left the larger problem unsolved.

The debate turned on the question of time. The limited impost would require new ratifications from the states. Rhode Island had already rejected the general impost. Would it accept a limited one? Would the other states? The process could take months. The army needed pay now.

The nationalists argued that the general impost could be implemented faster. The states had already ratified it, except for Rhode Island. If Rhode Island could be brought around, or if Congress could proceed without it, the revenue could flow immediately. The opposition argued that Rhode Island would never agree, and that proceeding without unanimous consent would violate the Articles of Confederation.

The legal question was murky. The Articles required supermajorities for certain decisions. Did the impost fall under those provisions? No one knew for certain. The Articles had been written in a different moment, by men who had not foreseen the financial crisis that independence would create. The government was operating without a clear constitutional framework for its most basic functions.

The nationalists pressed this deeper point. The Confederation was broken. It could not raise revenue. It could not pay its debts. It could not meet its obligations. The army’s crisis was only the most visible symptom of a systemic failure. The impost was a patch. What was needed was a new framework, a government with real power, a constitution that could sustain a nation.

Hamilton had already begun thinking along these lines. The impost was his immediate goal. But his ultimate goal was more ambitious. He wanted a stronger national government, one that could tax, regulate commerce, and enforce its laws. Newburgh had shown him the danger of weakness. An unpaid army was a threat to liberty. A government that could not pay its soldiers was a government that could not govern.

The compromise gathered support. The nationalists reluctantly accepted that they might not get the full impost. The opposition reluctantly accepted that something had to be done. The army’s memorials remained on the table, a reminder of what would happen if Congress failed.

Washington’s influence hovered over every discussion. His intervention at Newburgh had given Congress the breathing room to debate. But it had also created an expectation. The general had contained the crisis. Now Congress had to resolve it. The officers had sent their petitions. They waited for a response.

The response, when it came, would be inadequate. Congress could not agree on the full impost. It could not agree on a permanent solution to the revenue problem. It could barely agree on the limited compromise. The delegates argued, negotiated, and postured. They wrote letters to their states, seeking instructions that would not arrive in time. They blamed each other, Morris, Hamilton, the army, the states, the Articles themselves.

The debate had exposed the fundamental weakness of the Confederation. The government had no power to raise revenue. It had no power to compel the states. It had no power to meet its obligations. The army’s memorials had forced a confrontation with this reality. But the confrontation had not produced a solution. It had produced only another impasse.

The nationalists had achieved something, though not what they wanted. They had made the argument for federal revenue explicit. They had connected the army’s grievances to the structural weakness of the government. They had positioned the Newburgh crisis as an object lesson in the necessity of reform. The impost might fail. But the argument would endure.

Hamilton understood this. The debate concerned more than the impost; it concerned the principle. Every speech, every vote, every failure built the case for a new constitutional order. The army’s discontent was the immediate pressure. But the long-term goal was a government that could prevent such discontent from ever arising again.

Morris understood it too. His report on public credit had laid out the numbers. The debt was real. The obligations were binding. The government’s inability to meet them was not a temporary problem but a structural flaw. Morris wanted the impost to solve the immediate crisis. But he also wanted to demonstrate that the Confederation could not solve it. The failure itself was part of the argument.

The opposition understood it as well, though they drew different conclusions. They saw the nationalists’ strategy. They feared the consolidation of power. They resisted the impost because they opposed taxation and because they opposed the precedent it would set. The debate concerned revenue, but it also concerned sovereignty. Who would govern the United States? The states themselves, or a national government in Philadelphia?

The memorials from Newburgh had forced the question. The officers’ grievances had become the nationalists’ weapon. The army’s suffering had become the argument for a stronger government. This was the culmination of the strategy that Hamilton had outlined in his February letter to Washington: channel the crisis toward a predetermined solution, manufacture the emergency, then offer the remedy.

Washington’s role in this remained ambiguous. He had quelled the mutiny. He had channeled the officers’ anger into petition. He had given Congress the space to act. But he had also, perhaps unintentionally, given the nationalists their cover. His intervention had prevented the worst. It had also preserved the pressure. The army waited. The memorials sat on the table. The debate raged. And Washington’s prestige backed the argument that Congress had to act.

The general did not involve himself in the congressional debates. He remained at Newburgh, managing the army, negotiating the final stages of the war, preparing for whatever peace would bring. But his presence was felt. Every delegate knew that Washington had saved the republic from its own soldiers. Every delegate knew that Washington expected Congress to honor its obligations. Every delegate knew that Washington’s patience, like the army’s, was finite.

The debates in Philadelphia had begun with the reading of the memorials. They ended without resolution. The delegates could not agree on the impost. They could not agree on the compromise. They could barely agree on the language of a response to the army. The impasse was total.

The nationalists had not achieved their goal. The impost remained blocked. The revenue remained elusive. The government remained broke. But they had made their argument. They had connected the army’s plight to the Confederation’s weakness. They had demonstrated that the existing system could not function. They had laid the groundwork for the constitutional reform that would come four years later.

The opposition had not achieved their goal either. They had blocked the impost, for now. But they had not addressed the underlying problem. The army remained unpaid. The debt remained unpaid. The government remained unable to meet its obligations. The crisis had been postponed, not solved.

The officers in Newburgh waited. Their memorials had been read. Their grievances had been debated. Their patience had been noted. But they had not been paid. The Congress that had promised them compensation could not agree on how to deliver it. The states that were supposed to provide the funds could not be compelled to pay. The system that had sustained the Revolution now threatened to betray it.

Washington’s spectacles had turned the room in Newburgh. But they could not turn the votes in Philadelphia. The general’s intervention had contained the crisis. It had not resolved it. That work fell to the politicians, and the politicians were deadlocked.

The memorials lay on the table, symbols of a debt that could not be paid and a government that could not govern. The debate had produced words, arguments, recriminations. It had not produced money. It had not produced a solution. It had produced only a clearer understanding of the problem.

The problem was structural. The Confederation could not raise revenue. It could not compel the states. It could not meet its obligations. The army’s crisis was the symptom. The government’s weakness was the disease.

The nationalists had tried to use the symptom to force a cure. They had failed, for now. But they had made the diagnosis clear: what happened at Newburgh was no accident but an inevitable result of a system that could not function.

The officers’ petitions had forced a direct confrontation over the fundamental weakness of the Confederation. That confrontation had ended in stalemate. But the confrontation itself was the point. The nationalists had wanted to demonstrate the necessity of reform. They had done so, even in failure.
