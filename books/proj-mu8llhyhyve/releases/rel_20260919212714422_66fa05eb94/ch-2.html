Chapter 2

The Nationalist Gambit in Philadelphia

The letter from Alexander Hamilton to George Washington, dated February 13, 1783, arrived in Newburgh at a moment when patience had worn to its final threads. Hamilton had written it the same day rumors reached Philadelphia that a preliminary peace agreement had been signed in Paris. The timing was not coincidental. Hamilton understood something that many in Congress did not: peace, when it came, would bring its own dangers. He wrote to warn Washington of the possibility of impending unrest among the officers, but his letter carried a deeper current. Hamilton suggested using the army’s claims to prevail upon the states for the purpose of establishing a national funding plan. The army’s grievances were legitimate. The question was whether those grievances might be directed toward a political end.

Moving from the army camp to the halls of Congress, the view shifted. In Newburgh, officers counted their arrears and wondered how they would survive the winter. In Philadelphia, men who understood the mechanics of power counted something else. They saw an army that had not been paid in years, a Congress without the power to raise revenue, and a constitutional order too weak to resolve the contradiction. Washington saw the same facts. But where he saw a crisis to be managed, they saw a lever to be pulled.

Robert Morris, the Superintendent of Finance, had been staring at impossible arithmetic for months. The treasury was empty. Congress lacked the power to compel the states to provide funds. The national debt mounted daily, and with it the promises that government had made to its soldiers. Morris had tried every expedient: borrowing from foreign powers, issuing paper money, pleading with state governments. None of it had worked. The states, jealous of their independence, refused to furnish adequate funding. Rhode Island had single-handedly blocked the impost—a federal duty on imports that would have given Congress a reliable revenue stream. Without that power, Morris could not pay the army; without paying the army, the Revolution’s promises rang hollow. As of January 1, 1783, the public debt stood at $42 million.

Morris was not a man given to despair. He had made his fortune in shipping and privateering, lending his credit to the Revolutionary cause when others held back. He had signed the Declaration of Independence, wagering his wealth on American independence. Now, as Superintendent of Finance, he faced a different kind of gamble. Writing that he would not be the “Minister of Injustice,” he submitted his resignation to Congress, frustrated by his defeat in the tariff battle and by states that would not provide adequate funding. He thought he was being forced to run up debts the states were unwilling to pay. His resignation was not accepted, but it signaled the depth of the crisis.

The nationalists in Congress—Morris, Hamilton, James Madison, John Dickinson—understood the structural problem. The Articles of Confederation had created a government that could make promises but could not keep them: Congress could declare debts but could not raise money to pay them; states held taxation power and would not surrender it voluntarily. What was needed was a crisis sufficient to force a change—not collapse but a controlled emergency that would demonstrate the impossibility of the current arrangement and compel states to grant Congress power to meet its obligations.

Hamilton—the ambitious former aide-de-camp now representing New York in Congress—saw with particular clarity what might be done with such anger. He had served at Washington’s headquarters; he knew officers’ grievances from inside; he understood their sense of betrayal and fear that Congress would disband them without paying what it owed; he also understood their anger could be directed toward political ends if properly channeled through men they trusted.

Hamilton’s letter to Washington was careful. He warned of the danger of unrest. He suggested that the army’s claims might be used to pressure the states. But he did not explicitly advocate for threatening Congress with military force. That would have been too direct, too dangerous, too easily traced. Instead, he planted the seed. He made clear that the army’s discontent was real and justified. He implied that Washington might use that discontent as leverage. And he left the general to draw his own conclusions about how far such leverage might extend.

Washington wrote Hamilton back, declining to introduce the army. He understood what Hamilton was suggesting, and he refused. Washington had spent eight years building an army subordinate to civilian authority. He would not now use that army as a political instrument, even for a cause he supported. His refusal was polite but firm. He would not become the tool of a faction in Congress, no matter how noble that faction’s goals.

But Hamilton and Morris had not placed all their hopes on Washington’s cooperation. The nationalists had been working other channels. The Pennsylvania delegates were closely aligned with Morris’s Republican faction, a reflection of Republican strength in the state legislature. Many of Morris’s nationalist allies from other states, including Hamilton, James Madison, John Dickinson, and Washington’s own former aides, formed a network that extended through Congress, the army, and the state governments. They corresponded. They met. They discussed strategy. And they understood that Washington’s refusal did not end the possibility of using the army’s discontent—it merely meant that Washington himself would not be the instrument.

This chapter details the calculated political maneuver conceived by a faction of nationalists, including Robert Morris and Alexander Hamilton, who saw the army’s distress not as a crisis to be solved but as a lever to be pulled. Their correspondence reveals a cold political calculus: by allowing—or even subtly encouraging—the officers’ anger to build, they hoped to create a crisis so severe that the states and a reluctant Congress would be forced to grant the central government a permanent power of taxation.

The strategy required a kind of patience that Morris, for one, did not naturally possess. He was a man of action, accustomed to moving ships and credit across oceans. But he understood that this particular crisis could not be rushed. The army’s grievances were genuine. The officers had not been paid in years. They had been promised half-pay for life, a promise that many in Congress now wanted to revoke or commute. The soldiers had endured Valley Forge, the southern campaigns, the long attrition of a war that had devoured their youth. They were not actors in a political drama. They were men who had given everything and received nothing but promises.

Morris and Hamilton did not create this discontent. They did not need to. The discontent was already there, building through the frozen months at Newburgh, fed by cold cabins and empty commissaries and the endless waiting for a peace that might or might not arrive. What the nationalists provided was not the grievance but the direction. Left to itself, military discontent might dissipate in grumbling and individual acts of desperation. Properly channeled, it could become a political force.

The concept that would later be called crisis engineering was taking shape in Philadelphia, though no one used that phrase. The idea was simple: a political deadlock could be broken by a sufficiently dramatic emergency. The states would never grant Congress taxing power while things remained quiet. They would grant it only when the alternative became worse—when the threat of military upheaval made surrender of revenue seem like the lesser evil. The nationalists did not want actual violence. They wanted the threat of violence, credible enough to frighten the states into action, controlled enough to be defused once the desired legislation had passed.

This was a high-stakes strategy. It required managing forces that could easily escape control. The army was not a political instrument. It was a collection of men with real grievances, real anger, and real capacity for action. If the crisis went too far, if the officers decided to take matters into their own hands, the result could be civil war. The nationalists understood this. They believed they could manage the risk. They believed they could light the fuse and then extinguish it at the right moment.

Hamilton’s role in this strategy has been debated by historians. Some have argued that he was merely warning Washington of genuine danger, not advocating for its use. The text of his February 13 letter supports this interpretation on its surface. But Hamilton was not a man given to innocent warnings. Every word he wrote served a purpose. By informing Washington of the possibility of unrest, by suggesting that the army’s claims might be used to pressure the states, by outlining the specific legislative outcomes that might resolve the crisis, Hamilton was doing more than reporting. He was proposing. He was testing whether Washington might be brought around to seeing the army’s discontent as a political resource.

Washington’s refusal told Hamilton what he needed to know. The general would not cooperate. But that did not mean the strategy was dead. It meant that the nationalists would have to work through other channels. The army had other officers, other leaders, other points of influence. Washington was the commander, but he was not the only voice the soldiers heard.

Morris’s position as Superintendent of Finance gave him unique visibility into the army’s condition. He received the requisitions, the pleas, the increasingly desperate letters from quartermasters who could not purchase supplies, from paymasters who had no funds to disburse. He knew exactly how much the army was owed, how long it had been waiting, and how thin the officers’ patience had become. This knowledge was not merely administrative. It was political intelligence. It told him how much pressure the army could tolerate before it broke.

The financial crisis that Morris managed was not abstract. It translated directly into human suffering at Newburgh. When the treasury was empty, the commissary could not buy meat. When the states refused to pay, the paymaster could not distribute wages. When the impost was blocked, the promises made to soldiers became worthless paper. Morris understood this chain of causation. He also understood that he could not break it. He could only manage its consequences.

The nationalists’ strategy involved what might be called debt theater—the performative use of financial obligations as political currency. The army’s arrears were real, but they were also useful. They demonstrated the failure of the current system. They created a class of citizens—soldiers and officers—who had a direct interest in strengthening the federal government. And they provided a moral claim that could be wielded against those who opposed federal revenue. The nationalists did not create the debt, but they understood how to use it.

Congress was bitterly divided on the subject of finance. Rhode Island had prevented action on the impost. Other states had dragged their feet on requisitions, paying late or not at all. The treasury was empty, and Congress lacked the power to compel the states to provide the necessary funds for meeting its obligations. An attempt to amend the Articles of Confederation to grant Congress taxing power had failed. The nationalists had tried constitutional means and been defeated. Now they faced a choice: accept defeat, or find another route.

Hamilton had proposed a convention to revise the Articles of Confederation. The proposal was ignored. He would resign from Congress in mid-1783, frustrated by his inability to achieve reform through normal channels. But in early 1783, he was still trying. The army crisis offered a new opportunity. If the constitutional route was blocked, perhaps the pressure of circumstances could force a breakthrough.

The arrival on February 13 of rumors that a preliminary peace agreement had been reached in Paris heightened the sense of urgency among the nationalists. Peace would bring demobilization. Demobilization would scatter the army before its grievances were resolved. If the army was to be used as leverage, it had to be used while it still existed as a coherent force. Time was running out.

Hamilton’s letter to Washington was dated the same day the rumors arrived. This was not coincidence. Hamilton understood that the window for action was closing. If the army was to make a political impact, it would have to act before the peace was finalized and the soldiers sent home. The nationalists did not have months to plan. They had weeks, perhaps days.

The strategy that emerged from the nationalist correspondence was not fully articulated in any single document. It took shape through hints, suggestions, and shared assumptions. Morris wrote to Hamilton. Hamilton wrote to Washington. Others wrote to officers in the field. The network communicated in code, not of ciphers but of implication. Everyone understood what was at stake. Everyone understood what was being proposed. No one wrote it down in terms that could be used as evidence of treason.

This deniability was essential. The nationalists were proposing to use the threat of military force to influence civilian government. This was dangerous ground. If the strategy failed, if it was exposed, if the army actually marched on Philadelphia, the result would be catastrophe. The Revolution would have replaced one tyranny with another. The cause of self-government would be discredited. The nationalists who had pushed the strategy would be ruined, perhaps hanged.

But if the strategy succeeded—if the threat was credible enough to force action, if the crisis was contained before it became violence, if the impost was established and the federal government strengthened—the result would be a stronger union capable of meeting its obligations and defending its independence. The nationalists believed the risk was worth taking. They believed they could control the outcome. They believed that Washington, when presented with a crisis, would act to contain it, and that his containment would direct the pressure toward the desired political solution.

Washington saw through the strategy. His reply to Hamilton made clear that he would not introduce the army as a political instrument. After the crisis had ended, Washington warned of the dangers of using the army as leverage to gain support for the national funding plan. He understood that the nationalists were playing with fire. He refused to be the one who handed them the match.

But Washington’s refusal did not stop the strategy. It only changed its shape. If the commander would not cooperate, the nationalists would have to work around him. They would have to find other officers, other channels, other ways to ignite the crisis. Washington was the most important figure in the army, but he was not the only one. The army contained other leaders, other factions, other ambitions.

The nationalist plan existed as theory in Philadelphia. It required an intermediary to carry it to Newburgh, a catalyst to ignite the planned crisis among the officers, and a mechanism to ensure that the crisis produced the desired political outcome without actual violence. The theory was clear. The implementation remained to be worked out.

Morris continued to manage the treasury, which meant managing the absence of funds. He continued to issue certificates of debt, knowing they could not be redeemed. He continued to make promises on behalf of a government that lacked the power to keep them. Each promise added to the pressure. Each unredeemed certificate became evidence of the system’s failure. The debt mounted, and with it the sense of crisis.

Hamilton continued to work the political angles in Congress. He argued for the impost, for federal revenue, for a stronger central government. He was repeatedly defeated. The states would not surrender their power. Rhode Island blocked the impost. Other states paid only what they chose. Congress could pass resolutions but could not enforce them. The system was broken, and the nationalists could not fix it through normal means.

The officers at Newburgh waited. They did not know what was being discussed in Philadelphia. They did not see the letters that passed between Morris and Hamilton, Hamilton and Washington, the nationalist network that stretched across the states. They knew only that they had not been paid, that Congress had made promises it could not keep, that their service seemed to count for nothing in the ledgers of the new nation.

This was the raw material the nationalists had to work with. Not mercenaries who could be hired to threaten, but patriots who had given years of their lives and been rewarded with paper promises. Their anger was genuine. Their grievance was legitimate. The nationalists did not create the grievance. They shaped its direction.

The strategy required a kind of ruthlessness that its authors might not have acknowledged in those terms. To use the army’s distress as a political lever was to treat the soldiers’ suffering as instrumental. Their hunger, their cold, their fear for their families’ futures—these were not problems to be solved but resources to be exploited. The nationalists believed they were working for a larger good: a stronger union, a government capable of meeting its obligations, a nation that could survive in a hostile world. But the means they contemplated involved manipulating men who had already sacrificed everything.

Morris knew this. Hamilton knew it. They made their calculations anyway. They weighed the risk of unrest against the risk of inaction. They judged that the current system was unsustainable, that something would have to force a change, and that the army’s discontent was the most powerful force available. They did not wish violence on the country. They believed they could control the outcome. They believed they could create a crisis that would force reform without producing catastrophe.

Whether this belief was realistic would be tested in the coming weeks. The nationalist plan existed as theory. It needed a spark. It needed someone to carry the fire to Newburgh, to transform abstract discontent into concrete action, to organize the officers’ anger into a threat that Congress could not ignore.

Washington’s refusal to cooperate had deprived the nationalists of their most reliable channel to the army. But the army was large, and Washington was not its only leader. Other officers, other factions, other ambitions circulated through the camps. Men who believed that Washington had been too patient, too trusting, too willing to accept Congress’s broken promises. Men who might be receptive to suggestions that the army take a stronger stand.

The nationalist network began to explore alternative routes. Letters went out. Conversations took place. Suggestions were made. The strategy that could not be pursued through Washington would have to be pursued around him.

In Philadelphia, Morris watched the treasury drain toward zero. In Congress, Hamilton watched the impost fail and the states refuse to pay. In Newburgh, the army watched the winter drag on, the commissary empty, the paymaster’s window stay shut. Three groups, three perspectives, one gathering crisis.

The nationalists had made their calculation. The pressure would be allowed to build. They would not intervene to relieve the army’s distress, because that distress was the instrument of their policy. They would position themselves to channel the resulting crisis toward their desired end: a federal government with the power to tax, to pay its debts, to survive.

The plan was not written down in a single document. It emerged through correspondence, conversation, and shared understanding. It was not a conspiracy in the sense of a secret agreement among a few plotters. It was a strategy pursued by a faction that saw an opportunity and reached for it. The faction was powerful. Morris controlled the treasury. Hamilton was a rising force in Congress. Their allies included some of the most influential figures in American politics.

What they proposed was not technically illegal. They were not planning a coup. They were planning to use the threat of unrest to achieve a political outcome. The threat would come from the army, but the army would not have to act. The threat alone would be sufficient, if it was credible, to force the states to act. Once the impost was established, once federal revenue was secured, the crisis would resolve itself. The army would be paid. The government would meet its obligations. The nation would survive.

This was the theory. The practice would prove more complicated.

Washington’s refusal had introduced an element of uncertainty. The nationalists had assumed that Washington would cooperate, or at least acquiesce, in using the army’s discontent to pressure Congress. His clear refusal meant they would have to work around him. This increased the risk. Washington might move to suppress any unrest before it could be used as leverage. He might defuse the crisis before it produced the desired political result. He might, in protecting the army from manipulation, also protect Congress from the pressure the nationalists needed.

The nationalists had to move quickly. Peace was coming. The army would demobilize. The opportunity would disappear. If they were going to use the army’s discontent, they had to use it now.

Hamilton’s February 13 letter had set the strategy in motion. Washington’s refusal had modified but not stopped it. The nationalists would find another way. A messenger would be found to carry their words to Newburgh. A method would be devised to ignite the officers’ anger into action. The crisis would be created that would force the change.

In Philadelphia, the nationalists watched and waited. The army’s distress, Congress’s weakness, the states’ intransigence—each element of the deadlock they observed with the eye of a craftsman. They saw a chance to remake the government, to create the power that the Revolution had promised but never delivered. They were willing to risk much to achieve it. The question was whether they could control what they were about to unleash.

The nationalist plan existed as theory in Philadelphia, requiring an intermediary and a catalyst to ignite the planned crisis among the officers.
