Chapter 4

The General’s Unwanted Command

The petition lay on the desk before him. Washington picked up his quill, dipped it in the inkwell, and began the letter that would accompany the document south to Philadelphia. The date was March 1783. The officers had submitted their grievances in writing yet again, and he would forward them as he had forwarded dozens before. The phrases came automatically now—the respectful language, the careful appeals to Congress’s honor, the warnings of what might happen if the army’s patience finally broke. He wrote them out, sealed the packet, and set it aside for the next courier. He knew what the answer would be. It would be the same answer he had received for years.

The routine had become its own kind of burden. Washington had taken up residence at the Hasbrouck farmhouse in Newburgh in April 1782, making this small Hudson River settlement the Continental Army’s headquarters. Through the winter of 1783, he had watched his army diminish from want rather than battle. The men had not been paid in years. Congress had, in 1780, promised Continental officers a lifetime pension of half their pay when they were discharged. Then the financier Robert Morris had stopped army pay entirely in early 1782 as a cost-saving measure, arguing that the arrears would be made up when the war finally ended. But the war was ending now. Preliminary articles of peace had been signed in Paris. The army had done what was asked of it. The pay still did not come.

Washington’s position required him to transmit the broken promises. He received the petitions, the desperate documents from officers who had served for eight years without compensation, who had mortgaged their futures and whose families survived on credit and charity. He read them, endorsed them, sent them south. Then he waited for responses that rarely came, and when they did come, contained nothing but words. He was the buffer between an army that had earned its wages and a government that could not pay them. The role made him complicit in the failure, and he felt that complicity with every letter he wrote.

His correspondence from these months reveals a man calculating the erosion of his own authority. Washington wrote to Robert Morris in January, warning that the army’s patience had reached its limit. He described the anxious and painful sensations that pervaded the officer corps. He spoke of honor as the currency that had been spent and could not be recovered. Morris wrote back with assurances that he was doing all he could, that the states must be pressed to contribute their quotas, that a system of funding must be established. Washington had heard these assurances before. The words had begun to sound like rearrangements of the same words.

What made the position unbearable was that Washington agreed with his officers. He knew they were owed. He knew Congress had failed them. He believed the promises made in the name of the United States were binding, and that the failure to honor them stained the national character. But his role as commander-in-chief required him to suppress the very grievances he knew to be just. The commander who had led these men through the campaigns of 1776, through the Delaware crossing, through the victory at Yorktown, now found himself reduced to a functionary, processing paperwork for a bankruptcy he could not halt.

The army’s numbers had shrunk as the war wound down. The force that remained—perhaps 10, 000 men scattered across various posts, with the main body at Newburgh—existed in a strange limbo. They were still soldiers, still subject to military discipline, still bound to follow orders. But the purpose for which they had enlisted had largely been accomplished. The British had withdrawn from most positions. Peace negotiations proceeded in Europe. The soldiers waited, not for battle, but for the piece of paper that would discharge them and the money that would let them go home. They waited in the cold of a Hudson Valley winter, in log huts thrown together years ago, eating rations that grew shorter as the quartermaster’s supplies dwindled.

Washington understood that his own reputation was bound up in this waiting. The officers looked to him as their advocate, their protector, the man who had shared their hardships and would secure their rights. He had built his command on personal loyalty—on the willingness of men to follow him into situations that seemed impossible, because they trusted that he would not abandon them. That trust had been the foundation of the Continental Army’s survival. Now it became a liability. Every day that passed without pay, every petition that went unanswered, every promise that proved hollow, eroded the faith that Washington had spent eight years accumulating.

The correspondence with Morris grew more urgent as winter deepened. Washington warned of serious and alarming consequences if Congress failed to act. He described the irritated and discontented state of the officer corps. He used language that he had learned to use carefully—the language of prediction rather than threat, of consequences rather than demands. He was telling Morris what would happen if nothing changed, knowing that Morris already knew, knowing that Morris and his allies in Philadelphia might welcome the very crisis that Washington dreaded.

For the nationalists, the army’s distress was a resource to be deployed. Robert Morris, the superintendent of finance, had argued for years that the Articles of Confederation were inadequate to the needs of the new nation. The federal government could not levy taxes. It could only request funds from the states, and the states frequently refused or delayed. Morris wanted a system of national revenue—an impost on imports, a tax that would flow directly to the continental treasury and provide a steady stream of income to pay the nation’s debts. The states had rejected these proposals repeatedly. The nationalists needed leverage. They needed a crisis that would demonstrate the consequences of inaction.

The unpaid army was that crisis. Its suffering was real. Its grievances were just. And its potential for violence was genuine. The nationalists in Philadelphia—Morris, Gouverneur Morris, Alexander Hamilton—saw that the army’s anger could be directed toward political ends. If the officers threatened Congress, if they marched on Philadelphia, if they made clear that the continental government could not survive without the means to satisfy its obligations, then the states might finally grant the taxing power that the nationalists had sought for years. The conspiracy that would unfold at Newburgh was a controlled detonation, designed to create a political emergency that would produce a predetermined outcome.

Washington did not know the full extent of what was being planned. He did not know that Hamilton had written to him in February, warning of unrest while also suggesting that the army’s claims be used to pressure the states. He did not know that Morris and his allies were cultivating relationships with officers who might lead a movement. He did not know that the anonymous addresses that would appear in March were being composed by men within his own camp, with connections to the nationalist leadership in Philadelphia. But he sensed the danger. He understood that forces beyond his control were working on his army, and that his position as the buffer between the soldiers and the government made him uniquely vulnerable.

The presence of General Horatio Gates at Newburgh added another layer of complexity to Washington’s position. Gates had a history with Washington—a history that stretched back to the early years of the war and included one of the most uncomfortable episodes of Washington’s command. The Conway Cabal, as it came to be known, had been an attempt in late 1777 and early 1778 to replace Washington with Gates as commander-in-chief. Gates had just won the Battle of Saratoga, capturing an entire British army and securing what many considered the war’s decisive victory. Washington had suffered defeats at Brandywine and Germantown. A faction in Congress and among the officer corps had begun to question whether Washington was the right man to lead the army. They had looked to Gates as an alternative.

The cabal took its name from Thomas Conway, an Irish-born officer in French service who had been appointed inspector general of the army. Conway had written letters critical of Washington, and those letters had found their way into circulation. Washington learned of the campaign against him through Gates’s own adjutant, James Wilkinson, who had repeated compromising remarks while drunk. The affair had blown up publicly. Gates was forced to apologize to Washington for his role. He resigned from the Board of War and took an assignment as commander of the Eastern Department on October 22, 1778. Conway was wounded in a duel and eventually removed from his post. Washington survived the challenge to his command.

But the memory remained. Gates had not instigated the cabal, but he had not discouraged it either. He had allowed others to advance his name as a possible replacement. He had accepted the praise of Washington’s critics without rejecting their premise. Washington had forgiven him publicly and treated him with outward courtesy, but the trust between them had been broken. Now, in the winter of 1783, Gates resided at Newburgh as president of the Board of War, a position that placed him in proximity to the army’s highest councils. He was popular among certain officers—particularly those who had served under him at Saratoga, where he stood front and center in John Trumbull’s painting of the British surrender. He had his own following, his own reputation, his own claim to the gratitude of the nation.

Washington watched Gates with the wariness of a man who had been betrayed before. The rivalry was not active, not in any sense that could be named or documented. Gates made no public challenges to Washington’s authority. He performed his duties, attended the necessary meetings, signed the necessary papers. But his presence at headquarters was a reminder that Washington’s command was not uncontested, that there were men who had once thought him replaceable and might think so again. The discontent in the officer corps could find other leaders if Washington proved unable to deliver what the soldiers were owed. Gates, with his victory at Saratoga and his reputation as a fighting general, was an obvious alternative.

The structure of Washington’s command made him vulnerable to exactly this kind of challenge. He had built his authority on personal loyalty, on the willingness of officers to follow him because they believed in him. That belief had been tested repeatedly—by the failures of 1776, by the winter encampments, by the long stalemate that followed. Washington had survived each test by demonstrating his own commitment, his own willingness to endure what his men endured. But the test of 1783 was different. The question was not whether Washington would stand with his officers, but whether he could deliver for them. And he could not. The power to pay the army lay with Congress, and Congress had no money. Washington’s personal loyalty was worthless if it could not be translated into results.

The nationalists in Philadelphia understood this better than Washington did. They saw that his position was weakening, that his authority was eroding, that the gap between what his officers expected and what he could provide was growing wider every day. They also saw that Washington would never lead a mutiny. His commitment to civilian control of the military was absolute. He had spent eight years deferring to Congress, accepting its decisions even when he disagreed, submitting to its authority even when that authority was exercised badly. He would not change now. If the army rose against Congress, Washington would stand with Congress, not with his officers. The nationalists knew this. They counted on it.

The genius of the scheme that would unfold in March was precisely that it did not require Washington’s participation. It required his presence. It required him to be in the room, to be the figure that the officers looked to, to be the obstacle that they would have to confront if they chose the path of mutiny. Washington was not the target of the conspiracy. He was the mechanism by which the conspiracy would be resolved.

The nationalists were creating a crisis that would force Washington to choose—between his army and his government, between his loyalty to his officers and his loyalty to civilian authority. They expected him to choose the government. They expected him to suppress the mutiny. And they expected that the suppression would be so dramatic, so definitive, that it would demonstrate to Congress and to the states the necessity of paying the army’s wages and establishing a national revenue.

Washington was being cast in a role he had not chosen. The script was being written by others, in Philadelphia and in his own camp. He would be given no choice but to perform as they expected. The Debt Theater—the performance of financial obligation as political currency—required him to play the part of the loyal commander, standing between an angry army and a helpless government. His reputation, his honor, his carefully constructed identity as the servant of the republic, would be used to channel the explosion that the nationalists had engineered. He would be the safety valve. He would be the man who turned the crisis away from violence and toward the constitutional solution that the nationalists desired.

The petitions kept coming. Washington kept forwarding them. The answers kept not arriving. In his letters to Morris, Washington’s frustration began to show through the formal language. He wrote of the indignation of the officers, of their just claims, of the dangerous temper that was spreading through the camp. He warned that the army might take matters into its own hands. He was not threatening—he was predicting. He had seen the same pattern before, in the mutinies of the Pennsylvania and New Jersey lines in 1781. Those had been suppressed, but the conditions that produced them had not changed. The soldiers had not been paid. Their patience had not been rewarded. The same grievances that had produced mutiny then were producing something worse now.

The difference was that the war was ending. The soldiers knew that their leverage would vanish the moment they were discharged. A peace treaty would mean the dissolution of the army. It would mean the end of their collective power, their ability to demand what they were owed. If they did not act before peace was declared, they would never be paid at all. This was the logic that the anonymous addresses would exploit—the logic of desperation, of the closing window, of the need to act before it was too late. Washington understood this logic. He could not refute it. He could only ask for patience, for trust, for more time—when patience had been exhausted, trust had been betrayed, and time had run out.

His own position grew more precarious as the weeks passed. The officers looked at him and saw a man who could not help them. They respected him. They honored him. They would continue to follow his orders. But the reverence that had once been absolute now had cracks in it. Washington could feel the change. He could see it in the way officers spoke to him, in the petitions they presented, in the language they used. They were no longer asking him to intercede. They were telling him what they would do if Congress did not act. They were informing him of their intentions, not seeking his approval. The relationship had shifted.

The presence of Gates at Newburgh was a constant reminder of how far that shift might go. Gates had his own circle of officers, his own sources of information, his own channels of communication with Philadelphia. He was not openly disloyal. He was not plotting. But he was there, available, an alternative should Washington’s authority finally collapse. The nationalists who were engineering the crisis knew that Gates was in camp. They knew that he had connections to John Armstrong Jr., his aide-de-camp, who would write the anonymous addresses. They knew that the conspiracy, if it failed, might leave Washington weakened and Gates strengthened. They were playing a dangerous game, with outcomes they could not fully control.

Washington’s daily routine at Newburgh concealed the pressure that was building around him. He rose early, as he always had. He reviewed the reports from his subordinates. He wrote his letters. He received visitors. He inspected the troops. He performed the duties of a commander-in-chief in an army that no longer had battles to fight. The monotony of the routine was itself a kind of strain. There was nothing to do but wait—for peace, for pay, for the resolution that never came. The waiting was harder than the fighting had been. In battle, Washington had known what to do. In this new kind of war—the war of petitions and correspondence, of patience and politics—he felt his own inadequacy.

The farm that served as his headquarters had become a kind of prison. Washington could see the Hudson River from his window, the frozen waterway that connected Newburgh to the world beyond. He could see the huts where his officers lived, the parade ground where they assembled, the buildings that housed the supplies that were running out. He was surrounded by the evidence of his own failure—the failure to secure for his army what it had earned. Every face he passed was a reminder of unpaid wages, of promises broken, of trust betrayed. He had led these men. He had asked them to sacrifice. He had told them that their country would honor their service. And now their country could not, or would not, pay.

The correspondence with Hamilton in February had been particularly galling. Hamilton had written to warn Washington of possible unrest, but his letter contained something else—a suggestion that the army’s claims might be used to force Congress’s hand. Hamilton had served under Washington during the war. He had been one of Washington’s most trusted aides. He knew how Washington thought, how he would react. The letter was careful, couched in the language of concern, but its implication was clear: the army’s anger might be useful. Washington had responded with the outrage of a man who understood that he was being asked to countenance something he could not accept. He had rejected the suggestion. He had restated his commitment to civilian authority. He had made clear where he stood.

But Hamilton’s letter had planted something in Washington’s mind—the knowledge that men he had trusted were thinking along these lines, were seeing the army’s distress as an opportunity rather than a crisis. The letter was dated February 13, 1783. Within weeks, the anonymous addresses would appear in Newburgh, calling the officers to a meeting that could end in mutiny. Washington did not know who had written them. He did not know the full extent of the conspiracy. But he knew that the forces gathering around his army were not all friendly, and that the men who claimed to support him might have agendas of their own.

The winter wore on. The petitions accumulated. The promises from Philadelphia grew more elaborate and more empty. Washington continued to write, to forward, to wait. He was the commander-in-chief of an army that had won its war and lost its faith in the government it served. He was the servant of a Congress that could not pay its debts. He was the advocate for officers whose patience was exhausted. He was the rival of a general who waited in the wings. He was the target of a conspiracy he could not see but could feel, a scheme that used his own position against him.

By early March, the atmosphere at Newburgh had grown heavy with anticipation. The officers talked among themselves. They passed rumors. They shared their grievances. They looked at Washington and wondered whether he could still help them, or whether the time had come to help themselves. The anonymous addresses had not yet appeared, but the conditions that would make them effective were already in place. The army was angry. The government was paralyzed. The commander-in-chief was trapped between them.

Washington sat at his desk in the Hasbrouck farmhouse, composing another letter to Philadelphia. The petition before him was like all the others—a recitation of grievances, a request for justice, an appeal to honor. He would send it. He would wait for the answer. He would continue to play the role that had been assigned to him—the role of the loyal servant, the patient advocate, the man who believed in the system even when the system failed. But he knew, as he wrote, that the system was failing, and that the failure would soon become impossible to ignore.

The stage was set with a commander trapped between his army and his government, and a rival in camp—a charged atmosphere into which the anonymous addresses would now land.
