Chapter 6

Summons to the Temple of Virtue

The addresses gave voice to what they already believed. But the second address did something more. It gave them a time, a place, and a course of action. On the morning of March -12- 10, 1783, a second anonymous paper appeared in the camp at Newburgh, more urgent than the first. Where the initial address had merely called for a meeting of officers to consider their collective response to Congress’s failure, this one named the day and the hour: Saturday, March 15, at eleven in the morning. It named the location: the “Public Building,” known to every man in camp as the Temple of Virtue. The summons had the quality of an operational order. Someone had decided that grievance alone was insufficient. Grievance needed a vessel, a moment, a gathering. Someone had decided to convert written agitation into collective action.

The Temple of Virtue stood at the center of the encampment, a large wooden hall measuring forty by seventy feet. The men called it the “New Building” or simply “the Temple.” It served multiple purposes: a place of worship on Sundays, a venue for assemblies and courts-martial during the week. Its very name carried a weight of republican aspiration. The Society of the Cincinnati had not yet formed, but the officers who would later join it had already begun to think of themselves as a distinct body, bound by shared sacrifice and shared grievance. The Temple was where they gathered to hear orders, to receive news, to worship. Now it would be where they gathered to decide whether to defy their commander and march on Philadelphia.

The structure itself was modest by any architectural standard. Wooden walls, a simple roof, benches enough to hold several hundred men. But its symbolic importance far exceeded its physical dimensions. In a camp of log huts and canvas tents, the Temple was the only public building large enough to hold a general assembly. It was the closest thing the army had to a civic space. To call the officers there was to invoke a kind of authority that existed outside the normal chain of command. The anonymous address was not simply inviting men to talk. It was summoning them to a deliberative body that could claim, however tenuously, to represent the army as a whole.

The second address made explicit what the first had only implied. It spoke of “the crisis” and “the alternative” that remained if Congress continued to ignore their claims. It warned against those who would advise patience, urging officers to recognize that moderation had become another word for submission. The language was carefully calibrated. It did not call for mutiny in so many words. But it directed the officers’ attention toward a course that could only end in mutiny or its threat. A meeting of officers, called without authorization, to discuss their collective response to Congress—that meeting would be, by definition, an act of insubordination. The address did not need to name the destination. The road itself was enough.

Major John Armstrong Jr., aide-de-camp to General Horatio Gates, had written both addresses. This was known to few at the time, and Armstrong would not acknowledge his authorship for decades. But the fingerprints were there for those who knew how to read them. The style was educated, the arguments sophisticated, the classical allusions precise. Armstrong was a Princeton graduate, a former student of Richard Stockton, a man who had studied law before taking up arms. He knew how to construct an argument, and he knew how to conceal one. The addresses were not spontaneous outpourings of soldierly frustration. They were calculated documents, designed to move a specific audience toward a specific action.

The audience was the officer corps, and the action was a meeting. That much was clear. What remained unclear, to Washington and to others, was the purpose behind the meeting. Was it simply to petition Congress more forcefully? Was it to issue an ultimatum? Or was it to prepare for something more drastic—a march on Philadelphia, a confrontation with Congress, a military intervention in civilian politics? The addresses did not say. Their ambiguity was strategic. They allowed each officer to project his own hopes and fears onto the blank space where the agenda should have been.

Washington learned of the second address within hours of its appearance. The camp was small, and news traveled fast. An unauthorized meeting of officers, called by an anonymous hand, scheduled for a specific time and place—this was not something that could be ignored or allowed to proceed. Washington faced a choice. He could forbid the meeting outright, using his authority as commander-in-chief to order the officers to disperse. Or he could attempt to control it, to redirect its purpose toward a resolution he could accept. The first option risked open defiance. The second risked legitimizing the very gathering he sought to contain.

He chose the second course. On March 12, Washington issued general orders. He acknowledged that an anonymous address had been circulating, calling for a meeting of officers on March 15. He characterized this meeting as “disorderly” and “irregular,” pointing out that it had been called without proper authority. Then he announced that he would convene a meeting of his own—not to suppress the gathering, but to preempt it. The officers would meet on March 15, as the anonymous address had demanded. But they would meet under Washington’s authority, not in defiance of it. The meeting would be held at the Temple of Virtue, the same location specified in the anonymous summons. And Washington himself would preside.

This was a calculated risk. By calling his own meeting, Washington was acknowledging that the officers’ grievances were legitimate enough to warrant a formal response. He was also acknowledging that the anonymous addresses had struck a nerve. He could not simply dismiss the anger that had produced them. But he could try to channel it, to contain it, to prevent it from spilling over into open mutiny. The meeting would proceed. The question was what would happen when it did.

Washington’s general orders also included a request. He asked that the officers come to the meeting prepared to hear a report from the committee they had previously appointed to negotiate with Congress. This committee, led by General Henry Knox and including other senior officers, had been working for months to secure the army’s back pay and the promised half-pay pensions. Washington’s reference to the committee was a reminder that legitimate channels existed for addressing their grievances. It was also a reminder that those channels had not yet been exhausted. The implicit argument was clear: give the process more time. Trust in the institutions that had failed you. Trust in me.

But trust was precisely what the anonymous addresses had called into question. The addresses did not attack Washington directly. They did not need to. Their target was Congress, and their argument was that Congress could not be trusted to fulfill its obligations without pressure. Years of waiting had exhausted patience. Promissory notes had replaced the pay that had been promised. Half-pay pensions for life had been pledged, then watched as state legislatures balked at funding those commitments. A war had been won, and the reward was an order to go home empty-handed while speculators profited from distress. The addresses gave voice to what many already believed: betrayal had occurred.

The question of who was behind the addresses would occupy historians for generations. Armstrong’s authorship was not generally known at the time, though suspicions swirled around Gates’s headquarters. But the addresses did not emerge from a vacuum. They were part of a larger pattern of pressure, directed from Philadelphia as much as from Newburgh. The nationalist faction in Congress—Alexander Hamilton, Robert Morris, Gouverneur Morris, and others—had been working for months to create a crisis that would force the states to accept a federal taxing power. The Articles of Confederation were inadequate to the task of governing a nation, in their view. A stronger central government was necessary, and such a government required an independent source of revenue. The army’s grievances provided the leverage needed.

Hamilton had written to Washington in February, warning him of the possibility of unrest among the officers. His letter, dated February 13, 1783, arrived at Newburgh at a moment when patience had worn thin. Hamilton was explicit about the political opportunity the army’s discontent presented. He suggested that Washington should not be “too sensitive” to the “delicate” situation. The implication was clear: the army’s anger could be useful. It could be directed toward a larger purpose. Washington’s response was noncommittal, but he understood what Hamilton was suggesting. The nationalists wanted a crisis. They were prepared to let the army’s frustration boil over if that was what it took to force Congress to act.

The second anonymous address, appearing just days after Hamilton’s letter had time to be read and absorbed, cannot have been coincidental. The timing was too precise, the coordination too tight. Armstrong may have written the words, but the words served a purpose that extended beyond the camp at Newburgh. They were part of a strategy to create a political emergency. The nationalists in Congress wanted the officers to threaten mutiny. A credible threat could frighten the states into accepting a federal impost. Washington might prove unable to control the situation. And the result would be the constitutional changes sought all along.

Whether Armstrong knew the full extent of this plan is unclear. He was Gates’s aide, and Gates was a rival to Washington. But Gates was also a politician, a man who understood how power worked. If Armstrong was acting on Gates’s behalf, or with Gates’s knowledge, then the addresses were not simply an expression of soldierly frustration. They were a political maneuver, designed to advance a particular agenda. And if Armstrong was acting on instructions from Philadelphia, then the conspiracy extended beyond the camp at Newburgh to the halls of Congress itself.

The evidence is circumstantial but suggestive. Hamilton had been corresponding with Washington about the army’s discontent. Robert Morris, the Superintendent of Finance, had been warning Congress for months that the government could not meet its obligations without a federal taxing power. Gouverneur Morris, no relation to Robert but a fellow nationalist, had been advocating for a stronger central government in letters and speeches. These men were not in direct communication with Armstrong, as far as the historical record shows. But they were in communication with each other, and they shared a common goal. The crisis that the addresses created served their purposes perfectly.

In Philadelphia, the news from Newburgh arrived like a thunderclap. Reports of the anonymous addresses and the scheduled meeting spread through the city, reaching Congress and the state legislatures. The nationalists saw their opportunity. The army was on the verge of mutiny. If Congress did not act, the consequences could be catastrophic. But if Congress did act—if it granted a federal impost, if it created a reliable source of revenue—then the crisis could be averted. The argument was not subtle. It was a form of political blackmail, and its practitioners made no apologies for it.

The debate in Congress was intense. Some members were appalled by the idea of yielding to military pressure. They saw in the addresses a threat to the very principle of civilian control. To give in to the army’s demands under duress was to invite future demands, future threats, future crises. But others argued that the army’s grievances were legitimate and that Congress had failed to address them. The fault lay not with the soldiers but with the politicians who had promised more than they could deliver. The nationalists pressed their case. The crisis was real. The solution was obvious. Give the government the power to raise revenue, and the crisis would disappear.

Washington watched from Newburgh. He had no direct voice in the congressional debates, but his presence loomed over them. Everyone understood that the army’s response to the crisis would depend on what Washington did. If he sided with the officers, the threat to Congress would be overwhelming. If he opposed them, his authority might not be enough to contain them. The nationalists were counting on Washington to remain loyal to Congress, even as they created a situation that tested that loyalty to its limits. They wanted him to be the man who calmed the waters, but only after those waters had risen high enough to force change.

The days between March 10 and March 15 were filled with tension. Officers discussed the addresses in their huts, at their mess tables, on the parade ground. Some were ready to act. Enough waiting had occurred. Others were more cautious. Oaths, honor, obligations to the cause for which they had fought remained in memory. The addresses had forced a choice upon them, but the choice was not easy. To attend the meeting was to participate in an act of insubordination. To stay away was to abandon comrades, to accept the betrayal suffered, to go home with nothing.

The second address had made that choice harder by naming a specific time and place. The Temple of Virtue was not just a building. It was a symbol of everything the army had fought for: virtue, honor, sacrifice. To meet there was to claim the mantle of the Revolution itself. The anonymous author—or authors—knew what they were doing. They were not simply calling a meeting. They were summoning the officers to a sacred space, a place where the ideals of the Republic could be invoked against the government that had failed to uphold them.

Washington’s decision to convene his own meeting at the same time and place was a counter-move. He understood the symbolic power of the Temple. By taking control of the meeting, he was reclaiming that power for himself. The officers would gather at the Temple, but they would gather under his authority. They would hear from him, not from an anonymous address. They would be reminded of their oaths, their duty, their honor. And they would be asked to trust in the process that had already failed them.

The gamble was enormous. If Washington failed to convince the officers, the meeting could become a platform for mutiny. The nationalists in Philadelphia would get their crisis, but Washington would lose his authority, and the army would lose its discipline. If he succeeded, the crisis would be averted—but the underlying grievances would remain. The officers would still be unpaid. The pensions would still be unfunded. The betrayal would still be real. Washington was betting that he could buy time, that he could convince the officers to wait a little longer, to trust a little further. He was betting his reputation and his authority on a single meeting.

In Philadelphia, the nationalists watched and waited. They had set events in motion, but they could not control them. The army’s anger was real, and it had been building for years. The addresses had given that anger a focus, a direction, a deadline. But the outcome remained uncertain. Washington was the wild card. No one knew for certain what he would do or say. No one knew whether the officers would follow him or defy him. The meeting on March 15 would decide everything.

The Temple of Virtue stood ready. Its wooden walls had witnessed worship and courts-martial, celebrations and mournings. Now it would witness something unprecedented: a gathering of officers, summoned by an anonymous hand, preempted by their commander, in a camp that had not been paid, waiting for a peace that had already been signed. The contradictions piled up. The army had won the war, but it had not won the peace. The officers had served their country, but their country had not served them. The Revolution had promised liberty, but it had not delivered justice.

The second address had fixed the time and place. March 15, at the Temple of Virtue. The summons was explicit, the language urgent. “Fellow soldiers,” it began, and it ended with a call to action. The officers were being asked to choose: loyalty or self-preservation, obedience or resistance, patience or defiance. The choice would be made in a wooden hall, on a Saturday morning, in the presence of their commander-in-chief.

Washington prepared for the meeting in silence. He drafted no speech, at least none that survives. He wrote no letters explaining his intentions. He simply announced the meeting and waited. His silence was itself a form of pressure. The officers would have to attend, would have to listen, would have to decide. And Washington would have to find the words that could turn them from the course set by the anonymous addresses.

The conspirators, whoever they were, had achieved something remarkable. A diffuse grievance had been transformed into a scheduled action. The army’s anger had been given a shape and a deadline. Washington had been forced to respond, and Congress had been forced to pay attention. Whether this had been their intention all along, or whether they had simply seized an opportunity, the result was the same. The crisis was now. The meeting was now. The choice was now.

In the huts and tents of Newburgh, officers read the second address and wondered what would happen. Some wrote letters home, trying to explain the situation to wives and families who had also waited, who had also sacrificed, who had also been promised more than they received. Others spoke with their comrades, arguing for action or caution. The camp was alive with rumor and speculation. Would Washington forbid the meeting? Would he support their demands? Would he ask them to wait again, to trust again, to suffer again?

The answers would come on March 15. The Temple of Virtue awaited. The officers had been summoned. And Washington, who had led them through eight years of war, would have to lead them through this final, most dangerous test.

The nationalists in Philadelphia had wanted a crisis. They had gotten one. The officers in Newburgh had wanted justice. They had been given a meeting. And Washington, who wanted only to preserve the army and the Republic, had been given a choice: to forbid or to control, to suppress or to redirect. He had chosen to control, to redirect, to meet the crisis head-on. The meeting would proceed. The Temple would fill. And the fate of the army—and perhaps the Republic—would hang in the balance.

The second address had done its work. It had fixed the time and place, and it had forced Washington’s hand. The commander-in-chief could not ignore a summons to the Temple of Virtue. He could not allow an anonymous address to set the agenda for his officers. He had to act, and he had to act publicly. The conspirators had engineered a confrontation, and Washington had accepted it. The stage was set. The players were in position. And the Temple of Virtue, that simple wooden hall, stood ready to receive them all.

The summons had been issued. The officers were called to the Temple of Virtue on March 15. The stage was set for Washington’s final, decisive intervention.
