Chapter 17

The Silence of Horatio Gates

The inventory ledger from Traveller’s Rest listed the usual concerns of a Virginia planter returning to his land. Horses, cattle, tools, boundary markers. Gates had arrived in late 1783 with his military career effectively concluded and his wife Elizabeth dead that summer, leaving him to manage the estate alone. The pages showed purchases of farming equipment, notes on crop yields, records of transactions with neighbors. What they did not show was any mention of Newburgh, or of the anonymous addresses that had circulated through the army camp nine months earlier. The general who had presided over the most dangerous meeting of the war now recorded the price of seed corn.

Gates had been the senior officer present at the Temple of Virtue on March 15, 1783. When Washington entered uninvited and took the floor, Gates yielded his place at the head of the room. He watched the commander in chief read his letter, put on his spectacles, and turn the officers’ anger into acquiescence. Gates said nothing. His aide, Major John Armstrong Jr., was generally acknowledged as the author of the two anonymous addresses that had summoned the officers to that meeting. The addresses called the army to assert itself against Congress, a military challenge to civilian authority that might have ended in open mutiny. Armstrong, serving as Gates’s aide-de-camp, had set in motion the crisis Washington defused.

The ledger at Traveller’s Rest recorded none of this. Gates turned to the concerns of his plantation. He had been away from Virginia for years, first winning the Battle of Saratoga and then suffering the defeat at Camden, serving as Washington’s rival for influence within the officer corps. Now he planted and harvested. The silence of his papers matched the silence of his conduct.

Historians have long noted the absence of direct evidence about Gates’s role in the Newburgh affair. The historical record leaves uncertain how much the principal messengers—Colonels Brooks and Stewart—knew of the addresses’ origins. The intent of the nationalist politicians in Philadelphia remains a matter of inference rather than documentation. Gates’s own knowledge of Armstrong’s authorship before the fact has never been established with certainty. But the absence of evidence is itself a form of evidence. Gates never denied knowledge. He never explained his presence at the meeting. He never discussed the addresses in his correspondence or his later writings. He simply withdrew.

This withdrawal was not the retreat of a man with nothing to hide. It was the strategy of a man who understood that the best response to suspicion was silence. Gates had survived the political battlefields of the Continental Army long enough to know that accusations required proof, and that proof required witnesses willing to speak. No witnesses came forward. Armstrong himself never publicly acknowledged his authorship during Gates’s lifetime. The conspiracy, such as it was, dissolved into rumor and recrimination without ever reaching a formal inquiry.

Gates returned to Virginia and to Traveller’s Rest. He took a position as vice president of the Society of the Cincinnati and led its Virginia chapter. He accepted minor public service roles, including a brief position with the Virginia militia. He corresponded with former comrades. He managed his property. He lived as if nothing had happened at Newburgh beyond what everyone had seen: a general yielding the floor to his commander in chief when Washington arrived uninvited.

The contrast between Gates’s public silence and the persistent private whispers reveals the mechanics of reputation after a failed conspiracy. Those who had encouraged the crisis from Philadelphia—the nationalist politicians and financiers who had hoped to use the army’s grievance to force a federal taxing power—had no interest in public investigation. A public inquiry would have exposed their own correspondence, their own encouragement of military pressure on Congress. Washington himself had no interest in a public reckoning. He had turned the room at the Temple of Virtue. He had preserved the principle of civilian control. To pursue Gates or Armstrong would have required reopening wounds that had barely closed.

So Gates remained silent. His silence served everyone who had something to lose from a full accounting. The nationalists in Congress who had corresponded with army officers about using the pay crisis to force funding. The financiers like Robert Morris who had seen in the army’s desperation an opportunity to advance their own fiscal plans. The officers who had attended the meeting at the Temple of Virtue, many of whom had been willing to hear what the anonymous addresses proposed before Washington arrived. Washington himself, who had defused the crisis but had not resolved the underlying grievances that had made it possible. All of them benefited from Gates’s refusal to speak.

The inventory at Traveller’s Rest thus becomes a kind of negative evidence. Gates listed his possessions, his land, his crops. He did not record his role in the most significant crisis of the army’s final year. He did not note his conversations with Armstrong, if any, about the addresses. He did not write down his thoughts as he sat in the Temple of Virtue and watched the room turn. The absence of these entries is itself a record, a documentation of what Gates chose not to document.

In the years following 1783, published histories began to appear that alluded to the Newburgh affair. Some mentioned Gates by name, noting his presence at the meeting and his connection to Armstrong. Others alluded more vaguely to “certain officers” who had promoted disaffection. The whispers persisted. But whispers are not evidence, and Gates understood this. He let them whisper. He let the historians speculate. He let the former comrades who had been in the room that day wonder whether he had known what Armstrong was doing, whether he had approved, whether he had been waiting to see which way the wind would blow before committing himself.

He never gave them an answer. The silence was the answer.

Armstrong, meanwhile, went on to a public career that would have been impossible if the Newburgh addresses had been widely attributed to him at the time. He became a favorite of the Constitution. He served in the United States Congress. He became minister to France. He wrote and published, a man of letters as well as a man of affairs. The author of the anonymous addresses that had summoned officers to consider defying Congress moved through the early republic with his reputation intact. This too was a kind of evidence. A successful political career requires the acquiescence of those who know your past. Armstrong’s career suggests that many of those who knew what he had written chose not to speak of it.

Gates’s correspondence in these years reveals a man carefully managing his relationships. He wrote to Washington occasionally, with the deference appropriate to a former subordinate addressing his commander in chief. He wrote to other former officers. The letters are cordial, even warm at times. They discuss land, crops, mutual acquaintances, the small concerns of men who have shared a great enterprise now concluded. What they do not discuss is Newburgh. The word appears nowhere in Gates’s surviving correspondence from this period. The event that had brought the army to the edge of mutiny, that had required Washington’s personal intervention to defuse, that had revealed the depth of the officers’ desperation and the ambitions of nationalist politicians, went unmentioned.

This systematic avoidance cannot be accidental. Gates was not a man who forgot. For years, he had kept the army’s internal politics in his mind, calculating his position relative to Washington, relative to Congress, relative to the various factions within the officer corps. He had written thousands of letters during the war, many of them concerned with matters of rank, precedence, and reputation. He had defended himself against accusations of incompetence after Camden. He had lobbied for promotion and recognition. A man who writes so much about his own interests does not simply forget to write about the most significant event of his final year in service.

The conclusion is inescapable. Gates did not write about Newburgh because he chose not to write about Newburgh. He chose silence because silence was safer than explanation. Any explanation would have required either denying knowledge of Armstrong’s addresses, which no one would believe, or admitting knowledge, which would have implicated him in what looked very much like an attempted mutiny. Silence left room for doubt. Silence allowed his former comrades to believe what they preferred to believe: that Gates had been surprised by the addresses, that he had presided over the meeting only because the anonymous summons had named him, that he had yielded to Washington because he recognized the commander in chief’s authority. Silence allowed everyone to move on.

The containment of the Newburgh conspiracy’s political fallout thus depended in part on Gates’s willingness to contain himself. Armstrong’s addresses had reached the officers through his quarters. The anonymous summons had named him as the senior officer present. He had sat at the head of the room until Washington arrived. His presence had given the meeting a legitimacy it would otherwise have lacked. And then he had stepped aside and let Washington take control. He had not defended the addresses. He had not argued for the officers’ grievances. He had not opposed Washington’s intervention. He had simply watched, and then he had voted with the majority to reject the anonymous addresses and pledge continued patience with Congress.

This was not the behavior of a man who had engineered a conspiracy. It was the behavior of a man who had allowed himself to be used by others—perhaps by Armstrong, perhaps by the nationalist politicians in Philadelphia who had encouraged military pressure—and who had then recognized that the moment for action had passed. Gates was not a true believer in the cause of the addresses. He was a calculator, and his calculation at Newburgh had been that the crisis would either succeed without his open support or fail without his open opposition. Either way, he would survive.

Survival was Gates’s primary skill. The disastrous expedition to Canada in 1776 had ended with him attributing the failure to others. The victory at Saratoga had seen him claiming credit that rightly belonged to Benedict Arnold and Daniel Morgan. The defeat at Camden had prompted him to deflect blame onto his subordinates and the militia that had fled. Years of rivalry with Washington had never seen him openly challenging the commander in chief but never fully committing to him either. Now he would survive Newburgh by simply not talking about it.

The strategy worked. Gates lived another three years after returning to Traveller’s Rest. He died in 1786, taking whatever he knew about Newburgh with him. Armstrong outlived him by decades, dying in 1843, but never publicly acknowledged his authorship of the addresses during Gates’s lifetime. The conspiracy remained a matter of rumor and speculation, documented in private correspondence and alluded to in published histories, but never subjected to formal investigation. The army’s grievances remained unresolved. The officers went home unpaid. The certificates that represented their promised wages traded at deep discounts, enriching speculators while the men who had won independence struggled to farm their land or practice their trades.

Gates’s silence thus accomplished what Washington’s intervention had not. Washington had turned the room at the Temple of Virtue, but he had not resolved the underlying crisis of pay and funding. The nationalist politicians who had hoped to use that crisis to force a federal taxing power had failed in their immediate objective, but they would succeed within a few years when the Constitution created the very system they had sought. Gates’s silence contributed to this outcome by preventing the kind of public reckoning that might have exposed the manipulation of the army’s grievances for political ends. If Gates had spoken, if he had named Armstrong, if he had explained who had encouraged the addresses or what he himself had known, the result might have been a scandal that discredited the nationalist cause along with its military proxies. Instead, Gates said nothing, and the scandal remained contained.

The inventory at Traveller’s Rest thus documents more than a planter’s possessions. It documents a choice. Gates chose to record his cattle and his tools. He chose not to record his role in the most significant event of his final year in public life. He chose silence over explanation, withdrawal over engagement, survival over vindication. The ledger shows what he valued enough to write down. It also shows, by its omissions, what he valued enough to leave unwritten.

The army that had nearly mutinied dispersed into the countryside. The officers who had gathered at the Temple of Virtue returned to their farms and their trades. Washington went home to Mount Vernon, expecting to live out his days as a private citizen. The nationalist politicians continued their efforts to create a stronger federal government, efforts that would succeed within a few years. And Gates sat at his desk at Traveller’s Rest, entering figures into his ledger, recording what he owed and what he was owed, never writing down what had happened in the army camp at Newburgh.

The ledger from Traveller’s Rest survives as a document of its time. It shows a planter managing his affairs, recording his transactions, accounting for his property. It does not show a general accounting for his actions in the most significant crisis of the war’s final year. That absence is itself an accounting. Gates counted his cattle and his tools. He did not count the cost of his silence, or of the conspiracy he may have countenanced, or of the crisis he had presided over without intervening until Washington arrived. The cost was paid by others—by the officers who went home unpaid, by the republic that struggled to govern itself without adequate revenue, by the politicians who manipulated grievances they could not resolve.

Gates’s silence was his final service to the cause of containment. He contained the scandal within himself, and he took it with him to the grave. The whispers persisted, but whispers cannot impeach a reputation that no one is willing to defend. Gates offered no defense, because he offered nothing at all. He simply went home and tended to his land. The general who had once commanded the northern army and rivaled Washington for influence now competed only with the weather and the soil. It was a smaller arena, but a safer one. In the smaller arena, no one asked him about Newburgh.

The years passed. Gates served his term with the Society of the Cincinnati. He managed his plantation. He received visitors and corresponded with old friends. The new republic took shape around him, with a Constitution that created the federal taxing power the nationalists had sought, with a government strong enough to raise revenue and pay its debts. The army’s grievances were eventually addressed, though many of the officers had sold their certificates at a fraction of face value to speculators who profited from their desperation. The conspiracy that had nearly brought the army to mutiny became a footnote to history, documented in private letters and remembered in published accounts that named no names with certainty.

Gates died in 1786. His silence died with him. Armstrong lived on, but he too remained publicly silent about his role. The addresses remained anonymous in the official record, their author known to historians through private correspondence rather than public acknowledgment. The meeting at the Temple of Virtue entered the historical narrative as Washington’s moment—the moment when the commander in chief put on his spectacles and reminded his officers of his own sacrifices, turning their anger into acquiescence. Gates’s role in that meeting, his presence at the head of the room when the addresses had called the officers to assemble, his connection to the author of those addresses, faded into the background.

The ledger at Traveller’s Rest thus stands as a monument to what is not recorded. Gates wrote down his possessions and his transactions. He did not write down his thoughts or his actions during the Newburgh crisis. The document that survives is the document he created, and the document he created tells only the story he chose to tell. The rest is silence.

But silence speaks. Gates’s refusal to discuss Newburgh, his systematic avoidance of the subject in his correspondence, his withdrawal from public life into the management of his estate—all of these choices constitute a kind of statement. They say that Gates had something to hide, or at least that he believed he had something to hide. They say that the conspiracy, whatever its true nature, left a residue of guilt or fear that Gates was unwilling to confront. They say that the containment of the crisis required not just Washington’s intervention but Gates’s complicity in moving past what had happened without examining it too closely.

The nationalist lever had been pulled, but the army’s pay remained unfunded, and Horatio Gates had returned to Virginia in silence. The conspiracy had failed to achieve its immediate objective, but it had revealed the fault lines in the new republic—the tension between military sacrifice and civilian governance, between the promises made to soldiers and the fiscal constraints of a government without adequate power. Gates’s silence was one more piece of that revelation. It showed how the republic managed its crises, not by resolving them but by containing them, not by confronting the truth but by allowing the truth to remain unspoken.

Gates managed his reputation through silence. He managed his estate through documentation. The two strategies were complementary. By recording his mundane affairs, he created a paper trail that showed him as a planter, a citizen, a man of property and standing. By not recording his role in the Newburgh affair, he avoided creating a paper trail that could be used against him. The ledger was his defense, not because it defended him, but because it showed him doing something other than what he might have been doing if he had been involved in a conspiracy. The absence of evidence became his evidence of absence.

This strategy worked because the new republic had no mechanism for investigating the past. There was no standing committee of inquiry, no independent counsel, no process for subpoenaing witnesses and demanding testimony under oath. Congress could investigate, but Congress had its own reasons to avoid investigation, since nationalist politicians had encouraged the army’s pressure. Washington could investigate, but Washington had resolved the crisis and had no interest in reopening it. The courts had no jurisdiction over political matters. Gates’s silence was safe because there was no one with both the authority and the incentive to break it.

So Gates remained silent, and his silence became part of the historical record, a negative space that shapes our understanding of what happened at Newburgh. We know that Armstrong wrote the addresses. We know that Gates was present at the meeting the addresses called. We know that Gates yielded to Washington when Washington arrived. We know that Gates never spoke publicly about any of it. We know that Armstrong went on to a successful political career. We know that the nationalist politicians who had encouraged military pressure achieved their goals within a few years through constitutional means. We know all of this, but we do not know what Gates knew, or when he knew it, or what he might have done if Washington had not arrived at the Temple of Virtue.

The Newburgh affair, if fully investigated, might have destroyed reputations and ended careers. Gates’s reputation had already survived multiple crises, from the Canadian expedition to Camden. He had used patronage and politics to protect himself, cultivating allies and deflecting blame. But Newburgh was different. Newburgh looked like mutiny, and mutiny was a charge that could not be deflected through patronage. The only defense against such a charge was silence, and Gates deployed that defense with the same calculation he had brought to every other crisis of his career.

The calculation was correct. Gates died in 1786, respected enough to be mourned, forgotten enough to be safe. His silence had protected him. It had also protected the others who might have been implicated in the Newburgh affair—the politicians and financiers who had seen the army’s desperation as an opportunity. The conspiracy’s containment was thus a collective achievement, requiring not just Washington’s intervention but Gates’s complicity, not just the officers’ acquiescence but the politicians’ discretion. Everyone who knew what had happened chose not to speak of it, and the crisis passed into history as a moment of Washington’s leadership rather than a moment of nationalist manipulation.

The ledger at Traveller’s Rest thus documents the aftermath of the Newburgh conspiracy in a particular way. It shows Gates turning away from public affairs, away from the army, away from the political battles of the war years. It shows him becoming a planter, a citizen, a man of property. It shows him doing what many officers did after the war—going home and trying to rebuild lives interrupted by years of service. But it also shows, by its omissions, the cost of that return to private life. Gates did not write about Newburgh because writing about Newburgh would have required him to confront what he had done or what he had failed to do. He chose instead to write about cattle and tools and crops, and in choosing, he revealed what he valued and what he feared.

As Gates managed his reputation in silence, the physical artifact of Washington’s intervention—the spectacles—began its journey toward becoming a national relic.
