Chapter 25

The Last Garrison at Newburgh

The grave remains, and the questions remain, and the silence that Gates maintained for so long has become, in the end, his most lasting monument. But in May 1783, that silence lay years ahead. Gates still walked the Newburgh encampment in his major general’s uniform, still drew rations from the commissary, still waited with several thousand other men for an ending that would not quite arrive. He had rejoined Washington’s staff at Newburgh after his New England supporters in Congress repealed the call for an inquiry into his conduct at Camden, but rumors now implicated some of his aides in the Newburgh Conspiracy.

The Temple of Virtue was coming apart. Workmen with crowbars and hammers had begun unmaking the forty-by-seventy-foot building where, two months earlier, Washington had entered uninvited and turned the room. The New Building, as the army called it, had been erected as a barn-like structure for public assemblies, religious services, and courts-martial. Now the boards forming its walls were being pulled away, sorted, and stacked for transport. The nails were saved. Useful timber would be sold or repurposed. What had been a site of high drama was becoming a logistical problem, a quantity of lumber to be accounted for and disposed of. The empty space where officers had stood, where Gates had called the meeting to order, where Washington had put on his spectacles and read a letter, was reverting to ordinary ground.

This was the work of May. The political settlement had been reached in Philadelphia. Congress had approved commutation of half-pay pensions into five years of full pay. The officers had voted to express their “unshaken confidence” in Congress and their “affectionate attachment” to Washington. The crisis was over. But the army remained.

The Continental Army in spring 1783 existed as an army in name more than in fact. The peace treaty had been signed in Paris. British forces were preparing to evacuate New York. The war was finished. Yet the army had not been paid—not in any meaningful sense—and promises made in Philadelphia were promises of future payment, not present coin. Officers and soldiers who had accepted those promises now faced the mundane reality of disbanding a force that remained essentially unpaid. They would receive paper certificates attesting to what they were owed. They would carry those certificates home. In years to come, many would sell them for a fraction of face value to speculators who understood what veterans did not: that paper, in a republic without a taxing power, was a gamble on a future that might never arrive.

Administrative machinery ground forward. Furloughs were issued in batches. Men were sent home, told to await final settlement, told their country would remember their service. Certificates they received were printed forms with blank spaces for names, dates, and amounts. They were receipts for debt, not payment. They acknowledged that the United States owed money to the bearer and promised payment when Congress found the means. The means did not exist. States had not granted Congress power to raise revenue through taxation. The impost—the duty on imports Robert Morris had pleaded for—had been blocked by Rhode Island. Financial architecture of the republic remained incomplete.

Officers wrote letters. They kept diaries. They recorded what was happening in language mixing resignation with bitterness, gratitude with grievance. Most were not men given to dramatic gestures. They had chosen patience in March. They had trusted Washington and trusted Congress. Now they watched that trust converted into paper.

Colonel Henry Knox, who had stood with Washington at the meeting and helped draft the response to the anonymous addresses, observed dissolution with particular clarity. Knox understood the financial question better than most officers. He had been a bookseller before the war, a man of business as well as arms. He saw what was happening. Certificates being issued to soldiers were already trading at discount. Men who needed money now—who needed seed for planting, who needed to pay debts accumulated during years of service, who needed to feed families they had barely seen—were selling paper for whatever it would bring. The market was efficient and pitiless. A certificate promising one hundred dollars at some future date might fetch forty in hard money now, or thirty, or twenty. Buyers bet Congress would eventually pay. Sellers bet they could not afford to wait.

Knox himself would emerge from the war with substantial claims against the government. He had spent his own money equipping artillery at the start of the conflict. He had advanced funds to his men when the army could not. He kept careful records. He expected repayment. But he also understood that expectation and repayment were different things, and the gap between them was where lives were reshaped.

Logistical chaos of the final weeks defied easy description. The army had to be reduced to a skeleton force, sufficient to man frontier forts and protect against Native American attacks, while regiments were furloughed or discharged. Meanwhile, each state had its own army or militia, and eleven maintained navies. Wartime promises of bounties and land grants for service were not being met. The Continental Army was dissolving into a collection of state obligations, and states were not paying either.

Washington remained at headquarters, managing details of this unwinding. His correspondence in these weeks was voluminous. To Congress he wrote about disposition of stores, sale of surplus horses, fate of military hospitals. To state governors he wrote about militia forces that would replace Continental regiments. To his own officers he wrote about timing of departures. He was present, as he had always been present, but the nature of his presence had shifted. The crisis of March had required a performance. Dissolution of May required administration. Washington was good at administration. He had managed army logistics for eight years. But this was different. This was managing an ending.

The Temple of Virtue was not the only structure coming down. The entire Newburgh encampment was being emptied. Log huts that had sheltered troops through final winters, hospitals where the sick had lain, parade grounds where regiments had drilled—all were being dismantled or abandoned. The army had occupied Hudson Highlands for years. Now it was leaving, and what remained was a landscape of empty spaces and half-built structures, a place where something had happened and was now finished.

Departure of regiments followed a rhythm both orderly and melancholy. Men formed up for the last time. They received furlough papers and certificates. They listened to final words from officers, words trying to give meaning to what they had done and what they were now asked to accept. Then they marched away, in smaller groups, heading toward home states, carrying paper promises.

Some officers remained longer than their men. Responsibilities could not be immediately transferred. Accounts needed settling, equipment required inventory, reports demanded filing. They were ones who watched camp empty, who saw Newburgh’s population shrink day by day, who found themselves among a dwindling group of comrades in a place that had once held thousands.

Horatio Gates lingered. The major general who had presided over the March meeting, who had been named in anonymous addresses as the man officers should look to, had survived crisis with commission intact but reputation damaged. Washington had not dismissed him. Congress had not censured him. But everyone knew what had happened, or what was believed to have happened. Gates had been figurehead of a movement that might have become mutiny. His aide, Major John Armstrong, had written the addresses. Gates had not stopped them. He had not exposed them. He had called meeting to order. He had sat in the chair while Washington entered uninvited and took the floor.

Now Gates watched the army dissolve. His wife Elizabeth was ill. She would die in summer 1783, before year’s end. Gates himself would retire to his Virginia plantation, to Traveller’s Rest, where he would live another twenty-three years. But in May and June, he remained at Newburgh, still in uniform, still present for the ending.

Correspondence from these weeks tells a story of frustrated expectation. Officers wrote each other about certificates received. They compared amounts. They calculated what paper might be worth if sold, what it might be worth if held. Doubt found expression. Anger found expression. A weary resignation emerged that was perhaps more damaging than anger would have been.

One officer wrote of receiving his certificate as a piece of paper promising what he should never see. Another recorded that he had served his country for eight years and now went home with nothing but promises. A third observed that Congress had given them paper and kept whatever money existed. These were not men plotting rebellion. They were men accepting what they had been given and recording, in private letters not meant for publication, what that giving felt like.

Certificates themselves were objects of a particular kind. Printed on heavy paper, with formal language and blank spaces filled by clerks, they bore signatures of officers authorized to attest to debt. They were, in one sense, physical embodiment of the republic’s gratitude. They were also, in another sense, evidence of the republic’s failure. A certificate promising payment was also a certificate acknowledging non-payment. Every piece of paper carried within it the absence of what it promised.

Market for these certificates developed quickly. Speculators appeared at camps and in towns where veterans passed through. They offered hard money—coin, the real thing—at discounts that seemed insulting but many veterans found impossible to refuse. A man who had not been paid in years, who had a farm to return to or a business to restart or a family to support, might have no choice but to take what was offered. Speculators knew this. They were not villains, exactly. They were businessmen betting on the future of American credit. Effect on veterans was the same regardless of motive.

The army that had won independence was being paid off with paper, and paper was being bought up by men who had not fought, and men who had fought were going home with less than they had been promised. This was reality of May and June 1783. Political crisis had been averted. Financial crisis had not. Constitutional crisis—absence of federal taxing power—remained unresolved. Officers had been managed, their anger channeled into petitions and patience. But underlying grievance had not been addressed. It had been postponed.

Washington understood this. His letters from these weeks show a man trying to manage an ending while knowing ending was incomplete. To Congress he urged that certificates be made as secure as possible, that promises be kept, that veterans not be forgotten. He knew reputation of the new republic depended on how it treated those who had fought. He also knew his own reputation, and reputation of the government he would later lead, depended on the same thing. But Congress could not pay what Congress did not have. A taxing power could not be created by fiat. He could only manage dissolution as honorably as possible and hope future would be more generous than present.

Dismantling of Temple of Virtue continued. Building that had held the March meeting, that had witnessed Washington’s intervention, that had been stage for turning of conspiracy, was reduced to a pile of lumber. Workmen taking it apart may not have known what had happened there. They performed a task, followed orders, earned pay. Significance of site was not their concern. Building was coming down because army was leaving, and army did not need large assembly hall in a place where there would soon be no army to assemble.

By June, Newburgh encampment was shadow of what it had been. Regiments that had filled hills with huts and parades were gone. Officers who had gathered in March to consider anonymous addresses had scattered to homes. Washington himself would soon depart, first to meet Congress in Princeton, then to oversee evacuation of British from New York, then to resign his commission and return to Mount Vernon. Continental Army would cease to exist as significant force. A small garrison would remain, but army that had fought war was finished.

Dissolution was, in its way, as significant as crisis that preceded it. Newburgh Conspiracy had been moment of danger, point at which army might have turned against civilian government. Danger had passed. Washington had turned the room. Officers had voted for patience. Congress had made promises. But ending was not resolution. It was dispersion. Army did not disband in triumph. It disbanded in uncertainty, with paper instead of pay, with promises instead of performance.

Cost of this dispersion would be paid over following years. Veterans who carried certificates home would discover paper worth fraction of face value. Those who held on would wait decades for payment. Those who sold would receive pennies on dollar. Speculators who bought certificates would profit when, years later, Alexander Hamilton’s funding acts finally redeemed debt at full value. Men who had fought would not be ones who benefited from financial settlement they had helped force.

This was hollow core of Newburgh resolution. Conspiracy had been defused, but grievances that fueled it had not been addressed. Officers had been managed, but they had not been made whole. Political crisis had passed, but financial crisis persisted. Constitutional crisis—absence of federal taxing power—would not be resolved until 1787, and even then, resolution would benefit creditors more than soldiers.

Last regiments left Hudson Highlands in June. They marched south, toward New York, toward states from which they had come. Some would stop in city when British finally evacuated in November. Some would witness triumphant entry of American forces into city they had fought to liberate. But most would simply go home, carrying paper, carrying memories, carrying sense of what they had done and what they had not received.

Washington watched them go. Commander who had held army together through eight years of war, who had faced down conspiracy with few words and pair of spectacles, now faced quieter challenge of managing an ending. He was good at this. He had always been good at managing endings. He had managed retreat from Long Island, withdrawal from New York, survival of army through defeat after defeat. Now he managed disappearance of army into population from which it had come.

But disappearance was not complete. Veterans carried something with them. They carried memory of what they had done, memory of what they had been promised, memory of what they had received. They carried sense of grievance that would shape lives and, in some cases, shape politics of new republic. Some would become involved in Shays’ Rebellion in Massachusetts, uprising of debt-ridden farmers that some historians have seen as continuation of Newburgh spirit. Some would join Society of the Cincinnati, organization of former officers that some saw as potential hereditary aristocracy. Some would simply go home and try to rebuild lives interrupted by years of service.

Temple of Virtue was gone. Newburgh encampment was empty. Continental Army had dissolved. But paper remained. Certificates remained. Promises remained, unkept. And in hearts and minds of men who had accepted those promises, something else remained: sense that their country had not quite finished what it had started.

The veteran with his worthless certificate, carrying a sense of betrayal into the postwar republic.
