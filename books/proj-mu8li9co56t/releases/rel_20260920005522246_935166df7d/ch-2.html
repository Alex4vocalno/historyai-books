Chapter 2

The Forged Pedigree

The confident loops of administrative authority could be transferred from one sheet of paper to another. Artur Virgílio Alves dos Reis had practiced them until his hand moved without hesitation, until the shapes felt natural rather than copied. Now, in the winter of 1922, he was ready to deploy what he had learned.

He carried the diploma in a leather case purchased for the purpose, its interior lined with silk the color of old ivory. The document itself was printed on heavy parchment, its surface slightly irregular to the touch, with a watermark visible when held to the light. The seal of Oxford University appeared in crimson wax, affixed with a ribbon of navy silk. The Latin inscription named him Arturus Virgilius Alves dos Reis, awarded the degree of Bachelor of Engineering on the seventeenth day of June, 1919. The signatures of the Vice-Chancellor and the Public Orator followed, executed in a flowing hand that matched, to Reis’s satisfaction, the specimens he had obtained from university publications.

He presented it at a meeting in the offices of a Lisbon construction firm, sliding the case across a mahogany desk to a man whose name does not survive in the records but whose function is clear: he was a gatekeeper, someone who decided which contracts went to which engineers. The man opened the case, lifted the parchment by its edges, examined the seal against the window light. Reis watched without speaking. He had learned that explanation invited doubt, while silence suggested confidence earned elsewhere. The man returned the diploma to its case and asked about Reis’s experience in Angola. Reis described roads surveyed, harbors improved, administrative buildings constructed from plans he had never seen. The meeting ended with an agreement to collaborate on a municipal project in the northern suburbs.

The mechanism he had built was not a single deception but a system of credentials that generated further credentials, each layer of apparent legitimacy making the next more plausible. The Oxford diploma was not merely a false document. It was a prototype, a proof of concept that would guide everything that followed.

To understand its construction, one must return to the circumstances that made it necessary. Reis had returned from Angola in 1916 with nothing but the skills he had acquired there: the ability to move between official languages, to complete forms in ways that satisfied multiple audiences, to recognize the gap between what documents claimed and what they actually certified. His father was a financially troubled undertaker who ended up being declared insolvent.

Reis himself had abandoned his engineering studies after a single year to marry Maria Luísa Jacobetti de Azevedo, sister of Octávio Jacobetti de Azevedo and daughter of a family whose connections would prove more useful than any classroom instruction. The marriage gave him entry into Lisbon’s commercial networks, but entry was not position. Without a degree, he could not sign plans, could not certify structures, could not command the fees that made engineering a profession rather than a trade.

He considered his options. Portugal’s universities maintained careful records, and the verification of credentials was, even then, a routine procedure. But England was different. Oxford and Cambridge operated with a decentralized authority that Reis, from his observation of colonial administration, recognized as vulnerable to careful exploitation. The colleges issued their own documents. The university’s central registry was less accessible to foreign inquiry. And the prestige of an English degree, in the Lisbon of the early 1920s, carried a weight that discouraged close examination.

The forgery required three elements: the physical document, the supporting correspondence, and the social performance that would prevent anyone from looking too closely. Reis began with the physical. He obtained blank parchment from a supplier in Porto, selecting a stock that matched the weight and color of academic diplomas he had examined in the national library. The watermark presented a greater difficulty. He solved it by purchasing a quantity of genuine Oxford examination booklets from a London stationer, extracting the sheets that bore the university’s device and pressing them flat for reuse. The printing he arranged through a commercial press in Lisbon, providing the text in a composition he had prepared from published degree forms. The typeface was not exact—he lacked the original matrices—but the difference was visible only to someone who possessed an authentic diploma for comparison, and such people had no reason to examine his document.

The seals required more ingenuity. He modeled the university’s arms from photographs in The Oxford University Calendar, carving the design in reverse on a block of fine-grained wood. The crimson wax he compounded himself, testing formulations until he achieved the correct brittleness and color. The ribbon he purchased from the same London supplier that provisioned the examination booklets, selecting a shade that appeared in contemporary photographs of academic ceremonies.

The supporting correspondence was simpler. He drafted letters of reference from imaginary Oxford tutors, printing them on letterhead he had manufactured to match the university’s stationery. These he placed in a file that he carried to meetings, offering them only when specifically requested and then with apparent reluctance, as documents that had been provided as favors rather than requirements. The performance of reluctance was crucial. Eagerness to display credentials suggested their deficiency; the appearance of having better things to do than prove one’s qualifications suggested their abundance.

By 1922, the construction was complete. Reis had become, in the registers of Lisbon’s professional associations, Engenheiro Artur dos Reis. The title opened doors that had been closed to the colonial clerk. It admitted him to meetings where contracts were distributed, to social occasions where credit was arranged, to the confidence of men who would have dismissed his actual experience as insufficient but who accepted his apparent education as definitive.

The first test came in the construction sector, where the diploma secured him consulting work on projects he supervised in name only, collecting fees for signatures that appeared on drawings prepared by others. The arrangement was common enough to attract no attention: many established engineers operated similarly, lending their credentials to enterprises they did not personally direct. Reis distinguished himself by the thoroughness of his documentation, the promptness of his responses to official inquiries, and the apparent solidity of his professional standing. He paid his debts, maintained an office with a secretary, subscribed to the appropriate journals. The performance of respectability, diligently executed, became indistinguishable from respectability itself.

From construction he moved into finance, where the mechanisms of credibility operated with even greater force. In 1923 he became involved with a syndicate attempting to develop agricultural properties in the Portuguese colonies, using his engineering title to certify the feasibility of irrigation projects he had never inspected. The certificates were accepted by banks in Lisbon and London, which advanced funds against the expected productivity of lands that existed largely on paper. When the projects failed—as they were designed to do, having been constructed to absorb capital rather than generate returns—Reis was not among those pursued for liability. His role had been advisory, his compensation recorded as fees rather than profits, his involvement documented in ways that protected him while exposing others.

The pattern repeated. Each venture that failed left him with contacts, with knowledge of how financial institutions evaluated risk, with understanding of the points where their procedures could be satisfied without their interests being protected. Each success, however partial, confirmed the utility of his manufactured identity. The Oxford diploma had not merely admitted him to professional standing. It had demonstrated that institutional authority could be appropriated through the replication of its forms.

The lesson would guide his larger ambitions. Reis had observed that the modern economy operated through documents—contracts, certifications, authorizations—that moved through channels whose operators examined their form rather than their substance. A document that bore the correct signatures, used the correct language, arrived through the correct procedures, would be processed as genuine regardless of its actual validity. The system was designed for efficiency, not verification. It assumed that the cost of manufacturing convincing falsehoods exceeded the value of the frauds they might enable. Reis, operating with limited capital but unlimited patience, had discovered that this assumption was incorrect.

By 1924, he had refined his understanding of what might be called procedural hypnosis: the condition in which an institution’s own meticulous attention to formal requirements prevents it from noticing that the documents passing through its hands bear no legitimate connection to the facts they purport to establish. He had seen clerks approve loans on the strength of valuations he had prepared himself, the documents correct in every particular except the underlying reality they described. He had watched notaries authenticate signatures without inquiring into the authority of the signatories to bind the entities they claimed to represent. He had learned that the most powerful credentials were those that required no explanation, that carried their authority in their visible form rather than in any verifiable connection to the institutions they invoked.

His ambitions, however, had outgrown the scale of operations that his current position could support. The engineering practice generated respectable income but not transformative wealth. The financial ventures, while instructive, had taught him more about the limitations of small-scale fraud than about its possibilities. He needed a larger field, an institution whose authority could be leveraged at a scale commensurate with his growing understanding of how authority functioned.

The opportunity presented itself through his brother-in-law, Octávio Jacobetti de Azevedo, whose own career in colonial administration had followed a trajectory parallel to Reis’s but with more conventional foundations. Octávio had remained in Angola after Reis’s departure, rising through the administrative ranks to a position where he controlled significant aspects of the colony’s commercial regulation. The brothers-in-law maintained regular correspondence, and in these exchanges Reis discerned the outlines of a possibility that would occupy his attention for the following year.

Angola’s economy, like that of Portugal itself, operated through a restricted currency zone that limited the convertibility of escudos and created opportunities for arbitrage between official and unofficial exchange rates. The Banco Angola e Metrópole, established to finance colonial development, occupied a crucial position in this system, channeling metropolitan capital into Angolan enterprises while managing the repatriation of colonial profits. The bank’s operations were supervised by officials in Lisbon, but its day-to-day administration remained in Luanda, separated from oversight by the delays and difficulties of Atlantic communication.

Reis studied the bank’s structure with the attention he had once devoted to Oxford letterhead. He obtained its annual reports, analyzed its balance sheets, traced the relationships between its directors and the colonial administration. He identified the points where documentation moved between Angola and Portugal, where signatures were required, where procedures created intervals during which actions could be taken without immediate verification. The engineering of financial processes, he concluded, differed from the engineering of physical structures only in the materials employed. Where a bridge required steel and concrete, a financial instrument required paper and confidence. Both could be constructed by those who understood their principles.

His plan took shape gradually, modified by each new piece of information he acquired. The initial conception involved the Banco Angola e Metrópole directly: he would establish a relationship with the bank, position himself as a trusted intermediary, and eventually exploit that position to extract funds through manipulated transactions. But this approach, he recognized, would yield limited returns and expose him to personal liability. The bank’s losses would be traced to his specific interventions; his engineered credentials would not protect him from criminal prosecution.

The alternative was more ambitious and, if successful, more secure. Rather than extracting funds from an existing institution, he would create an institution that could generate its own funds. The authority to issue banknotes, he had learned, was not exclusively reserved to the Bank of Portugal. Under certain circumstances, authorized banks could issue notes against specific collateral, their circulation limited by regulatory requirements that were, in practice, verified through documentary rather than physical inspection. If he could establish a bank with such authorization, and if he could manipulate the documentary basis of that authorization, he might create a source of currency that would be accepted as genuine because it would, in fact, be genuine—printed by legitimate means on legitimate plates, indistinguishable from notes issued through proper channels.

The insight transformed his thinking: the difference between genuine and fraudulent currency was not in the notes themselves but in the authorization of their production. A note printed without authority was counterfeit, detectable through comparison with legitimate issues. A note printed with apparent authority, by established processes, on official plates, was simply money, its validity established by the very mechanisms that had produced it. The fraud lay not in the object but in the procedure that authorized its creation.

To execute this plan, Reis required three elements that his current position could not supply: access to the Bank of Portugal’s confidence, a mechanism for issuing apparently authorized documents, and a partner with sufficient standing to front the enterprise. The first two he could construct through the methods he had perfected. The third presented a more complex problem.

He found his solution in the social networks that his engineering title had opened to him. Through his professional associations he met men whose names carried weight in Lisbon’s commercial circles: landowners with colonial interests, merchants with government contracts, investors with appetites for speculative ventures. These men did not require detailed explanation of his plans. They required confidence in his competence, which his credentials supplied, and confidence in his connections, which he manufactured through the same techniques he had applied to his diploma. He mentioned meetings with officials he had never met, referenced correspondence he had never received, alluded to arrangements he had never made. The allusions, offered casually and without elaboration, suggested a network of influence that his listeners could imagine but not verify.

Among these contacts was a figure whose identity must remain partially obscured by the surviving records, a man with established standing in colonial commerce and a history of ventures that operated in the margins of regulatory tolerance. This man—let us call him the representative, for that was the function he performed—agreed to serve as the public face of the enterprise Reis was constructing. The representative provided legitimacy; Reis provided the operational design. Their arrangement was documented in agreements that existed in multiple versions, each tailored to its intended audience, the discrepancies between them creating spaces of ambiguity that Reis would later exploit.

By the autumn of 1924, the structure was substantially complete. Reis had established the appearance of a connection to the Bank of Portugal through correspondence that he had composed himself, using letterhead preserved from his ministry days and signatures practiced until they matched the authentic specimens he had obtained. He had prepared contracts that purported to authorize his representative to act on behalf of entities that existed only in the documentation he had created. He had arranged for the establishment of a new bank, the Banco Angola e Metrópole de Lisboa, whose name suggested continuity with the established colonial institution while its legal structure placed it under his effective control.

The remaining requirement was the physical production of the notes themselves. This could not be accomplished in Portugal, where the Bank of Portugal maintained exclusive relationships with established security printers. But England, Reis had learned, operated differently. The firm of Waterlow and Sons, printers of banknotes for governments and institutions throughout the world, accepted commissions from authorized representatives of foreign banks, their verification procedures designed to prevent commercial disputes rather than to detect elaborate frauds. If he could present himself, or his representative, as acting with proper authority, the technical production of genuine banknotes would follow as a matter of routine.

The approach to Waterlow required careful preparation. Reis spent the winter of 1924 studying the firm’s history, its current management, its procedures for accepting new commissions. He learned that Sir William Waterlow, the managing director, had recently completed his term as Lord Mayor of London and had been elevated to a baronetcy, honors that suggested a man susceptible to the prestige of established institutions. He prepared a portfolio of documents that would establish his representative’s standing: the contracts he had forged, the correspondence he had manufactured, the credentials he had accumulated through his years of engineered respectability.

The final element was the letter of introduction. Reis obtained this through a commercial printer in the Netherlands, the Joh. Enschedé company, whose relationship with Waterlow provided a channel that would bypass initial skepticism. The letter, composed in terms that Reis had dictated, described his representative as acting on behalf of the Bank of Portugal in a confidential matter of colonial finance, the discretion required by political circumstances preventing the normal procedures of direct communication.

He reviewed these materials in his office on a December evening, the lamp casting shadows across the documents spread across his desk. The Oxford diploma hung on the wall behind him, its seal catching the light. He had come to this point through a chain of deceptions that had grown progressively larger, each success confirming the methodology that the first forgery had established. The diploma had proven that institutional authority could be appropriated. The engineering practice had proven that professional credibility could be manufactured. The financial ventures had proven that documentary procedures could be satisfied without substantive verification. Now he was prepared to test these principles at a scale that would transform them from demonstrations into history.

The documents were complete. The arrangements were in place. The representative would travel to London in the new year, carrying credentials that would open doors which had never admitted anyone of Reis’s actual origins.

He gathered the materials into his case, each document in its appointed place, each seal and signature performing its function in the structure he had constructed. Outside, the streets of Lisbon descended toward the river, the city that had witnessed his transformation from frustrated clerk to active conspirator, from man without position to architect of an enterprise that would challenge the foundations of Portuguese finance. The case closed with a sound of finality. The mechanism was complete. What remained was to set it in motion.
