Chapter 4

The Contract from the Bank of Portugal

In the autumn of 1924, with the rains beginning to soften the limestone facades of Lisbon’s banking quarter, Artur dos Reis faced a problem that no amount of colonial experience or forged credentials could solve directly. He needed paper—not ordinary paper, but the kind that carried the implicit authority of a sovereign institution. The Bank of Portugal’s own stationery, with its watermarked crest and the particular weight that official correspondence acquires through decades of use, had become the missing component in a scheme that had occupied his imagination since his imprisonment in Porto. To execute what he had conceived, the metal plates would need to be retrieved from guarded vaults, the heavy presses prepared for production, and genuine five-hundred-escudo notes run off from the Bank of Portugal’s own engraved plates, perfect in every detail. But first, someone had to manufacture the permission.

The decisive pivot from ambition to actionable conspiracy came in a rented room somewhere in Lisbon’s commercial district, where Reis and his collaborators transformed a prison-cell conception into physical form. The scheme had begun in the cells of Porto, where Reis conceived what became known as the Portugal Bank Note Affair: a contract forged in the name of Banco de Portugal, the central bank responsible for issuing banknotes and partly private at the time, authorizing his syndicate to print currency for colonial development. Now that conception had to acquire paper, ink, and signatures.

The forger’s art depends on understanding the grammar of institutional self-regard. To counterfeit a contract from a central bank, one must grasp the visual signatures of authenticity—the watermark, the letterhead, the particular shade of ink—and something more fundamental: how bureaucracies recognize their own authority. Reis possessed neither the diplomatic connections nor the bureaucratic experience to fabricate such a document alone. What he had was access, through the network of his colonial service, to a man whose career had provided precisely the missing expertise.

António Bandeira entered the conspiracy at this juncture, his value measured not in capital but in credentials. A former Portuguese diplomat, Bandeira had served in postings that left him with two essential assets: access to official stationery accumulated through years of correspondence, and an intimate knowledge of the protocols that governed transactions between the metropole and its colonies. The diplomat’s career had followed the trajectory of Portuguese influence—Rio de Janeiro, London, the African territories—each posting depositing in his possession the material residue of bureaucratic life. Letterheads, memorandum forms, the particular blue-grey paper preferred for confidential communications: these were the raw materials of institutional mimicry.

The collaboration between Reis and Bandeira unfolded in the autumn of 1924 through meetings whose locations are not recorded but whose purpose is clear from the document that emerged. The contract they constructed was a carefully calibrated instrument, its terms designed to exploit the specific vulnerabilities of a colonial power anxious about its overseas possessions. The document purported to authorize the printing of 500-escudo notes for use specifically in Angola, with the currency to be issued through a new financial institution, the Banco Angola e Metrópole. This construction served multiple functions. It explained why the Bank of Portugal itself would not handle the distribution. It provided commercial cover for the movement of large quantities of currency. And it wrapped the entire operation in the legitimizing rhetoric of colonial development, that persistent ideology which transformed extraction into progress and private profit into national purpose.

The physical fabrication of the contract required attention to details that would escape casual observation but might arrest the scrutiny of a professional printer. Bank of Portugal stationery was not publicly available; it circulated through restricted channels, accumulating in the files of correspondents, contractors, and former officials. Bandeira’s diplomatic career had placed him within these channels. The paper itself—its weight, its watermark, the precise alignment of its printed elements—had to match specimens familiar to any London printer who would eventually receive it. Waterlow & Sons, on Threadneedle Street, had executed contracts for the Bank of Portugal before. Their familiarity with authentic documents created both opportunity and risk: they possessed the expertise to detect anomalies, but also the professional habit of recognizing and accepting legitimate authority.

The contract’s language was drafted to reassure through excess of formality. It specified quantities, dates, and protocols with the exhaustive particularity of genuine bureaucratic instruments. The notes were to carry the date of 17 November 1922, a retrospective dating that would explain their non-appearance in current Bank of Portugal records. They were to feature Vasco da Gama, the explorer whose image adorned existing Portuguese currency, ensuring visual continuity with legitimate issues. The total face value of 100 million escudos, equivalent to nearly one percent of Portugal’s nominal GDP, was distributed across 200, 000 individual notes, a volume large enough to transform regional economies but not so large as to immediately destabilize the metropolitan currency.

This attention to plausible scale reveals the conspirators’ understanding of monetary mechanics. A fraud too modest would fail to generate the capital necessary for its intended purpose: the acquisition of Angolan enterprises and, ultimately, a controlling position in the central bank itself. A fraud too ambitious would trigger immediate detection through the mechanisms of note registration and serial number tracking that the Bank of Portugal maintained. The contract’s specifications occupied a calculated middle ground, sufficient to attract serious commercial partners while remaining within the outer bounds of colonial financial operations.

The fiction of the Banco Angola e Metrópole provided essential institutional cover. By creating a plausible intermediary between the printing operation and the ultimate circulation of the notes, the conspirators constructed a narrative that could survive initial scrutiny. The bank existed on paper—incorporated, capitalized, staffed with nominal directors—while lacking the operational infrastructure that would normally accompany such an institution. This hollow structure was not a defect but a feature, designed to pass through the brief period of visibility that the fraud required before the notes could be absorbed into the colonial economy.

The document’s signatures presented the most delicate technical challenge. The Governor of the Bank of Portugal maintained a signature whose characteristics were known to financial professionals. Reis and Bandeira could not obtain a specimen directly, but official documents circulating through commercial channels provided indirect access. The signatures that appeared on the contract were therefore reconstructed approximations, derived from documents that had passed through colonial administration and through contractors who had done business with the central bank. The result was plausible rather than perfect—a distinction that mattered less than one might expect given how international financial correspondence actually worked.

For this document to function it had to be deliverable as well as convincing. The conspirators understood that Waterlow & Sons would not proceed on a single sheet however impressive its appearance; decades spent handling sovereign contracts had taught them verification through multiple channels. So they built an apparatus around it: supporting letters arriving separately to confirm authenticity; eventually personal introductions arranged so trust could accumulate step by step as it did between legitimate institutions.

The colonial context provided essential cover for anomalies that would have aroused suspicion in metropolitan transactions. Portugal’s African possessions operated under administrative conditions where documentation was often delayed, communications irregular, and the boundary between official and commercial activity necessarily porous. The contract’s specification of Angolan circulation exploited this environment, invoking a territory where Portuguese oversight was extensive in theory and intermittent in practice. The notes would enter circulation far from the institutional centers where their authenticity might be questioned, absorbed into economies where currency was scarce and the appearance of substantial new issues might be welcomed rather than suspected.

The fabrication of the contract thus represented a strategic analysis of institutional vulnerability as much as a technical achievement. Reis and Bandeira had identified a point where the formal requirements of international finance intersected with the informal practices of colonial administration, creating a zone of ambiguity that a properly constructed document could exploit. The Bank of Portugal’s own authority—its reputation, its established relationships, its position within the institutional architecture of Portuguese capitalism—became the instrument of its subversion.

The physical document, once completed, acquired a weight beyond its material substance. It was carried, presumably by Reis himself, through the commercial channels that connected Lisbon to London. The journey—by rail to Paris, by channel packet to Dover, by train to the City—transformed a local forgery into an international instrument. Each stage of transit added to its apparent legitimacy, placing it within the normal currents of diplomatic and commercial correspondence. By the time it reached Threadneedle Street, it had accumulated the invisible patina of documents that have traveled through proper channels.

The contract’s arrival in London coincided with a period of particular opportunity. Sir William Waterlow, whose family firm had executed printing contracts for sovereign clients across four generations, was actively seeking to expand his colonial business. The firm’s acquisition by De La Rue in 1961 lay far in the future; in 1924, Waterlow & Sons operated as an independent entity, proud of its reputation and protective of its relationships with central banks. Sir William had been disappointed in 1922 when the Bank of Portugal’s note printing contract went to a competitor, and he remained alert to possibilities of recovering this business. The contract that arrived from Lisbon, purporting to represent a significant colonial operation authorized by the central bank itself, addressed this commercial ambition directly.

The timing was fortuitous in another respect. Portugal’s political situation in late 1924 remained unsettled, with the military interventions that would culminate in the 1926 coup already visible in prospect. This instability, which would eventually contribute to the fraud’s exposure, initially served to obscure it. Commercial transactions that might have attracted scrutiny in periods of administrative continuity passed with less attention when government itself seemed provisional. The contract’s reference to colonial development resonated with the developmental rhetoric that successive Portuguese administrations employed to justify their African possessions, making its purposes appear politically aligned even when procedurally irregular.

The document that Reis and Bandeira had constructed was designed to succeed because of its institutional context, not despite it. The very features that made the Bank of Portugal vulnerable—its colonial responsibilities, its semi-private structure, its dependence on international printers for currency production—were incorporated into the contract’s explanatory framework. The forgery did not conceal its relationship to institutional power but weaponized it, converting the bank’s own legitimacy into an instrument of its exploitation.

The technical precision of the contract’s fabrication can be inferred from its subsequent career. Waterlow & Sons, receiving the document, did not immediately identify it as fraudulent. The firm’s procedures for authenticating foreign contracts—procedures developed through decades of experience and formalized in internal protocols—failed to detect the deception. This failure was systemic rather than merely individual, revealing the limitations of verification methods that relied on documentary appearance and established correspondence patterns rather than direct institutional confirmation. The contract had been designed to pass through these procedures, and it did.

The specific terms of the authorization—200, 000 notes of 500 escudos, dated retrospectively, designated for Angolan circulation through a specified intermediary—created a framework within which subsequent actions could be justified. Each element of the contract served to constrain and direct the behavior of those who accepted it, limiting their freedom of action while appearing to expand their commercial opportunity. The printer who accepted this document found himself bound by its specifications, unable to deviate from its terms without jeopardizing the apparent legitimacy of the entire transaction.

The contract’s existence also transformed the legal and moral position of its beneficiaries. Reis, holding this document, could present himself not as a speculator seeking currency but as an agent executing a sovereign authorization. The distinction mattered for his subsequent dealings with commercial partners, with colonial administrators, and eventually with the judicial system. The contract provided not merely the means of obtaining banknotes but the narrative framework within which their possession could be explained and defended.

The fabrication of this document thus represented the decisive investment of the conspiracy. The resources expended in its construction—Bandeira’s diplomatic access, the acquisition of appropriate materials, the careful drafting of terms—were substantial relative to the conspirators’ available capital. But they were trivial compared to the value that the contract would eventually command. A single sheet of paper, properly constructed, would unlock access to printing plates, press time, and ultimately to currency that could be exchanged for real assets across two continents.

The contract’s journey to London marked a transformation in the nature of the conspiracy. What had originated in the speculative imagination of an imprisoned colonial official, developed through conversations whose content can only be reconstructed from their results, became a material object capable of generating physical consequences. The document’s presence in Sir William Waterlow’s offices created a situation in which institutional procedures would operate to produce the very currency that the conspirators required. The forgery had succeeded in its immediate purpose: it had become operational.

Artur Virgílio Alves Reis, born in Lisbon on 8 September 1896, would eventually be recognized as the perpetrator of one of the largest frauds in history against the Bank of Portugal in 1925, the affair that would become known as the Angola e Metrópole scandal. At one point, he had duplicated one of the most secure currencies in Europe using nothing but institutional trust and the authority of a single convincing document.

The specific mechanisms by which Waterlow & Sons would be persuaded to act on this document—how the contract would be supplemented by correspondence, how personal introductions would be arranged, how the final authorization would be extracted—lay in the immediate future. The contract itself, now in transit or already arrived, had created the necessary condition for these subsequent developments. It existed in the world as a physical fact, carrying with it the accumulated authority of its forged signatures and its plausible institutional origins.

The men who had constructed this instrument remained in Lisbon, separated from their creation by the distance that would protect them from immediate association with its consequences. Their work was complete in its essential form. What remained was the management of its effects: the coordination of printing schedules, the preparation for circulation, the gradual conversion of paper currency into tangible assets. The contract had provided the blueprint for these operations; its terms would continue to shape their execution, constraining choices and creating opportunities in patterns established by its original fabrication.

The forged contract, now a physical object in dos Reis’s possession, was en route to London, creating the necessary condition for the decisive meeting.
