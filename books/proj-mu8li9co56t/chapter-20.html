Chapter 20

The Bank Sues the Printer

The envelope arrived at Waterlow & Sons’ offices in London Wall with the particular weight of official paper, the kind that announces its importance before the seal is broken. A clerk from the High Court of Justice, King’s Bench Division, had carried it through the morning traffic of 1927, delivering into the hands of a receptionist the formal writ of summons that initiated Banco de Portugal v. Waterlow & Sons. The document named damages exceeding £600, 000, a figure that translated the abstract catastrophe of fraudulent banknotes into the concrete language of sterling liability. It addressed itself solely to the printer, demanding recompense for what the Portuguese criminal trial had established but not repaired. Artur Virgílio Alves dos Reis, already serving his sentence in a Lisbon prison, found no mention here. The law had turned its attention to institutions and their obligations.

This was the mechanism by which the fraud’s second life began. The Portuguese criminal court had assigned guilt to individuals; the civil suit in London would assign cost to an institution. The distinction mattered enormously. A prison sentence measured retribution in years of liberty lost. A damages claim measured it in assets transferable, in the capacity of one corporate entity to absorb another’s catastrophic error. The Banco de Portugal had spent two years since the December 1925 exposure absorbing the fraudulent notes, redeeming them from a public that could not distinguish genuine currency from the Waterlow-printed counterfeits, stabilizing a currency whose legitimacy had been technologically compromised. That expenditure had emptied reserves and damaged confidence. The criminal verdict against Reis and his associates offered no path to recovery. The London suit was the path.

Sir William Waterlow received the news in the offices where his family had conducted business since the eighteenth century. At fifty-six, he occupied the summit of British commercial respectability. Knighted in 1919 for services to the printing industry, he had served as Alderman of the City of London and stood within reach of its highest civic office. The firm he directed held contracts with the Bank of England, with colonial governments across the Empire, with foreign states that trusted British craftsmanship to safeguard their currencies. The writ threatened this edifice not with criminal accusation—with which Waterlow might have cooperated against the actual forgers—but with civil negligence, the imputation that his own procedures had enabled the fraud.

The legal architecture of the case rested on a paradox that would occupy the courts for years. Waterlow had printed genuine banknotes from genuine plates using genuine paper. The notes were not counterfeit in the technical sense. They were unauthorized, produced in response to forged documents that appeared to emanate from the Banco de Portugal itself. The bank’s claim insisted that this distinction collapsed before the printer’s duty of care. A firm entrusted with the manufacture of national currency could not discharge its responsibility by mere technical compliance with apparent instructions. Verification through independent, official channels was required. Waterlow had accepted the representations of Karel Marang, acting for a syndicate whose credentials existed only on forged paper, and had manufactured currency that the bank had never authorized.

The defense would construct its counter-narrative around good faith. Waterlow had received what appeared to be official contracts, complete with the Banco de Portugal’s seal and the purported signatures of its governor and secretary. The documents had passed through the firm’s ordinary verification procedures. The order itself was not anomalous: Waterlow had printed Portuguese currency before, knew the specifications, had no reason to suspect that this request differed from legitimate business. The firm positioned itself as victim alongside the bank, its trust exploited by criminals of exceptional sophistication. The negligence, if any, lay in the bank’s own security arrangements, which had allowed its seals and signatures to be so convincingly reproduced.

This opposition between verification and trust, between institutional duty and reasonable reliance, would structure the litigation. Beneath the legal arguments moved a more elemental force: the translation of Portuguese catastrophe into British financial exposure. The Banco de Portugal had engaged London solicitors who understood that their client’s recovery depended on establishing that Waterlow’s error fell within a recognizable category of commercial negligence for which English law assigned liability. This required a forensic examination of the transaction that the Portuguese criminal trial had treated summarily. The discovery process would excavate every document, every meeting, every decision point in the chain that connected Marang’s initial approach to the delivery of banknotes in London docks.

The discovery records, compiled through 1928 and 1929, revealed a sequence of missed signals. Marang had first approached the Dutch firm Joh. Enschedé in 1924, presenting sample Portuguese notes and inquiring whether that firm could match their quality. Enschedé had examined the samples, identified them as Waterlow’s work, and declined the business. This refusal had sent Marang to Waterlow itself, where his approach was received without the suspicion that the Dutch firm’s rejection might have warranted. The discovery materials showed Waterlow’s staff noting the apparent authenticity of Marang’s documents, the plausibility of his explanation that the Banco de Portugal wished to establish a secondary printing arrangement for colonial circulation. No independent inquiry was made to Lisbon. The seals were examined visually, not authenticated against registered specimens. The signatures were compared to previous orders, not verified with the signatories.

From these materials, the bank’s legal team constructed a narrative of institutional failure. Waterlow was a privileged contractor, bound by relationships of trust that imposed obligations beyond ordinary commercial dealing. The firm had accepted this status in its long history of currency printing. It could not now retreat to the position of a mere vendor, liable only for defects in manufacture. The duty it had breached was rooted in the special character of currency production. A printer who manufactured banknotes without confirming that the order genuinely emanated from the issuing authority participated in the degradation of monetary confidence, however innocently.

Waterlow’s discovery responses emphasized the practical difficulties of such verification. The firm had processed thousands of orders across decades of international printing. To require independent confirmation of every document bearing apparent official authentication would paralyze the business. The seals and signatures presented by Marang had withstood the scrutiny applied to countless legitimate orders. The fraud’s success testified not to Waterlow’s negligence but to the forgers’ skill, which had deceived everyone who encountered their documents until the final unraveling in December 1925. The firm had been no more negligent than the Banco de Portugal itself, whose own officials had initially accepted the fraudulent notes as genuine.

This last point carried strategic weight. Waterlow’s defense sought to establish that the bank’s losses resulted not from the printing but from the circulation, from decisions made in Lisbon to honor and absorb the fraudulent notes. The causal chain connecting Waterlow’s manufacturing error to the bank’s financial damage was not direct but mediated by the bank’s own subsequent choices. This argument threatened to fragment liability, to distribute the fraud’s cost across multiple institutional failures rather than concentrating it on the London printer.

The parallel trajectories of the two central institutional actors—Banco de Portugal and Waterlow & Sons—illustrated how the fraud’s aftermath redistributed power and vulnerability. The Portuguese bank, despite its catastrophic exposure, had initiated the litigation from a position of sovereign necessity. Its currency had been compromised; its reserves depleted; its political masters demanding explanation and recovery. The lawsuit was the instrument of that demand, transforming internal crisis into external claim. The bank’s legal team operated with the urgency of an institution whose legitimacy remained under repair, whose capacity to govern the monetary system required visible effort to recover losses.

Waterlow, by contrast, faced the litigation as a threat to accumulated prestige rather than immediate survival. The firm remained solvent, its contracts with other governments continuing, its technical capacity undiminished. But the lawsuit exposed a vulnerability in its business model that extended far beyond the Portuguese claim. If central banks could recover losses from printers who had acted in good faith on forged documents, the entire architecture of international currency printing required revision. Every contract would demand enhanced verification procedures; every order would carry potential liability for failures of authentication that printers were not equipped to prevent. The case threatened to impose on private firms a responsibility for document verification that exceeded their competence and their commercial role.

Sir William Waterlow’s personal situation embodied this institutional tension. In 1929, even as the litigation proceeded through its preliminary stages, he was elected Lord Mayor of London, the 602nd to hold that office. The honor represented the culmination of a civic career built on commercial success and public service. It placed him at the ceremonial center of British financial governance, the physical representative of the City whose institutions his firm served. This public ascent occurred simultaneously with private crisis. The pressures of the Portuguese litigation, the internal disputes within Waterlow & Sons about responsibility and response, the accumulating legal costs and reputational damage, eroded his position within the firm he had directed. The honors accumulated externally while authority dissolved internally.

The creation of his baronetcy in October 1930 formalized this division. Sir William Waterlow, Baronet, Lord Mayor of London, stood in public as the embodiment of British commercial integrity. Within the printing house in London Wall, his influence waned as the litigation strategy demanded decisions that he resisted or could not control. The lawsuit had become a force independent of any individual will, driving toward judgments that would outlast the careers of those who had initiated it.

The trial itself opened in the High Court in 1930, before a judge whose name would become associated with one of the most complex commercial cases in English legal history. The evidence filled thousands of pages, the arguments ranged across contract law, agency principles, and the special duties of currency printers. Expert witnesses examined the technical characteristics of the fraudulent notes, the quality of the forgeries that had deceived Waterlow, the procedures that might have detected them. The court heard from Portuguese officials about the bank’s internal controls, from Waterlow managers about their verification practices, from document experts about the seals and signatures that had passed between Marang and the London firm.

The central evidentiary problem remained the forged letters that had authorized the printing. These documents bore the Banco de Portugal’s seal and the purported signatures of its governor. Waterlow had accepted them as genuine; the bank insisted they were transparently defective to proper examination. The court’s assessment of this disagreement would determine whether Waterlow’s reliance had been reasonable or negligent. But the assessment itself was complicated by the passage of time, by the destruction of some records, by the inherent difficulty of reconstructing the appearance of documents that had been designed to deceive.

The bank’s case emphasized the omissions. No telegram had been sent to Lisbon to confirm the orders. The seals had not been compared against registered specimens held by the firm from previous contracts. The anomaly of receiving authorization for substantial currency production through intermediaries rather than through established official channels had gone unchallenged. Each departure from rigorous verification represented a failure of the standard of care that the bank’s position as issuing authority demanded.

Waterlow’s response emphasized the actions taken. The documents had been examined according to established procedures. The signatures had been noted as consistent with previous specimens. The order’s specifications had matched existing knowledge of Portuguese currency requirements. The firm had applied commercial rather than forensic standards to documents that appeared authentic. To demand more was to impose on private contractors responsibilities that belonged to sovereign institutions.

The trial’s duration—months of testimony and argument—reflected the difficulty of choosing between these positions. The court was asked to find facts and to allocate responsibility across a chain of events that included criminal forgery, commercial reliance, and sovereign monetary management. The judgment would establish precedent for the relationship between central banks and their contractors, defining the boundaries of trust and verification in international currency production.

When the judge delivered his decision, it favored Waterlow on the central question of liability. The firm had acted without negligence in accepting the forged documents. The standard of care required of a commercial printer did not extend to independent verification of apparent official authorizations. Waterlow had not breached its contract or its duty. The Banco de Portugal’s claim failed.

The judgment was a victory for Waterlow in the immediate litigation, but its consequences extended beyond the parties. For the Portuguese bank, the ruling represented a second catastrophe. The criminal trial had established individual guilt without providing financial recovery. The civil trial had denied institutional recovery against the only solvent defendant capable of absorbing the loss. The millions of escudos expended in absorbing the fraudulent notes remained unrecovered, a hole in the national finances that the judgment now declared permanent.

For Waterlow, the victory proved pyrrhic. The trial had exposed the firm’s procedures to exhaustive public examination, revealing vulnerabilities that other clients might note. The cost of the litigation, measured in legal fees and management attention, compounded the reputational damage of having been deceived. Sir William Waterlow’s position within the firm continued to deteriorate; civic honors proved unable to protect his commercial authority. The firm itself would struggle in subsequent decades, its preeminence in currency printing eroded by competitors and by the enhanced scrutiny that the Portuguese case had brought to the industry.

The litigation’s discovery phase extended across twenty-six months, a duration that itself became a weapon in the contest between the parties. The Banco de Portugal’s legal team, led by solicitors who had made their reputations in imperial commercial disputes, understood that delay favored the defendant. Waterlow & Sons possessed deeper reserves of working capital and could sustain the grinding expense of document production, witness preparation, and procedural maneuvering indefinitely. The Portuguese bank, by contrast, operated under political constraints that intensified with each passing quarter. Its governor faced parliamentary committees demanding explanation for the unrecovered losses; its note-issuing authority required visible effort to repair the damage done to monetary confidence. The discovery process thus became a test of institutional endurance as much as evidentiary completeness.

The interrogatories exchanged between the parties revealed the distance separating Portuguese and English legal assumptions. The Banco de Portugal’s questions assumed a hierarchical model of commercial relationship, in which a printer of currency occupied a subordinate position to the issuing authority, bound by duties of deference and verification that transcended ordinary contract. Waterlow’s responses assumed a horizontal model of commercial dealing, in which parties of equivalent sophistication negotiated at arm’s length, each responsible for its own authentication procedures. These incompatible frameworks—one rooted in the administrative law of continental central banking, the other in the common law of English commercial practice—would require the court to choose between fact patterns and between legal cultures.

The expert testimony on printing technology occupied weeks of trial time, testimony that the judge would later describe as necessary to understanding the case but peripheral to deciding it. Forensic document examiners compared the forged authorization letters against genuine specimens from the Banco de Portugal’s archives, identifying discrepancies in seal impression depth and signature stroke characteristics that Waterlow’s staff had failed to detect. The bank’s experts argued that these defects were visible to trained examination; Waterlow’s experts countered that they became visible only through retrospective comparison with known genuine documents, an advantage unavailable to staff processing what appeared to be routine business correspondence. The testimony illuminated without resolving the central question: whether a reasonable printer, confronted with these particular documents in December 1924, should have recognized their fraudulent character.

The personal testimony of Karel Marang, delivered by deposition from Dutch custody since he could not be compelled to appear in London, provided the narrative thread that connected the forged documents to their ultimate effect. Marang described his own suspicions about the legitimacy of the enterprise, suspicions he had suppressed in pursuit of commission. He detailed the preparation of the authorization documents, the acquisition of genuine Banco de Portugal letterhead through bribery of a Lisbon bank official, the careful study of signature specimens that enabled the forgeries to pass visual inspection. His account confirmed what the documentary record suggested: that the fraud had succeeded not through technical brilliance alone but through exploitation of institutional habits and commercial expectations that neither Waterlow nor the Banco de Portugal had reason to question until exposure became unavoidable.

The litigation’s effect on Waterlow’s commercial relationships emerged gradually through discovery of correspondence with other government clients. Colonial administrations in Africa and Asia, central banks in Latin America and the Middle East, all sought reassurance that their own currency contracts were not similarly vulnerable. The firm found itself defending its handling of the Portuguese order and its general procedures against implied criticism from clients who had previously accepted its authority without question. This defensive posture consumed management attention and strained relationships that had sustained the business through decades of imperial expansion. The Portuguese lawsuit, whatever its eventual outcome, had already altered the firm’s position in the international market for currency printing.

Sir William Waterlow’s testimony in his own defense occupied three days, a performance that legal observers noted for its combination of dignified assurance and evident strain. He described the verification procedures that had governed his firm’s operations throughout his tenure, procedures that had successfully prevented fraud in thousands of transactions.

The immediate aftermath of the High Court judgment found the Banco de Portugal confronting an emptied arsenal. The criminal process had consumed the conspirators. The civil process had exonerated the printer. The losses remained, concrete and unallocated, pressing against the bank’s reserves and its political standing. The judgment had established that no English court would assign liability for the fraud’s financial damage to the party that had physically created the fraudulent notes. This legal determination left the bank with only one remaining mechanism for recovery: an appeal to a higher authority, to the final appellate jurisdiction that could reconsider the allocation of responsibility between sovereign institution and commercial contractor.

The House of Lords awaited.
