Chapter 13

The Arrest in the Rua do Ouro

A memorandum, dated 6 December 1925, arrived at police headquarters that late morning. It bore the signature of an examining magistrate and the seal of the Lisbon criminal court, naming one man for charges of falsidade, fraude, and currency crimes. The document itself was unremarkable—standard paper and type, the bureaucratic instrument of a state that had until forty-eight hours earlier shown no particular interest in a certain bank. What transformed this paper was not its content but its timing, following the removal of the option of professional discretion by a newspaper’s previous edition. The fraud was public, it was known, and it demanded a body in custody.

The police moved in the early afternoon. They found Reis not at his residence, where he had not slept since the newspaper appeared, but at the bank’s headquarters on the Rua do Ouro. The building’s facade maintained its calculated respectability: polished brass, marble thresholds, the painted name of an institution that had accumulated deposits, extended credit, and conducted itself for eight months as a legitimate competitor in Portuguese finance. Inside, the afternoon’s ordinary business had already been disrupted by telephone calls, by depositors appearing with unexpected urgency, by the janitor’s report that strangers had been observed photographing the building from across the street. The police presented their warrant to the bank’s porter, ascended to the second floor, and entered Reis’s office without ceremony.

What they found was a man engaged in the business of normalcy. Reis was seated at his desk, papers arranged before him, correspondence awaiting signature. The office contained the accumulated apparatus of his constructed authority: framed photographs of mining concessions in Angola, a map of Portuguese Africa marked with the locations of enterprises he had acquired, the leather-bound minute books of subsidiary companies. The police commissioner read the warrant aloud.

Reis listened without interruption. He requested permission to gather certain personal documents; the request was denied. Officers instructed him to stand, to place his hands behind his back, to accompany them to the waiting vehicle. He complied with each step, maintaining throughout a demeanor that witnesses later described as “perfectly calm” and “without visible agitation.”

The man who had duplicated one hundred million escudos in Bank of Portugal notes—perhaps the largest sum ever produced by a single counterfeiting operation—submitted to arrest with the composure of a businessman interrupted at his desk. At a point, he had duplicated 1% of the Portuguese GDP, a scale that would lead to the collapse of the country’s financial system.

The physical removal of Reis from the Rua do Ouro took less than twenty minutes. Its consequences unfolded across the remainder of the day with the chaotic energy of institutional panic. The police, having secured their principal, returned to seal the bank’s premises. Clerks who had arrived that morning expecting ordinary employment found themselves detained, questioned, released in stages as their lack of seniority became apparent. The bank’s cash reserves—genuine currency, earned through eight months of legitimate banking operations conducted with fraudulent capital—were counted, inventoried, removed under guard to the Bank of Portugal. The same institution whose printing plates had produced the notes now found itself custodian of the proceeds. No one remarked on this symmetry; there was too much to do.

The confusion within the Banco Angola e Metrópole headquarters derived from a fundamental uncertainty about the nature of the crisis. Was this a liquidity event, a run on deposits requiring the suspension of payments? A criminal investigation, requiring the preservation of documents and the identification of responsible parties? Or a political scandal, requiring the distancing of prominent men whose names appeared on the bank’s letterhead? The police themselves operated without clear instructions beyond the warrant’s mandate. They sealed doors, posted guards, prevented the removal of files. They did not, because no one had ordered them to, pursue the systematic identification of the bank’s depositors, creditors, or business partners. The arrest had created a physical vacuum at the center of the institution. No mechanism existed to determine what should fill it.

This procedural chaos revealed a structural truth that the fraud’s success had obscured. The Banco Angola e Metrópole had operated for eight months as a fully functional bank because it had performed all the visible functions of banking: accepting deposits, discounting commercial paper, extending credit to enterprises, maintaining correspondent relationships. Its legitimacy had been performative, established through repetition rather than foundation. The arrest of Reis did not immediately invalidate these performances. The bank’s clerks had executed genuine transactions with genuine currency. Its debtors remained obligated. Its creditors retained claims. The institution’s collapse would require not merely the removal of its founder but the administrative recognition that the entire enterprise had been constructed on a void. That recognition moved slowly. The police guarded a building whose legal status remained, for several critical days, genuinely uncertain.

The public dimension of the crisis compounded this administrative paralysis. The O Século revelation had created what the Portuguese state could not ignore: a constituency of informed citizens who demanded visible response. The newspaper’s account of the fraud—its scale, its audacity, its penetration of the Bank of Portugal itself—had transformed a technical financial irregularity into a national scandal. The government, already weakened by political instability, could not afford the appearance of complicity or indifference. The arrest of Reis satisfied this imperative without resolving the underlying questions. It produced a defendant without producing a theory of the crime. It demonstrated state capacity without demonstrating state comprehension.

The transformation of Reis from mastermind to defendant occurred in the hours following his arrest with a speed that surprised even his captors. He was transported not to the ordinary police lockup but to the Cadeia do Limoeiro, the ancient prison near the waterfront whose cells had held political prisoners, common criminals, and, in previous eras, enemies of the Inquisition. The selection of this facility suggested an unspecified gravity, a recognition that the charges transcended ordinary criminality. Reis was processed, photographed, assigned a cell. He requested access to legal counsel. The request was granted with a promptness that indicated official uncertainty about procedural requirements. The state had captured a man it did not yet know how to charge.

The materials Reis had accumulated—contracts with Waterlow & Sons, correspondence with the Bank of Portugal’s own officials, share certificates, property deeds, mining concessions—remained in place at the Rua do Ouro and at subsidiary locations throughout Lisbon. The police had sealed the headquarters but had not yet begun systematic examination. This delay was not merely tactical hesitation. The archive constituted a record of such complexity that no single investigator could comprehend its scope. The fraud had operated across multiple jurisdictions, through multiple corporate vehicles, with the participation of men who occupied positions of established social respectability. The arrest warrant named Reis alone. The question of who else might be named, and when, and on what evidence, remained entirely open.

The immediate institutional response to this uncertainty revealed the characteristic pattern of Portuguese administrative crisis: the multiplication of committees without the concentration of authority. The Ministry of Finance convened an extraordinary session on the evening of 6 December. The Bank of Portugal’s governor, requested to attend, provided a statement of such calculated vagueness that participants later disagreed about whether it constituted admission or denial of institutional knowledge. The bank’s own position was genuinely difficult. Its printing plates had produced the notes. Its officials had corresponded with Reis. Its inspection procedures had failed to detect the unauthorized production. Yet the bank was also, in formal terms, the principal victim of the fraud. This dual status—simultaneously responsible and injured—would shape its institutional response for the seven years of litigation that followed.

The parliamentary dimension of the crisis emerged with equal speed and equal confusion. Deputies who had received the O Século edition with their morning coffee found themselves confronted by constituents demanding explanation, by opposition members demanding inquiry, by the governing coalition demanding solidarity. The Chamber of Deputies suspended its ordinary business to receive a statement from the Minister of Finance. The statement, delivered in the late afternoon, acknowledged “irregularities” in the issuance of banknotes, promised “exhaustive investigation,” and declined to specify whether the government’s own officials might bear responsibility. The Chamber’s response—formal acceptance of the statement, referral to committee, adjournment—preserved the appearance of institutional function while postponing all substantive determination.

The personal consequences for Reis’s associates unfolded in a pattern of graduated exposure. José Bandeira, the bank’s vice-president and Reis’s principal collaborator, had departed Lisbon on 5 December for Porto, where he maintained business interests and family connections. His absence was noted by police but not initially pursued; the warrant named Reis alone, and Bandeira’s status remained, for several critical days, administratively undefined. Other participants—the Dutch commercial representative Karel Marang, the German engineer Adolph Hennies, the various Portuguese businessmen who had accepted directorships and shareholdings—responded to the crisis according to their individual calculations of exposure and protection. Some presented themselves voluntarily to police headquarters. Others retained legal counsel and maintained silence. A few departed abruptly for Spain, their subsequent movements tracked by consular reports that would not be assembled into coherent intelligence for several weeks.

The Banco Angola e Metrópole’s depositors experienced the crisis with a directness that official proceedings could not capture. The bank had attracted deposits through a combination of generous interest rates and the apparent solidity of its African investments. Many depositors were middle-class Portuguese, civil servants, small merchants, professionals, who had been attracted by returns unavailable from established institutions. The arrest of Reis and the sealing of headquarters transformed these deposits into frozen claims of uncertain value. The ordinary operations of the bank, check clearing, withdrawal authorization, interest payment, ceased without formal announcement. Depositors who appeared at the Rua do Ouro encountered police guards and posted notices. The notices cited “temporary administrative measures” without specifying their duration or their legal basis. The gap between institutional appearance and operational reality that Reis had exploited in constructing his fraud now reappeared in the state’s management of its consequences.

The international dimension of the crisis, still latent on 6 December, shaped the institutional response without yet entering public consciousness. The Bank of Portugal’s notes had been printed in London by Waterlow & Sons, a firm of established reputation whose managing director, Sir William Waterlow, had served as Lord Mayor of London and moved in the highest circles of British commercial and political life. The contracts authorizing this printing, fabricated by Reis, executed by Waterlow, constituted a legal and diplomatic complexity that no Portuguese authority could address unilaterally. The arrest of Reis resolved nothing about the status of these contracts, the responsibility for their execution, or the liability for their consequences. The Portuguese state had captured a criminal without yet comprehending a crime whose international ramifications would eventually reach the House of Lords.

The evening of 6 December found Reis in his cell at the Limoeiro, the first night of many months of pre-trial detention. The conditions of his confinement were not harsh by the standards of Portuguese prisons: a private cell, access to writing materials, meals delivered from outside. His legal counsel, engaged within hours of arrest, began the work of constructing a defense that would evolve substantially over the coming months. The initial strategy, denial of personal involvement, attribution of responsibility to subordinates or associates, would give way to more sophisticated approaches as the documentary evidence accumulated. Reis himself, according to subsequent accounts, spent these first hours in calculation rather than despair. The arrest had ended his operational freedom but had not yet determined his legal fate. The complexity that had protected the fraud’s construction might yet protect its architect.

The state’s own calculations proceeded in parallel and in partial secrecy. The examining magistrate appointed to conduct the preliminary investigation faced a procedural challenge of unprecedented scope. Portuguese criminal procedure required the identification of specific offenses, the assembly of supporting evidence, the formal indictment of named defendants. The Reis case involved offenses whose technical character, currency fraud, document forgery, corporate manipulation, crossed multiple jurisdictional boundaries. It involved evidence scattered across London, Lisbon, Porto, Luanda, and the mining districts of Angola. It involved defendants whose social connections and financial resources would enable sustained legal resistance. The magistrate’s initial orders, preservation of documents, identification of witnesses, provisional seizure of assets, established the framework for an investigation that would continue for more than a year.

The political management of the scandal required parallel attention. The government, aware that its own officials had corresponded with Reis and that its own bank had printed the notes, sought to establish distance without admitting prior negligence. Public statements emphasized the “foreign” dimensions of the fraud, the London printing, the Dutch and German participants, while minimizing the Portuguese institutional failures that had enabled its execution. This narrative strategy, partially successful in the immediate crisis, could not hold as the documentary record emerged. The contracts with Waterlow bore Portuguese signatures. The Bank of Portugal’s own inspection procedures had failed. The Banco Angola e Metrópole had operated in plain sight on one of Lisbon’s most prominent commercial streets.

The arrest of Reis thus served multiple functions that its official characterization as criminal apprehension could not fully capture. It satisfied public demand for visible response. It provided a physical location for the attribution of responsibility. It created a procedural framework, criminal prosecution, through which the scandal’s complexities might eventually be ordered. But it also, and perhaps primarily, represented the substitution of coercive for administrative authority. The state had failed to detect the fraud through its ordinary supervisory mechanisms. It had failed to prevent the fraud through its regulatory procedures. It had failed to comprehend the fraud even after its exposure by a newspaper. The arrest demonstrated that the state retained, at minimum, the capacity to remove individuals from circulation. This capacity, exercised belatedly and incompletely, became the foundation of all subsequent state action.

The following days brought a cascade of administrative measures that revealed both the scope of the crisis and the limitations of the state’s response. The Bank of Portugal, acting under emergency authority, assumed direct administration of the Banco Angola e Metrópole’s assets and liabilities. This assumption, presented as protective of depositors, also served to control information. The bank’s examination of the seized documents proceeded without public oversight, its findings communicated selectively to government officials. The parliamentary committee established to investigate the fraud received testimony from Bank of Portugal officials but not, initially, from the arrested principal. The examining magistrate’s investigation proceeded in judicial secrecy, its developments reported only through official bulletins of studied vagueness.

The international dimension of the crisis emerged into partial visibility through diplomatic channels. The Portuguese ambassador in London, instructed to ascertain the British government’s position regarding Waterlow & Sons, received responses of calibrated non-commitment. The British Foreign Office, aware that a major London firm stood accused of having printed fraudulent currency for a foreign government, sought to prevent the scandal’s escalation into diplomatic contention. Sir William Waterlow himself, informed of the arrest and its implications, maintained public silence while initiating private consultations with legal counsel and political contacts. The distance between London and Lisbon, which had enabled the fraud’s execution through delayed communication and mutual incomprehension, now shaped the crisis’s management through similar mechanisms.

The arrest of Reis on 6 December 1925 thus marked not a resolution but a transformation. The fraud’s operational phase, eight months of note production, bank operation, and commercial expansion, had ended with the suddenness of a severed connection. Its legal phase would extend for years, through Portuguese criminal proceedings and English civil litigation, through parliamentary inquiries and diplomatic negotiations, through the eventual House of Lords decision that would allocate financial responsibility among institutions and individuals. The man in the Limoeiro cell had become the fixed point around which this extended process would revolve. His personal fate, trial, conviction, sentence, imprisonment, would be determined by Portuguese law. The larger question of how such a fraud had become possible, and who beyond its immediate author bore responsibility for its consequences, remained open and would remain open long after the initial crisis had passed from public attention.

The concrete consequence of this arrest, visible in the immediate days that followed, was the emergence of liability as the organizing principle of institutional response. The Bank of Portugal, having failed to prevent the fraud, now sought to limit its financial exposure. The Portuguese government, having failed to supervise the bank, now sought to limit its political exposure. Waterlow & Sons, having printed the notes, now sought to limit its legal exposure.

Each institution moved to protect itself, and in so doing created the adversarial structure that would shape all subsequent proceedings. The arrest had secured the conspirator. It had not secured agreement about the conspiracy’s meaning, its cost, or its proper allocation among the parties who had enabled its execution. That pressure, for an authoritative determination of responsibility that transcended national boundaries and institutional self-interest, was already finding its way toward London, toward Threadneedle Street, toward a cable that would arrive in Sir William Waterlow’s offices with the force of a question that could no longer be deferred.
