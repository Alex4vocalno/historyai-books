Chapter 19

The Trial of Alves dos Reis

The indictment lay on the presiding judge’s bench in the Palácio da Bola, its first page visible to the gallery: Autos de Accusação contra Artur Virgílio Alves dos Reis e outros. The date was 17 May 1926, five months since the confiscation of Banco Angola e Metrópole’s wealth and the warrants that had sent Reis fleeing toward Africa. The document’s physical presence established the trial’s method: the Portuguese state would attempt to reconstruct a transnational conspiracy through the accumulated paper of its own institutions. The clerk who had prepared the docket had numbered its pages in a hand that betrayed no awareness that the case beneath his fingers would outlast the republic whose courts he served.

Prosecutor José de Sousa Monteiro rose to present the state’s foundational exhibit. He held the Bank of Portugal’s note register for series 2 of the five-hundred-escudo denomination, its leather binding cracked at the spine from twenty years of archival storage. The ledger’s pages recorded serial numbers 000, 001 through 200, 000. Monteiro read the entries aloud, his voice carrying the particular cadence of a man who understood that bureaucratic thoroughness could acquire dramatic weight. The numbers in the register stopped at 100, 000. The notes whose serial numbers filled the second half of the book, 100, 001 through 200, 000, existed only in the version that had passed through Waterlow’s printing presses in London and Reis’s hands in Lisbon.

The prosecution’s architecture rested not on confession but on documentation. The gap in the ledger was irrefutable. The Bank of Portugal had never authorized the printing of the second hundred thousand notes. Yet they had been printed, on the bank’s own plates, with the bank’s own designs, and circulated through a bank that Reis controlled. Monteiro’s task was to build from this documentary certainty a narrative of criminal intention that would survive the defense’s counter-narrative of authorized patriotism.

The state’s sequence unfolded across the trial’s first week. The forged Oxford diploma appeared next, identified by the university’s actual registrar as bearing a seal that had never existed and signatures that matched no college officer. The document had served its purpose in 1922, when Reis presented it to Lisbon construction firms and, more critically, to the network of contacts who would later facilitate his approach to the Bank of Portugal. The prosecution emphasized the diploma’s material qualities: the weight of its parchment, the color of its ribbon, the particular red of its wax seal. These details mattered because they demonstrated premeditation. A man who took such care with credentials he would use only indirectly was a man planning for scale.

The contract with Waterlow & Sons followed, its multiple pages entered as Exhibit C. The document purported to authorize Reis and his syndicate to receive banknotes for economic development in Angola, with repayment structured through future colonial revenues. Monteiro had secured from London the original signed copy, and he directed the court’s attention to its anomalies. The signatures of the Bank of Portugal’s governor and deputy governor were examined by a graphologist who testified that their pressure points and letter formations deviated from authenticated specimens. The contract’s reference to “special conditions” governing the notes’ production and transport—conditions that had allowed Reis to bypass the Bank of Portugal’s normal inspection protocols—was read aloud, its language of bureaucratic discretion now exposed as the instrument of its subversion.

Most damaging was the presentation of the forged board minutes, the document that had purported to record a meeting authorizing the contract. The prosecution established that no such meeting had occurred. The minutes’ date corresponded to a day when the governor was in Paris on bank business. The supposed attendees had been in three different cities. The minutes existed only to legitimate the contract, and the contract existed only to legitimate the printing. The chain of forgery was complete, each link supporting the next in a structure of deliberate fabrication.

The court heard from João Teixeira Direito, the Bank of Portugal inspector whose suspicions had initiated the investigation in Porto. Direito testified to the method that had exposed the fraud: the comparison of deposit records from Banco Angola e Metrópole against the official note registers, the discovery that the serial numbers of notes being presented for exchange fell in ranges the bank had never issued, the gradual recognition that the notes themselves were indistinguishable from legitimate currency in every respect except their documentary origin. His testimony carried the authority of a man who had followed procedure and found it insufficient, who had trusted the evidence of his eyes and been forced to doubt the institutions that had trained them.

Yet even as the prosecution built its edifice of fact, the trial’s central tension became apparent. The state could prove what had been done. It struggled to establish why, in a manner that would satisfy the requirements of criminal law.

The defense began its presentation in the trial’s second week. Alves dos Reis did not take the stand himself; his counsel, Dr. António de Abranches Ferrão, advised against exposing him to cross-examination. But his presence in the courtroom shaped every argument. The defendants who did testify, including António Bandeira, the former diplomat who had assisted in the contract’s preparation, advanced a single coherent narrative. They had believed the operation legitimate. They had understood themselves to be executing a secret state policy, authorized at the highest levels, designed to fund colonial development without the parliamentary scrutiny and public alarm that open appropriation would have provoked.

This defense traded on plausible context. Portugal’s First Republic was widely understood to operate through hidden arrangements, personal networks, and discretionary funds whose purposes could not withstand daylight examination. The Banco Angola e Metrópole’s operations—loans at rates below market, investments in Angolan enterprises, the gradual accumulation of shares in the Bank of Portugal itself—could be read as the implementation of a coherent developmental strategy rather than personal enrichment. The defendants testified to conversations with unnamed officials, to assurances received in private meetings, to the atmosphere of authorized secrecy that had surrounded their activities.

The prosecution’s response revealed the limits of its position. Monteiro could demonstrate that the authorizing documents were forged. He could not, within the confines of Portuguese criminal procedure, definitively establish that no secret authorization had existed. The foreign co-conspirators whose testimony might have clarified the defendants’ actual beliefs were beyond the court’s reach. Karel Marang van Ysselveere, the Dutch financier who had structured the currency exchange operations, had fled to the Netherlands and declined to appear. Adolph Hennies, the German adventurer who had assisted in the London arrangements, had similarly escaped Portuguese jurisdiction. Their absence was not merely procedural inconvenience; it was structural void. The defense could claim that these men had shared the defendants’ understanding of legitimate state sponsorship, and no contradictory witness could be compelled to appear.

Most significantly, Sir William Waterlow remained in London. The Portuguese court possessed no mechanism to secure his testimony regarding what he had been shown, what he had believed, and what representations had convinced him to release banknotes to Reis’s agents without the Bank of Portugal’s standard authorization. The contract with Waterlow & Sons was entered into evidence, but its interpretation remained contested. The prosecution read it as the culmination of fraud; the defense suggested it represented genuine, if clandestine, state business conducted through unconventional channels.

The trial’s energy shifted accordingly. What had begun as methodical reconstruction became mired in epistemological uncertainty. The prosecution established that the documents were forged. The defense responded that documents were not the only form of authorization. The prosecution traced the money’s movement through Banco Angola e Metrópole’s accounts. The defense argued that the money’s use—developmental investment rather than personal consumption—demonstrated public purpose. The gap between documentary forgery and criminal intent yawned wider with each exchange, and the court possessed no instrument to close it.

The financial dimension of the fraud proved equally resistant to judicial resolution. The note register could establish which serial numbers had been issued illegitimately. Bank records could show which deposits had been made in the fraudulent notes. But the trial could not trace where the bulk of the money had ultimately gone. The complex web of currency exchanges, international transfers, and investments in Angolan enterprises and Bank of Portugal shares remained opaque. Reis’s counsel successfully objected to prosecutorial requests for detailed financial disclosure on the grounds that such matters fell outside the criminal charges and risked prejudicing future civil litigation. The state was left with proof of unauthorized printing and circulation, but without accounting for the proceeds.

This opacity was not accidental. The defense strategy deliberately conflated the fraud’s mechanics with its purposes, rendering each partially visible through the other. If the purpose was genuinely developmental, the mechanics of secret authorization became more plausible. If the mechanics were demonstrably fraudulent, the purpose must have been criminal enrichment. The trial could not resolve this mutual implication because the evidence necessary to do so—testimony from foreign participants, access to international banking records, disclosure of the full financial trail—lay beyond national jurisdiction.

The political context pressed upon the courtroom with increasing weight. The trial’s May-June 1926 dates coincided with the terminal crisis of the Portuguese First Republic. The nationalist military coup of 28 May 1926, which would bring the Ditadura Nacional to power, occurred during the proceedings’ final days. The court’s authority derived from a state that was visibly dissolving. This circumstance did not directly affect the verdict; Reis and his co-defendants were convicted on the documentary evidence. But it shaped the trial’s historical position. The mechanisms of republican legality were being exercised for perhaps the last time, and their limitations were being demonstrated in the case that most thoroughly exposed the republic’s institutional vulnerabilities.

The verdict was delivered on 25 June 1926. Reis received a sentence of twenty years’ imprisonment. António Bandeira and the other Portuguese defendants received lesser terms. The court found that the documentary evidence of forgery was sufficient to establish criminal guilt regardless of the defendants’ claimed beliefs about state authorization. The judgment emphasized the material facts: the unauthorized printing, the forged signatures, the circulation of currency that the Bank of Portugal had not issued. These facts admitted of no interpretation that would negate criminal liability.

Yet the judgment’s very definiteness exposed what remained unresolved. The sentence addressed individual guilt. It did not address systemic failure. The Bank of Portugal’s losses—the 200, 000 notes, their equivalent value, the damage to its currency’s international standing—remained unrecovered. The foreign participants whose testimony might have clarified the full scope of the conspiracy had escaped accountability. The financial mechanisms through which the fraud’s proceeds had been laundered and invested remained undisclosed.

The trial had demonstrated something the republic’s final months made viscerally apparent: national institutions could establish individual criminality without securing institutional redress. The note register, the forged contracts, the testimony of inspectors—these materials had proven sufficient to imprison Reis. They had proven insufficient to trace the money, compel foreign testimony, or restore the Bank of Portugal’s losses. The gap between criminal justice and financial recovery was not merely procedural; it was structural, built into the mismatch between the fraud’s transnational operation and the court’s national competence.

The final sessions had revealed this structure with particular clarity. Monteiro’s closing argument had emphasized the national interest in establishing definitive accountability. The defense’s final statement had insisted on the international dimensions that exceeded any national court’s competence. Both positions were accurate. The fraud had exploited precisely this gap between national jurisdiction and transnational operation, between documentary procedure and actual authorization, between individual criminality and institutional vulnerability. The trial’s achievement was to render this gap visible through its own inability to close it.

The physical deterioration of the documents themselves became a silent witness to the trial’s limitations. The Oxford diploma, examined under the courtroom’s gas lamps, had begun to show the discoloration of cheap parchment improperly aged. Its ribbon, that particular shade of crimson that Reis had selected to suggest ancient collegiate authority, had faded at its exposed edges to something approaching pink. The prosecution made much of these material failures, arguing that they demonstrated the haste and corner-cutting of criminal enterprise. Yet the defense seized upon the same evidence to advance its counter-narrative: would a genuine state operative, working with unlimited official resources, have produced better forgeries? The poor quality of the diploma became, in Abranches Ferrão’s hands, evidence of improvisation rather than institutional backing, of a patriot forced to work with his own limited means rather than a forger operating with calculated deception.

The courtroom’s atmosphere shifted perceptibly when the discussion turned to the Banco Angola e Metrópole’s actual operations. Witnesses from the bank’s staff described a institution that had functioned, however irregularly, as a genuine financial intermediary. Loans had been made to Angolan coffee planters. Currency had been exchanged for legitimate commercial purposes. The bank’s shareholders—many of them drawn from Lisbon’s professional classes—had received dividends that, while generous, were not obviously fraudulent in origin.

This operational reality complicated the prosecution’s effort to portray the bank as merely a laundering mechanism. Monteiro found himself forced to argue a subtle distinction: that the bank’s legitimate functions had served as cover for its illegitimate ones, that normal banking operations had provided the plausible context within which fraudulent notes could circulate without immediate detection. The argument was legally sound but narratively unsatisfying, requiring the jury to hold in mind two contradictory images of the same institution.

The defense exploited this cognitive burden with skill. Bandeira’s testimony, delivered in the measured tones of a former diplomat accustomed to parliamentary scrutiny, emphasized the bank’s developmental achievements. He spoke of the Dala coffee plantation, of the Benguela railway extension, of credit extended to Portuguese settlers who would otherwise have faced exclusion by foreign-controlled banks. These were not, he insisted, the activities of criminals. They were men implementing a vision of colonial development that the republic’s fractious politics had made impossible through ordinary legislative appropriation. The prosecution could object that developmental intent did not authorize forgery, but it could not entirely dispel the impression that these defendants had accomplished something—however purchased—that their own state had failed to achieve.

The court’s struggle with Reis’s personal finances illustrated the broader evidentiary impasse. The prosecution had assembled records of property acquisitions: the villa at Estoril, the automobile, the furnishings that had accompanied his brief period of social prominence. But these visible expenditures represented a fraction of the fraud’s proceeds. The bulk of the money—hundreds of thousands of contos—had disappeared into the machinery of currency exchange and colonial investment. Monteiro attempted to argue that this very opacity demonstrated criminal sophistication, the deliberate construction of financial complexity to obscure the trail. Abranches Ferrão responded that such complexity was the necessary consequence of operating outside normal channels, that secret state business required secret state methods. The court was left to choose between two interpretations of the same absence, with no evidentiary basis to prefer either.

The international dimension pressed upon the proceedings with particular force during the examination of the London arrangements. The prosecution read aloud the correspondence between Reis’s intermediaries and Waterlow & Sons, letters in which British commercial formality encountered Portuguese bureaucratic improvisation. The contrast in tone—Waterlow’s cautious inquiries about authorization, the Portuguese side’s assurances of special circumstances—might have illuminated the question of mutual knowledge had Waterlow himself been available for examination. Without him, the letters remained ambiguous. They could be read as the careful documentation of a suspicious printer seeking reassurance, or as the knowing complicity of a firm accustomed to irregular arrangements in colonial business. The prosecution favored the former reading; the defense, with strategic silence about Waterlow’s actual beliefs, allowed the latter possibility to hover unexamined.

The trial’s middle weeks were dominated by technical testimony that threatened to dissolve the narrative tension entirely. Expert witnesses debated the precise sequence of plate preparation at Waterlow’s works, the chemical composition of the banknote paper, the mechanical details of serial number imposition. These proceedings served the prosecution’s methodical purpose but risked alienating the gallery and, more critically, the jurors from the human drama of the case. Monteiro’s challenge was to maintain the connection between technical detail and criminal responsibility, to ensure that each demonstration of how the fraud had been accomplished reinforced the conclusion that it had been accomplished knowingly. The defense, by contrast, welcomed the drift toward technicality, understanding that complexity served their narrative of authorized sophistication rather than criminal deception.

The political atmosphere of Lisbon in late May 1926 intruded upon the courtroom in ways that the presiding judge could neither acknowledge nor entirely exclude. Rumors of military movements circulated through the city. The republic’s final ministries came and went with bewildering rapidity. Jurors and witnesses alike arrived with news of troop concentrations and cabinet resignations, their attention divided between the trial’s deliberative pace and the capital’s gathering crisis. This distraction was not merely incidental. It reinforced the defense’s implicit argument that the trial addressed matters that had already been superseded, that the republic whose laws were being invoked was itself passing from the scene.

Reis was led from the courtroom to begin his sentence. The ledger that had initiated the prosecution’s case was returned to the Bank of Portugal’s archives. The contract with Waterlow & Sons was placed in the court’s file, awaiting the civil litigation that would eventually carry the dispute to London and, years later, to the House of Lords. Sir William Waterlow, whose family firm would finally be acquired by De La Rue in 1961, had been dismissed from the proceedings by geography and jurisdiction, his testimony as absent as the money itself.

The judgment stood as a record of individual criminality, complete and, within its own terms, justified. The fraud’s larger consequences—financial, institutional, international—remained unaddressed by the only judicial process that had attempted to comprehend them. The trial concluded with a prison sentence but leaves the fraud’s financial wreckage unaddressed and the bank’s losses unrecovered, creating the imperative for the Banco de Portugal to seek restitution in another court.
