Chapter 18

Waterlow’s Defence Takes Shape

The conspirators were in custody, delivered to the state for judicial reckoning. A junior clerk at Waterlow & Sons deposited a solicitor’s memorandum into the firm’s confidential register on the morning of January seventh, 1926. The volume, bound in green leather, had previously held records of municipal contracts from the City of London and warrants for livery companies. The document summarized an emergency conference held the previous evening at the chambers of Linklaters & Paines.

Sir William Waterlow had sat with his senior directors and their legal counsel to assess the damage emanating from Lisbon. The memorandum noted that the Bank of Portugal had publicly declared two hundred thousand five-hundred-escudo notes to be forgeries.

It recorded that Artur Virgílio Alves dos Reis, born in Lisbon on September eighth, 1896, now identified as a Portuguese criminal who had perpetrated one of the largest frauds in history, against the Bank of Portugal in 1925, languished in Limoeiro prison awaiting trial. Portuguese authorities were preparing suit against the London printers for damages variously estimated at between three hundred thousand and one million pounds sterling. The clerk initialled the entry and closed the cover.

The sound of the latch marked the moment when corporate shock crystallized into survival strategy.

The defense that emerged from those January consultations did not begin with simple denial. Sir William Waterlow, who had served as the six hundred and second Lord Mayor of London and carried the bearing of municipal dignity acquired during his tenure at Mansion House following his education at Marlborough College, understood that refusal alone would not suffice. The facts assembled by his solicitors were damning. Waterlow & Sons had indeed printed banknotes identical to legitimate Portuguese issues using plates entrusted to their care.

They had delivered these notes to agents of dos Reis and his Dutch associate Karel Marang. They had accepted payment without direct authorization from the Bank of Portugal itself. The firm faced dissolution as a financial entity and as a reputation built since 1810. It was finally acquired by De La Rue in 1961. Sir William Waterlow had been dismissed from its board long before that absorption, but in January 1926 such distant consolidation remained unimaginable. The immediate threat required a posture more sophisticated than disclaimer, a narrative that could transform Waterlow from perpetrator into victim.

The first element of their defense rested upon the manner of approach. Marang had not appeared out of the void. He had carried a letter of introduction from Joh. Enschedé en Zonen, the venerable Dutch printing house established in Haarlem since 1703. The Dutch firm had examined sample notes provided by dos Reis’s syndicate and identified them as Waterlow’s work, advising that only the original London printer could produce identical duplicates from existing plates.

This referral from one eminent house to another provided scaffolding for Waterlow’s argument: they had been approached through channels carrying every appearance of commercial propriety. The transaction had been kept quite confidential to avoid embarrassing prominent silent partners and risking the whole deal being scotched in face of political opposition, an explanation aligning with Waterlow’s experience of colonial enterprises requiring discretion. If Enschedé had validated the approach, then Waterlow’s acceptance of the commission appeared not reckless but professionally justified.

The second pillar required demonstrating that forged documents presented by dos Reis and his brother-in-law José Bandeira carried sufficient verisimilitude to deceive reasonable men exercising due diligence. The contract purportedly authorized by the Bank of Portugal bore official stationery with seals matching institutional precedent. Accompanying correspondence replicated bureaucratic form with meticulous precision. These documents suggested sophistication implicating not merely forgers’ skill but administrative vulnerability at the highest levels. If a Lisbon clerk with a forged Oxford diploma could replicate authorization documents convincing enough to persuade a major London printer, a printer accustomed to handling sovereign currency, then fundamental safeguards had failed elsewhere. The argument took shape gradually. Waterlow had examined what any reasonable commercial house would examine. That examination proved insufficient only because another institution had failed its primary duty to secure its own instruments of authority.

The third element concerned apparent respectability of clients themselves. To Waterlow’s directors in late 1924, dos Reis appeared not as Artur Virgílio Alves dos Reis, perpetrator against the Bank of Portugal in what history would call the Angola e Metrópole affair, at one point having duplicated one hundred million escudos worth of currency, but as businessman representing consortium interests in Portuguese Africa backed by German capital seeking development concessions. José Bandeira carried additional cachet as brother to António Bandeira, Portuguese Ambassador to The Hague, providing aura of diplomatic connection validating claims of official backing. The explanation offered for secrecy, that political sensitivities in Lisbon required discreet handling to avoid opposition scuttling necessary projects, resonated with Waterlow’s experience dealing with foreign governments where legitimate business proceeded through indirect channels sanctioned by protocol if not public advertisement.

These three arguments, legitimate approach, plausible forgeries, credible clients, coalesced into posture of aggrieved victimhood that rapidly transformed into counter-accusation. During February strategy sessions held at Waterlow’s offices on Great Winchester Street, directors reviewed Portuguese accusations not as justified pursuit of restitution but as cynical attempt by embarrassed officials to offset catastrophic losses by scapegoating foreign craftsmen. More significantly, solicitors developed theory placing Bank of Portugal itself in comparative negligence. If dos Reis could obtain plates and paper specifications sufficient to produce undetectable duplicates using legitimate facilities, then someone within bank must have been either complicit or criminally negligent safeguarding proprietary materials.

This defense relied upon a particular institutional blindness: Waterlow had followed every required protocol, and that protocols proved insufficient became Portuguese failure rather than English oversight. The formal steps of commercial verification had been observed with scrupulous care. Each signature checked against precedent, each seal compared to known specimens, each procedural requirement satisfied before production commenced. That this compliance produced fraudulent currency pointed not to printer’s negligence but to systemic collapse in Lisbon’s administrative controls.

Sir William’s personal struggle during these weeks remained largely invisible to subordinates but acute within private deliberations. Educated at Marlborough College where honor codes governed conduct as strictly as academic achievement, he confronted choice pitting dignity against survival. Settlement remained possible. Quiet acceptance of partial responsibility negotiating damages might preserve modicum of reputation through contrition, though potentially bankrupting firm. Alternatively they could fight, adopting adversarial stance solicitors recommended, accusing sovereign nation’s central bank of negligence or potential complicity, risking alienation of City establishment upon which Waterlow depended for municipal contracts and social standing.

The decision toward confrontation hardened through February as Portuguese authorities pressed claims and evidence accumulated from Lisbon painted increasingly damaging portrait of dos Reis operations. The notes had been utilized through Banco Angola e Metrópole to purchase Angolan enterprises and controlling shares in the central bank itself. Each new disclosure from Limoeiro prison forced legal team to sharpen counter-narrative. Details emerged of diplomatic pouches smuggling currency, of banks giving loans without receiving deposits, of a financial architecture designed to convert printed paper into institutional power. They began arguing that Bank of Portugal’s note registers must have been poorly maintained if duplicate serial numbers could circulate months without immediate detection. They questioned how plates supposedly under exclusive control could replicate so precisely unless internal security had been compromised.

Political instability gripping Portugal complicated calculations. Although this period remains little understood historically, this crisis exerted strong influence upon nationalist military preparing coup d’état of May twenty-eighth nineteen twenty-six, bringing Ditadura Nacional to power and ending First Republic. Sir William watched developments anxiously. Regime change might alter litigation strategies, produce favorable evidence, or inflame nationalist sentiment against English commercial interests already suspected of German involvement in dos Reis schemes.

The transformation from victim to accuser required careful calibration. Waterlow’s solicitors drafted memoranda emphasizing the sophistication of forged documents while simultaneously documenting every instance where Bank of Portugal’s internal procedures appeared to facilitate rather than prevent fraud. They compiled instances where legitimate correspondence from Threadneedle Street had been intercepted or duplicated, where authorization protocols had been circumvented by individuals with no official position, where security surrounding note plates had proven porous enough to allow criminal replication. The cumulative effect was to suggest not merely negligence but systematic administrative failure that made Waterlow’s own compliance nearly irrelevant.

The solicitors’ memoranda from these early weeks reveal a mounting obsession with procedural minutiae that would characterize the entire defense. Linklaters & Paines assigned a junior partner named Harold Whitmore to compile every instance where Waterlow’s verification methods had conformed to established City practice. Whitmore produced a ledger running to forty-three pages, detailing comparable transactions with the Bank of England, the Imperial Bank of Persia, and the National Bank of Egypt. In each case, he noted, Waterlow had accepted authorization through correspondent banks or government ministries rather than direct central bank communication. The pattern suggested not deviation from standard practice but adherence to it. The Portuguese demand for direct Bank of Portugal authorization, Whitmore argued, represented an exceptional requirement that no international printer could reasonably anticipate, particularly when approaching parties presented credentials from established diplomatic and commercial channels.

This documentary strategy carried risks that Sir William recognized with growing discomfort. To prove that Waterlow had followed normal practice required demonstrating that normal practice was itself inadequate to prevent fraud. The defense thus threatened to implicate the entire London printing trade, potentially alienating competitors who might otherwise offer tacit support. Sir William raised this concern during a directors’ meeting on January twenty-third, noting that “our justification may prove our industry’s indictment.” The minutes record no response from his colleagues, suggesting either dismissal of the danger or unwillingness to confront its implications. The silence marked a characteristic pattern in these consultations: strategic objections raised, acknowledged, and then submerged beneath the momentum of adversarial preparation.

The personal dimension of Sir William’s dilemma extended beyond commercial calculation to questions of family legacy and civic identity. His uncle, Sir Sydney Waterlow, had established the firm’s reputation for municipal service through gifts of public parks and philanthropic housing. His own tenure as Lord Mayor in 1929-1930—still three years distant but already anticipated in political gossip—depended upon maintaining the standing that now trembled under Portuguese accusations. The Mansion House speech he would eventually deliver, celebrating London’s resilience after the General Strike, would require careful avoidance of any subject touching upon banknote fraud or continental litigation. These temporal pressures, distant yet determining, shaped his tolerance for risk in ways he barely acknowledged to his legal advisors.

The solicitors’ developing theory of comparative negligence drew upon emerging forensic analysis of the forged documents themselves. Examination revealed that the Bank of Portugal seals reproduced upon dos Reis’s authorizations differed microscopically from official specimens, variations detectable only through magnification and comparison with archival holdings. This discovery produced contradictory impulses within the defense team. On one hand, it demonstrated that Waterlow’s examination had been insufficiently rigorous. On the other, it suggested that the forgeries represented not amateur deception but professional replication using materials obtained through institutional compromise. The second interpretation prevailed in strategy discussions, supported by Sir William’s insistence that “no London house possesses equipment to detect corruption in Lisbon’s own archives.”

The argument regarding plate security acquired particular urgency as Portuguese investigators revealed details of how dos Reis had obtained technical specifications. The original plates remained, Waterlow maintained, under their exclusive London control; the duplicates had been produced using measurements derived from circulating notes and informed speculation regarding manufacturing processes. Yet this distinction collapsed under scrutiny. If circulating notes provided sufficient information to produce undetectable counterfeits, then the Bank of Portugal’s currency design itself constituted a security failure. Waterlow’s solicitors began compiling expert testimony from engravers and papermakers regarding the inherent vulnerability of any note sufficiently circulated to serve as commercial currency. The circularity of this position—blaming the victim for possessing currency that attracted crime—did not deter its development, so thoroughly had adversarial logic displaced mutual comprehension.

The Dutch connection to Joh. Enschedé en Zonen required particular diplomatic handling. Marang’s approach through Haarlem provided Waterlow’s most persuasive evidence of commercial propriety, yet it also implicated a respected competitor in the transaction’s verification.

Sir William dispatched a personal letter to the Enschedé managing director on January fifteenth, requesting confirmation of their referral and assessment of Marang’s presentation. The reply, received ten days later, offered carefully qualified support: Enschedé had indeed examined sample notes and identified Waterlow’s workmanship; they had advised that only the original printer could produce identical duplicates; they had not, however, investigated the legitimacy of the underlying transaction or the authority of those presenting the samples. This distinction between technical competence and commercial due diligence became crucial to Waterlow’s narrative.

They had relied upon Enschedé’s expertise regarding printing capabilities, not their judgment regarding transaction validity—a separation that allowed Waterlow to claim professional consultation while disclaiming responsibility for its limits.

The psychological atmosphere within Great Winchester Street during these weeks combined desperate urgency with strange periods of administrative normalcy. The firm continued processing contracts for colonial administrations, municipal corporations, and private banks, the routine of legitimate business providing cover for strategic consultations that occurred in early mornings and late evenings. Junior clerks observed their principals gathering in Sir William’s corner office with increasing frequency, emerging with expressions of constrained tension that contrasted with the deliberate cheerfulness of public interaction. This bifurcation between private crisis and public composure replicated, at institutional scale, Sir William’s personal struggle between honor and survival. The firm became, in effect, two organizations: one performing its historical functions with mechanical precision, the other constructing elaborate arguments for its own exculpation.

The decision to pursue counter-claims rather than negotiated settlement crystallized during a critical consultation on February eleventh. Portuguese solicitors had transmitted, through diplomatic channels, a proposal for arbitration under London Chamber of Commerce rules, suggesting shared responsibility and proportional damages. Sir William initially favored acceptance, recognizing that prolonged litigation would consume resources and attention regardless of outcome. His directors overruled him, persuaded by Linklaters’ assessment that any admission of partial responsibility would establish liability foundations for claims far exceeding arbitration estimates. The minutes record Sir William’s acquiescence with characteristic formulation: “The House determines, and I submit.” The passive construction masked active concession, a pattern that would recur throughout his remaining association with the firm he had inherited and would eventually lose.

The aggressive defense strategy required substantial financial commitment at a moment of constrained liquidity. Waterlow & Sons had extended credit to dos Reis’s syndicate that would never be recovered; the Portuguese litigation threatened attachment of remaining assets; municipal contracts, while continuing, generated insufficient margin to support both operations and legal costs. Sir William authorized mortgage of certain freehold properties and negotiation of credit facilities with Coutts & Co., leveraging personal relationships established during his municipal career. These financial arrangements remained confidential even within the firm, recorded only in Sir William’s private papers and the bank’s institutional archives. The personal guarantee he provided, ultimately exceeding forty thousand pounds, represented a substantial portion of his independent wealth and established stakes that would influence his subsequent resistance to settlement proposals.

The evolving relationship between Waterlow’s legal position and emerging criminal proceedings in Lisbon created persistent strategic complications. Portuguese authorities sought to coordinate civil and criminal phases, hoping that dos Reis’s conviction would establish facts prejudicial to Waterlow’s defense. Linklaters advised vigorous objection to any evidentiary sharing between proceedings, arguing that criminal standards of proof and civil liability requirements operated upon distinct principles. This technical position, while legally defensible, produced awkward implications: Waterlow’s interests aligned, in effect, with dos Reis’s interest in preventing establishment of criminal facts that would support civil claims. The defense team never acknowledged this alignment explicitly, yet their procedural objections consistently served to delay and complicate the Portuguese criminal prosecution. Sir William’s private notes reveal awareness of this convergence, expressed with characteristic reticence: “Our case and the fraudster’s, temporarily congruent. Distasteful necessity.”

The international dimensions of the dispute expanded as German interests in the dos Reis syndicate became clearer. Karel Marang’s connections to Hamburg banking circles, initially presented as evidence of commercial respectability, now appeared potentially compromising given postwar sensitivities regarding German financial penetration of Portuguese colonies. Waterlow’s solicitors developed elaborate arguments distinguishing between Marang’s Dutch nationality and his German associations, emphasizing the former while minimizing the latter. This ethnic parsing, conducted with the casual prejudice of the period, aimed to preserve Waterlow’s claim of having dealt with established European commercial interests rather than suspect continental speculators. The argument’s fragility—Marang was indisputably Dutch, yet indisputably connected to German capital—required careful presentation that consumed substantial drafting resources.

The institutional pressures upon the Bank of Portugal, meanwhile, suggested opportunities that Waterlow’s strategists proved reluctant to exploit directly. Revolutionary instability in Lisbon, escalating through February toward the May coup, threatened the very officials pursuing claims against London. A more cynical defense might have encouraged this instability, hoping that regime change would produce abandonment of litigation or destruction of documentary evidence. Sir William explicitly rejected such tactics during a strategy session on February twenty-fourth, insisting that “we shall not profit from Portuguese disorder.” The minutes record this intervention without editorial comment, yet its presence suggests awareness among directors that alternative paths existed. Sir William’s refusal marked a boundary that adversarial logic would not cross, preserving some residue of municipal honor even as legal arguments grew increasingly accusatory.

The final consultations of February established the documentary architecture that would govern seven years of subsequent litigation.

By late February strategy sessions concluded with resolution toward aggressive defense. When Sir William authorized filing counter-claims demanding Bank of Portugal produce internal correspondence regarding plate security and custody protocols, documents Portuguese authorities refused to surrender citing state secrets, the adversarial positions between Waterlow & Sons and Bank of Portugal locked into place. The course was set for seven years of litigation. The concrete consequence was felt immediately in London and Lisbon: diplomatic channels cooling, solicitors exchanging increasingly acrimonious memoranda, formerly cordial commercial relationships between nations now strained through determination of two institutions neither willing to acknowledge how systemic failures had enabled criminal substitution of genuine currency with printed fraud.

The green leather register would accumulate many more entries before resolution came.
