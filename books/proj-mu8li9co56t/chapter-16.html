Chapter 16

The Governor’s Denial

On the mahogany desk of Camacho Rodrigues lay a dated tally sheet, a ledger compiled from the sealed strong room of the Banco Angola e Metrópole. Its columns recorded hundreds of thousands of 500-escudo notes, each bearing the Bank of Portugal’s own design, each printed from plates that should have remained under singular institutional control.

The document was not accusatory in itself; it simply enumerated, transforming the abstraction of fraud into the particularity of notes counted and found duplicate. The governor had governed through six years of postwar instability and currency crises. He understood that central banking operated through a singular promise: one issue, one authority, one guarantee.

What the sheet disclosed made nonsense of this singularity. The same plates, the same designs, the same institutional voice had produced two streams of currency where only one should exist. Waterlow and Sons Limited had printed 200, 000 banknotes of 500 Portuguese escudos with an image of Vasco da Gama, with the date of 17 November 1922, to a total face value of 100 million escudos—equivalent to nearly one percent of Portugal’s nominal GDP at the time.

The notes were not counterfeit in any technical sense. They were genuine Bank of Portugal notes, printed with genuine plates, that had nevertheless entered circulation through a mechanism of fraud. This distinction mattered legally and catastrophically. Counterfeiting could be detected, denounced, prosecuted. Duplication struck at the foundation of what a banknote meant. The promise was no longer singular. The authority was no longer exclusive. The guarantee had been rendered, by the bank’s own technical apparatus, into something that could be replicated without the bank’s knowledge.

Camacho Rodrigues studied the document with the attention of a man who recognized his own institutional voice in the mechanism of the crime. The forged letters that had persuaded Waterlow & Sons to release the plates had carried the authority of his office. The contract authorizing the special issue had been executed in the name of the bank he commanded. The fraud had operated by wearing the mask of his institution, and now the mask would be examined for what it concealed.

The meeting assembled that December 15 brought together the men who would manage this examination. The Minister of Finance, Aníbal Ferreira de Macedo, arrived with the gravity of a political appointee who understood that his portfolio now contained a detonation. The bank’s senior directors arranged themselves with the nervous formality of executives confronting procedural failure. The judicial investigators, led by the examining magistrate who had ordered the seizure, carried the authority of a state whose own authority had already been compromised. The governor presided, but his position was defensive from the outset. He was simultaneously victim and suspect, guardian of the currency and man whose guardianship had proved permeable.

The numbers were reviewed. The 500-escudo note represented a concentration of value that made its duplication especially dangerous—roughly twenty-five pounds sterling at contemporary rates. The total ran to millions of contos, sufficient to distort Portuguese monetary conditions and to fund the acquisition of real assets throughout the empire. The Banco Angola e Metrópole had used these notes to purchase controlling interests in colonial enterprises, to extend credit to affiliated companies, and—most audaciously—to accumulate shares in the Bank of Portugal itself. The fraud had not merely stolen value; it had used stolen value to buy a voice in the governance of the institution from which it was stolen.

Camacho Rodrigues received this information with the controlled demeanor his position required. He inquired about serial numbers, dates of issue, the distribution of notes through the Banco Angola e Metrópole’s operations. He sought clarification on the relationship between notes seized in Porto and those found in Lisbon. Through these questions he established that he was conducting an inquiry rather than answering charges. But the distinction was fragile. Each question revealed what he had not known, what his procedures had not detected, what his authority had not prevented.

The meeting produced a decision that would govern the bank’s posture. The governor would issue a public statement acknowledging that irregularities had been discovered in connection with certain banknote issues. The statement would emphasize that the notes remained technically valid obligations of the Bank of Portugal, not counterfeit, and that holders need not fear for their value. This was essential to prevent panic. It was also essential to prevent the examination that might reveal how the irregularities had occurred. The statement would minimize the scale, describing the duplication as limited and contained. An internal review would determine whether any employees had been complicit. The judicial investigation would be acknowledged but not encouraged. The priority was stability.

This decision represented institutional self-preservation at work. The Bank of Portugal was not merely a victim. Its credibility was itself a form of currency, to be defended even at the cost of full disclosure. The governor understood that the complete story—how forged documents had deceived a London printer, how plates had traveled under diplomatic cover, how notes had entered circulation through a bank whose controlling shareholders included the forger—would raise questions about the Bank of Portugal’s vigilance more damaging than the fraud itself. The institution had to appear in control of a situation that had already escaped its control.

The public statement was drafted that evening and released the following morning. It appeared in the government bulletin and was carried by Lisbon newspapers already following the emerging scandal. The governor’s words were calibrated with precision. He referred to “certain anomalies” in banknote issue, to “irregular procedures” reported to the competent authorities. He assured the public that the Bank of Portugal’s obligations remained fully secure, that circulating notes retained their legal value, that the institution was taking all necessary measures to prevent recurrence. The statement did not name the Banco Angola e Metrópole. It did not acknowledge the scale of duplication. It did not explain how the anomaly had been discovered or what it consisted of. The governor spoke as custodian of confidence, and confidence required the suppression of certain facts.

The response revealed the limits of institutional authority in a political system where information could not be fully contained. O Século, the Porto newspaper that had broken the story on December 5, continued its coverage with the tenacity of a provincial publication pursuing national interest. Its reporters maintained sources in the judicial investigation and in the Bank of Portugal’s own staff. They understood what the governor’s statement omitted. The contrast between official minimization and newspaper elaboration created a space of public doubt that would prove difficult to close. Readers who followed both versions encountered not reassuring authority but a contest of authorities, each with its own interest in what was disclosed and what concealed.

The governor’s private maneuvers during these same hours pursued different objectives. While his public statement sought to contain the crisis, his confidential inquiries sought to measure its depth within his own institution. Directors responsible for note issue and for relations with foreign printers were summoned. Files concerning the Banco Angola e Metrópole’s establishment and early operations were reviewed. Camacho Rodrigues established, to his evident discomfort, that the forged documents bearing signatures difficult to distinguish from authentic specimens had exploited procedures that did not require the verification that might have detected fraud. The bank’s own regulations, designed to facilitate efficient operation, had created the openings that the forger exploited.

This discovery placed the governor in acute strategic difficulty. To acknowledge procedural inadequacy was to accept responsibility for inadequacy. To insist on procedural adequacy required explaining how procedures had failed. The middle course he adopted treated the fraud as exceptional event produced by exceptional criminality, requiring exceptional response rather than systemic reform. Staff were directed to prepare a detailed inventory of all notes issued through the Banco Angola e Metrópole, to trace their distribution through the banking system, to identify assets acquired with them. This was prudent administration. It was also construction of a dossier necessary for legal action against conspirators and against Waterlow & Sons themselves.

The question of legal action against the London printer introduced further complexity. Waterlow & Sons had executed the print order in good faith, deceived by documents appearing to carry Bank of Portugal authority. Yet the printer had released plates to an agent of the Banco Angola e Metrópole without direct authorization that bank regulations would have required. The governor understood that the Bank of Portugal possessed potential claim against Waterlow & Sons for negligence, perhaps for damages. He understood also that pursuing such claim would require exposing the full extent of his own institution’s procedural failures, would invite scrutiny of forged documents by English courts, would transform Portuguese scandal into international litigation lasting years. The decision to pursue or forgo this action would shape institutional response for the duration of the crisis.

His inquiries extended to the political connections of the Banco Angola e Metrópole. He learned what judicial investigators had already discovered: that founders included men with access to highest government levels, that the diplomatic pouch used to transport plates had been arranged through channels reaching the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, that rapid accumulation of colonial concessions had been facilitated by officials who had accepted the bank’s notes as legitimate currency. These connections explained how the fraud had operated so effectively and so long. They also explained why certain government voices urged caution, delay, avoidance of precipitate action. The fraud had penetrated not only technical operations but the network of interests connecting financial and political power in the Portuguese Republic.

Camacho Rodrigues received this information with recognition that his institution was embedded in a system that had enabled its own violation. The governor of a central bank in a small, indebted, imperial nation operated with constraints that could not be acknowledged in public. The bank’s independence was partial, its authority contingent, its reputation dependent on alliances that could not be exposed to examination. The fraud had exploited these conditions by creating appearance of official authorization at every stage. To pursue the fraud thoroughly would be to pursue these alliances, to risk the political destabilization the bank was supposed to prevent.

His response to this dilemma was characteristic of his institutional position. Cooperation with judicial investigation would extend to establishing criminal responsibility of immediate perpetrators. Resistance would meet any inquiry threatening to extend to political connections of the Banco Angola e Metrópole or to procedural failures of his own institution. Documentation necessary for potential claim against Waterlow & Sons would be prepared while deferring any decision to initiate such claim until political situation clarified. Public statements would maintain posture of guardian of monetary stability confronting isolated criminal episode. This was not cover-up in conventional sense. It was managed disclosure, designed to preserve the institution whose credibility had been compromised by the very disclosures necessary to punish the compromise.

The mechanism of this strategy can be traced in documents passing between governor’s office and Ministry of Finance during the week following seizure. Assurances were requested and received that government would support bank’s position that notes remained valid obligations. A proposal was made and accepted that bank conduct parallel investigation of fraud’s financial consequences, with findings to be shared with judicial authorities at bank’s discretion. A proposal that bank immediately publish full inventory of duplicated notes was resisted successfully, on grounds that such disclosure would create unnecessary alarm. Each exchange advanced the governor’s objective of controlling tempo and extent of public knowledge.

The cost of this control was borne by clarity of public understanding. The governor’s statements created narrative of contained anomaly that would prove difficult to sustain as more information emerged. Contrast between official minimization and newspaper revelations generated suspicion that bank had something to conceal—which in fact it did: not complicity in the fraud, but conditions of its own vulnerability. The mechanism of institutional self-preservation, by seeking to protect reputation, contributed to erosion of that reputation. The governor faced choices in which every option carried damage, and his selections among these options would be judged, in retrospect, as contributions to damage rather than its mitigation.

The pressure of these choices was visible in his conduct during meetings of December 16 and 17. The examining magistrate was received with formal courtesy that barely concealed resistance to scope of judicial inquiry. Questions were raised whether magistrate’s authority extended to examination of Bank of Portugal records, suggesting that monetary administration was matter of executive rather than judicial competence. An offer was made that bank’s own legal staff assist in preparation of charges against conspirators, which would have placed institution in position of investigating its own violation. These were not maneuvers of a man confident in his position. They were defensive tactics of authority confronting limits of its authority.

The magistrate persisted. Physical evidence lay before him: notes seized in Porto, documents recovered from Banco Angola e Metrópole, testimony of bank employees who had handled suspicious currency. He understood that governor’s cooperation was necessary for comprehensive account of how plates had been released and duplication concealed. He understood also that this cooperation would be partial, calibrated to protect interests the governor would not name. The magistrate’s persistence and governor’s resistance created dynamic that would govern investigation’s progress: contest between judicial and institutional authority, each with its own conception of where public interest lay.

This contest was not merely personal. It reflected structure of Portuguese state, in which Bank of Portugal occupied ambiguous position. Partly private, partly public, technically independent but politically embedded, the bank was accountable to multiple constituencies whose interests did not coincide. The governor’s denial—his insistence that fraud was contained, that procedures were sound, that crisis could be managed without systemic change—was attempt to reassert control over authority signal that had already been captured and exploited. The attempt could not fully succeed. The authority had been demonstrated to be transferable, reproducible, detachable from institution that claimed to monopolize it. The governor’s statements, however carefully crafted, could not restore the singularity that the fraud had dissolved.

Practical consequences of this impasse began emerging in final days of December. The judicial investigation, lacking full access to Bank of Portugal records, developed its own channels of information. Police, acting on magistrate’s orders, identified and began to locate principal conspirators. Newspaper coverage, feeding on official reticence, expanded its narrative of institutional failure. International financial community, alerted by Lisbon correspondents of London and Paris banks, began assessing implications of scandal for Portuguese credit. The governor’s strategy of managed disclosure was overtaken by events that could not be managed.

On the morning of December 18, police officers presented themselves at the Lisbon residence of Karel Marang, the Dutch trader who had served as intermediary between the conspirators and Waterlow & Sons. Marang was not present. His servants reported that he had departed two days earlier, carrying traveling cases, offering no explanation of destination or expected return. The same morning, similar inquiries at the offices of the Banco Angola e Metrópole found the premises locked, the staff dispersed, the principal directors unavailable for questioning. The network that had operated with such confidence through autumn of 1925 was dissolving under pressure of investigation that could no longer be contained by governor’s maneuvers of delay and deflection.

The authority vacuum that the fraud had exploited now constrained those who sought to pursue it. Camacho Rodrigues had attempted to reassert institutional control over a credibility that had been demonstrated to be separable from his institution. His denial had purchased temporary stability at cost of permanent suspicion. The notes remained in circulation, their value technically guaranteed by the very bank whose guarantee had been forged. The conspirators were dispersing, their assets frozen or transferred, their names becoming subjects of international notice. And the mechanism of power that the governor had sought to protect—procedural, financial, political—was exposed as mechanism that could be replicated by anyone who understood its operations well enough to counterfeit its signatures.

The pressure of managed disclosure, of denial maintained against accumulating evidence, of institutional self-preservation pursued at cost of public trust, transformed the governor’s position from defensive to reactive. Each statement required modification by subsequent statement. Each minimization was contradicted by revelation. The bank that had sought to control the tempo of scandal found itself unable to control even the tempo of its own response. And in this transformation, the conditions were created for the pursuit that would follow: not judicial investigation alone, but police action, international inquiry, the transformation of administrative anomaly into criminal manhunt.
