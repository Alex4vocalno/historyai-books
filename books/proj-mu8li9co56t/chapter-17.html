Chapter 17

The Flight from Estoril

The telegram arrived at the Setúbal police post on the evening of 7 December 1925, its text punched through the paper strip in the clipped abbreviations of official urgency: warrants issued, subjects named, ports to be watched. The duty officer decoded the message with the methodical patience of a man who had spent twenty years in maritime police work and understood that most telegrams outran the events they described. This one did not.

The subjects named were already within the district’s boundaries, or would be, and the vessel they had apparently arranged for passage was riding at anchor in the outer harbor, its departure scheduled for the following noon. The officer filed the telegram in the station’s overnight log, noted the instructions for morning distribution to harbor patrols, and returned to his interrupted meal. The state, when it finally moved, moved with the deliberate thoroughness of machinery that had spent weeks pretending it would not need to engage at all.

The pressure of managed disclosure, of denial sustained against mounting proof, of institutional self-preservation purchased with the last reserves of public confidence, had transformed the governor’s posture from guarded to reactive, from reactive to openly alarmed. Each communiqué from the Bank of Portugal required emendation by its successor. Each reassurance was undercut by fresh revelation. The governor’s statement of 4 December, insisting that all notes presented for examination had proved genuine, was still being reprinted in Lisbon papers when the Porto branch’s own inspection began turning up discrepancies that could not be explained away.

His office found itself issuing corrections to its corrections, a rhythm of retreat that made official pronouncement sound increasingly like official panic. By the morning of 5 December, the police headquarters at Rua do Ouro had begun receiving orders that moved beyond information-gathering to active intervention. The documents that survive from those hours—telegrams, warrants, duty rosters—show an administrative apparatus finally engaging with a crisis it had spent weeks trying to contain through assertion alone.

The arrest warrant for Artur Virgílio Alves dos Reis was prepared on 6 December 1925. It named him as principal in a fraud against the Bank of Portugal and the Portuguese state, citing the unauthorized emission of banknotes through the London printer Waterlow & Sons. The same instrument named his associates: Karel Marang, the Dutch trader whose commercial connections had helped place the fraudulent currency; José Bandeira, the former diplomat who had carried the forged authorization to London; and several others whose roles in the Banco Angola e Metrópole’s operations had become clear through the previous week’s examinations. The warrants were distributed to police stations across Lisbon and to the port authorities at Cais do Sodré, with copies sent by telegraph to the coastal garrisons at Cascais and Setúbal. The net was being drawn with the deliberate thoroughness of a state that had finally recognized its own vulnerability.

Reis was not in Lisbon when the warrants issued. He had spent the previous night at a hotel in Estoril, the seaside resort where Lisbon’s prosperous families maintained villas and where, in December, the Atlantic winds stripped the last leaves from the imported palms along the Avenida Marginal. The hotel’s register recorded his arrival on the evening of 4 December, accompanied by his wife and his brother-in-law. He had given his occupation as “engineer” and his residence as “Lisbon,” particulars that were technically defensible and practically meaningless. The choice of Estoril was characteristic: close enough to the capital for business, distant enough to permit the illusion of retreat, associated with wealth and therefore with the respectability that Reis had always understood as a form of armor.

The first warning reached him through the hotel’s telephone exchange on the morning of 6 December. A call from his Lisbon office, taken by his brother-in-law, conveyed that police had been seen entering the Banco Angola e Metrópole’s headquarters on the Rua do Ouro. The details were fragmentary—the caller had hung up abruptly—but sufficient. Reis understood immediately that the structure he had built was collapsing, and that his own position within it had shifted from architect to fugitive. His response, reconstructed from subsequent police interviews and from the memoir he composed years later, revealed a man whose capacity for decisive action in construction had never extended to comparable clarity in retreat.

He spent the morning in his hotel room, moving between the window that overlooked the gardens and the writing desk where he composed a series of telegrams. The first was to Karel Marang in Amsterdam, warning of developments and requesting immediate consultation. The second was to a contact in a government ministry, inquiring whether diplomatic channels might still be available. The third was to his lawyer, arranging a meeting for the following day in Lisbon—a schedule already overtaken by events. Each message was drafted, reconsidered, redrafted. The telegrams that were actually dispatched, preserved in the postal records, show a man trying to maintain operational control over circumstances that had already escaped it. He was still composing when his wife returned from a walk along the seafront with news that uniformed police had been seen making inquiries at the railway station.

The decision to flee, when it finally came, was not Reis’s alone. António Bandeira, the former diplomat whose signature on forged documents had helped persuade Waterlow & Sons to print the unauthorized notes, arrived at the hotel shortly after midday. Bandeira had spent the previous day in Lisbon, attempting through former colleagues to ascertain the extent of official knowledge. What he had learned confirmed the worst: the Porto inspection had found notes whose serial numbers duplicated legitimate issues, a discovery that made denial impossible. The Bank of Portugal’s own staff were now cooperating with judicial investigators, providing access to records that Reis and his associates had assumed would remain protected by institutional loyalty or mutual complicity.

Bandeira’s arrival forced a choice. The two men conferred in Reis’s room, with Reis’s brother-in-law maintaining watch in the corridor. Their discussion, as later reported by Bandeira to police, turned on two questions: whether escape was still possible, and what resources remained available for the attempt. Reis argued for immediate flight to Spain, using the coastal road that connected Cascais to the frontier at Vila Real de Santo António.

Bandeira, drawing on his diplomatic experience, pointed out that border posts would already have received the warrants, and that a man traveling with his wife and visible luggage would attract precisely the attention they needed to avoid. He proposed instead a more circuitous route: south to Setúbal, then by coastal steamer to the Algarve, with the possibility of securing passage to North Africa from the port of Faro. This plan had the advantage of using maritime routes less thoroughly policed than the land frontier, and of drawing on Bandeira’s residual contacts in the consular service.

The disagreement between them was not merely tactical. It reflected a deeper divergence in how each man understood their situation. Reis, according to his own account, still believed that the fraud could be presented as a legitimate if irregular banking operation, that the political connections they had cultivated would ultimately protect them, and that the essential task was to avoid custody long enough for these defenses to be mobilized. Bandeira, with longer experience of how states abandoned inconvenient servants, understood that their former protectors would now be positioning themselves to deny all knowledge. His concern was not with clearing their names but with physical escape, and his patience with Reis’s strategic fantasies was wearing thin.

They compromised on immediate removal from Estoril. Reis dispatched his wife and brother-in-law by train to the family property at Sintra, with instructions to maintain normal appearances and to deny knowledge of his whereabouts. He and Bandeira would travel separately to a prearranged meeting point in Lisbon, there to finalize arrangements for departure. This division of forces, though presented as prudent, already contained the seeds of their failure. Reis could not abandon the apparatus of his constructed identity—the wife, the respectable connections, the appearance of legitimate business—while Bandeira could not fully commit to a flight that Reis himself seemed unwilling to complete.

The journey to Lisbon took three hours. Reis traveled by hired automobile, using a route that avoided the main coastal highway in favor of inland roads through Queluz and Benfica. Bandeira went by train, carrying a diplomatic passport that he believed would protect him from casual inspection. They reunited at a café near the Rossio station, chosen for its anonymity and its proximity to the maritime agencies of the Praça do Comércio. The afternoon’s business was passage: Bandeira had telegraphed ahead to contacts in the shipping lines, seeking berths on any vessel departing for Spanish, African, or South American ports.

The responses were discouraging. The German line reported no departures for three days. The British service had suspended operations pending the holiday season. The Portuguese national carrier required advance booking and official clearance for passengers to its African territories—clearance that would now certainly be denied. Only one possibility remained: a coastal trader was scheduled to depart Setúbal on 8 December with cargo for Luanda and Lisbon. Its master, approached through an intermediary, indicated willingness to accept passengers without formal documentation for a substantial fee.

The vessel represented both opportunity and trap. Its destination was Angola, the colony where Reis had first constructed his fraudulent credentials and where he still maintained commercial interests that might provide cover for continued operations. But it was also the destination he had been traveling toward when police surveillance had forced him into hiding, and its departure schedule allowed forty-eight hours during which discovery remained possible. Reis hesitated. Bandeira pressed. The compromise they reached—Reis would board at Setúbal while Bandeira attempted to secure alternative arrangements through diplomatic channels—preserved their alliance while ensuring that neither man fully trusted the other.

They separated at dusk. Bandeira took rooms at a pension near the Alfama, using a name from his consular service that he believed would not appear on police lists. Reis traveled south by the last train to Setúbal, carrying a single suitcase and the cash reserves he had maintained against precisely this contingency. The night passage through the industrial suburbs and the salt flats of the Tagus estuary gave him hours of enforced reflection, the rhythm of the wheels offering a counterpart to the arguments that continued in his mind.

He had constructed an empire of paper and persuasion, and it had functioned precisely as designed until the moment when someone had thought to compare one number against another. The fragility of his achievement was now visible to him with painful clarity. What had seemed a masterwork of institutional manipulation was revealed as dependent on the continued inattention of clerks.

The Setúbal that received him was a working port, smaller than Lisbon and therefore more thoroughly policed by a force with fewer distractions. Reis took lodgings at a fisherman’s tavern near the docks, paying in advance and explaining his presence as preliminary to a business voyage. The proprietor, accustomed to irregular comings and goings, asked no questions. The vessel lay at anchor in the outer harbor, visible from the tavern’s upper window, its masts and funnel black against the December sky. Reis watched it through the evening, measuring his situation against its promise of departure.

The telegram reached him at nine in the morning. It was from his wife, relayed through the Sintra post office, and it conveyed that police had visited the family property seeking his whereabouts. His brother-in-law had been taken for questioning. The network of protection he had assumed was already being dismantled, person by person, connection by connection. More disturbing was the postscript: a Lisbon newspaper had published that morning a detailed account of the fraud, naming him explicitly and describing the issuance of arrest warrants. The governor’s managed disclosure had finally collapsed into open accusation. The state was no longer containing the scandal but feeding it, sacrificing the principals to preserve the institution.

Reis’s response to this information, as he later described it, was not fear but anger. The anger was directed not at himself, nor even at the Bank of Portugal officials who had exposed the fraud, but at the political allies who had accepted his support and were now positioning themselves as discoverers rather than beneficiaries. He composed in his mind a series of defenses, arguments, counter-accusations that might yet turn the narrative in his favor.

The banknotes were genuine, printed by authorized contractors from official plates. The Banco Angola e Metrópole had operated as a legitimate financial institution, serving commercial needs that established banks had neglected. Any irregularities in authorization were administrative matters, not criminal offenses, and should be addressed through civil negotiation rather than police action. These arguments, which had sustained his confidence through months of operation, now served to delay recognition that the moment for argument had passed.

He spent 7 December in the tavern, venturing out only to confirm the vessel’s departure time and to purchase provisions for the voyage. The ship’s master, a heavy man with scarred hands, required payment in advance and made clear that his obligations extended only to transportation. No questions would be asked about Reis’s documentation, but no protection would be offered if authorities boarded in Portuguese waters. The terms were harsh and the price extortionate, but Reis had no alternative. He handed over the equivalent of three months’ salary for an ordinary clerk, watching his liquid resources diminish with the transaction.

Bandeira’s situation in Lisbon had deteriorated similarly. His diplomatic contacts, approached through the informal channels that had served him in the fraud’s construction, responded with the careful neutrality of men who sensed infection. The ministry, he was informed, had no knowledge of any authorization for his activities and no capacity to intervene in a domestic criminal matter. Former colleagues who had accepted his hospitality now declined his calls. The consular passport that he had believed would provide cover was, he discovered, already listed in police bulletins as a document possibly carried by a fugitive. The apparatus of state that he had manipulated was now deployed against him with the same efficiency he had once exploited.

Their reunion on the morning of 8 December was forced by circumstance rather than planned. Bandeira, finding his Lisbon contacts useless, had traveled to Setúbal in the hope of joining Reis’s departure. He reached the tavern at dawn, having walked from the station through streets still empty of official presence. The two men confronted each other across the tavern’s single table, each seeing in the other the failure of his own calculations. Reis had believed that political connections would shield them; Bandeira had believed that diplomatic status would secure passage. Both beliefs had proved illusory, and their mutual recognition of this fact dissolved the alliance that had sustained the fraud.

The argument that followed was witnessed by the tavern proprietor, who later provided police with a detailed account. Reis insisted that Bandeira’s presence on the vessel would endanger them both, that the former diplomat’s distinctive appearance and known identity made him a liability in any escape attempt. Bandeira countered that Reis’s own notoriety was now equal to his own, that the newspaper accounts had made his face familiar to every police constable in Portugal, and that separation offered no protection to either. The dispute turned on money as well as strategy. Reis had paid for his passage; Bandeira had exhausted his reserves in the failed Lisbon negotiations. Each accused the other of having drawn more than his share from the common fund. The tavern proprietor, listening from the kitchen, heard voices raised in the accents of educated men unaccustomed to contradiction.

The vessel’s departure was scheduled for noon. At eleven, a police launch was observed making routine inspection of ships in the outer harbor. Reis, watching from the window, interpreted this as confirmation of his warning to Bandeira; Bandeira, watching the same scene, interpreted it as evidence that delay would be fatal. They reached no agreement. When Reis left for the docks shortly before the appointed hour, Bandeira followed at a distance, maintaining the pretense of separate travel that each believed might yet protect him.

The arrest occurred on the quay. Police records indicate that the Setúbal constabulary had received the Lisbon warrants by telegraph on the evening of 7 December, with supplementary instructions to monitor all departures for African and South American ports. The officer who approached Reis, a sergeant who had served fifteen years in the maritime police, asked first for his papers and then, receiving unsatisfactory responses, for his name. Reis provided a false identity, supported by documents that the sergeant recognized immediately as inadequate. The confrontation that followed was brief. Reis attempted to offer explanation, then protestation, then—according to the police account—attempted flight toward the ship’s gangway. He was restrained without difficulty. The suitcase he carried contained clothing, cash in several currencies, and a notebook with entries that would later assist prosecution.

Bandeira, witnessing this from the shelter of a warehouse doorway, made no attempt to intervene. His subsequent movements, traced through police reports and his own later testimony, took him back through Lisbon and north toward the Spanish frontier at Vila Verde da Raia. He traveled by a series of local trains and hired vehicles, using names and stories that varied with each transaction. The diplomatic passport that had failed to secure official protection proved adequate for the informal negotiations of rural transport, and he crossed into Spain on the night of 9 December, believing himself temporarily secure. Spanish authorities, notified of the Portuguese warrants through standard intergovernmental channels, arrested him at Irun on 10 December and returned him to Portuguese custody by the following morning.

The capture of the principals did not end the flight of their associates. Karel Marang, in Amsterdam, received Reis’s warning telegram but interpreted it as opportunity rather than alarm. He spent 6 and 7 December attempting to secure control of funds deposited in Dutch banks, only discovering on 8 December that these accounts had been frozen at the request of Portuguese authorities acting through diplomatic channels. His own attempt at flight, by train to Germany and thence toward Switzerland, was intercepted at the Dutch-German frontier, where his distinctive appearance and foreign accent attracted the attention of customs officials who had received circular descriptions. He was returned to Amsterdam for extradition proceedings that would occupy the subsequent months.

The Banco Angola e Metrópole’s other directors and employees, less strategically significant and therefore less thoroughly sought, presented themselves voluntarily at police stations on 6 and 7 December, professing ignorance of any irregularity and offering cooperation with investigators. Their statements, preserved in the judicial file, show a remarkably consistent pattern: each had understood the bank to be a legitimate enterprise, each had executed instructions without knowledge of their fraudulent basis, each was shocked—shocked—to learn of the deception practiced upon them. The credibility of these protestations would be tested at trial, but their immediate effect was to populate the prosecution’s witness list with individuals who had been, weeks before, enthusiastic participants in the conspiracy’s commercial operations.

Reis himself, in the Setúbal police post and subsequently in the Lisbon judicial prison, maintained the defensive posture that had characterized his final hours of freedom. He demanded to see his lawyer, refused to answer questions without legal consultation, and insisted that his activities had been legitimate business operations distorted by political enemies. The notebook confiscated at his arrest, examined by investigators, contained notations that supported this interpretation in part: lists of political contacts, records of contributions to electoral campaigns, calculations of voting strength in the Bank of Portugal’s shareholder meetings. The fraud had indeed served political purposes, and Reis’s determination to reveal these connections was not merely self-protective but potentially destabilizing to a republic already shaken by scandal.

The state’s response to this threat was procedural. Reis was held incommunicado for seventy-two hours, a period during which his lawyer’s petitions for access were denied and his family was informed only that he was “assisting with inquiries.” The interrogations conducted during this period, as later described in court testimony, focused not on the fraud’s mechanics but on his political associations, not on the unauthorized notes but on the uses to which their proceeds had been put. The investigators were building a case that would present Reis as an isolated criminal rather than a participant in systemic corruption, a narrative that would protect institutions by sacrificing individuals.

The transfer to Lisbon occurred on 10 December, under guard and by night, a precaution against the public demonstrations that his case had begun to attract. The prison at Limoeiro, where he was confined pending trial, had housed political prisoners of every affiliation since the republic’s founding, and its population recognized in Reis a type they knew well: the entrepreneur of influence whose operations had exceeded the tolerance of those who had protected him. His fellow inmates, as he later reported, were less interested in his guilt or innocence than in the practical question of how much money remained accessible, and through what channels.

The conspirators were in custody, delivered to the state for judicial reckoning.
