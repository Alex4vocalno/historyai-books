Chapter 8

The Pragmatic Turn (1997–1998)

The frustration in the room was palpable, a product of success rather than failure. In February 1998, in Palo Alto, a group of software developers and industry thinkers gathered around a table not to write code, but to choose words. They were united by a shared conviction that the collaborative model of software development had proven itself beyond doubt, and by a shared annoyance that the business world refused to see it. The technical validation was complete; the community had scaled, and the legal skirmishes with proprietary interests—specifically the unsettling 1995 invoice from The Santa Cruz Operation (SCO) that had cast a shadow over Linux—had been navigated.

Yet the language they had inherited was now their greatest obstacle. To corporate executives and investors, the phrase “free software” was toxic. It conjured not the philosophical freedoms of the GNU Manifesto, but “free of charge”—a suggestion of anti-commercial hobbyism, unreliable code, and a vaguely threatening ideological stance. The movement stood at a threshold, possessing a superior engineering method but lacking the vocabulary to make it palatable to the institutional power it needed to influence. The decision made that day was one of deliberate, pragmatic rebranding.

They would launch an “Open Source Initiative” and promote a new term: “open source.” This was not an evolution of principle, but a calculated shift in marketing, designed to reframe a proven collaborative model in the instrumental terms of business—reliability, cost, and speed. The open-source movement’s pragmatic turn began not with a compiler or a kernel patch, but with a conscious choice of lexicon aimed at bridging the chasm between the hacker’s bazaar and the executive’s boardroom.

This strategic pivot was a direct response to pressures that had been building since the Linux kernel’s explosive growth in the mid-1990s. By 1997, the operating system commonly referred to as Linux—the product of combining Linus Torvalds’s kernel with the GNU system utilities—was a formidable, globally maintained platform. Its technical viability was no longer in question. Yet its very success underscored a deeper problem. The “free software” label, and the moral philosophy carried by Richard Stallman and the Free Software Foundation, acted as a powerful social and rhetorical barrier.

As free software developed, the Free Software Foundation began to look how to bring free software ideas and perceived benefits to the commercial software industry. It was concluded that FSF’s social activism was not appealing to companies and they needed a way to rebrand the free software movement to emphasize the business potential of sharing and collaborating on software source code. The term was formalized in 1998 with the founding of the Open Source Initiative, succeeding the free software movement initiated in the 1980s, with the intention of overcoming the ambiguity of the word “free” — meaning both freedom and gratis — to better appeal to the business world.

Corporate lawyers heard “free” and thought of uncontrollable code and lost intellectual property; managers perceived a social movement rooted in anti-corporate sentiment, not a legitimate development methodology. The enduring tension between open collaboration and proprietary control, now fully manifest and legally fraught, had crystallized into a crisis of communication. The community had won the engineering argument but was losing the narrative war. To cross into the mainstream, it needed a new story, one that translated the bazaar’s chaotic genius into the sober language of business benefits.

The intellectual blueprint for this translation had been published just months earlier. In his 1997 essay “The Cathedral and the Bazaar,” Eric S. Raymond analyzed the Linux development model, contrasting the traditional, closed “cathedral” style of building software with the open, decentralized “bazaar” model he observed. His argument was meticulously pragmatic. He framed the bazaar’s success not as a triumph of ethics, but of practical outcomes: “Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow.” He pointed to the virtues of early and frequent releases, of treating users as co-developers, of harnessing decentralized curiosity for quality control. The essay provided a ready-made, business-friendly narrative for the collaborative model that had proven itself in the crucible of the Linux kernel and the legal scrutiny of the previous chapter.

Thus, the gathering in Palo Alto functioned as a council of translators. Attendees like Raymond and publisher Tim O’Reilly understood that to capitalize on the model’s validation, they needed to surgically decouple it from its philosophical origins. The term “open source” itself was suggested at that meeting by Christine Peterson in 1998. It was selected for its neutrality and its positive, transparency-focused connotations. It described a practical characteristic—the availability of source code for peer review and modification—rather than an abstract ideal of liberty. Shortly thereafter, the Open Source Initiative (OSI) was founded as a dedicated advocacy and stewardship body. The term was formalized in 1998 with the founding of the Open Source Initiative, succeeding the free software movement initiated in the 1980s, with the intention of overcoming the ambiguity of the word “free” — meaning both freedom and gratis — to better appeal to the business world.

Its first major act was not to draft a new license but to author a definition. The OSI’s Open Source Definition, adapted from the Debian Free Software Guidelines, listed ten criteria focusing on permissions for use, modification, and redistribution. It was a functional specification for collaborative software, a checklist of practical conditions deliberately devoid of ethical proclamation.

This deliberate reframing ignited an immediate and escalating conflict with the established camp of free software idealism. For Richard Stallman and the Free Software Foundation, this was not a friendly modernization but a fundamental betrayal—a deliberate dilution of the movement’s core purpose. Stallman argued that “open source” advocated merely a development methodology while willfully ignoring the ethical ends. The FSF’s position held that software must be free to protect essential user freedoms: the freedom to run, study, change, and share. These were inalienable rights, not optional engineering tactics.

The new open-source advocates countered that emphasizing these freedoms first scared away the very corporations and governments whose adoption could achieve the most practical good for software liberty in the long term.

It was a classic schism between purity and pragmatism, between holding fast to principle and compromising to propagate influence. The conflict played out across essays, conference talks, and fervent online debates. Stallman famously likened the difference to that between “a campaign for a cleaner city and a campaign against littering.” One aimed at a fundamental, virtuous state of affairs; the other addressed a visible symptom with a practical solution. The open-source proponents retorted that their campaign against the “litter” of buggy, insecure, vendor-locked software was the only winnable battle that could realistically lead to a cleaner digital environment.

They actively crafted and promoted a new sales pitch to press and industry analysts. Their talking points were meticulously designed for corporate ears: reduced total cost of ownership, elimination of vendor lock-in, superior security through transparent peer review, accelerated innovation by pooling R&D across organizational boundaries. The rhetoric of “users’ rights” was shelved in favor of the language of ROI and competitive advantage.

This rhetorical shift was mirrored and reinforced by a consequential evolution in legal strategy—a clear example of what this history terms License Drift. Stallman had crafted the GNU General Public License (GPL) as a “copyleft” instrument, designed to perpetuate freedom through a viral clause: any derivative work distributed must also be released under the same free terms. It was a powerful legal tool for idealism, ensuring free software could not be co-opted into proprietary products.

For many businesses, however, the GPL’s reciprocity requirement was a major deterrent. It appeared to dictate their own licensing strategies and threatened their core proprietary assets. The pragmatic turn thus actively embraced more permissive licenses like the BSD license or the emerging Apache License. These licenses allowed source code to be viewed, modified, and even incorporated into proprietary, closed-source products with minimal obligations, often requiring only attribution. They were engineered as tactical tools to remove legal friction for corporations. Advocates argued this permissive strategy would seed open-source code everywhere, even deep within proprietary systems, creating a vast shared foundation and de facto influence.

Critics, including Stallman, saw it as a surrender of essential leverage, allowing commercial interests to absorb the commons without any guarantee of giving back, thereby hollowing out the long-term project of software freedom.

A strong counter-explanation for this period might argue that open source’s ascendance was primarily a deterministic outcome of superior networked engineering efficiency and economic logic. According to this view, its institutional forms and conflicts were superficial epiphenomena of an underlying, inevitable trend toward decentralized, modular production in software.

The evidence from 1997-1998 complicates this technological determinism. The technical viability of the bazaar model was undoubtedly a necessary condition for its spread, but it was not sufficient. Without the deliberate, contentious effort to rebrand and reframe—to create the “open source” narrative and institutionally back it with the OSI—the model might well have remained culturally ghettoized, persistently perceived as ideologically hostile to business practice. The corporate world was not logically destined to adopt a model wrapped in the rhetoric of anti-corporate activism. Efficiency alone does not dismantle deeply ingrained cultural and institutional barriers.

The institutionalization of this new vocabulary was swift and methodical. In the weeks following the Palo Alto meeting, Tim O’Reilly leveraged his publishing network and industry connections to orchestrate a media campaign that framed “open source” as an emerging business trend rather than a social cause. He understood that trade press and analysts operated on a different clock than hacker communities; they required packaged narratives, market categories, and quotable experts. O’Reilly thus positioned figures like Eric Raymond as industry analysts in their own right, translating the bazaar model’s dynamics into the language of disruptive innovation and competitive strategy. This conscious media engineering was critical. It ensured that when journalists sought to cover Linux’s rising profile, they now had an alternative, less charged term to use—one that came pre-vetted by a recognizable industry figure like O’Reilly rather than emanating solely from the Free Software Foundation’s ideological headquarters.

Concurrently, the nascent Open Source Initiative began functioning as a standards body for the term itself, a move that subtly mirrored corporate practices of brand management. By stewarding the Open Source Definition and approving licenses that conformed to it, the OSI provided a seal of conceptual consistency. For a corporate legal department evaluating a new software component, this offered a manageable heuristic: if the license was OSI-approved, it belonged to this new category of “open source,” with known characteristics and risks. This bureaucratic framing was intentionally dull—it turned revolutionary collaborative practice into a matter of compliance checklists—which was precisely its strength as a tool for diffusion into risk-averse institutions.

The push for pragmatic acceptance also tapped into a broader business zeitgeist of the late 1990s. Corporate IT departments were grappling with the dual pressures of preparing for the Y2K date-change crisis and navigating the chaotic expansion of the internet. In this environment, arguments about reliability and cost reduction found exceptionally fertile ground. Open-source advocates adeptly linked their model to these universal pain points. They presented peer-reviewed open-source code as inherently more auditable and secure for mission-critical systems, a potent argument in an era suddenly anxious about systemic software failure. The promise of escaping vendor lock-in resonated with managers weary of punitive licensing fees from dominant proprietary vendors like Microsoft and Sun Microsystems. Thus, the rebranding succeeded not only because it invented a new term but because it connected an existing model to pre-existing corporate anxieties and strategic goals.

Within the developer community itself, reaction to this pragmatic turn was mixed and often generational. Many younger programmers who had cut their teeth on Linux in university labs saw no contradiction between using open-source tools and pursuing lucrative careers in the burgeoning dot-com sector. For them, Raymond’s pragmatic arguments aligned with their own experience; they had seen better software emerge from open collaboration and saw no need to foreground an ethical struggle they hadn’t personally inherited from the 1980s hacker culture Stallman represented. Older stalwarts of the free software movement, however, viewed the new terminology with deep suspicion, fearing it would create a fork not just in language but in community solidarity. Online forums and mailing lists became battlegrounds where accusations of “selling out” met counter-accusations of “purity politics” that hindered real-world progress.

This internal tension highlighted a fundamental asymmetry in resources between the camps. The Free Software Foundation operated as a principled but thinly staffed nonprofit, sustained by donations and Stallman’s formidable personal stamina as a speaker and evangelist. The open-source camp, by contrast, quickly attracted tacit support and even funding from commercial entities that stood to benefit from its success—not just O’Reilly Media but also from startups and established firms exploring open-source business models. This difference in material backing amplified the reach of the pragmatic message, enabling professional marketing efforts, high-quality conference organization, and polished publications that simply outstripped what the purely idealistic wing could produce.

The emphasis on permissive licensing as a tool for corporate adoption warrants closer examination, as it represents a profound strategic divergence from copyleft philosophy. Permissive licenses like BSD or MIT were not new; they originated in academic environments where sharing code was customary with minimal strings attached. Their revival and promotion in 1998 were deliberate tactical choices. Advocates argued that by allowing corporations to embed open-source code into proprietary products without obligation, these licenses would act as a “trojan horse” for openness. The hope was that once companies grew dependent on these shared components and experienced their benefits internally, they might gradually open other parts of their own codebase or at least become advocates within their industry for open standards. This was a theory of incrementalism—gain influence through utility first, ethical conversion later.

Stallman countered that this theory was naive and historically blind. He argued that permissive licensing amounted to unilateral disarmament, giving corporate interests all they wanted—high-quality shared infrastructure—without securing any guarantee that freedom would propagate in return.

The shift required human agency: a group of actors making a conscious strategic choice to accelerate history by changing its vocabulary and legal tools. The institutional form of “open source,” as opposed to “free software,” was not mere surface noise but a critical catalytic intervention that altered the trajectory of adoption.

The success of this rebranding was neither instant nor universal. Throughout 1998, the two terms coexisted in an uneasy truce within the community, often used by individuals to signal their philosophical allegiance. Yet momentum steadily gathered behind the pragmatic frame. Major industry publications like InfoWorld and Wired began adopting “open source.” Market analysts at firms like Gartner and Forrester started producing reports on its implications. The term provided a safe, neutral conceptual container for executives and IT managers to explore this collaborative world without first having to publicly endorse a philosophy they distrusted or even understood. It allowed the method to be evaluated on its purported practical merits. The ultimate test of this new framing’s viability, however, would not emerge from within the software community’s own debates.

It would come from a commercial player in crisis, a corporation willing to bet its very survival not just on using open-source software, but on embracing the open-source idea as a core strategic weapon. This test arrived with stunning rapidity, presenting the newly coined terminology and its pragmatic philosophy with its first real-world, high-stakes crucible. The choice of words made in the quiet of Palo Alto was about to be validated or shattered by a monumental business decision made just miles away in Mountain View. That decision would thrust the rebranded movement onto the front pages of the Wall Street Journal and into the harsh light of corporate strategy, offering an unprecedented chance to prove its commercial logic on a Fortune 500 stage. The pragmatic turn had crafted its message; now it needed a messenger desperate enough to shout it from the rooftops.
