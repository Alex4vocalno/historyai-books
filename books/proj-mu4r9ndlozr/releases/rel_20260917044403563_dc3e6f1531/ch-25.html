Chapter 25

The Canal Built on French Failure

Seen from above, the landscape of the newly acquired canal zone presented a scarred tableau of abandonment and arrested ambition. A vast, unfinished trench cut through the continental divide, its jagged edges softened by encroaching vines and choked with debris. Across the scarred terrain, the relics of a previous endeavor lay scattered: rusting machinery, silent dredges, and listing locomotives on forgotten sidings, while a ghost town held rows of empty wards.

On 4 May 1904, the United States formally took control of the canal property, inheriting from the French a depleted workforce and a vast jumble of buildings, infrastructure, and equipment, much of it in poor condition. The Isthmian Canal Commission (ICC), established by Congress and reporting to Secretary of War William Howard Taft, was given control of the Panama Canal Zone to oversee construction. The commission was directed to avoid the inefficiency and corruption that had plagued the French 15 years earlier. The New Panama Canal Company, the French successor to the Compagnie Universelle, had maintained the concession in salable condition under the terms agreed with Colombia and now with the newly independent Republic of Panama. The sale price was forty million dollars. The transfer did not represent a clean start. The acquisition was of a ruin.

The first American surveyors who walked the spoil banks at Culebra carried copies of French engineering maps. The maps showed contours, elevations, and proposed alignments that the French had surveyed at considerable cost over six years. Some were faded. Some were water-damaged. The surveyors cross-referenced what the maps showed against what the terrain revealed. The French had moved roughly 30 million cubic yards of earth and rock across the isthmus before the liquidation in February 1889. A significant portion lay in the Culebra section, where the cut had reached a depth that American engineers would later calculate as useful to their own alignment. The earth had not moved again. It sat in neat spoil banks along the route, a physical ledger of what the French had spent and what they had accomplished before the money ran out and the fever took the workforce.

The inventory that the ICC compiled in the second half of 1904 ran to hundreds of items: old French locomotives, some repairable; dredges of various designs, some sunk at their moorings; buildings at Empire, Cristóbal, and Gatún that could be re-roofed and reoccupied; hospitals at Colón and Panama that still held iron bed frames and ceramic tile. The Panama Railroad, running 47 miles from Colón to Panama City, remained operational though its rolling stock was aged. The railroad was itself a French inheritance, purchased by the Compagnie Universelle in 1881 from the original American company that had built it in 1855. The Americans now owned the railroad twice over: once as the successor to the French purchase, once as the successor to the original American builders.

Three tracks now ran in parallel. The engineers surveyed the cut. The medical officers surveyed the hospitals. The commission in Washington surveyed the political and financial question of what kind of canal to build. Each track confronted the same French inheritance and reached a different conclusion about its value.

The engineering track moved first. John Findley Wallace, the first chief engineer appointed by the ICC, arrived on the isthmus in May 1904 and departed in June 1905, having concluded that the conditions were unworkable under the constraints Congress had imposed. His successor, John Frank Stevens, arrived later that year and made a decisive break with the French plan. Stevens examined the Culebra Cut, the Chagres basin, and the surveys that the French had left behind.

He then recommended that the United States abandon the sea-level canal and build a lock canal instead. The lock design would dam the Chagres River at Gatún, creating an artificial lake 85 feet above sea level, and would lift ships through three locks on the Atlantic side and three on the Pacific. The excavation through the continental divide would still be massive, but the total volume of earth to be removed would be far less than a sea-level channel would demand.

The French had debated this exact question at the 1879 congress. The sea-level plan had won, not because every engineer present believed it superior, but because Ferdinand de Lesseps had insisted that a lock canal was unworthy of the age that had built Suez. The congress proceedings recorded the objections. The Chagres River, which rose by several meters during the rainy season, would flood any sea-level trench. The geology of the Culebra ridge was unstable. The volume of excavation required to cut a sea-level channel through the divide exceeded what any company could finance. These objections had entered the record. They had been overruled. The company had spent nine years and several hundred million francs proving them correct.

Stevens did not need to rediscover these problems. The French record had already identified them. What Stevens did was act on what the French record had established and the French company had refused to process. The lock canal was an admission that the sea-level plan was not technically impossible but organizationally and medically unsustainable. The isthmus could be cut at sea level, given unlimited time, unlimited money, and a workforce that did not die. The Compagnie Universelle had none of these. The United States, possessing greater resources, chose not to test whether it could obtain them.

The medical track ran parallel and proved more consequential. When the Americans took control on 4 May 1904, the hospitals at Colón and Panama still stood. The French had built them well — spacious, tiled, well-ventilated, with iron bed frames that could be disassembled and sterilized. They were excellent hospitals. They had been excellent hospitals when the French were dying in them. Between 1881 and 1889, an estimated 22, 000 men had perished on the isthmus, the majority from yellow fever and malaria. The hospital registers of Colón and Panama recorded the names and dates of admission. The diagnoses were entered in a clinical hand. The dates of death followed, sometimes within days. The registers survived. The ICC medical officers consulted them.

Colonel William C. Gorgas, appointed chief sanitation officer in 1904, arrived on the isthmus with a specific theory. The French medical establishment had rejected the mosquito-vector theory of yellow fever transmission when it was first proposed. The French hospital at Colón had continued to treat yellow fever patients in open wards, with standing water in containers, and with screens on only some windows. The hospital itself was a breeding ground. Gorgas had read the French mortality records. He had also read the work of the Cuban sanitarian Carlos Finlay and the American Walter Reed Commission, which had demonstrated in 1900 that the Aedes aegypti mosquito carried yellow fever. Gorgas applied this knowledge to the isthmus.

The sanitation campaign began in 1905. Gorgas’s crews drained standing water, oiled pools where mosquitoes bred, installed screening on every building, and isolated yellow fever patients in mosquito-proof wards. The campaign was methodical, expensive, and initially resisted by engineers who considered it a distraction from construction. The French had spent fortunes on hospitals and doctors. Gorgas spent his on killing mosquitoes. The difference was causal. French medicine had treated the disease after transmission. American sanitation prevented transmission. Yellow fever disappeared from the canal zone by 1906. Malaria rates fell more slowly but fell. The workforce that the French had lost to disease, the Americans kept.

The third track, in Washington, concerned the political decision. Congress had authorized the purchase of the French assets and the construction of a canal. It had not definitively specified whether the canal would be at sea level or with locks. The debate in Congress in 1905 and 1906 replayed, in compressed form, the debate at the Paris congress of 1879. A sea-level canal was more elegant. A lock canal was more practical. The sea-level plan had the prestige of the original conception. The lock plan had the weight of the French evidence against it. Congress chose locks. The decision was made not on abstract engineering grounds but on the concrete record of what the French had encountered and what they had failed to overcome.

The three tracks converged. The lock design reduced the excavation required. The sanitation campaign preserved the workforce to perform it. The French excavations at Culebra provided the starting point. American engineers did not begin a new cut. They widened, deepened, and extended the cut the French had begun. The alignment was the same. The spoil banks the French had built became the foundations for American rail lines and American dumps. The French railroad carried American workers. The French hospitals, re-screened and re-staffed, treated American patients — though far fewer of them died.

The tension between French claims and Panamanian memory ran through this inheritance. French engineers, including Philippe Bunau-Varilla, argued that the French excavations had saved the Americans years of labor and millions of dollars. Bunau-Varilla, who had served as an engineer on the isthmus during the French period and who had acted as intermediary in the negotiation that transferred the canal property to the United States, maintained this position in his memoirs and public statements. The French work, he insisted, was not a waste but a down payment. The figures he cited — cubic yards moved, kilometers surveyed, structures built — were checkable against the company’s own reports. They were substantial. They were real. The earth had been moved. The maps had been drawn. The cut had been started.

Panamanian laborers and local residents remembered the French period differently. For them, the French excavations were not a down payment but a graveyard. The 22, 000 dead — West Indian, Colombian, French, Italian, Spanish, Chinese — lay in cemeteries at Colón and Panama and in unmarked graves along the route. The hospital registers recorded the names of some. Others were entered only as numbers. The registers, which the Americans consulted, listed the cause of death with clinical precision. The registers did not record the grief.

The French and Panamanian accounts differ on the meaning of the same excavations. For the French, the cut at Culebra was evidence of progress interrupted. For the Panamanians, it was evidence of lives consumed. Both accounts were accurate. The earth had been moved. The men had died.

The canal that the Americans built did not resolve this difference. It compounded it. The American success made the French excavations useful, which made the French deaths useful, which made the grief harder to express and easier to bury.

The excavation at Culebra continued from 1904 through 1913. The American engineers deepened the cut to a design elevation that accommodated the lock-canal plan. The French had excavated to a width and depth that served a sea-level channel. The Americans widened the cut further. The geology that the French had encountered — the unstable shale and basalt of the Culebra ridge — produced the same landslides. The American engineers documented slides at Culebra that carried hundreds of thousands of cubic yards of rock back into the cut within days of removal. The French reports had described the same phenomenon. The American reports described it again. The difference was that the American workforce, protected from disease and supplied with adequate financing, could remove the slide material and continue. The French workforce, dying and underfunded, could not.

The landslides at Culebra were not a problem the Americans solved. They were a problem the Americans endured. The cut was wider than the French had planned because the slides required gentler slopes. The design was adjusted. The excavation continued. The French experience had established that the geology was hostile. The American experience confirmed it. The French had failed not because the geology was impossible but because the combination of hostile geology, uncontrolled disease, and insufficient organization exceeded what the Compagnie Universelle could sustain. The Americans faced the same geology. They controlled the disease. They organized the work. They succeeded.

The first lock gates at Gatún closed in 1913. The Gatún Dam, which impounded the Chagres River to create Gatún Lake, was completed that year. The dam was built across the valley the French had surveyed. The lake covered terrain the French had mapped. The locks used a design that bore no resemblance to the lock plan Gustave Eiffel had contracted to build for the Compagnie Universelle in 1887, when the company had finally and belatedly abandoned the sea-level concept. Eiffel’s locks, designed for a two-level canal at a different location, were never built. His design, published in his 1906 memoir, was presented as a mature engineering solution. The design was, in fact, a last-ditch conversion that the company had ordered when the sea-level plan was already collapsing. The Americans built their own locks.

The French inheritance at Gatún was not Eiffel’s design but the Chagres itself — the river that the 1879 congress had been warned about, the river that the French had never controlled, the river that the American dam now held.

The canal opened to traffic on 15 August 1914. The SS Ancon, an American steamship, transited the canal from Atlantic to Pacific. It passed through the Gatún locks, crossed Gatún Lake, entered the Culebra Cut — now renamed the Gaillard Cut — and emerged at Balboa. The transit took several hours. The excavation had taken ten years of American work and nine years of French work before it. The earth that the French had moved was part of the channel. The maps the French had drawn had guided the American alignment. The hospitals the French had built had housed the American medical campaign. The railroad the French had purchased had carried American workers and American supplies. The cut the French had started was the cut the Americans finished.

The completion of the canal produced a new historical judgment. The Compagnie Universelle du Canal Interocéanique, which had collapsed in liquidation in 1889 and whose directors had stood trial before the Tribunal of the Seine in 1893, appeared in a different light once its excavations had become part of a functioning waterway. The company had been judged as a fraud. The trial of 1893, which had reached the Chamber of Deputies and exposed the bribery of 150 legislators, had established that judgment in the public mind. The financial scandal — the lottery bonds, the slush funds, the bribes paid to journalists and politicians — was the dominant memory. The canal’s completion did not erase this memory. But it complicated it.

The excavations were real. The surveys were real. The maps were accurate. The cut was usable. The company had been corrupt. It had also been competent, in part, and its competence had produced a physical inheritance that the Americans had purchased and used.

The judgment shifted from fraud to tragedy. Not because the fraud had been excused, but because the tragedy had been redeemed.

This shift was not universal. The French engineers who had worked on the isthmus and survived to see the canal’s completion emphasized the value of what they had built. Bunau-Varilla, who had devoted his career after 1889 to keeping the French project alive and transferring it to the Americans, insisted that the French contribution was essential. The Panamanian laborers who had buried their countrymen in the French cemeteries did not share this emphasis. The West Indian workers who had dug the French cut and then dug the American extension remembered the French period as a catastrophe. The American engineers who used the French maps and the French excavations acknowledged the inheritance but did not dwell on it. Their reports cited the French surveys. Their payrolls did not cite the French dead.

The counter-explanation that the Panama project was simply beyond what the 1880s could engineer or treat medically has merit. The sea-level plan required the removal of a volume of earth that exceeded what the available labor force, dying at the rates the hospital registers recorded, could have moved in any plausible timeframe. The Chagres River flooded the cut. The geology produced slides. Yellow fever killed between a third and a half of the French workforce in some seasons. These conditions were not failures of will or intelligence. The conditions were real. The French company did not invent them. It encountered them.

But the conditions were not the whole story. The 1879 congress had been warned about the Chagres. The engineers who surveyed the route reported the floods. The medical officers who treated the fever knew the mortality rates. The company’s own bulletins published the figures. The decision system of the Compagnie Universelle did not process these warnings as constraints on the plan.

It processed them as problems to be overcome by capital, by reputation, by the name of Ferdinand de Lesseps. The confidence capital that had built Suez — the conversion of a personal reputation into investable funds — was applied to Panama as though the isthmus were a larger Egypt. The isthmus was not a larger Egypt. The Chagres was not the Nile. The fever was not the climate of Port Said. The company’s decision system could not distinguish between these facts and the name-driven optimism that had raised 400 million francs from French investors.

The American success forces a reckoning with this distinction. The Americans faced the same isthmus, the same Chagres, the same Culebra ridge, the same diseases. They did not possess superior engineering genius. Their engineers were competent, some of them exceptional, but the basic technology of steam shovels, dredges, and dynamite was not materially different from what the French had employed. The Americans possessed superior organization, superior financing, and — critically — the knowledge that the French failure had produced. The knowledge was specific. The French had proved that the sea-level plan was unsustainable. The Americans built locks. The French had proved that yellow fever was the decisive variable. The Americans killed the mosquitoes. The French had proved that the Chagres could not be managed by diversion alone. The Americans dammed it. Each of these decisions was a direct response to a documented French failure.

The conversion of French failure into American foundation altered the judgment of the Compagnie Universelle in a way that the 1893 trial had not. The trial had judged the company as a criminal enterprise. The canal’s completion judged it as a tragic precursor. The trial had examined the bribes. The canal examined the cut. The trial had heard from politicians and financiers. The canal heard from the earth. The earth said that the French had moved 30 million cubic yards of it. The earth said that the alignment was correct. The earth said that the cut was usable. The earth did not say that the company had been honest. The earth did not say that the investors had been wise. The earth said that the work had been real.

The Republic’s Absorber — the mechanism by which a private failure was converted into a public verdict that protected the state from systemic blame — had operated in 1893. The trial had convicted the corrupt and exonerated the Republic. The canal’s completion performed a similar absorption at a different scale. The American government purchased the French failure for forty million dollars. The purchase converted the French collapse into an American asset. The Americans did not absorb the blame for the French failure. They absorbed the excavations. The blame remained with the Compagnie Universelle, but the excavations became American property. The conversion was not moral. It was commercial. The Americans bought the cut. The cut became part of their canal. Their canal succeeded. The success reflected back on the French excavations and made them appear, in the new judgment, not as a waste of capital but as a first attempt.

The confidence capital that had been destroyed on the isthmus between 1881 and 1889 did not disappear. It was converted. The French investors who had lost their savings did not recover them. But the reputation that Lesseps had deployed to raise those savings — the reputation of the man who had built Suez — survived in a new form. The canal’s completion validated the idea that a canal across Panama was possible. The idea had been Lesseps’s. The execution had been American. The validation reflected backward. The Compagnie Universelle had been right that a canal could be built. It had been wrong about how. The wrongness had cost 22, 000 lives and 1.8 billion francs. The rightness had produced the excavations the Americans used. The historical judgment held both.

The men who had died building the French cut were buried along the route. Their graves were unmarked. The canal was open. The American success forced a new reckoning: what did it imply about French engineering, finance, and national competence that the same isthmus, the same river, the same ridge, and the same diseases that had destroyed the Compagnie Universelle had yielded, within a decade, to a different organization with different decisions? The reckoning did not excuse the fraud. It did not restore the losses. It did not resurrect the dead. It established that the failure had been specific, not inevitable, and that the specificity was the legacy.
