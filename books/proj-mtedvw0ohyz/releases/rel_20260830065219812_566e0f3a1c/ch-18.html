Chapter 18

The Quiet Life in Sanremo

The municipal ledger for commercial licenses in Sanremo recorded a new entry in the spring of 1922. On a line for the proprietor’s name, a clerk wrote “Pietri, Dorando.” Under type of business, he inscribed “caffè e pasticceria.” In the space for the establishment’s name, he noted, without ceremony, “Bar Dorando.” The document was formulaic, indistinguishable from those for a dozen other new shops. Its power lay in its context and the conscious choice it represented. For the man who submitted the application, this stamped sheet of paper was the foundational charter for a new life—an instrument of erasure. Here, amid dry bureaucracy, Dorando Pietri formally concluded his career as a public spectacle and inaugurated his existence as a private shopkeeper.

The energy that had once propelled him onto global stages was gone, dissipated into the collective exhaustion of the age. This license was its quiet tombstone. It codified a retreat into the measurable, controllable rhythms of commerce, a world governed by inventory lists and daily receipts, not by headlines and cheering crowds.

Sanremo was a deliberate selection. It offered neither the fraught familiarity of his native Carpi nor the metropolitan bustle where his past might be recognized. It was a town of resort hotels and palm-lined promenades, defined by seasonal transience and polite anonymity. The clientele were tourists, winter residents, and convalescents, their attention fixed on climate and scenery. In such an environment, the name “Dorando” on a café awning could pass as a proprietor’s whimsical touch, not an incitement to historical inquiry.

His move followed the failure of a hotel venture he had undertaken with his brother, a business failure that underscored he was not as successful an entrepreneur as he was an athlete.

The bar itself was a modest space. Its operations followed the unremarkable cadence of provincial Italian life. Pietri opened early for the workers’ morning coffee. He managed the lull of late morning, oversaw pastry preparation in the small kitchen, and presided over the gentle resurgence of activity during the afternoon passeggiata and the evening aperitif. This was a life built on repetition—a stark, deliberate contrast to the discontinuous, hysterical schedule of his touring years, where each new city had demanded a reenactment of a single, catastrophic afternoon.

This stable, localized existence found its personal counterpart in a second document. In December 1924, Dorando Pietri married Teresa Dondi, a woman from his own region of Emilia-Romagna who possessed no connection to athletics or public performance. The marriage certificate, like the business license, was a record of conventional, private settlement. It promised domestic continuity, not dramatic arc. The couple made their home in an apartment connected to the bar, their days entwined with its daily cycle. They had no children. Their world contracted to the radius of the café, the local market, and the seafront.

By all surviving accounts, Pietri proved a diligent and respected shopkeeper—quiet, reserved, and intensely private. The management of the Bar Dorando required a stamina of a different order than the marathon: the patience for ledger-keeping, the resilience to endure slow seasons, the social tact to maintain a reliable clientele. He demonstrated these qualities in full. The man who had famously been unable to navigate the final 385 yards of an Olympic race without repeated collapse navigated two decades of small business in a tourist town without any visible crisis.

This quiet competence existed in a state of perpetual, low-grade tension with the very myth that had financed the café’s fixtures. The establishment’s name was its central paradox. “Bar Dorando” was both a claim to simple proprietorship and a permanent, public inscription of his other identity. For the occasional visitor versed in sporting lore, the sign was a jarring artifact. The myth of Pietri was not extinct; it was being steadily fossilized in the official histories of the modern Olympic movement. The marathon distance of 26.2 miles—that bureaucratic accident of the 1908 race born from the royal whim to start at Windsor Castle—was gradually being ratified by tradition into a global standard. Every time a new marathon was organized, the ghost of the White City Stadium finish was implicitly invoked. And here, serving espresso beneath that ghost’s own name, was the living man who had given it form.

He was inevitably asked. Journalists on assignment, passing athletic officials, nostalgic tourists would sometimes trace the name on the awning to its source. Their requests followed a familiar script: an interview, a commemorative photograph, a quote for an Olympic anniversary feature. Pietri’s response became as ritualized as his morning routine: a polite, unwavering refusal. He did not grant interviews. He declined to pose for pictures behind his own bar. He turned down invitations to civic celebrations or sporting commemorations. When conversation strayed toward the past, he would offer only vague and pleasant deflections, insisting the past was simply the past. He kept no museum of his own history.

The choice of Sanremo was not merely geographical but profoundly temporal. Pietri arrived on the Ligurian coast during the early years of Benito Mussolini’s regime, a period when Italy was being reshaped through a rhetoric of virile nationalism, disciplined vigor, and imperial ambition. Sport was swiftly becoming a central pillar of fascist propaganda—a tool to forge a physically robust citizenry and demonstrate national prowess. The regime actively cultivated and celebrated its athletic heroes, incorporating their images into a grand narrative of Fascist renewal.

In this atmosphere, a figure from Italy’s recent sporting past—especially one associated with a moment of such global resonance and poignant drama—could have been a valuable asset for local fascio officials seeking to animate parades with living history. Yet Pietri’s retreat was absolute. His bar was not a gathering place for political discussions or squadristi; it was a neutral ground for coffee and pastries. This quietude in the face of a state that increasingly demanded public participation and visible enthusiasm was itself a statement. By focusing exclusively on the ledger book and the espresso machine, Pietri performed a kind of civic invisibility, opting out of the new script being written for Italian manhood. His celebrated endurance was now channeled into enduring the political climate without engaging with it.

The daily mechanics of the Bar Dorando were those of any small hospitality business in an era before refrigeration. Success depended on a web of local relationships and physical labor. Each morning began with assessing deliveries of milk, coffee beans, flour, and sugar—commodities subject to interwar supply chains and, later, the autarkic policies of the 1930s. The pasticceria required its own precise alchemy: sourcing fresh eggs and butter, managing the oven’s temperamental heat, ensuring pastries were both appealing and affordable to a clientele whose economic circumstances tightened through the Depression.

Pietri’s role was manager, accountant, and often barman—a multitasking proprietor whose presence was the establishment’s keystone. His famous physical discipline found new expression: the steady hands to operate a gleaming Gaggia machine, the stamina to remain on his feet for twelve hours, the calm to mediate minor disputes. The athleticism was now entirely functional, stripped of spectacle.

This immersion in material concerns created a protective barrier. Conversations with regulars orbited local matters—the weather’s effect on tourism, the price of goods, neighborhood gossip—not the international sporting past. The mundanity of his enterprise became a shield. The man serving an amaro after dinner was first and foremost “Signor Pietri,” the bar owner; “Dorando,” the global icon, remained a secondary, dormant identity known only to those who made specific inquiry.

His marriage to Teresa solidified this insulated existence. Their partnership was a joint venture in domestic and commercial stability. Teresa likely handled aspects of kitchen management or household accounts, her presence reinforcing the bar as a family entity rather than a personal monument. Their childlessness simplified their social world, allowing their energies to be concentrated entirely on maintaining their self-contained universe against the economic and political currents of the era.

Pressure from the outside world arrived episodically, in waves coinciding with Olympic anniversaries or major sporting events. The 1932 Los Angeles Games and later the 1936 Berlin Olympics prompted editors to seek nostalgic angles, turning their gaze back to 1908. A letter or a journalist’s visit would pierce the quiet of the bar like an unexpected draft. Pietri’s refusals were consistent but not hostile; they were exercises in polite finality.

To understand their depth is to recognize what he was refusing: not merely an interview, but a specific narrative reassignment. The fascist state, had he assented, would have likely framed his story as one of early Italian grit and honorable sacrifice—a proto-fascist resilience misunderstood by effete Edwardian officials. Other seekers might have wanted a tale of personal redemption or peaceful contentment. Each request sought to extract him from his chosen context—the proprietor behind the counter—and reinsert him into their own narrative framework. His silence preserved his autonomy. It kept his story incomplete in public terms, denying it a neat concluding chapter written by anyone else.

There is profound historical significance in this sustained act of withholding. The modern media apparatus that had created “Dorando” operated on a logic of extraction and republication; it required fresh content to sustain interest. By providing none—by becoming a closed archive—Pietri effectively decommissioned himself as a source. He demonstrated that while spectacle could create celebrity, it could not compel perpetual collaboration from its subject. In Sanremo, Pietri proved it was possible for the raw material of global news to retire from circulation and reclaim a sovereign self defined by daily choices utterly disconnected from its origin point.

His relationship with physical space mirrored this internal withdrawal. The apartment connected to the bar formed a compact universe: living quarters directly adjoining workplace, overlooking perhaps a quiet courtyard or side street. The rhythms were circadian and seasonal—the summer influx followed by quiet winter months when Sanremo belonged more to its residents than to visitors.

Within these confines, objects from his past life existed as private possessions rather than public exhibits. Queen Alexandra’s cup, if stored away, was not a symbol of shame or triumph; it was simply an artifact from another life. Its value was purely personal and thus irrelevant to commerce or history. This physical compartmentalization—the past locked in a trunk, the present displayed on shelves behind the bar—was a masterful strategy for managing identity.

The Bar Dorando thus operated on two distinct levels: as a functioning business serving real needs in real time, and as an unintentional monument to narrative refusal. For every hundred customers who saw only a café name, one might see a haunting irony: that sport’s most famous loser had won his long game by mastering obscurity. He had traded the unbearable weight of symbolic meaning for the manageable weight of sacks of coffee beans. This trade-off is central to understanding his two decades in Sanremo not as decline but as consolidation: an active construction project where every day laid another brick in an ordinary life he had chosen precisely because it would be unworthy of international reportage.

The onset of World War II brought new layers of austerity and anxiety to Liguria, disrupting Sanremo’s insulated rhythms with rationing, shortages, blackouts, and fears of coastal vulnerability. Yet within this larger societal convulsion, Pietri’s small world maintained its fundamental contours. The bar likely adapted, offering what simple comforts it could amidst scarcity; its role as a community fixture perhaps deepened even as its material offerings diminished. This final external pressure tested a resilience built over two decades, proving that sanctuary, once built, could endure even as history stormed outside its doors.

The gold cup presented by Queen Alexandra—the symbol of the sympathy that had transformed his disqualification into celebrity—was reportedly stored out of sight, a private relic stripped of public meaning. The walls of the Bar Dorando were not adorned with framed headlines or iconic images of his distress. The space was dedicated to the daily commerce of food and drink, not to the curation of memory.

This studied silence was, in itself, a powerful assertion of control. For over a decade, Pietri’s identity had been authored by others: by officials who handled his fallen body, by journalists like Conan Doyle who composed his heroic narrative, by impresarios who packaged his collapse for the music-hall stage. His story had been a public commodity, its value determined by its reproducibility. Now, in Sanremo, he reclaimed the fundamental authorial right: the right to withhold, to redact, to remain silent. His refusal to participate was the logical, final act of his retreat.

If the modern sporting spectacle crystallized in 1908 was indeed a machine for generating reproducible media narratives—a system where drama and media-constructed heroes were bundled into a marketable product—then Pietri chose to withdraw his raw material from the production line. The “heroic loser” archetype that his disqualification had helped codify continued as a potent trope elsewhere, but its original model now refused all further performances. The tension between the immortal public myth and the aging private man did not escalate toward dramatic confrontation; it was resolved through two decades of steadfast, quiet omission.
