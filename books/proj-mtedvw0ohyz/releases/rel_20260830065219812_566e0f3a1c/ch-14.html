Chapter 14

The Distance Settles

The footsteps that had echoed around the world were now audible only to him, fading into the mundane sounds of a life after fame. In the summer of 1920, those footsteps were an echo from a different life, a private memory. But on a clerk’s desk in Antwerp, they were being translated into a permanent, public fact.

The document was the official programme for the Games of the VII Olympiad. Its cover was stiff card, its interior pages a dry listing of events, dates, and rules.

Under the heading for the marathon, scheduled for August 22, it stated the distance with bureaucratic finality: 42.750 kilometres. A simple conversion revealed the figure to be 26 miles, 385 yards. There was no asterisk, no explanatory footnote, no hint of novelty or debate. It was presented not as a choice but as a given, the self-evident distance for an Olympic marathon.

To an observer in 1920, this might have seemed merely logistical, a number pulled from a file of previous Games.

Yet its reappearance in Antwerp, after a catastrophic world war and two Olympiads that had used different lengths, was neither obvious nor inevitable. It was the answer to a question that had not yet been formally asked, the silent triumph of a story over a committee.

This sheet of paper, listing events and start times, was the physical proof of a gravitational shift. The odd, arbitrary measurement born from a British royal family’s viewing preferences at Windsor and a stadium architect’s plan in Shepherd’s Bush had, twelve years later, begun to shed its accidental origins. It was becoming the distance.

Dorando Pietri, the small Italian confectioner whose collapse had animated that London distance, was by 1920 a man in his mid-thirties whose competitive zenith lay firmly in the past. He had toured, he had raced for pay, and he had failed in his bid for Olympic redemption in Stockholm. The machinery of spectacle had consumed his prime and moved on, leaving him to navigate an identity untethered from the act that had defined it.

Yet the stage upon which he had stumbled, that specific and peculiar stretch of road from Windsor Castle to the White City Stadium, was experiencing a second life entirely separate from his own. His personal narrative had concluded its most dramatic arc, but the Narrative Distance he had christened with his agony was just beginning its institutional journey. The programme in Antwerp was its passport.

Why this number? The question is deceptively simple, and answering it requires tracing a chain of causality backwards through the war years, a chain where official decree followed rather than led.

The first link was Antwerp itself. The 1920 Games were an exercise in resurrection, an attempt to revive the Olympic ideal from the ashes of the Western Front. Organisers faced a multitude of profound decisions, from selecting a host city that had been occupied territory to integrating bereaved nations into the ceremony. The marathon distance was, on the surface, a minor logistical one. They had options. The 1908 London distance was just one of several used in major competitions since Pietri’s race.

The 1912 Stockholm marathon had been run over a different, shorter course of approximately 40.2 kilometres, a deliberate choice to approximate the classic Athenian route. The event’s legendary origin was itself a fuzzy ancient benchmark—roughly 40 kilometres from Marathon to Athens. There was no binding international law, no central sporting authority that had decreed 42.195 km as the sole permissible length.

Yet Antwerp chose London’s number. The choice was practical, but the practicality was itself born from narrative weight. The London marathon was the most famous, most dramatic, and most widely reported marathon in Olympic history. Its finish was the most photographed athletic moment of its era.

To select its distance was to connect the new, fragile peace-time Games to the pre-war apogee of Olympic theater and pathos. It was a legitimising gesture, a way to borrow the aura of that seminal event for a festival trying to prove the world could be put back together. The distance had become, in the minds of athletes, coaches, and the press, the authentic test, the true crucible for marathoners.

They had come to see 26 miles, 385 yards not as an oddity but as the standard for the ultimate race. Antwerp, consciously or not, ratified a consensus that had been forming in the athletic imagination since July 1908. This consensus had grown despite, not because of, official sanction from the sport’s nascent governing bodies.

The International Association of Athletics Federations (IAAF) was founded in 1912, the same year Stockholm opted for a different course. Its early focus was on codifying track and field events where standards could be neat and mathematical: 100 metres, 1, 500 metres, the precise weight of the discus. The marathon was an administrative outlier, a messy, road-based event with folkloric origins and historical imprecision. The IAAF showed little urgency in freezing one specific, inconvenient number from a single Games. Why would they?

The marathon was not a geometric abstraction; it was a story. And bureaucracies are not designed to standardize stories. The evidence of variability after 1908 is clear and underscores the IAAF’s initial reluctance.

In Britain, the Polytechnic Harriers’ marathon, the direct successor to the Olympic race on largely the same roads, maintained the London distance in 1909 and 1910. This was local continuity, a matter of convenience and tradition for a single club, not international law. Stockholm’s different measure in 1912 proved that other Olympic organisers felt no compunction to replicate London’s model. The marathon, in the immediate post-1908 years, remained fluid in length, a loose concept rather than a fixed product.

This fluidity is the strongest evidence against the idea that the 26.2-mile distance was an inevitable bureaucratic outcome. It was not. It was a contingent one, waiting for a force to solidify it.

That force was narrative. The 1908 race had been a global sensation, amplified by telegraphy and mass-circulation newspapers. Pietri’s collapse, the assistance, the disqualification, the moral victory—this sequence had forged an indissoluble link in the public and athletic mind between supreme marathon drama and the specific, gruelling challenge of 26 miles, 385 yards. That distance became the stage. The drama could not be separated from its setting.

When people recalled the greatest marathon, they recalled Pietri’s final yards in the White City Stadium; those yards were the culmination of that specific distance. The race’s reporting, its photography, and its endless retelling in newspapers and music halls had sanctified the entire route. The accident of its length—the desire to start at Windsor so the royal children could witness the dawn departure, and to finish in front of the royal box at the stadium—was transfigured by what transpired upon it.

The Narrative Distance was born: an arbitrary physical measurement transformed into an immutable standard solely because it was the territory upon which a foundational story occurred. This concept explains what broader forces like media technology cannot: why this specific number, and not another approximation of 40 kilometres, prevailed.

The First World War created a four-year hiatus in international competition, but this interlude served to harden the legend, not dissolve it.

Without new Olympic marathons to provide fresh benchmarks or alternative dramas, the memory of 1908 sat unchallenged, its dramatic contours softening into myth while its precise measurements remained in record books. The distance became synonymous with the peak of the event itself. When the war ended and the world of sport reassembled, the pull of that pre-war pinnacle was powerful. Antwerp’s organisers were not making a radical statement; they were, they likely felt, choosing the most famous and respected standard available.

It was a bottom-up adoption. The market of athletic prestige demanded it. The athletes lining up in Antwerp in August 1920 knew the history. They knew they were being asked to confront the same challenge that had broken Dorando Pietri and crowned Johnny Hayes. That knowledge alone added extra miles to the course.

The Antwerp marathon itself, won decisively by Hannes Kolehmainen of Finland, served to validate the distance’s competitive legitimacy. It was a brutally hot day, and the race exacted a heavy toll, but it concluded without the chaotic, controversial finish of 1908. This was crucial.

It proved the London distance was not a fluke tied to a single dramatic collapse but a viable, severe, and fair test of endurance. Kolehmainen’s victory, achieved in a time slower than Hayes’s 1908 mark, underscored the challenge. The distance could be mastered, but it would always demand the utmost. Its successful staging in Antwerp stripped it of its purely British idiosyncrasy and made it an international benchmark. Now the distance had two iconic Olympic finishes: one of tragic controversy, one of pure, hot, suffering triumph. The story was becoming a tradition.

Now the pressure of consensus shifted to the bureaucrats. The IAAF could no longer treat the marathon’s length as an open question. The event had been run over the same distance at two of the last three Olympic Games, and the one exception, Stockholm, now seemed the outlier. Athletic practice—the lived experience of competitors and the expectations of the sporting public—had created a de facto standard. The narrative had done the work of unification that committees had hesitated to undertake.

In 1921, at its congress, the IAAF formally ratified what the competitive world had already adopted.

Marathon races were first held in 1896, but as its ruling noted with bureaucratic finality—a line item in a list of regulations—the distance was now standardized at 42.195 km (26 miles, 385 yards).

It presented itself as the imposition of order upon chaos; in reality it was an official endorsement of a consensus forged by story and cemented by repeated practice since July 24th 1908 when Dorando Pietri began his race at Windsor Castle at 2: 33 pm among fifty-six competitors on what would become this official length from then onward until today’s races are still measured against this same exacting yardstick born out from one afternoon’s drama alone! This formal codification between 1914-1921 is thus truly paradoxical legacy left behind after all else faded away except these numbers themselves remain forevermore etched into rulebooks worldwide thanks largely due their association with such unforgettable human tragedy unfolding right before our eyes during those fateful moments back then…

The Narrative Distance became a permanent feature of the sporting landscape, more powerful than any top-down decree.

For Pietri, this institutional fossilisation of his moment of tragedy was an abstract legacy, separated from the daily reality of his life in Italy. He would never race an Olympic marathon again. Yet it cemented the stage upon which he had performed, casting it in stone for every marathoner who would follow. The distance was now permanent.

This permanence created a new, unyielding pressure. The archetypal marathon performer—the lone figure battling exhaustion, chasing glory on the road—now had a fixed and famous distance to conquer. The template for heroic failure was set.

And for the man who had authored that template, whose own body had first given the distance its meaning, this posed a silent, inescapable challenge. The stage he had made eternal was now a measuring stick against which any subsequent effort, including his own, would be judged. The world had agreed on the length of the race.
