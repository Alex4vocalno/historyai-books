Chapter 16

The War and the Forgotten Man

The document was a single sheet of paper, headed “Lista Passeggeri” and dated 20 March 1915. It recorded the departure of the S.S. Canopic from the port of Naples, bound for New York.

Among the names was one that would have resonated, briefly, six years earlier: Dorando Pietri, aged thirty, listed as a “commerciante,” a merchant. The manifest noted his last permanent residence as Carpi, Italy, and his final destination as the Hotel Marlton in Manhattan. There were no special annotations, no indication of celebrity. It was a routine administrative record of a transatlantic crossing, one of thousands generated in that final spring of a neutral America.

But for Pietri, it represented something else entirely: a last, desperate attempt to reconnect with the circuitry that had once made him famous. The path that had opened with his collapse in 1908—the path of professional opportunities, of global fame born from failure—had not merely narrowed after Stockholm. By 1915, it had been severed. This passenger list was its ghost, a paper trail leading back to a world that no longer existed.

That world, the one he was sailing toward in a futile hope of reviving, had been a specific and recent invention. Before August 1914, Dorando Pietri’s professional life was not that of an athlete in any amateur sense.

It was that of a performer within a nascent international entertainment industry, one directly engineered from the melodrama of the London marathon. The public subscription in the Daily Mail, spearheaded by Arthur Conan Doyle, which raised funds to establish him in a bakery, had been merely the first act of a swift commercialization. Conan Doyle had proposed the fund, contributing the initial five pounds, and it reached a considerable sum of three hundred. The sentiment was leveraged with remarkable speed.

By November 1908, he was racing Johnny Hayes in a sold-out Madison Square Garden. Over the next few years, he crisscrossed the United States and Europe for a calendar of exhibition races and music-hall appearances. His value was narrative, not merely athletic. He was the man who had collapsed, the hero who had lost, the figure whose very disqualification had made him more famous than the official winner.

This economy depended on a fragile set of conditions: peace, which allowed the free flow of people and capital across borders; a mass-circulation press hungry for human-interest stories; and an entertainment sector with the resources and inclination to stage sporting melodrama as live spectacle. It was a closed, self-reinforcing circuit. The press amplified the drama, the drama sold tickets to the exhibitions, and the exhibitions generated more press. Pietri was both a product and a proof-of-concept for this system.

The system’s mechanics were precise. A promoter would secure a venue, often a velodrome or a large theater. Advertisements would highlight the “Marathon Hero” or the “Tragic Dorando,” trading on the instantly recognizable imagery of his collapse. The race itself was often over a shortened distance, designed for maximum excitement rather than endurance, a direct translation of the 1908 finale’s tense, cinematic climax into a replicable commercial product. The results were secondary; the point was to witness the legend in motion, to participate in a retelling.

This was not sport evolving organically through broader socio-technological forces like telegraphy or urbanization, though those forces enabled it. This was a particular cultural form invented around a single event and its most marketable participant. The 26.2-mile distance, a bureaucratic accident of the 1908 course, was already becoming institutionalized as the marathon’s official length precisely because of the mythic weight that race had accumulated—myth built substantially around Pietri’s journey. His career demonstrated that the distance, the drama, and the celebrity were parts of an interconnected package.

The outbreak of war in August 1914 did not simply interrupt this circuit. It dissolved its foundational elements. Transatlantic travel became dangerous, then politically fraught, then impossible for citizens of belligerent nations. The press, in Britain, France, Germany, and soon Italy, turned its immense machinery toward war reporting, propaganda, and the grim taxonomy of casualty lists. The public appetite for sentiment shifted catastrophically.

The pathos of a single man collapsing on a cinder track was supplanted by the industrialized tragedy of thousands collapsing in the mud of Flanders or on the Carso plateau.

The music halls and theaters that had hosted Pietri’s appearances either closed, converted to patriotic revues and recruitment rallies, or found their audiences had no patience for trivial sporting melodrama. The economic oxygen was cut off. Contracts for exhibitions, which had sustained him, evaporated. The flow of newsprint that had carried his name now carried names of a different order: battles, treaties, fallen soldiers.

Pietri’s voyage to New York in the spring of 1915 was therefore an anachronism, a personal attempt to defy this systemic collapse. America was neutral. Perhaps, the thinking must have gone, the circuit could still function there. Perhaps the American public, removed from the trenches, might still have the capacity for his kind of story. The passenger manifest is the stark evidence of the attempt.

But the attempt failed. The tour, if it was ever formally arranged, left no trace of success. No record exists of packed houses or renewed press fascination. The machinery had rusted. American promoters now dealt in different commodities.

The public gaze was fixed across the Atlantic, on the war that was consuming the very nations from which Pietri’s fame had sprung. He was a specter from a receding past, a curious artifact in a present that had no use for him.

He returned to Italy, a nation that had just entered the war on the side of the Allies in May 1915. His professional world was not paused; it was obliterated.

The final, formal document of this erasure was his military conscription paper. The Italian army, mobilizing millions for a war of unprecedented scale and attrition, had no category for “global sports icon.” It had categories for age, residence, and fitness. Dorando Pietri, former confectioner, was assessed and assigned. The transformation was now bureaucratically complete.

The man whose individual struggle had been broadcast worldwide, dissected in newspapers, and orchestrated into a commercial touring package became a number in a vast, impersonal system. The contrast was absolute. The 1908 marathon had elevated the individual, crafting a hero from a loser.

The war subsumed the individual into the mass, where heroism was anonymous and loss was statistical. Pietri traded the white shorts and sweater of the runner for a soldier’s uniform. The arena of his performance shifted from the measured, public oval of a track to the chaotic, hidden contours of a battlefield.

This was the completion of a trajectory. The fame born in 1908 had always been parasitic on a particular set of peaceful, commercial, media-driven conditions. It was a cultural formation, not a personal attribute. The war proved that formation’s profound contingency. It was not robust enough to survive a historical cataclysm.

The modern spectator sports culture it pioneered—with its dependence on international travel, mass media amplification, and sentimental narrative—was revealed as a phenomenon of a specific, pre-war moment. When those conditions vanished, the culture itself went dormant, and its protagonists became instantly obsolete.

Pietri’s experience as a soldier remains largely unrecorded, as befits the anonymity of total war. But the mere fact of his conscription underscores the argument.

He was not drafted because he was Dorando Pietri; he was drafted in spite of it. His celebrity granted him no exemption, offered no parallel track. It was irrelevant. The forces that had once magnified his every step now ignored his existence.

He became, for all intents and purposes, a forgotten man. The audiences that had cheered his name were now elsewhere, consumed by a different and more terrible drama. The reporters who had chronicled his training were now chronicling troop movements. The promoters who had booked his tours were out of business or dealing in other goods. The circuit was broken, its wires cut.

The war did not just erase his present career; it actively erased the past’s relevance. The concerns of 1908—the disputes over amateurism, the exact measurement of the marathon distance, the moral quandary of officials assisting a fallen runner—seemed, by 1916, to belong to another epoch, a quaint and distant age of innocence. The monumental scale of the war reshaped public memory, compressing and distorting pre-war events.

Pietri’s drama shrank in retrospect, becoming a minor anecdote from a lost world. This was the true eclipse of his celebrity: not a fading, but a forcible relegation to irrelevance by a historical event that rewrote all prior priorities.

His return to civilian life after the war—a return not of triumph but of survival—would thus be to a landscape utterly changed. The commercialized spectacle he had embodied was gone. The circuits were not just silent; their very infrastructure had been repurposed or decayed.

The man who disembarked from the Canopic in 1915, hoping to revive his career, had already encountered this new reality. His failure in America was the definitive proof. There was no market to reconnect with, because the market itself—a network of taste, attention, and capital—had been dismantled. He was a performer without a stage, a hero without a narrative, because the theater had been burned down and the audience had dispersed to more urgent business. The pressure of this completed obsolescence was absolute.

The machinery of Pietri’s pre-war fame operated with a precision that masked its underlying fragility. Promoters functioned as impresarios, booking not just an athlete but a narrative package. They negotiated with theater managers for dates that avoided clashes with other spectacles, coordinated with newspapers for advance publicity, and arranged travel logistics that kept Pietri moving between venues in a continuous loop of performance and profit. His appearances were seldom mere races; they were theatrical events, often preceded by short films or lantern slides showing his London collapse, ensuring every audience member, whether in Cleveland or Manchester, understood the story they were paying to witness. The runner himself became a curated exhibit, his body the primary relic of a modern passion play.

This entire edifice relied on an uninterrupted flow of information and people across borders that, after August 1914, ceased to be neutral channels and became strategic assets or deadly hazards. The transnational telegraph wires that had carried race results and human-interest stories now hummed with diplomatic cables and military dispatches. Steamship companies like the White Star Line, which had facilitated tours, saw their vessels requisitioned for troop transport or refitted as hospital ships. The very concept of leisurely transatlantic travel for entertainment evaporated. For performers like Pietri, whose market was transcontinental, this was not an inconvenience but an existential severance. The circuit didn’t just lose power; its physical pathways were dismantled or repurposed for the war effort.

Pietri’s 1915 voyage to America, therefore, was a journey into a perceptual void as much as a geographic one. While America remained neutral, its cultural landscape was already transforming under the war’s shadow. The press, though not yet consumed by domestic casualty lists, was saturated with war reports and debates over preparedness and intervention. The appetite for light European melodrama, even one as well-crafted as Pietri’s, had diminished amid graver concerns. His status as an Italian citizen also became a complicating factor after Italy entered the war in May 1915, casting him not as a universal hero but as a national of a newly embattled ally, his story suddenly framed by a different, more urgent kind of patriotism.

His conscription papers formalized this shift from global symbol to national subject. The bureaucratic process that assessed him would have noted his occupation—likely still listed as commerciante or atleta—but such distinctions held no weight in the machinery of mass mobilization. The Italian army of 1915-1918 was a levelling institution, grappling with the horrific demands of trench warfare on the Isonzo front. Its need was for bodies capable of holding a line or mounting an assault, not for ones capable of captivating crowds over 26 miles.

It asked a single, concrete question of the man: what do you do when the system that defined you, that gave your specific talents meaning and value, vanishes? The answer, for Dorando Pietri, was written in the mundane script of millions of his contemporaries. You soldier on. You return to the place you started from, before the circuit ever electrified your name. You become a local figure again, a former athlete, a veteran, a man with a past that feels increasingly like someone else’s story.

The global icon was gone, leaving behind only the historical artifact, waiting for a later age to dust it off and wonder at the brief, brilliant flash of his fame. The war had settled the matter. The marathon of 1908 had invented modern sport’s template, but the war of 1914 proved that template was fragile, a creation of its moment. And the man at the center of that creation was now stranded in time, his celebrity not just faded but functionally void, a currency from a regime that no longer governed. The path had not just narrowed.

It had been plowed under, leaving him standing on empty ground, with nowhere left to run.
