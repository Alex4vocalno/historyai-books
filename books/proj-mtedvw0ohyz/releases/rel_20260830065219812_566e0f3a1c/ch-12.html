Chapter 12

The Machinery of a Modern Myth

The contract was a standard form from a Milanese confectioner, its printed clauses interrupted by blanks filled in with a steady, unfamiliar hand. Dorando Pietri’s signature, when it came, was a practiced flourish at the bottom, finalizing the transfer. The document granted exclusive use of his name and likeness for a line of packaged chocolates.

It was early 1910, not long after his return from South America, and the act was purely administrative. There was no race to win here, no stadium to enter, only the conversion of a right into revenue.

The man who signed was an athlete whose competitive days were narrowing; the entity he licensed was a myth entering its commercial maturity. The contract did not require him to run a single step. It merely required him to be Dorando Pietri, which was now a function of commerce as much as sport.

This signature was a pivot point, visible only in retrospect. His athletic journey was reaching its natural terminus.

He had run his final marathon in Buenos Aires on the 24th of May, 1910, achieving his personal best time of 2h 38min 48.2s. The fact was a private irony: the peak of his physical capacity at the very distance that made him famous coincided with the end of his viable career in it.

The South American tour had been less a sporting circuit than a commercial exhibition, a schedule where his presence was the product and the race results often secondary. He returned to Italy with earnings that would have been incomprehensible to the confectioner’s apprentice from Carpi three years earlier: 200, 000 lire in prize money over his brief, intense professional career.

He invested a significant portion in a tangible asset—a hotel—a businessman’s prudent hedge against an athlete’s perishable capital. Yet the larger economic activity sparked by his name was flowing into channels he did not own or operate.

The chocolate contract was one such channel. His signature monetized the residual sentiment of 1908, converting the public’s memory of a stricken runner into a sweet, shelf-stable commodity.

The “Dorando” brand would trade not on his current speed or future victories, but on the durable, endlessly recyclable image of his past collapse. This was the machinery of a modern myth finding its operational rhythm.

Between 1910 and 1911, Pietri’s persona was systematically packaged, licensed, and sold through the nascent systems of early twentieth-century mass culture. The process followed a logic alien to athletic competition. Sport concerns itself with peaks and troughs, victory and defeat, the inevitable decline of the body. Commercial brand management, in its infancy but already discernible, concerns itself with replication, recognition, and the extension of a narrative’s profitable life.

The Pietri phenomenon achieved a critical abstraction in these years. The emotional core of the London marathon—the repeated falls, the disputed finish, the Queen’s pity—had proven to be infinitely reproducible. It was a story that had sold newspapers, filled music halls, and now could move merchandise. The network that managed this abstraction was largely of others’ making.

It consisted of agents like the influential William Bankier, who brokered his stage tours; publishers who saw the value in an authorized biography; and manufacturers who recognized that a famous name could move cocoa beans and sporting goods.

Pietri’s role shifted from performer to licensor, from athlete to asset. His physical presence was no longer the primary product; his signature and his likeness were. The chocolate was merely the most literal form of consumption.

Its emergence signals a key phase in the “Spectacle Script” first drafted in the mud and cinders of the White City Stadium. The script’s components—the dramatic arc of near-victory, the human tragedy of collapse, the moral ambiguity of the assistance, the royal denouement—were now being analyzed and replicated not just by journalists and orators, but by promoters and factory owners. They had identified a formula. The script could be detached from its original actor and embedded in other media, other products. A bar of chocolate could evoke the entire parable: the bittersweet taste of heroic failure, the accessible luxury of shared sentiment.

Other artifacts followed the same commercial logic. Souvenir postcards, printed in large runs, froze Pietri in his moment of iconic distress—on the track, supported by officials, or in a studio portrait styled as the noble invalid. These were not spontaneous mementos but manufactured ones, produced by photographic firms that had identified a steady market. The postcard turned a public event into a private possession, a slice of history one could mail to a friend. Each purchase reaffirmed the myth’s hold, each image a brick in the growing edifice of his commercial identity.

The authorized biography represented a more ambitious piece of packaging. While the exact publication date remains obscure, its appearance in this period is telling. A biography codifies; it seeks to become the definitive version. It transforms ephemeral newspaper reports, partisan commentary, and folk memory into a permanent, saleable history. This volume would have been the work of publishers and likely a ghostwriter, part of a network extending the Pietri brand into the literary marketplace.

The confectioner’s contract was not an isolated innovation but a symptom of a broader commercial awakening in Italy and across the Atlantic world. The early 1910s witnessed the rapid maturation of branding and endorsement as formal business practices, moving beyond the casual use of a famous face on a trade card.

Companies were beginning to understand that a recognizable persona could confer legitimacy and emotional resonance on a mass-produced good, transforming it from a mere product into a vessel for shared narrative. Pietri’s management, particularly through agents like Bankier, positioned him at the forefront of this shift. His name was not merely advertised; it was leased, becoming an integral component of a product’s identity.

This systematic licensing required a legal and promotional infrastructure that itself was newly robust, comprising standardized contracts, royalty agreements, and coordinated advertising campaigns in illustrated magazines and daily newspapers. The “Dorando” chocolate thus existed at the intersection of evolving trademark law, advances in color lithography for packaging, and a public increasingly accustomed to purchasing not just an item, but a piece of a story.

This commercial abstraction was further enabled by Pietri’s physical removal from elite competition. No longer measured against the stopwatch and the world’s best marathoners, his market value became purely symbolic, immune to the depreciating effects of athletic decline.

His exhibition schedule, meticulously arranged through his agents, functioned less as a sporting tour than as a traveling brand activation. Each stop—whether in a provincial Italian piazza or a South American velodrome—was less about the race result and more about the ritual re-enactment of his myth for a paying audience. The local promoters who booked him were selling nostalgia and proximity to legend; Pietri’s jogged victory in a handicapped race against local amateurs was simply the live proof of authenticity that justified the souvenir postcards and program sales. His body, still fit but no longer extraordinary, became a reliable prop in a standardized commercial routine. This circuit insulated his financial worth from his competitive worth, creating a sustainable economic model that relied on consistent, controlled repetition of his core narrative rather than on unpredictable athletic triumph.

The production of his authorized biography must be understood within this framework of commercial stabilization. In an era before radio and film could permanently capture a voice or movement, the printed book offered the most durable vessel for a public figure’s curated legacy. A biography transformed a living person, subject to the vagaries of future performance and scandal, into a fixed literary character. For Pietri, a book promised to canonize the “Spectacle Script,” freezing the emotional arc of 1908 as the definitive account and drowning out any contradictory or less marketable memories. The publishers investing in this project were not philanthropists; they were betting on the longevity of the Pietri brand and the public’s desire to own a tangible, “official” version of a story they already knew.

The biography’s text would inevitably smooth over the complexities and controversies—the precise nature of the assistance, the internal disputes with officials, the grueling realities of the professional tour—to present a seamless, inspirational saga perfectly suited for the middle-class bookshelf. It was the ultimate act of packaging, converting ephemeral newsprint into a lasting commodity that could be sold and resold for years.

Simultaneously, the souvenir postcard trade operated on a more immediate, visceral level. Firms like Stereoscopic Company and other European publishers mass-produced images that captured Pietri at his most iconic—and most marketable—moments: the collapse, the assisted struggle, the recovery. These were not candid shots but carefully selected and often staged images designed to elicit a specific emotional response: pity, admiration, sentimental identification.

The postcard’s format was key to its commercial success; it was cheap to produce, easy to distribute, and served a dual function as a collectible and a piece of mail, allowing the sender to participate in spreading the myth. Each purchase was a micro-transaction that affirmed the cultural value of Pietri’s story, and each card that traveled through the postal system acted as a tiny, self-replicating advertisement for the Pietri industry. This economy of images created a visual shorthand for his legend, ensuring that even those who had never seen him run would recognize the tableau of his defeat.

The machinery, however, did not run autonomously. It required the active, if sometimes passive, compliance of Pietri himself.

Its purpose was not to uncover new truth but to solidify a profitable narrative—to standardize the legend for mass distribution. In doing so, it placed Pietri’s story on the shelf alongside other serialized adventures and celebrity lives, a commodity to be bought and consumed. The biography served as the official transcript of the “Spectacle Script,” ensuring that each retelling would hew to the established, market-tested plot points.

This commercial machinery was lubricated by the very media structures that had amplified his story initially. Newspapers like the Daily Mail, which had so fervently covered the 1908 marathon and his subsequent tours, now provided the advertising space for “Dorando” products and the announcements for his exhibition appearances. The line between reportage and promotion became indistinct. His name in a headline sold copies; his name on a product, advertised in those same pages, generated revenue for a separate commercial stratum. The ecosystem was becoming symbiotic and self-sustaining.

Pietri’s ongoing activities—the exhibition races across Europe, his final competitions in Italy and Sweden—were less noteworthy as athletic contests than as publicity events that fed the machine. They were live enactments of the brand, maintaining name recognition even as the athletic performances themselves waned.

His final races underscore this divergence. After the personal best in Buenos Aires, his competitive calendar continued, but its nature changed. He ran a 15-kilometer race in Parma on the 3rd of September, 1911, and won. His very last race was in Gothenburg, Sweden, in October of that same year. He was twenty-six years old.

The results were almost beside the point. These events were contractual obligations, pieces of a touring schedule that existed because the myth had created an audience, not because the athlete was at his peak. The man was still running, but the persona was now doing most of the heavy lifting. T.
