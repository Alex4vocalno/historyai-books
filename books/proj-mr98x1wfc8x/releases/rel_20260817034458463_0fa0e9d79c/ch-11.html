Chapter 11

The Final Defeat and Enduring Legacy (1961-1970)

Freighters offloaded artillery shells, aviation gasoline in rubber bladders, and powdered milk stamped with Cyrillic labels at the Red Sea port of Hodeidah; convoys then climbed thirteen switchbacks toward Sanaa while snipers picked off drivers from basalt ridges above. By late May 1967, Egyptian logisticians calculated that sixty thousand soldiers in Yemen were consuming supplies at a rate that would have sustained three divisions along the Israeli frontier for half a year. Four brigades constituting Egypt’s strategic reserve—the Fourth Armored Division stripped of its T-54s because they could not negotiate mountain grades; elements of the Third Infantry Division trained at Frunze for desert mechanized war; independent paratroop battalions that should have served as Cairo’s rapid-reaction force—were instead dug into highland siege lines where altitude sickness sometimes killed more men than enemy bullets. The mountains north of Taiz swallowed supply trains in monsoon mudslides. Egyptian pilots flew MiG-17s so low through valleys to avoid radar that wings clipped cliff faces; maintenance crews counted flight hours against rock walls rather than Israeli jets.

On paper these units remained part of Egypt’s order of battle; in reality they served as an imperial constabulary scattered through villages where poisoned wells demanded permanent garrison. Cairo’s planners discussed this hemorrhage only in whispers because honest assessment implied that the President’s post-Suez gamble—restoring pan-Arab credibility through revolutionary intervention—had opened an institutional wound that would not close. That wound began in October 1962 when Imam Ahmad died and republican officers seized Sanaa expecting modest aid; instead they received Egyptian regiments because Nasser could not stomach another Arab capital escaping his orbit so soon after Damascus had slipped away. Where Syrian secession had dissolved in administrative confusion—clerks stuffing misfiled deeds into crates before fleeing Aleppo—the Yemeni opportunity promised muscular redemption through force rather than paperwork. Within eighteen months an airlift of advisors became an expeditionary corps fighting Saudi-financed royalists armed with British rifles; within three years the commitment devoured fifteen percent of Egypt’s annual budget at a moment when industrialization plans required capital at home. This overextension directly caused the humiliating collapse of his forces during the Six-Day War, stripping away the ideological gains achieved a decade earlier.

The highlands degraded Egypt’s military doctrine as thoroughly as they bled its treasury. Crews rotated home after nine-month tours having learned counterinsurgency patrolling useful only for internal security; tankers forgot long-range desert maneuver because they spent their deployment guarding mountain passes as static infantry; signals officers accustomed to line-of-sight radio in flat Sinai found granite escarpments blocking transmission and resorted to couriers on donkeys for division-level communications. By early 1967 readiness reports—those few that reached Nasser unfiltered—noted ammunition stockpiles depleted by Yemen expenditures and pilot training hours below thresholds for combat against modern air forces. Yet when Israeli officials warned that closing the Straits of Tiran would constitute casus belli, Nasser’s inner circle read the threat through the prism of Suez rather than military reality. They remembered British ultimatums delivered and defied; they remembered Eisenhower’s Treasury halting sterling support; they remembered international pressure rescuing Egypt before Anglo-French divisions secured their objectives.

Israel possessed no imperial parliament vulnerable to American financial leverage, no United Nations forum where its survival could be bargained away by great powers. Israeli survival was calculated in hours measured by jet fuel endurance and tank tread wear; Nasser calculated in diplomatic weeks measured by Security Council sessions—a temporal mismatch fatal to armies positioned for political theater rather than operational necessity. When Egyptian armor rumbled into Sinai during the third week of May it moved without adequate air cover because frontline fighter squadrons were undergoing post-Yemen overhaul or deployed southward toward Arabia. Field commanders who requested permission to disperse aircraft according to wartime protocols were denied; dispersal might signal alarm to foreign embassies watching Cairo International Airport from rooftop terraces. The morning of June 5 arrived with meteorological clarity over northeastern Egypt—hot by seven o’clock with visibility unlimited across flat desert—and ended Egyptian sovereignty over Sinai before noon. Israeli pilots approaching from the Mediterranean flew below radar coverage along commercial air corridors before climbing sharply over their targets.

Egyptian commanders who should have received automatic alerts instead received conflicting telephone calls from air defense headquarters whose lines jammed with civilian traffic because no one had activated military priority circuits on what was technically still peacetime footing inside Egypt proper. By eight-fifteen smoke columns rose above Almaza airbase northeast of Cairo where rows of MiG-21s sat fully fueled for afternoon patrols; by nine-thirty every major base within two hundred miles of the frontier reported catastrophic losses; by eleven Field Marshal Amer was issuing orders from Qasr al-Nil Street whose wording suggested he believed only isolated raids had occurred rather than systematic annihilation. The ground war that followed was less combat than dissolution. Israeli generals in Tel Aviv tracked Egyptian movements through radio intercepts made possible because Egyptian signals units transmitted operational orders en clair rather than encrypted—another symptom of an army whose technical training budgets had been diverted years earlier to sustain Yemen logistics chains stretching across three seas.

Nasser spent June fifth through June eighth receiving fragmentary reports delivered by aides too frightened to synthesize them honestly. When he finally understood that Sinai was irretrievable he understood also that his military reputation—the foundation of his political authority since Free Officer days—had vaporized along with his air force on its aprons. His broadcast on the evening of June ninth attempted candor within a career built upon strategic deception about Egyptian capabilities.

Standing before cameras with eyes hollowed by seventy-two hours without sleep he declared himself fully responsible for what he termed “the setback,” acknowledged that political calculation had overridden military prudence, announced his resignation effective immediately—and then stepped back while technicians cut transmission because he could not continue through visible emotion. What followed confounded foreign embassies that had predicted military catastrophe would finally sever his bond with the masses. By dawn on June tenth millions filled Abbasiya Square and Tahrir Square carrying placards demanding his return rather than vengeance against Israel. Women from popular quarters who had lost sons at Mitla Pass wept for him instead of cursing him. Army units sent to secure government buildings found themselves surrounded by civilians chanting his name until officers removed their caps and joined rather than risk being crushed against railings. The dynamics ran deeper than orchestration, though security services certainly amplified them.

They represented an emotional transaction whose terms had been set eleven years earlier during those November weeks when British tanks withdrew from Port Said under American financial pressure while Nasser remained standing atop a platform promising dignity restored. Suez had made him personally synonymous with Egyptian independence from imperial control; no subsequent failure—not Yemen’s drain nor Sinai’s collapse—could dissolve that prior achievement because it existed in popular memory as experiential truth rather than policy analysis. A population that had endured colonial occupation since childhood measured political legitimacy less by territorial control than by symbolic defiance against foreign masters; having lost territory under Nasser they nonetheless retained memory of having once won dignity through him against greater powers than themselves or Israel. Thus when crowds rejected his resignation they were not forgiving strategic incompetence so much as insisting upon preserving their only available vessel for national self-respect. He rescinded his departure within twenty-four hours but governed thereafter under conditions fundamentally altered.

The army high command stood discredited, though never fully purged; purges would have required admitting how deeply corruption and cronyism had penetrated promotion systems during Yemen years. Soviet military presence increased exponentially yet demanded payment through basing rights and diplomatic alignment that constrained Egyptian maneuverability more than any Western aid conditionality had done before Suez. The canal remained closed, blocked by wreckage and warfare, its revenues zeroed precisely when foreign exchange reserves most needed replenishment. Across Sinai Israeli divisions now occupied positions thirty miles east of Ismailia, staring across water at an Egyptian army rebuilding itself from scrap metal. Nasser faced this ruin through a combination of public bravado and private physical collapse. His doctors placed him on strict regimens requiring reduced work hours and smoking cessation; he ignored them entirely. He continued chain-smoking through twelve-hour strategy sessions reviewing War of Attrition plans drafted by Soviet advisors whose presence inside Egyptian air defense installations represented tacit admission that indigenous technical capacity had been hollowed out by years of ideological promotion over engineering competence.

Between July 1967 and August 1970 Egyptian artillery along the canal fired hundreds of thousands of shells toward Bar Lev Line fortifications, achieving negligible territorial gain at enormous ammunition cost. Commando raids across Great Bitter Lake succeeded tactically on several occasions only to provoke devastating Israeli retaliatory strikes deep into Delta agricultural zones; Israeli Phantom jets bombed military factories near Hilwan causing production losses Egypt could not replace without East German industrial credits. Soviet-manned SAM-2 batteries shot down enough Israeli aircraft by late 1969 to impose modest attrition costs but simultaneously created zones where Egyptian sovereignty existed only by Soviet permission. Nasser observed these developments from an increasingly domestic existence. He received Arab heads of state at his suburban villa rather than Abdin Palace partly because stairs exhausted him, partly because he preferred controlling access absolutely. Visitors noted walls decorated simultaneously with maps showing Palestine under liberation someday alongside charts tracking wheat import deficits month by month. His conversations drifted between grand visions of eventual recovery and bitter acknowledgments that recovery required decades he likely lacked.

By the summer of 1970, diabetes combined with advanced arteriosclerosis had produced edema, forcing him to conduct meetings with his feet elevated while aides pretended not to notice. When he accepted Secretary Rogers’ ceasefire proposal in August, he did so recognizing it froze Israeli occupation in place while offering merely a cessation of further losses. It represented strategic breathing space purchased at an ideological cost, since accepting American mediation contradicted the anti-imperial principles he had championed since nationalizing the Suez Canal Company. Yet he accepted because his heart literally could not sustain another mobilization.

On September 28, following an Arab League summit meant to reconcile Jordan’s King Hussein with Palestinian factions after Black September violence, Nasser returned home visibly exhausted. He retired early complaining of chest pains. Shortly after eight o’clock, physicians attending him recognized a massive myocardial infarction. He died before midnight, surrounded by family members including young Abdel Hakim Abdel Nasser, who would later labor preserving archives documenting both the triumphs and catastrophes defining his father’s era.

The announcement before dawn on September twenty-ninth produced spontaneous paralysis throughout Cairo. Radio stations suspended music, replacing playlists with Quranic recitation. Factories emptied without management permission. Peasant cooperatives upriver learned via word-of-mouth carried by felucca pilots before official broadcasts reached village loudspeakers. By noon, millions converged upon central Cairo, overwhelming every security barrier erected for an orderly procession. Foreign dignitaries, including kings who financed Yemeni royalists against him, presidents who conspired during the UAR’s dissolution, and generals who commanded NATO forces during the Suez invasion, arrived simultaneously, finding themselves swept into human currents beyond protocol control.

The funeral cortege attempting passage toward the burial ground moved barely faster than a walking pace through streets carpeted with discarded shoes lost by mourners crushed against vehicles. Soldiers who survived Yemen highlands or Sinai retreats stood rigid, saluting the coffin while tears cut channels through the dust coating their faces. Palestinian refugees pressed photographs from Rafah camps alongside faded news clippings showing Nasser at Port Said on November twenty-third, nineteen fifty-six. For six hours, Egypt ceased functioning as an administrative state, becoming instead a theater of collective grief directed toward a single figure whose actual governance produced material ruin, yet whose symbolic significance exceeded any institutional boundary capable of containing it.

Anwar Sadat assumed the presidency constitutionally within forty-eight hours, taking the oath before the remnants of the Revolutionary Command Council amid continued popular stupor. He inherited apparatuses whose operational capacities bore little resemblance to the mythological expectations surrounding them. The Treasury held foreign exchange reserves covering three weeks of imports. The Army required complete Soviet re-equipment from boots upward. Sinai remained occupied, the Canal blocked, and the agricultural Delta’s east bank was within artillery range of foreign occupation.

The population simultaneously demanded the recovery of lost territory, the maintenance of subsidized bread prices, and the continuation of the rhetorical confrontation with imperialism, yet resisted acknowledging the dependency on superpower support necessary for achieving any of these objectives. Above all, Sadat inherited a governance system, personalist and centralized around the deceased leader, whose image could not be publicly questioned without appearing to betray the national identity itself.

Nasser’s funeral closed an era defined by paradox. The military defeat demonstrated the structural weaknesses of a personalized autocracy and pan-Arab overextension, yet the public mourning demonstrated how thoroughly the anti-imperial mythology of Suez had insulated the leader from the consequences of his own decisions. Sadat stood before the microphones on October first, nineteen seventy, knowing his survival required navigating between material bankruptcy and the beatification surrounding his predecessor’s memory.

Egypt existed suspended between ledger accounts showing a catastrophic deficit and emotional accounts showing an inexhaustible credit accumulated during a single week a decade earlier, when three colluding empires stumbled at Port Said and withdrew, leaving behind a revolutionary symbol heavier than any state budget could measure or challenge.

A purge in the weeks after his reinstatement clarified the new architecture of power while exposing its limitations. Field Marshal Abdel Hakim Amer, the architect of Yemen’s logistical catastrophe and Sinai’s defensive collapse, found himself summoned to villa interrogations where Revolutionary Command Council members he had once commanded now read accusations of conspiracy and dereliction. When Amer’s body was discovered in military custody—officially suicide by poison, though the bullet wounds in his chest suggested otherwise—the message penetrated every command echelon: military autonomy had ended, replaced by direct presidential oversight that bypassed traditional hierarchy. Yet this centralization solved nothing regarding the state’s material bankruptcy. The Khartoum Summit of August 1967, convened ostensibly to forge solidarity against Israeli occupation, exposed instead the hollowness of pan-Arab financial solidarity; oil-producing monarchies offered annual subsidies of approximately two hundred million sterling conditioned upon Egyptian withdrawal from Yemen, tacitly acknowledging that the southern Arabian adventure had become an unaffordable luxury when Sinai lay under foreign control. Nasser accepted these terms not because he abandoned revolutionary principles but because the Central Bank held hard currency reserves sufficient for only six weeks of essential grain imports, and the Soviet Union had begun demanding gold payments for wheat shipments previously extended on credit. The “Three Nos” declaration—no negotiation, no recognition, no peace with Israel—provided rhetorical cover for strategic paralysis, allowing the state to maintain its confrontational posture while its economy contracted by fifteen percent and inflation eroded the purchasing power of urban salaried classes who had formed the revolution’s social base. Austerity measures eliminated the consumer subsidies that had distinguished Nasser’s populism from the monarchical regimes he had overthrown, creating silent tension between the masses who had demanded his return in June and those same masses facing meat shortages and rolling blackouts by December.

The War of Attrition that followed was never purely an Egyptian-Israeli contest; it became a laboratory for Soviet military doctrine tested through proxy flesh and Egyptian soil. When Israeli Phantom raids struck deep into the Delta in March 1968—destroying the Abu Zaabal steelworks and the Helwan aircraft factory with precision that suggested intelligence penetration of Egyptian command structures—Nasser requested Moscow’s intervention not merely as arms suppliers but as combat participants. The arrival of Soviet air defense divisions, complete with MiG-21 squadrons flown by Soviet pilots wearing Egyptian insignia and speaking rehearsed Arabic phrases over radio nets, restored technical competence to air defense but surrendered operational sovereignty in ways that Suez-era independence would have found intolerable. Egyptian officers found themselves excluded from radar installations where Cyrillic technical manuals replaced Arabic protocols; Soviet generals vetoed Egyptian offensive operations they deemed suicidal, effectively creating a military protectorate along the canal zone where Egyptian sovereignty existed only by foreign permission. This dependency extended to the civilian population. The government evacuated three hundred thousand residents from Port Said to Ismailia, creating a ghost corridor where agricultural land fell fallow and ancient irrigation systems silted from neglect, where palm plantations died for lack of tending and villages became free-fire zones. Refugees crowded into Cairo’s expanding slums, transforming the social geography of the capital as surely as the military geography of Sinai had been transformed. The “barrier” concept—static defense behind concrete bunkers and missile belts—suited Soviet defensive doctrine but contradicted the mobile warfare Nasser’s army had theoretically trained for since the 1950s, institutionalizing a strategic passivity that would define Egyptian military posture for generations and creating a generation of commanders who knew only how to dig in rather than maneuver.

The physical deterioration that accompanied these strategic reversals began in earnest during the winter of 1968, when a myocardial infarction struck him during a routine meeting with Soviet advisors at Kubbeh Palace. The attack was concealed from the public and from most of the cabinet; for three days the state apparatus functioned without acknowledging that its president lay in an oxygen tent, receiving directives written in a hand that grew increasingly illegible as morphine dulled the pain. When he resumed public appearances, photographers were forbidden to shoot his profile, where jaundice had yellowed the skin, or to capture the full length of his frame, which had swollen with fluid retention that no diuretic could fully drain. His physicians, imported from East Germany and the Soviet Union, prescribed absolute rest and the elimination of nicotine; instead he increased his consumption to three packs daily, smoking through meetings while oxygen tanks stood discreetly behind velvet curtains. The contradiction between medical necessity and political survival became absolute: to rest was to admit mortality, and to admit mortality was to invite the succession struggles that would fragment the coalition he had spent eighteen years constructing. Thus he continued the twelve-hour days, reviewing War of Attrition casualty reports that showed exchange ratios favoring Israel by margins that would have been unacceptable in any conventional military calculus, yet which he authorized because the alternative—strategic silence—would have dissolved the myth of resistance that justified his continued rule. By 1969, visitors to his office noted that he conducted meetings from a reclining position, his legs elevated on pillows, his breathing audible between sentences, yet his mind remained sharp enough to detect hesitation in subordinates’ voices, maintaining the terror of personal authority even as his body failed.

The crisis that consumed his final energies originated not along the canal but in the streets of Amman, where King Hussein’s Bedouin units had opened fire on Palestinian commandos in September 1970, threatening to unravel the remnants of Arab solidarity precisely when the Rogers Initiative required unified Arab response. The civil war in Jordan represented the ultimate contradiction of Nasser’s pan-Arab project: the Palestinian resistance he had championed since Suez now threatened the stability of a fellow Arab state whose monarch he had tried to overthrow in 1958 and 1963. Nasser summoned the combatants to Cairo for an emergency Arab League summit, though his own physicians warned that the stress of mediating between Hussein’s monarchical legitimacy and Yasser Arafat’s guerrilla romanticism might trigger fatal cardiac arrest. For seventy-two hours he shuttled between delegations housed in separate wings of the Nile Hilton, his bodyguards supporting him through corridors when arrhythmia caused dizziness, his voice dropping to a whisper that required aides to lean close to hear the terms of compromise. He secured a ceasefire agreement that preserved Palestinian organizational autonomy while acknowledging Jordanian sovereignty, a diplomatic achievement that observers noted required the last reserves of his personal authority—authority derived not from the current weakness of the Egyptian state but from the accumulated moral capital of Suez, which even Hussein acknowledged by embracing him before cameras despite their years of enmity over Yemen and the UAR. When the final communiqué was signed, Nasser returned to his villa knowing he had purchased Arab reconciliation at the cost of his own physiological reserves; the triumph was pyrrhic, the settlement temporary, but the performance of indispensability had been maintained until the final curtain fell.

The enduring paradox of his legacy crystallized during those funeral hours when the same crowds that had rejected his resignation in 1967 now mourned his irreversibility with a grief that transcended political evaluation. The myth of Suez functioned not as historical memory but as active political architecture: it provided the emotional vocabulary through which Egyptians interpreted their subsequent sufferings, transforming military defeat into temporary setback and economic privation into necessary sacrifice for dignity. This alchemy operated because the 1956 triumph had been personal and visible—the single leader defying three empires—while the 1967 catastrophe was systemic and diffuse, the failure of institutions rather than the failure of charisma. Historians would later document the structural rot—hollowed officer corps, personalized decision-making, diversion of resources to foreign adventures—yet the popular narrative retained the inverse causality: that Nasser had been betrayed by subordinates, by allies, by the inevitable corruption of bureaucracy, never by his own strategic hubris.

The canal he had nationalized remained closed, blocked by the war he had provoked, yet the closure itself became testament to his unyielding refusal to accept imperial terms. Thus the ledger of material ruin—occupied territory, bankrupt treasury, Soviet dependency—could not outweigh the emotional account of sovereignty defended. Anwar Sadat inherited not merely a state but a narrative structure that demanded the continued veneration of Nasser’s image even as Sadat’s own policies would gradually dismantle the Nasserist economic edifice. The photograph of the young colonel ascending the podium at Port Said in 1956, arms raised in victory, would hang in every government office for decades thereafter, outshining the later images of the aging president reviewing troops in Yemen or the hollow-eyed broadcasts of June 1967, because it represented the moment when Egypt had mattered to history not as victim but as agent, a moment that subsequent failures could not retrospectively unmake, and a standard of defiance that would haunt his successors whenever they contemplated accommodation with the powers he had once humbled.
