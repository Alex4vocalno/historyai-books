Chapter 2

Crucible of Defeat and the Free Officers (1948-1952)

The rifles arrived wrapped in Cosmoline so old it had fossilised into amber shellac. In late May 1948, Major Gamal Abdel Nasser stood beside a flatbed truck at an assembly area near al-Arish, watching his men pry open crates stamped with depot dates predating El Alamein by two years at least. Beneath which lay barrels pitted with rust, some so deeply scored a cleaning rod pushed through emerged trailing flakes of iron oxide mixed with congealed grease, blackened from storage warehouses where inspection had stopped as a matter of routine since before Rommel was driven from Africa forever ago. Anyway, nobody bothered to check the condition of weapons issued to troops advancing northward toward what headquarters maps optimistically labelled the Tel Aviv corridor, expecting a swift march to restore Arab honour and erase the stain of the Balfour Declaration thirty-one years earlier, a fresh wound still suppurating in the collective memory.

Nasser spent two days organizing cleaning details instead of moving forward. He requisitioned diesel fuel, boiled water in mess tins, borrowed wire brushes from the motor pool, scraped corrosion with bayonet tips, and improvised solvents, none being proper lubricants accompanied by the shipment. His battalion staff section worked eighteen-hour shifts sorting functional rifles from irreparable ones, discovering bolts seized after three rounds, others simply unable to seat a cartridge in the chamber without hammer force applied gingerly, risking detonation of the primer and premature discharge wounding the soldier operating the weapon, ironically more dangerous to the user than to the target enemy combatant positioned a few kilometres distant across the empty desert scrubland, indistinguishable on the horizon shimmering with heat haze distorting distance, making it impossible to judge range accurately, compounding difficulties already overwhelming in the logistical nightmare unfolding around them minute by hour, day by week, month ahead, culminating in the encirclement at Faluja village on the southern front in October of the same year.

There, roughly four thousand Egyptian troops were cut off from supply lines, surrounded by Israeli columns racing southward from Beersheba, completing a pincer movement to sever the retreat route back to the Gaza Strip proper, leaving an isolated garrison defending a perimeter roughly five miles across at its widest point, exposed to constant artillery and mortar bombardment and probing attacks at nightfall, testing defences and seeking a weakness exploitable for a breakthrough attempt that never fully materialised, the Israeli command preferring containment to a costly assault, letting starvation accomplish what a direct attack might fail to achieve given the defenders’ desperation, born of knowing abandonment was a certainty, hardening their resolve for survival while simultaneously eroding faith in the institutions that had placed them in a position requiring such resolve initially, a paradox generating a particular bitterness distinguishing the Faluja veterans among the postwar officer corps permanently afterward, marking a psychological boundary separating the men who served in the pocket from everyone else regardless of rank, seniority, or decorations earned elsewhere in campaigns before, since, or after.

The conflict concluded with armistice agreements signed at Rhodes in February nineteen forty-nine, formally ending hostilities without resolving the underlying political questions, leaving frontline participants suspended in an ambiguous status, neither prisoners nor free combatants, awaiting a diplomatic resolution for weeks and months beyond the ceasefire technically taking effect.

The local pocket region specifically delayed implementation due to disagreement over demarcation lines and exact withdrawal schedules, producing an extended limbo period. During this, Nasser conducted endless conversations with fellow officers, sitting in foxholes dug months earlier, now crumbling at the edges and filling with sand whenever the wind blew across the exposed positions. Facing the Israeli lines visible in the distance within binocular range, they observed enemy movements, routine patrol changes, and supply deliveries arriving regularly and punctually, demonstrating an organisational competence contrasting painfully with their own situation. This reinforced conclusions forming gradually throughout the siege regarding the nature of the failure producing their current circumstance.

The causal chain extended backward beyond tactical errors and immediate command decisions toward fundamental structural deficiencies embedded in the institutional architecture of the monarchy itself, maintained deliberately according to a growing conviction. This was shared increasingly among the junior officers sharing watches during the nighttime hours, discussing politics, history, economics—anything to distract their minds from empty stomachs and the constant low-level fear that artillery bombardment could resume at any moment if negotiations collapsed entirely. That possibility seemed real, given the mutual suspicion characterising talks mediated by United Nations personnel struggling to maintain impartiality amid competing narratives of responsibility and blame attribution.

Regarding the origins and continuation of the conflict, it was generally acknowledged as a multilateral complexity defying simple assignment of fault to a singular party. Yet, the Egyptian participants increasingly focused on internal explanations rather than external ones, locating the primary cause of defeat within the system that sent them to fight inadequately equipped, supplied, supported, and led—ultimately betrayed. The interests of the nation it claimed to serve, protect, and defend—the sovereign territory, integrity, and dignity of a people subjected to foreign domination for decades or centuries, depending on the historical timeframe referenced—varied with the participants’ backgrounds, education levels, political awareness, and the sophistication of the analytical frameworks applied to interpreting the experience.

A common denominator remained a sense of betrayal experienced personally and collectively, shaping subsequent career trajectories, decisions, commitments, and actions taken afterward. This included the formation of a clandestine organization that eventually overthrew the monarchy in the July nineteen fifty-two coup, executed in a precisely coordinated manner reflecting lessons learned painfully from the Palestine campaign regarding the necessity of secrecy, discipline, and a centralised command structure. They aimed at avoiding infiltration and penetration by the security services, as the palace maintained extensive informant networks monitoring the officer corps for suspected disloyalty and potential subversion, requiring countermeasures.

These countermeasures were developed gradually, testing the reliability of individuals recruited cautiously, building a cell structure, limiting knowledge through compartmentalising information, and distributing operational responsibilities to minimise damage possible from a single arrest compromising the entire network. This principle was derived from studying the failures of previous conspiracies attempted earlier in the decades, including abortive efforts in the immediate postwar period that were crushed efficiently, demonstrating the regime’s capacity for repression and its survival of multiple challenges despite its apparent weakness, corruption, and inefficiency.

Characterising governance overall suggests durability rooted not in popular legitimacy but in the coercive apparatus and foreign backing. The British military presence in the canal zone provided the ultimate guarantee of survival against internal threats. The perceived existential nature of these threats determined response patterns, observable consistently across successive administrations. Governments and cabinets were reshuffled periodically, changing personalities and policies marginally, adjusting tactics in response to pressures and demands from constituencies, both domestic and international, balancing competing interests and maintaining an equilibrium on a precarious and unstable foundation.

This foundation eroded progressively through accumulating grievances and unresolved tensions, with structural contradictions inherent to the colonial situation perpetuated indefinitely absent a fundamental transformation of the political, economic, and social order established in the nineteenth century and consolidated in the twentieth century under various legal instruments, treaties, agreements, protocols, and conventions regulating the relationship. The occupying power and occupied territory population were subject to an administrative control exercised indirectly through native institutions, preserved as formally autonomous but actually dependent financially, militarily, and politically. Externally determined parameters limited sovereignty and the scope of action available to rulers, constraining choices and presenting dilemmas in managing expectations.

Populations became increasingly aware of alternatives available elsewhere in the decolonising world, observing developments in India, Indonesia, Vietnam, and elsewhere, drawing conclusions applicable to their own circumstances and adapting strategies accordingly. They pursued objectives with varying methods and degrees of radicalism, commitment, and consistency. Success or failure outcomes were contingent on factors of circumstances, timing, leadership quality, organisational capacity, and external support or opposition encountered. Obstacles were overcome and opportunities exploited, with advantages maximised and disadvantages minimised through calculations, assessments, and judgments made under uncertainty, with incomplete information and imperfect knowledge of unpredictable consequences and unintended effects.

The defective rifles were merely the most visible symptom of a procurement system rotted through by palace cronyism. King Farouk’s court had long treated military supply contracts as vehicles for personal enrichment rather than for national defense. Middlemen like Elias Andraos—the king’s Armenian-born fixer—brokered deals with European arms manufacturers who dumped surplus stocks left over from two world wars onto unsuspecting clients unable to inspect shipments before payment cleared Swiss escrow accounts numbered untraceably beneath layers of shell companies registered in Liechtenstein, Luxembourg, Panama, and Liberia—jurisdictions chosen precisely for the opacity that afforded financial transactions that were immune from scrutiny and parliamentary oversight.

Fiscal transparency was nonexistent in a constitutional monarchy where the king appointed and dismissed cabinets at whim, dissolving legislative bodies whenever the opposition threatened his prerogatives. Revenue derived from cotton exports, customs duties, and Suez Canal transit fees was flowing directly into the treasury, bypassing the formal budgetary process altogether and enabling discretionary spending patterns so opaque that even senior ministers never fully comprehended the scope and magnitude of the diversion of funds toward palaces, estates, yachts, European vacations, gambling casinos on the Riviera, mistresses, and apartments in Paris, London, Rome, Geneva, Zurich, and New York—wherever his fancy dictated.

This lifestyle was maintained regardless of the condition of the armed forces expected to defend the realm against existential threats from external enemies and internal subversion simultaneously, while lacking the basic tools to accomplish the mission assigned to them. Paper organizational charts were bearing little resemblance to the reality encountered in field conditions in Palestine in autumn nineteen forty-eight.

Within weeks of arrival at the front lines near Isdud, then Majdal, and then ultimately the encircled perimeter around a village called al-Faluja, located roughly thirty kilometers northeast of Gaza town, itself insignificant strategically except by happenstance geography placed directly in the path of advancing Israeli columns executing Operation Yoav. This operation was designed to split the Egyptian forces, isolate the coastal strip, and cut the retreat route southward toward the Sinai desert wastes. Beyond the reach of relief columns dispatched by headquarters staff officers who remained safely distant in rear areas, communicating directives via unreliable radio transmissions often garbled by static interference, atmospheric conditions, and terrain obstacles limiting the effective range of equipment already obsolete. Of prewar vintage, purchased secondhand from Belgian and Czechoslovak stockpiles dating to the mid-nineteen-thirties, they were lacking spare parts, maintenance manuals, and trained technicians. Repair of damaged components relied on improvised solutions and jury-rigged repairs attempted under desperate circumstances, frequently causing additional malfunctions and compounding original problems, exponentially multiplying the difficulties facing troops expected to hold positions indefinitely without resupply, reinforcement, or evacuation of the wounded possible, given the Israeli encirclement was complete, cutting all roads, tracks, and paths leading outside the perimeter.

This reduced the garrison to dependency on internal stocks, dwindling rapidly under constant expenditure of ammunition, food, water, medical supplies, fuel, lubricants, spare parts, batteries, communications wire—everything required to sustain a modern military force to twentieth-century standards, already minimal at baseline from inadequate preparation and peacetime neglect. This was compounded by the active hostility of institutional superiors, whose primary concern appeared to be protecting their own positions and privileges rather than prosecuting a successful campaign or achieving the stated objectives of liberating Palestine, restoring Arab dignity, and reversing the humiliation of the nineteen forty-eight defeat, which was never acknowledged officially. Instead, propaganda broadcasts from Radio Cairo celebrated imaginary victories, with troops advancing everywhere and the enemy retreating confused and disorganized. These reports contradicted eyewitness testimony from soldiers experiencing the opposite reality firsthand, generating a cognitive dissonance resolved eventually by the conclusion of a deliberate deception perpetrated knowingly and systematically at the highest levels of the command structure, extending upward to the palace itself, with the monarch personally involved in decisions affecting the prosecution of the war effort.

This included the notorious incident of faulty ammunition supplied to certain units, resulting in casualties from friendly fire incidents and unexploded shells landing harmlessly in enemy territory, while their own artillery pieces exploded prematurely, killing the gun crews operating them. Such stories circulated widely among the junior officer corps afterward, fueling suspicion that something was fundamentally wrong beyond mere incompetence or ordinary bureaucratic inefficiency typical of any large organization, public or private sector, anywhere in the world throughout history, suggesting active malice and deliberate sabotage—treasonous behavior punishable by death under military law applicable in wartime circumstances. It was never investigated, and no one responsible was prosecuted; accountability was absent entirely, reinforcing the perception of impunity enjoyed by the elite.

Connected patronage networks linked the palace, landowners, industrialists, bankers, and the comprador bourgeoisie, dependent on British protection. Their economic interests were tied to cotton export markets, Lancashire textile mills, Manchester financial houses, and London City banks and insurance and shipping companies controlling global trade routes. Imperial preference systems, Commonwealth preference arrangements, and the Sterling Area currency bloc mechanisms ensured the Egyptian economy remained subordinate to metropolitan capital accumulation processes, extracting surplus value from agricultural labor. The peasantry produced raw materials exported at cheap prices, while manufactured goods were imported at expensive prices, maintaining a chronic trade deficit offset by invisible earnings from tourism, canal transit, and remittances from expatriate workers abroad. These were temporary expedients masking a structural dependency perpetuated by colonial treaty arrangements dating from eighteen eighty-two, the occupation of nineteen fourteen, the protectorate declaration, and the nineteen twenty-two independence, which was nominal and retained reserved points for defense, security, communications, foreign affairs, and the Sudan condominium administration, effectively continuing the occupation indefinitely regardless of formal legal status changes. These were cosmetic adjustments and window dressing designed to placate nationalist sentiment, which was growing increasingly vocal and organized, demanding genuine sovereignty, the complete evacuation of British troops, the termination of unequal treaties, and the abolition of the capitulations and extraterritorial privileges for foreign nationals residing in Egypt, who enjoyed immunity from local jurisdiction. Courts and consulates exercised judicial authority over their own citizens, and disputes involving Egyptians were adjudicated in mixed tribunals applying codes and laws drafted by European legal experts trained in continental traditions unfamiliar to Islamic jurisprudence and customary practices. The local populations were resentful of the imposition of alien norms, values, and institutions, undermining the indigenous cultural and religious identity and the sense of selfhood and collective belonging. The community and the nation, imagined, constructed, contested, and negotiated continuously throughout the modern period, began with Napoleon Bonaparte’s expedition in seventeen ninety-eight, inaugurating an era of direct European intervention in Middle Eastern affairs and accelerating the Ottoman decline, fragmentation, and territorial losses of Balkan provinces and North African regencies. Autonomous principalities and eventually independent states emerged in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries through a combination of internal reform movements and external pressures from great power rivalries and competing imperial ambitions.

The monarchy, they concluded, had not simply failed the army; it had functioned exactly as designed, a buffer state for British imperial interests dressed in the vestments of Egyptian sovereignty. The king’s Italian-speaking court, his string of European mistresses, his gambling debts, his reliance on the British ambassador’s advice over that of his own ministers—these were not personal vices alone but the visible plumage of a system in which loyalty was measured by one’s distance from the aspirations of the peasants whose sons filled the ranks. The proof lay in the procurement ledgers that Nasser and his fellow major Abd al-Hakim Amer later compared—bilious nights in Cairo apartments after the armistice, spreading out records of antiquated ordnance next to receipts for imported champagne, silk curtains, and the gold-plated bathroom fixtures that Farouk had ordered for the Abdeen Palace. This stark contrast between royal excess and military failure became the Free Officers’ blueprint for revolution. The final catalyst arrived in January 1952, when British forces attempted to disarm an auxiliary police barracks in Ismailia, killing forty-one Egyptians and triggering anti-Western riots in Cairo that left parts of the city in flames. The monarchy’s inability to protect its citizens or assert sovereignty over its own territory confirmed the officers’ diagnosis: the state was not merely weak, it was complicit. On 23 July 1952, their coup overthrew King Farouk.
