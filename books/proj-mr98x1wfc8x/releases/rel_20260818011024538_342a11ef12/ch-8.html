Chapter 8

The Diplomatic Triumph and Imperial Withdrawal (November 1956-March 1957)

The treasury memorandum lay face down on the mahogany desk in the Oval Office, its figures a cold ledger of national collapse. President Dwight Eisenhower had removed his reading glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose, the gesture of a man confronting an unpleasant but mathematically inevitable equation. The document, prepared overnight by the Treasury Department under Secretary George Humphrey, charted the unspooling of the British pound sterling. Since Anglo-French bombers had first struck Egyptian airfields on 31 October—and with brutal acceleration since paratroopers had dropped onto Port Said on 5 November—the Bank of England’s gold and dollar reserves had hemorrhaged at a rate of fifteen million dollars a day. The run was gaining momentum. Each hour Britain maintained its airborne brigade on Egyptian soil pushed the sterling area closer to a terminal liquidity crisis, a credit default that would shatter the post-war currency arrangements and drag the entire Western financial architecture down with it. Eisenhower did not need to hear Anthony Eden’s strained voice on the transatlantic telephone line to understand the choice he was making. He had already forced an end to the combined British, French and Israeli invasion of Egypt, publicly disavowing his allies at the United Nations and using financial and diplomatic pressure to make them withdraw.

The American refusal to intervene constituted a decision as strategic as any military order. By simply doing nothing—by withholding bilateral dollar credits, blocking emergency drawing rights at the International Monetary Fund, and permitting currency markets to punish sterling with their impersonal verdict—the United States was condemning the British Empire to capitulation. The mechanism of coercion was bureaucratic, almost bloodless in its execution, but its leverage was absolute. At Bretton Woods in 1944, Britain had accepted a financial order in which the dollar stood as the world’s reserve currency, backed by American gold. The pound sterling remained a major trading and reserve unit only because of imperial preferences and sterling balances held by Commonwealth nations. That structure had left London perpetually vulnerable to a crisis of confidence: any sustained sell-off could exhaust reserves faster than the Treasury could borrow. In November 1956, everything conspired to trigger exactly such a crisis.

The Suez invasion disrupted oil shipments through the canal and pipelines, threatened wider Middle Eastern instability, and convinced international investors that Britain was embarking on a costly colonial war without American backing. The markets voted with their feet: sterling plunged from $2.78 to $2.76 in days, then threatened to break through lower support levels that would trigger cascading margin calls and force devaluation. Humphrey had briefed Eisenhower with brutal clarity. The British had already drawn down their IMF quota earlier in the year; further assistance required American approval under weighted voting rules. The US executive director at the Fund had been instructed to block any new draw until Britain accepted a cease-fire and withdrawal. Simultaneously, Humphrey’s Treasury refused to intervene in foreign exchange markets to buy pounds or extend a stabilization loan from the Exchange Stabilization Fund. Without those lifelines, the Bank of England’s reserves—barely two billion dollars at the start of November—would be drained within weeks.

Britain would face an impossible choice: impose draconian import controls and domestic austerity that would shatter Tory electoral prospects, or devalue sterling and default on its international obligations, destroying its status as a global financial center. Either path meant abandoning any independent imperial foreign policy. Eisenhower’s decision was not born of anti-colonial idealism alone. He viewed Eden’s adventure as a reckless gamble that handed Soviet Premier Nikolai Bulganin a propaganda victory and threatened to inflame Arab nationalism across a region vital to Western oil supplies. But his instrument was not moral condemnation; it was hard power expressed through ledgers and credit lines. On 6 November, as British commandos fought house-to-house in Port Said and French paratroopers secured the southern approaches to the canal, Secretary of State John Foster Dulles telephoned British Foreign Secretary Selwyn Lloyd with an ultimatum dressed as friendly advice: unless a cease-fire was announced within hours, Washington would not only continue blocking IMF funds but would also permit—perhaps even facilitate—a massive sell-off of sterling holdings by US banks and allied governments. The crisis demonstrated that the United Kingdom and France could no longer pursue their independent foreign policy without consent from the United States.

The message was unmistakable: your empire ends here. In London, Chancellor of the Exchequer Harold Macmillan had spent the previous forty-eight hours oscillating between bravado and panic. A decade earlier he had championed imperial grandeur; now he stared at Treasury ledgers that told an irreversible story. On 4 November he had still assured Cabinet that financial difficulties could be managed if victory came quickly. By 6 November he understood that victory was irrelevant: even if Anglo-French forces seized every inch of the canal within twenty-four hours—as military commanders insisted they could—the economic verdict would be rendered before any soldier reached Suez City. At an emergency Cabinet meeting that morning, Macmillan laid out the figures with what one colleague described as “the air of a man announcing a death in the family.” Reserves were falling at eighteen million dollars daily; an IMF drawing required American consent that would not come while troops remained; without it, Britain could not meet its dollar obligations beyond December. To continue fighting meant national bankruptcy. Eden sat pale and twitching at the head of the table.

His health had been deteriorating for months under the strain of sleepless nights and amphetamine use; his judgment had narrowed into a tunnel vision that equated Nasser with Hitler and withdrawal with appeasement. But even he could not dispute arithmetic. The Cabinet minutes recorded no formal vote—none was needed. The Prime Minister rose and announced that he would telephone French Premier Guy Mollet to inform him that Britain would accept a cease-fire effective midnight. He did not consult Paris in advance; he presented it as an accomplished fact born of necessity. When Mollet heard Eden’s quavering voice on the line, begging for understanding, he collapsed into furious silence and then burst into tears. France’s military investment—its paratroopers already deep into Port Said, its Mystère jets flying sorties over Egyptian positions—was being abandoned by its closest ally because of a currency crisis orchestrated from Washington. The cease-fire took effect at midnight on 6 November, freezing Anglo-French forces along a line twenty-three miles south of Port Said but leaving them in possession of key canal entry points. Troops were still in Port Said and on operational manoeuvres; Port Said had been overrun, and the military assessment was that the Suez Canal could have been completely taken within 24 hours.

Israeli forces held most of Sinai and were advancing toward Sharm el-Sheikh at the peninsula’s southern tip. Tactically, all three powers had achieved their immediate military objectives within ninety-six hours. Strategically, they had already lost. The announcement stunned field commanders who had been preparing final assaults and infuriated junior officers who saw timidity in London’s capitulation. But far from Egyptian battlefields, in Cairo’s radio studios and government offices, Gamal Abdel Nasser understood before almost anyone else what had just been handed to him. Nasser had spent the invasion days in a state of controlled desperation overlaid with defiant public performance. As Israeli columns sliced through Sinai and Anglo-French bombs cratered Cairo’s airfields, he had ordered Egypt’s modest air force dispersed to bases in Syria and Saudi Arabia rather than face destruction on the ground—a prudent but hardly heroic measure. He had supervised the scuttling of dozens of vessels in the Suez Canal itself: old freighters, dredgers, tugboats packed with cement blocks and sunk at precise locations to block navigation channels completely. The Suez Canal was closed from October 1956 to April 1957.

Operation “Suez Cement,” as Egyptian engineers sardonically named it, transformed the waterway into an impassable obstacle within seventy-two hours. Ships already in transit were trapped in Great Bitter Lake, their crews left to watch helplessly as war engulfed both shores. The physical canal—the prize for which three nations had conspired—was now a submerged junkyard. But while Nasser managed military retreats and logistical sabotage with grim efficiency, his true battlefield lay elsewhere: in the ether of radio waves and in the imagination of millions across the Arab world. From his office adjacent to Radio Cairo’s broadcasting headquarters on Sharia al-Galaa, he dictated communiqués and speeches that transformed tactical disasters into epic resistance narratives. Voice of the Arabs transmitters beamed his voice across North Africa and the Levant with enough power to be heard in Baghdad coffee houses and Damascus souks simultaneously. “We are fighting alone,” he declared on 2 November, “but we are not alone in spirit.” He framed every retreat as strategic withdrawal, every sunk ship as an act of national sacrifice that denied imperialists their spoils.

He invoked not just Egyptian nationalism but pan-Arab solidarity: Algeria’s revolutionaries were brothers; Syria’s Baathists were comrades; Saudi Arabia’s oil would be turned against aggressors if necessary. The effect was electric not because Egyptians believed every word—many in Cairo knew perfectly well that their army had been shattered—but because Nasser articulated a truth deeper than operational reality: that imperial powers could no longer impose their will by force alone if subjected to sufficient political and economic counter-pressure. When news arrived on 6 November that Britain had accepted a cease-fire without having secured even half its military objectives, Nasser’s gamble appeared vindicated beyond imagination. He had nationalized the canal expecting diplomatic stalemate at worst; instead he had provoked an invasion that collapsed under its own contradictions within days because Washington chose to break London rather than let London break him. In those first hours after Eden’s announcement, Nasser’s personal situation transformed from besieged commander to triumphant statesman with disorienting speed.

On 7 November he addressed crowds massed in Tahrir Square from a balcony draped with Egyptian flags and portraits of himself in military uniform—the same uniform he had worn since his days as a Free Officer colonel plotting against King Farouk four years earlier. His voice cracked with exhaustion but carried through loudspeakers across an audience estimated at half a million people who had flooded into central Cairo spontaneously upon hearing news of British capitulation. “We have won,” he told them simply—two words that became instant legend across transistor radios from Rabat to Aden. Yet even as he spoke those words under blazing Cairo sun while white-uniformed police struggled to contain surging crowds pressing toward his balcony railings—even then he understood viscerally what public euphoria obscured: Egypt remained occupied across much of Sinai by Israeli forces entrenched within forty miles of Suez Canal’s eastern bank; Port Said lay under Anglo-French military administration with its civilian population brutalized by house-to-house fighting that left hundreds dead; forty-seven sunken vessels blocked every navigation channel in what had been Egypt’s primary source of hard currency revenue; oil pipelines through Syria were severed; foreign exchange reserves depleted by wartime emergency purchases left Cairo dependent on emergency Saudi and Soviet aid simply to import wheat through Alexandria harbor before winter grain shortages triggered urban unrest.

The political victory that now defined his career rested entirely upon diplomatic processes still unfolding thousands of miles away—processes over which he exerted little direct control beyond rhetorical amplification through Voice of Arabs broadcasts, monitored obsessively by foreign ministries worldwide. At United Nations headquarters in New York, along the East River waterfront where a glass-curtain skyscraper rose above autumn fog banks rolling off the Long Island Sound, Canadian External Affairs Minister Lester Pearson worked furiously behind closed Security Council chamber doors, drafting resolution language that would transform the crisis into a durable institutional precedent rather than a mere triumphalist footnote for anti-colonial movements worldwide.

Pearson understood better than most diplomats present what structural danger Suez posed: if the United Nations proved incapable of constraining great-power military aggression violating Charter principles so flagrantly, the organization itself might collapse in credibility at the exact moment Cold War tensions required multilateral crisis-management mechanisms robust enough to contain superpower confrontations without escalating to nuclear brinkmanship. His proposal—the creation of the first armed international peacekeeping force, deployed between belligerent forces under UN command rather than national control—represented a conceptual breakthrough unprecedented since the organization’s founding a decade earlier.

On November 4 the General Assembly passed the Canadian resolution establishing the United Nations Emergency Force (UNEF) tasked with supervising the cease-fire, monitoring troop withdrawals, and ensuring freedom of navigation once clearance operations were completed. Crucially, the resolution language avoided explicit condemnation of Britain, France, or Israel while simultaneously demanding withdrawal of “all foreign forces” from Egyptian territory — a formula sufficiently ambiguous to gain Soviet support while preserving American diplomatic cover to pressure allies to comply without total public humiliation. Pearson would later receive the Nobel Peace Prize for this innovation; Nasser immediately grasped the utility of a force composed of neutral Scandinavian, Indian, and Latin American contingents rather than great-power troops, providing a buffer protecting Egyptian sovereignty while internationalizing the occupation problem beyond a bilateral confrontation with former imperial powers. UNEF deployment began within days: Danish and Norwegian battalions arrived at Port Said on November 15, establishing observation posts along demarcation lines separating the Anglo-French enclave from Egyptian-held territory south of the city. Without further guarantee, the Anglo-French Task Force had to finish withdrawing by 22 December 1956, to be replaced by Danish and Colombian units of the UNEF.

By December 22 the last British and French combat troops evacuated Port Said under UN supervision, boarding landing craft transports headed for Cyprus and Malta, leaving behind smoldering ruins in neighborhoods destroyed during the amphibious assault phase of Operation Musketeer. Israeli withdrawal proved far more protracted and politically contentious. While the Anglo-French retreat occurred rapidly under combined financial pressure, American coercion, and Soviet nuclear saber-rattling, Israel possessed a different calculation entirely: having seized the entire Sinai Peninsula including the Gaza Strip and Sharm el-Sheikh controlling the Straits of Tiran access to the Red Sea, Israeli Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion viewed these territorial gains as essential bargaining chips for ensuring future security guarantees and preventing a recurrence of fedayeen raids and the blockade of southern shipping routes previously imposed by Egypt. Throughout November and December 1956 Israeli diplomats argued forcefully in Washington, London, and Paris that unilateral withdrawal without binding Egyptian commitments to demilitarize Sinai and provide international guarantees for freedom of navigation through the Straits of Tiran would constitute strategic surrender rewarding aggression. The Eisenhower administration rejected this logic categorically.

Dulles informed Israeli Ambassador Abba Eban bluntly in December meetings at the State Department’s seventh-floor conference room overlooking the Potomac River’s winter grayness: the continued occupation of the Sinai constituted a violation of the UN Charter, undermining American credibility throughout the Arab world at precisely the moment Cold War competition required demonstrating Western respect for the sovereignty of non-aligned nations. Economic leverage supplemented diplomatic pressure: the US suspended all aid disbursements pending compliance; Export-Import Bank loan applications were frozen indefinitely; private American investment flows toward Israel were effectively halted by Treasury Department signals suggesting preferential treatment was unavailable to any government defying UN resolutions Washington itself had co-sponsored.

The Soviet Union simultaneously exploited the crisis rhetorically while offering limited tangible support to Egypt: arms shipments resumed through Czechoslovak intermediaries; technical advisors assisted Egyptian engineers in assessing the canal blockage damage; propaganda broadcasts from Radio Moscow amplified Nasser’s defiance while carefully avoiding direct military confrontation with the Anglo-French forces still present in the region. Bulganin’s notorious letters threatening rocket attacks against London and Paris represented a theatrical gesture devoid of operational substance — Soviet intermediate-range ballistic missile capability remained years away from deployment — yet the psychological impact upon European public opinion, already traumatized by memories of World War bombardment, proved a significant factor in accelerating domestic political pressure on the Eden and Mollet governments.

By late January 1957 Ben-Gurion confronted domestic political reality: Israeli public opinion overwhelmingly supported withdrawal given international isolation and the economic costs of occupation; the military establishment recognized holding Sinai indefinitely required mobilization of reserves crippling to the civilian economy; intelligence assessments warned prolonged occupation risked provoking renewed Egyptian guerrilla warfare draining manpower and morale. On January 22 a Knesset debate saw opposition parties demand immediate compliance with UN resolutions; coalition partners threatened resignation unless the government accepted a phased withdrawal schedule acceptable to Washington. Nasser meanwhile played a dual game skillfully: publicly insisting on unconditional Israeli withdrawal before any negotiations regarding canal clearance or navigation rights, privately signaling through intermediaries a willingness to accept de facto demilitarization arrangements provided sovereignty was formally recognized. This posture maintained a maximalist rhetorical stance satisfying domestic pan-Arab constituencies while creating diplomatic space for gradual compromise acceptable to international mediators. Canadian UNEF commander General E.L.M. Burns shuttled between Cairo, Jerusalem, and Damascus throughout February negotiating withdrawal modalities acceptable to both sides without explicit concessions either party could characterize as capitulation. The Israelis refused to host any UN force on Israeli-controlled territory and withdrew from the Sinai and Gaza in March 1957. The final Israeli withdrawal was completed on March 8, 1957, when the last armored personnel carriers crossed the Negev border demarcation line established by the 1949 armistice agreements.

Egyptian civilian administration resumed in the Gaza Strip under nominal UN supervision; the Sharm el-Sheikh garrison was replaced by UNEF contingents guaranteeing freedom of navigation through the Tiran Straits without formal Egyptian recognition of Israeli maritime rights — an ambiguous arrangement functional precisely because its ambiguity allowed both governments to claim diplomatic victory while avoiding a confrontation neither desired. All Egyptian prisoners of war were released simultaneously; Israeli prisoners captured during early Sinai fighting were returned under Red Cross auspices. French forces had totally withdrawn from Egyptian territory, while Israel completed its withdrawal in March 1957 and released all Egyptian prisoners of war. For Nasser personally, March 1957 represented an apogee of political prestige unimaginable eighteen months earlier when Western diplomats dismissed him as a junior officer leading an impoverished developing nation. He had nationalized the Suez Canal defying Anglo-French ultimatums; survived a tripartite military invasion that destroyed his armed forces; emerged from the crisis possessing unchallenged control of a waterway generating revenues for development projects; transformed himself into a symbol of anti-imperialist resistance inspiring nationalist movements across Africa, Asia, and Latin America; established Egyptian leadership of a pan-Arab movement previously dominated by Hashemite dynasties in Baghdad and Amman; and gained Soviet patronage without sacrificing non-aligned credentials by balancing Cold War pressures adroitly. Yet this triumph contained seeds of future catastrophe embedded within the very mechanisms producing victory.

Military defeat disguised political success, creating a dangerous institutional delusion within the Egyptian armed forces. General Abdel Hakim Amer interpreted the collapse of the Sinai defenses as evidence of incompetent field commanders rather than systemic organizational deficiencies requiring fundamental reform. The officer corps saw promotions accelerated based on political loyalty rather than professional competence. Soviet weapons deliveries resumed after the crisis, creating an illusion of technological parity without addressing the training, maintenance, and logistics weaknesses exposed during the October-November fighting.

Nasser himself increasingly conflated rhetorical mobilization capabilities with actual military capacity — misreading the profound structural gap between the pan-Arab mythos his Voice of the Arabs broadcasts projected and the physical reality of a hollowed-out state apparatus. Its administrative and technical cadres remained thin and overstretched, managing a population explosion and urban migration patterns that strained infrastructure beyond capacity.

On March 12, 1957, Nasser visited Port Said, personally inspecting the devastation wrought during the November fighting. He was accompanied by a retinue of journalists and photographers documenting his triumphal progress through streets still rubble-strewn months after the last British soldier had departed.

Standing atop the ruined customs house overlooking the northern entrance of the canal, where forty-seven sunken vessels created a surreal maritime graveyard — with masts and funnels protruding from the water at angles defying gravity, and oil slicks shimmering iridescent under the Mediterranean sun — the president delivered a speech broadcast live nationwide, declaring that Egypt had defeated imperialism forever through the willpower and sacrifice of ordinary citizens. Crowds cheered deliriously; women ululated from balconies draped in patriotic bunting; children scrambled onto wrecked armored vehicles abandoned during the fighting, posing for photographs distributed worldwide by wire services.

But behind the camera frames capturing the iconic images circulated globally, symbolizing an anti-colonial triumph, lay an immediate administrative reality demanding urgent attention: the canal clearance required removing forty-seven vessels displacing a combined tonnage exceeding a quarter-million metric tons, embedded in mud and silt accumulated over decades; specialized salvage equipment was nonexistent in Egypt, requiring emergency procurement contracts with European salvage firms charging premium rates payable in hard currency, while reserves were depleted. The estimated cost of clearance operations exceeded sixty million dollars — a sum equivalent to the entire annual cotton export revenues — necessitating negotiations with the World Bank and bilateral lenders imposing austerity conditions that were politically unpalatable to domestic constituencies expecting post-victory prosperity rather than belt-tightening sacrifice.

Nasser understood this contradiction intellectually yet the emotional momentum and political capital accumulated during the crisis rendered acknowledgment impossible. He stood amidst rubble possessing absolute prestige unprecedented in modern Arab history — a leader who defied empires, survived, and emerged stronger — yet facing the immediate unglamorous task of clearing sunken ships and rebuilding a shattered state apparatus whose material foundations remained dangerously fragile beneath the mythic superstructure constructed upon diplomatic triumph rather than institutional capacity. That tension between soaring political legitimacy and hollow military-administrative reality would define the next decade of Egyptian history, culminating in catastrophe in June 1967 when structural weakness was exposed mercilessly during the Six-Day War, destroying the illusion of pan-Arab unity forever. The crisis strengthened Nasser’s standing and led to international humiliation for the British—with historians arguing that it signified the end of its role as a superpower—as well as for the French.

At the Treasury Department, Secretary George Humphrey had spent the weekend of 3–4 November closeted with his senior staff in the vault-like suite overlooking Fifteenth Street, running the same grim calculations over and over on adding machines that clattered through the small hours. The Bank of England had reported reserve losses of $45 million on Friday alone, accelerating toward the $100 million weekly rate that Humphrey’s deputy, W. Randolph Burgess, had warned would trigger an irreversible sterling collapse. What made the situation more lethal than a straightforward current-account deficit was the structure of sterling liabilities themselves: Kuwait, Qatar, and the Trucial States held enormous sterling balances accumulated from oil royalties under treaties requiring them to deposit revenues in London. When the bombing began, those Gulf rulers—advised by American oil company executives who understood the fragility of the pound—began quietly converting their sterling into dollars through Swiss intermediaries. Simultaneously, India, still the largest single holder of sterling reserves, signaled through its reserve bank governor that continued adherence to the sterling area depended on London’s ability to guarantee convertibility.

Both would sell if the US refused to provide a backstop. Humphrey understood that this was not a run that could be stopped by bank-rate hikes or sterilization operations: it was an architected vulnerability embedded in the imperial financial order itself. He drafted a one-page memorandum for Eisenhower, stripped of all diplomatic niceties, arguing that to shield Britain from the consequences of its own adventure would be to underwrite an indefinite occupation that Moscow would exploit, the Arab world would radicalize, and the dollar would ultimately pay for. Eisenhower, reading it on the morning of 5 November, knew that the Treasury Secretary’s numbers were unarguable: Britain held less than $2 billion in reserves, owed nearly $4 billion to sterling area holders upon demand, and faced a run that would exhaust its position in six weeks at the current pace. The President scrawled a single word at the bottom of the memo—Hold—transmitting through a bureaucratic monosyllable the full weight of American financial power against its closest ally.

In London, Harold Macmillan’s conversion from lion to lamb occurred not as a sudden Damascene epiphany but as a slow suffocation by ledger paper.
