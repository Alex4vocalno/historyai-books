Chapter 7

Invasion and the Zenith of Defiance (October-November 1956)

The Anglo-French ultimatum arrived in Cairo and Tel Aviv at precisely 4: 15 p. m. On 30 October 1956, a single sheet of diplomatic prose whose contradictions were visible to anyone who troubled to read it. Addressed simultaneously to the governments of Egypt and Israel, it demanded that both parties cease all warlike action within twelve hours, withdraw their forces to a distance of ten miles from the Suez Canal, and accept the temporary occupation of Port Said, Ismailia, and Suez by British and French forces “to guarantee the freedom of passage through the Canal.” The document’s central fallacy was not hidden between its lines but stated plainly in its logic: it treated Egypt and Israel as coequal belligerents in a conflict that, on the morning of 30 October, the world understood to consist solely of an Israeli armored thrust into the Sinai Peninsula. At the hour the ultimatum was handed to the Egyptian foreign ministry, Israeli mechanized columns were still thirty miles east of the canal, advancing across open desert.

There was no Egyptian aggression to match; no Egyptian force had crossed an international frontier. The ultimatum demanded that Egypt withdraw from its own territory, from positions it held in sovereign defense, to make way for an occupying force whose stated purpose—safeguarding free passage—was already being met by Egyptian pilots guiding ships through the waterway without interruption. The document was not a diplomatic gesture. It was a physical artifact of imperial miscalculation, a piece of paper whose mechanical flaw revealed the suffocating secrecy of Sèvres: Britain and France had written the script for an Israeli attack, then handed themselves a role as peacekeepers before the first act had been publicly performed. The ultimatum’s twelve-hour deadline was calibrated to fail. Nasser had no intention of accepting terms that would place foreign troops on Egyptian soil, and even if he had wavered, his military command could not physically comply with a demand to withdraw all forces ten miles west of the canal within half a day.

The Egyptian army was already scrambling to meet a threat that had materialized with shocking speed. At dusk on 29 October, Israeli paratroopers had dropped onto the eastern approaches of the Mitla Pass, forty miles inside Sinai, while four mechanized columns crossed the armistice line near Nitzana, Kuntilla, and Ras al-Naqb. By dawn on 30 October, Israeli Centurion tanks were racing west along three axes, their commanders under orders to reach the canal within forty-eight hours. The Egyptian garrison in Sinai numbered some thirty thousand men, but its deployments had been shaped by parade-ground logic rather than battlefield necessity. Heavy coastal batteries pointed uselessly toward an Israeli navy that did not exist; fortified positions at Rafah and Abu Ageila were isolated strongpoints rather than integrated defensive belts; supply dumps held barely two weeks of ammunition for a force designed for internal security, not maneuver warfare against a motorized enemy. Field Marshal Abdel Hakim Amer, Nasser’s closest friend and commander-in-chief, received reports of the Israeli advance with a mixture of disbelief and paralysis.

His headquarters in Cairo issued a stream of contradictory orders—counterattack at Kuntilla, hold at Abu Ageila, fall back toward Ismailia—that left brigade commanders interpreting signals through a fog of static and rumor. The Israeli operation, codenamed Musketeer by its distant Anglo-French sponsors but known locally as Kadesh, was executed with a competence that made Egyptian deficiencies starkly visible. In thirty-six hours, armored columns covered distances that Egyptian planners had assumed would take a week to contest. At Thamad, an Israeli brigade overran an Egyptian battalion that had been positioned without adequate anti-tank weapons; survivors reported seeing their own artillery shells bounce off the glacis plates of advancing Shermans. At Abu Ageila, a complex night assault by infantry and engineers broke through perimeter minefields and silenced key bunkers before dawn, leaving the central road junction in Israeli hands by midday on 31 October.

Egyptian units that might have formed a coherent defensive line were instead ordered by Amer to conduct a general withdrawal toward the canal zone—a retreat that quickly became a rout under continuous air attack once Anglo-French bombers joined the campaign. The Israeli air force had already achieved local superiority over Sinai; Egyptian MiG-15s and Vampires were caught on the ground or outflown by Mystères and Ouragans flown by pilots with recent combat experience. By the evening of 1 November, Israeli spearheads had reached al-Qantarah on the canal’s eastern bank, having advanced over one hundred and fifty miles in three days at a cost of fewer than two hundred killed. The Sinai campaign was not a battle; it was a race against a collapsing opponent. Yet even as his army disintegrated on the sand roads of Sinai, Nasser’s political authority entered its absolute zenith. The distinction is essential to understanding what followed.

Military defeat on this scale would normally destroy a leader’s credibility; in Nasser’s case it produced the opposite effect because he immediately reframed the invasion not as an Egyptian failure but as an imperial conspiracy against Arab sovereignty. Within hours of receiving the ultimatum on 30 October, he broadcast a defiant speech over Cairo Radio that was simultaneously relayed by loudspeakers in every major city. He told Egyptians that Britain and France had colluded with Israel—a charge that Eden’s government was still publicly denying—and that the “tripartite aggression” was aimed not at the canal but at the overthrow of Egypt’s revolutionary government. He announced that he would not resign or flee; he would remain in Cairo and fight. He ordered the distribution of rifles to civilian volunteers and called for a general mobilization of popular resistance committees that had been quietly organized since July.

That evening, crowds surged through Alexandria and Port Said carrying portraits of Nasser torn from newspapers and magazines; students barricaded university gates with furniture and sandbags; workers at the Helwan steel complex refused evacuation and began fashioning crude anti-aircraft mounts from scrap iron. The security state that Nasser had constructed over four years—the mukhabarat networks, the single-party apparatus, the controlled press—now pivoted from internal repression to external defiance with a speed that surprised even its architects. The Anglo-French bombing campaign began thirteen hours after the ultimatum expired, at dusk on 31 October. Operation Revise Phase I sent waves of Canberras and Valiants from bases in Cyprus and Malta against Egyptian airfields at Almaza, Inchas, and Cairo West. The objective was straightforward: destroy the Egyptian air force on the ground before it could interfere with amphibious landings planned for early November. In this narrow military sense, Phase I succeeded.

By dawn on 1 November, over one hundred Egyptian aircraft had been destroyed or damaged beyond repair; surviving planes were withdrawn to bases in Upper Egypt and Syria where they posed no immediate threat to Mediterranean shipping lanes. But the bombing also killed civilians in densely populated quarters near Almaza airfield—Egyptian government figures claimed over one hundred dead in Cairo alone—and transformed what had been a distant armored thrust in Sinai into an intimate assault on urban neighborhoods. The imagery of rubble and body bags undercut every British claim that Operation Musketeer was a limited police action to separate combatants and protect the canal. When Prime Minister Eden addressed the House of Commons on 1 November to justify the intervention, he faced a Labor opposition that shouted him down with cries of “collusion” and “resign.” The political damage was compounded by news that Egypt had blocked the Suez Canal itself—Nasser had ordered ships scuttled at both ends and along its length—thereby halting all traffic through a waterway whose protection was supposedly the entire rationale for military action.

The amphibious and airborne assault on Port Said was launched on 5 November after days of increasingly frantic diplomacy at the United Nations. The operational timetable had been delayed repeatedly by political hesitation in London; Eden’s cabinet was split between those who wanted to press ahead regardless of international condemnation and those who feared economic retaliation from Washington. The delay gave Nasser time to reinforce Port Said with units pulled back from Sinai and to flood its streets with armed civilians indistinguishable from regular soldiers. At 05: 15 on 5 November, six hundred British paratroopers from 3rd Battalion, Parachute Regiment dropped onto Gamil airfield west of Port Said; within ninety minutes they had secured the runway and begun receiving reinforcements flown in by transport aircraft. Simultaneously, French paratroopers landed at Port Fuad on the eastern bank of the canal entrance, seizing key bridges with minimal resistance. The following morning, 6 November, Royal Marine commandos came ashore at Port Said itself under covering fire from destroyers lying offshore.

British Centurion tanks rolled onto beaches that had been prepared by naval gunfire and aerial bombardment; by midday they were pushing into the city’s commercial center against stubborn but uncoordinated resistance from Egyptian regulars and militia. The fighting in Port Said was intense but brief. Egyptian defenders—a mix of army infantry, national guard units hastily armed with rifles and grenades, and civilian volunteers—fought from rooftops and alleyways with weapons that included Molotov cocktails and antique rifles pressed into service from museum collections. British forces advanced methodically, calling in air strikes on buildings identified as strongpoints; French units cleared Port Fuad with flamethrowers and grenades. By late afternoon on 6 November, organized resistance had collapsed, though snipers continued firing from upper windows well into darkness. British commanders estimated they could occupy the entire city within another twenty-four hours; French troops were already probing south toward al-Qantarah along the canal’s east bank.

Tactically, Operation Musketeer had achieved its immediate objectives: Port Said was captured, the northern entrance to the canal was under Anglo-French control, and Israeli forces held nearly all of Sinai east of the waterway. But at precisely this moment—the moment when military success seemed most tangible—the operation’s political foundations crumbled entirely. On the afternoon of 6 November, London received word that a run on sterling had accelerated during trading hours; Britain’s gold reserves were draining at a rate that threatened to exhaust them within weeks unless American financial support was forthcoming. President Eisenhower had made clear through private channels that such support would not be extended while British forces remained on Egyptian soil. This stance was not merely opposition to the use of force; it was a strategic calculation that an imperial war would disrupt global trade and alienate the Middle East from the West, risking the region’s alignment with the Soviet Union. Simultaneously, Soviet Premier Nikolai Bulganin dispatched notes to Eden, Mollet, and Israeli Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion hinting at “the use of force” to restore peace—a threat that was largely bluster given Soviet logistical constraints but that resonated ominously against memories of Budapest, where Soviet tanks were crushing a Hungarian uprising even as Anglo-French bombers struck Cairo suburbs.

The confluence of financial pressure, American disapproval, Soviet menace, and domestic parliamentary revolt proved irresistible. At midnight on 6 November—less than forty-eight hours after paratroopers had jumped onto Gamil—Eden announced a ceasefire effective immediately. The ceasefire order arrived while British troops were still maneuvering through Port Said’s streets. Centurion tanks halted mid-advance; infantry companies dug in around intersections they had expected to clear within hours; French commanders received orders to stop their southward push toward al-Qantarah with forward patrols already in sight of their objective. The abrupt cessation created a tactical vacuum that neither side knew how to fill: British forces controlled roughly two-thirds of Port Said but faced thousands of armed Egyptians who remained uncaptured and uncooperative; Israeli units held all of Sinai but had no clear mandate for occupation or withdrawal; French paratroopers at Port Fuad sat waiting for instructions that contradicted every expectation set during their briefing sessions on Cyprus.

The Sèvres Protocol had been designed for a swift fait accompli—a surgical strike completed before international opinion could mobilize—but its execution had been anything but swift or surgical. Delays between phases had allowed political opposition to accumulate; each day’s bombing generated new diplomatic condemnations; each hour’s debate in New York eroded whatever legal fig leaf remained. Nasser emerged from his military headquarters on 7 November into a Cairo transformed by rumor and celebration. His army lay shattered across Sinai; his air force was a wreckage of burned airframes; his canal was blocked by sunken ships that would take months to clear; Port Said was under foreign occupation. And yet crowds chanting his name filled Tahrir Square as if Egypt had won a great victory rather than suffered a comprehensive battlefield defeat.

The paradox was not lost on foreign observers: British diplomats cabled home reports describing Nasser’s prestige as “higher than ever” while their own prime minister faced demands for resignation; American intelligence assessments noted that Arab nationalist movements from Baghdad to Casablanca were rallying around Nasser’s defiance; Soviet propaganda broadcasts hailed him as a hero of anti-imperialist struggle while carefully omitting mention of Hungary. The zenith of Nasser’s political authority—the moment when his name became synonymous with Arab dignity across twenty countries—coincided precisely with the nadir of his state’s material power. The mechanism that produced this inversion is worth examining closely because it reveals something fundamental about mid-twentieth-century decolonization struggles. Nasser had never sought to defeat Britain and France militarily; no responsible Egyptian officer believed such an outcome possible given disparities in naval power alone. His strategy, which succeeded, was to survive the initial assault and expose the financial and diplomatic vulnerabilities of the invading powers. The crisis demonstrated that Britain and France could no longer pursue independent foreign policy without American consent.

What he sought was to convert military defeat into political victory by exploiting three vulnerabilities inherent in any imperial expedition launched without American consent during an era of Cold War bipolarity: financial dependence on American liquidity markets; diplomatic exposure at United Nations forums where newly independent states now held majorities; and domestic fragility within European democracies where conscript casualties generated parliamentary crises far faster than professional armies could achieve operational objectives. Each vulnerability proved fatal within days because Eden’s government had constructed its plans inside an information bubble that excluded precisely those voices—American treasury officials, Commonwealth prime ministers without prior consultation, Labor whips counting parliamentary votes—that could have warned against proceeding. The ultimate stress test for Nasser’s security state came not during battle but during its aftermath: could an apparatus built for internal control survive weeks when foreign troops occupied sovereign territory? The answer turned out to be yes, because Nasser immediately reoriented every coercive institution toward patriotic mobilization.

Police stations became arms distribution points; mukhabarat officers shifted from monitoring dissidents to organizing resistance cells behind Anglo-French lines; state-run newspapers printed editions hourly proclaiming victories real and imagined while suppressing news of military reverses until they could be reframed as heroic stands. This fusion of repression with nationalism gave Nasser’s government a resilience that foreign intelligence services consistently underestimated—they saw only authoritarian fragility without grasping how quickly authoritarianism could repurpose itself when an external enemy provided legitimacy for emergency measures. By mid-November, United Nations Emergency Force contingents began arriving in Egypt under a mandate to supervise withdrawal of all foreign forces from Egyptian territory. The first blue-helmeted soldiers landed at Abu Suweir airfield on 15 November; within weeks they would deploy along the canal and across Sinai as buffers between retreating British-French units and advancing Egyptian administrators.

For Eden personally, Suez marked terminal decline: his health collapsed shortly after announcing ceasefire; by January he would resign as prime minister under doctor’s orders recommending complete rest away from political life—a recommendation he followed by flying to Jamaica for convalescence while his Conservative Party struggled to manage fallout from what even sympathetic observers now called “Eden’s war.”

For Mollet’s French government, similarly humiliated, withdrawal confirmed suspicions among Gaullist officers that parliamentary democracy could not prosecute imperial wars effectively—a suspicion that would fester into rebellion within two years over Algeria. For Nasser, standing amid rubble-strewn streets where British tanks still idled under ceasefire orders, awaiting evacuation terms negotiated thousands of miles away between diplomats who had never visited the Suez region personally, the moment represented vindication beyond any expectation reasonable analysis would have predicted six months earlier, when the Aswan funding withdrawal seemed a catastrophic blow against development plans dependent entirely upon external financing arrangements suddenly terminated without the warning notice period customary international practice might ordinarily provide under normal circumstances between allied governments sharing mutual interests in regional stability, Cold War competition, Soviet containment strategies, Middle Eastern oil security, Western European reconstruction coal supplies, maritime trade routes, Indian Ocean and Mediterranean littoral strategic chokepoints, and global commerce patterns established since the nineteenth century imperial expansion era, now unraveling faster than any cabinet minister in London, Paris, Washington, or Moscow could fully comprehend, let alone control through traditional instruments of gunboat diplomacy, economic sanctions, treaty obligations, alliance commitments, and collective security mechanisms inherited from the World War Two settlement, itself the product of a previous generation’s failure to manage great power rivalries without catastrophic violence on a global scale twice within three decades.

In the living memory of elders sitting around cabinet tables in October and November of nineteen hundred and fifty-six, wondering privately whether their grandchildren would study maps showing British bases in Cyprus, Malta, Aden, and Singapore still colored pink in imperial preference trade zones, or whether those maps would instead show national boundaries drawn according to the principles of self-determination, national sovereignty, and anti-colonial solidarity, principles articulated most forcefully by the Egyptian colonel whose army they had just crushed, whose canal they had just occupied, whose political survival they had just ensured beyond all calculation or intention when they signed the secret protocol at the Sèvres villa outside Paris, believing they could reverse history through coordinated military force applied quickly enough to prevent international reaction from mobilizing effectively before a fait accompli was presented to the world as an irreversible accomplished fact, with settlement negotiations on favorable terms preserving imperial prestige, commercial interests, and the strategic position in the Middle East region of vital importance to the Western security architecture in the Cold War geopolitical order.

A chessboard extending from Berlin, Korea, Vietnam, Algeria, Cuba, and the Congo to countless other theaters framed the competition between superpowers whose priorities diverged fundamentally from those of the former colonial metropoles. These metropoles were struggling to adapt to diminished circumstances in a post-imperial age, dawning whether they acknowledged the reality or continued to deny the evidence accumulating daily. Newspaper headlines, diplomatic cables, intelligence reports, and economic data showed an irreversible shift in global power distribution away from London and Paris toward Washington, Moscow, New Delhi, Beijing, Cairo, Accra, Jakarta, Havana, and other capitals. An emerging world order was built upon the ruins of European empires, destroyed not primarily by military defeat in the colonies themselves but by financial exhaustion, political division, and domestic populations unwilling to sustain overseas commitments requiring conscription, taxation, and sacrifice at levels deemed unacceptable.

Democratic electorates were increasingly skeptical that imperial missions were justified. The civilizing rhetoric of the nineteenth century sounded hollow to the ears of a postwar generation raised amidst welfare state expectations: full employment, social security, housing, education, and health services. These competing demands on limited national budgets were strained by Cold War defense expenditures, nuclear weapons programs, conventional force deployments, and a global network of bases, alliances, and commitments stretching resources beyond sustainable limits. This was already visible to informed observers by the mid-nineteen fifties, even before the Suez crisis demonstrated conclusively the limits of military power to achieve political objectives when confronted by a determined nationalist movement enjoying diplomatic support from a superpower willing to exploit imperial overreach to advance its own strategic interests.

Regional influence and global standing were contested in United Nations forums and propaganda battles for the hearts and minds of populations in decolonizing territories, who were watching closely. Would Western powers respect the principles they claimed to uphold—the United Nations Charter, the universal declaration of human rights, the self-determination of peoples—or would old habits of intervention and domination prevail despite rhetorical commitments to new international norms? These norms were emerging slowly and unevenly across a contested terrain of ideological struggle defining the twentieth century’s second half, an era of decolonization and Cold War nuclear brinkmanship. An unprecedented human capacity for destruction combined with an unprecedented human aspiration for freedom, dignity, self-governance, material progress, development, and economic justice. The redistribution of global wealth, resources, and power relationships inherited from the colonial era was now subject to fundamental renegotiation, whose outcome remained uncertain in the autumn of nineteen fifty-six as a ceasefire took hold in Port Said.

In the streets, British soldiers sat waiting for orders, wondering whether they would ever see home again or whether some new crisis would extend their deployment indefinitely, far from families and children growing up without fathers, absent for years of overseas service. The empire was already crumbling beneath them even while they held ground won at the cost of comrades killed, wounded, and maimed, with psychological scars invisible and permanent, altering the lives, futures, families, and communities back home. Britain, France, Israel, and Egypt alike were all caught up in forces larger than any individual nation, leader, movement, or ideology could comprehend, control, direct, or manage.

Toward predictable outcomes, a stable equilibrium, and a lasting peace settlement requires resolving the underlying conflicts, interests, grievances, aspirations, and contradictions fueling the crisis in the first place, rather than merely pausing violence temporarily until the next round of confrontation. This is inevitable given unresolved fundamental issues of sovereignty, territorial integrity, national rights, resource control, strategic waterways, global commons governance, and collective security arrangements acceptable to all parties involved.

Permanent members of the Security Council, the non-aligned movement, and emerging bloc states refuse to choose sides in a Cold War binary alignment pattern, seeking an alternative path of development and an independent foreign policy of non-alignment and active positive neutralism. This involves mediating conflicts, promoting peaceful coexistence, disarmament, decolonization, and economic cooperation through a North-South dialogue on terms favoring developing countries, raw material producers, and commodity exporters. They are dependent upon fair prices, stable markets, and access to technology, capital investment, and assistance without strings attached or neocolonial conditions perpetuating dependency relationships. These are the old imperial patterns disguised in new forms of economic domination, cultural penetration, and ideological influence through soft power and hard power combinations varying according to circumstances.

Regional dynamics, local leadership qualities, mass mobilization capacities, institutional resilience, and the degree of popular support and legitimacy for governments facing external intervention are key. Threats of internal subversion, coups, and instability are endemic features of the postcolonial state formation process everywhere in Africa, Asia, the Middle East, Latin America, the Caribbean, and the Pacific islands. Microstates and territories with emerging sovereignty face incomplete and contested borders, disputed resources, ethnic conflicts, religious divisions, sectarian violence, terrorism, insurgency, and counterinsurgency cycles of violence, repression, and resistance in endless loops of historical causality that are difficult to break, as they are deeply embedded in structures, economies, societies, psychologies, and collective memories.

The ultimatum was a confession of collusion, a document so nakedly contrived that it might have been written as satire had it not been dispatched by two permanent members of the United Nations Security Council. In London, the Foreign Office telegram that transmitted the ultimatum to Cairo ran to eleven paragraphs, but its essential absurdity was that it demanded Egypt withdraw from the Canal Zone—its own sovereign territory—while Israeli columns were still fighting their way toward it. The text made no mention of the fact that the Anglo-French naval task force had been steaming toward the eastern Mediterranean for days, that paratroopers were already boarding aircraft in Cyprus, or that the whole operation had been choreographed at a villa in Sèvres only a week earlier. The ultimatum was not a diplomatic instrument; it was a theatrical prop, a scrap of paper designed to give the appearance of great-power impartiality while covering the tracks of a pre-planned invasion. When the Egyptian cabinet met in emergency session at 5: 30 p. m.

, Nasser read the document aloud, then placed it on the table and said, according to the minutes taken by his secretary, “It is not an ultimatum; it is a declaration of war by other means.”
The military reality behind the ultimatum was already in motion. At 3: 00 p. m. That afternoon, ninety minutes before the document was delivered, Israeli paratroopers of the 890th Battalion had dropped into the eastern approaches of the Mitla Pass, a narrow defile seventy kilometers east of the Suez Canal. The drop was the opening gambit of Operation Kadesh, the Israeli plan to sweep across the Sinai Peninsula and seize the canal’s eastern bank. The force that crossed the border on the afternoon of 29 October was not a defensive militia but a modern armored corps, spearheaded by 200 French-supplied AMX-13 tanks and supported by Mystère fighter jets that had been secretly flown to Israel in the preceding weeks.

The Israeli General Staff had calculated that the Egyptian army, for all its Soviet-supplied T-34s and jet fighters, was a brittle instrument, top-heavy with politically appointed officers, poorly trained in combined-arms warfare, and deployed in static defensive positions that could be outflanked by rapid armored thrusts through the desert. That assessment proved accurate with punishing speed. By midnight on 30 October, Israeli columns had advanced fifty kilometers along three axes—coastal, central, and southern—and had seized the key crossroads at Abu Ageila.
