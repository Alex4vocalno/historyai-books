Chapter 10

The First Official

The Duke of York’s soldiers crossed where the paving of Whitehall ended and the City’s ancient cobbles began. Their boots struck the stones in unison. They carried axes toward the burning core. This advance marked a new phase of intervention, unauthorized by any charter. At that same hour, Samuel Pepys climbed the stairs by the water-side at Whitehall Palace. He brought news that would trigger this martial law.

Pepys had walked from Seething Lane to the river in the early morning. He had seen the fire’s glow against the dawn sky from his window. The Thames carried him westward against the current of boats fleeing downstream with household goods. He ascended to the King’s closet in the Chapel. People gathered about him in the antechamber. They pressed for news. He gave them an account of the streets he had passed. His words dismayed them all.

Word was carried in to the King. Pepys was called immediately. He stood before Charles II and James, Duke of York. He told them what he had seen at London Bridge. He described the falling houses and the wind driving the flames. He did not exaggerate. The facts were alarming enough. He warned that unless His Majesty commanded houses pulled down, nothing would stop the fire from consuming the medieval City. Pepys’s own diary records his words: “I did tell the King and Duke of Yorke what I saw, and that unless his Majesty did command houses to be pulled down nothing could stop the fire.”

Charles listened with the attention of a monarch who had spent years managing naval affairs. Since the Restoration, he had attended Admiralty Board meetings with regularity. He understood logistics, chain of command, and the movement of resources against an unrelenting force. He turned to his brother. James received his orders without delay. He would proceed to the fire with full royal authority to command demolitions and coordinate the defense.

This decision represented a breach in the constitutional fabric of the realm. The City of London governed itself under charters granted by successive monarchs. The Lord Mayor held jurisdiction within the walls. The King ruled the nation but did not normally command within the Corporation’s streets. Yet Charles recognized that municipal authority had dissolved under physical pressure. The fire knew no boundaries. Neither could the response.

James gathered the Trained Bands and the royal guards, men who had served in foreign garrisons and understood demolition and discipline. They formed ranks with mechanical efficiency, checked their muskets, and shouldered their axes. From the Tower garrison and from the trained bands of Westminster they came, carrying the tools of siege warfare. They approached the City as if it were an enemy fortress. The enemy was flame.

They moved toward the burning wards with purpose. Their advance represented a suspension of normal jurisdiction. They crossed into Corporation streets with powers no precedent had sanctioned. The Corporation’s ancient privileges did not cover royal soldiers pulling down private homes without writ or warrant. Yet they came with the King’s word, and that word carried the force of necessity.

The soldiers did not knock at doors. They broke them down. They did not ask permission; they took it from the King’s commission. Martial law, improvised on the cobblestones.

Meanwhile, Lord Mayor Thomas Bloodworth had returned to the conflagration. He found Pudding Lane had become a furnace. The smoke rolled in black clouds above the housetops. The fire had outpaced every bucket brigade organized by the wards. He stood amid the chaos, holding his dignity and civic authority like a shield against the heat.

The smoke stung his eyes. The heat radiated from the cobblestones. He had left his bed to come here. The scale of the disaster overwhelmed his capacity to command. He wore his chain of office, the gold symbol of his municipal authority. He attempted to give orders. The noise of the fire drowned his voice.

The legal mechanisms of the Corporation required consent for demolition. Bloodworth could not compel the inhabitants to sacrifice their properties. The chain of command had collapsed under physical pressure. His orders went unheeded. The aldermen looked to their own houses. The jurisdiction that seemed so clear in the charters—the City governing itself, the King ruling the nation—had become a fiction consumed by flame.

The Duke of York reached the burning wards by mid-morning and established posts on the perimeter of the fire. Three courtiers received command of each station, carrying papers authorizing demolition from Charles himself. The royal strategy was to create firebreaks by pulling down houses to starve the flames of fuel. The courtiers stood at the edge of the burning zone, pointed to houses that must fall, and overruled the owners’ protests. Their authority came from the King, not the Corporation.

Bloodworth watched the soldiers enter his jurisdiction. Their axes rose and fell without municipal permission. He sent a messenger to Whitehall. He reported that he was pulling down houses. But he admitted defeat. The fire overtakes us faster than we can do it.

The messenger rode through streets filled with refugees. He carried Bloodworth’s contradictory message. The Mayor was pulling down houses. The Mayor was defeated. The King read the dispatch with growing alarm. The gap between written orders and ground-level execution yawned open.

James found the Mayor in the smoke-filled streets. He offered additional soldiers to coordinate the demolition. Bloodworth refused them. He cited his dignity and civic authority. He would not admit that municipal power had dissolved. He would not yield his jurisdiction to a royal prince who held no City office. The conflict between jurisdictions paralyzed the defense. The fire did not wait for their negotiation.

The refusal was final. Bloodworth held to his legal position. He was the Lord Mayor. The Duke was a royal intruder in the Corporation’s eyes. Yet the fire erased the boundary between their spheres. The Lord Mayor turned from the Duke. He went home to bed. He abandoned the City while the flames still rose. The Corporation’s chain of command ended at his doorstep. The defense now rested entirely with the royal troops.

His bed was in his house on Hart Street. He lay there while the smoke drifted past his windows. He could hear the shouting. He failed to sleep. The fire advanced through the wards he had left unguarded. His absence marked the complete collapse of municipal authority. The Duke’s men worked on, but they lacked coordination with the local wards. The demolition proceeded in fits and starts.

Bloodworth had hoped to turn the inhabitants’ attention from safeguarding their own possessions to fighting the fire. He had theorized that, with no hopes of saving anything left, they might desperately endeavor the quenching of the flames. The psychology of property defeated this strategy. People carried their furniture instead. They saved their beds and chairs. They lost their roofs and walls. No coordinated defense emerged from the citizenry. The calculation was human and wrong.

James and his guards rode through the streets all that Sunday and into Monday. They attempted to keep order among the panicked crowds. They rescued foreigners from the mob’s suspicions. The mobs suspected French and Dutch residents of arson. They threatened violence. James intervened personally. He pulled endangered foreigners from the crowd. He placed them under military protection. The accusations were false, but the fear was real.

King Charles II sailed down from Whitehall in his barge. He observed the burning from the river. He saw the gap between his written orders and the ground. The institutional collapse was visible. Some streets stood intact while others vanished. The river reflected the flames. The King saw the destruction from midstream. He saw the gaps where his men had worked. He saw the continuous line where they had not. The pattern told the story of failure.

He watched his brother’s efforts. He saw the uneven pattern of defense. The fire moved where the Mayor had abandoned command. It spread westward. The King returned to Whitehall to issue further instructions. He consulted with the Duke. He sent for more men. He tried to apply naval discipline to the streets. But the City was not a fleet. Bloodworth was not a captain who obeyed signals.

Without effective coordination, the demolition proceeded in fragments. Some houses came down before the flames arrived. Others burned while the axes still struck. The fire dictated the pace. The wind drove it forward through the narrow lanes. The firebreaks were partial. The wind came from the east. It had dried the timber for months. It carried sparks across the gaps. The fire jumped the breaks.

Monday wore on. The Duke’s soldiers worked with military precision. They cleared sections of Thames Street. They secured the northern wards. But they could not cover every front. The axes bit into oak beams. The soldiers worked in shifts. They drank small beer. They sweated. They cleared the buildings to the ground.

The fire outflanked them. It moved where Bloodworth’s jurisdiction had begun. The gap in command allowed the flames to spread. The soldiers were too few. The City was too large. They numbered in the hundreds. The streets numbered in the thousands. They could not be everywhere. They chose the major thoroughfares. The lanes burned freely.

The Lord Mayor slept in his house. The Duke’s men pulled down walls without municipal writ. The legal authority and the physical power operated separately. The fire advanced through the gap between them.

At the posts, three courtiers directed the demolition. Citizens watched their property destroyed. Some resisted. The Duke rode up and down. He enforced the royal will. His sword was visible. His authority was clear. But it was not enough.

His horse was tired. He changed horses at the Royal Exchange. The animal was lathered. The Duke rode on. He did not rest. Yet the fire continued its advance. It moved toward the wealthier wards. It threatened the Royal Exchange. It threatened the guild halls. The Duke’s men fell back before the heat.

The flames advanced unchecked. They consumed the wooden City. The royal authority was nominally supreme, but nominally was not enough. The fire did not respond to command. It consumed the connections between streets. Ships could be abandoned. The City could not. Ships were isolated. The streets were connected. The fire used these connections.

The axes fell with a rhythm that mocked the chaos around them. Each stroke required three men working in rotation: one to strike, one to rest, one to clear the chips. A single timber frame house could withstand an hour of such labor before its skeleton yielded, and even then the rubble remained, a smoking barricade that blocked the streets more effectively than it stopped the flames. The soldiers worked by the logic of siege warfare, believing that cleared ground meant safety, but fire traveled through air as readily as across beams.

Sparks leaped the gaps they created, carried by the superheated wind that moved through the canyon-like streets. The Duke’s men cleared a house only to watch the one behind it ignite from within, the fire having entered through a window or under a door, rendering their demolition merely a preparation of fuel. They learned too late that in a city built of pitch-soaked oak and lath, destruction did not equate to defense; the very material they piled in the thoroughfares became pyres that burned longer and hotter than the standing structures, blocking retreat as surely as advance.

The streets that the soldiers needed to move through had become impassable arteries of human despair. Thousands of Londoners dragged feather beds, chests of silver, and rolled tapestries through the cobblestone maze, creating bottlenecks at every intersection where the way narrowed. The Trained Bands, accustomed to forming squares on open fields or holding bridgeheads against foreign enemies, found themselves unable to maneuver through the press of bodies and possessions. Orders to clear the way required force that James had forbidden; he had instructed his men to protect the fleeing citizens, not drive them back into the flames with pike and musket.

The result was paralysis. Carts loaded with household goods overturned in the confusion, spilling their contents to be trampled or abandoned. The soldiers watched as citizens chose to rescue a looking-glass rather than form a bucket line, calculating that their property might survive even if their neighbors’ houses burned. This individual calculus multiplied across fifty thousand souls created a traffic jam of salvation that the royal authority could not untangle.

Bloodworth’s refusal to surrender authority stemmed not merely from pride but from the cold arithmetic of municipal law. The City’s charters, hard-won from Stuart monarchs and fiercely defended by the Corporation’s lawyers, placed liability for unauthorized destruction squarely upon the Mayor’s person. If he ordered a house pulled down and the fire never reached it, the owner could sue him for damages in the City courts, and precedent suggested he would pay from his own estate. The Duke of York operated under sovereign immunity; his brother’s word shielded him from suit. Bloodworth had no such protection.

He stood in Pudding Lane calculating that if he overstepped his commission, he would face financial ruin in the quiet courtrooms that would survive the flames. This legal architecture, designed to protect property rights against arbitrary royal power, now protected nothing but the fire’s advance. The Mayor was trapped by the very liberties he was sworn to defend, a prisoner of parchment while the city burned around him.

Charles II observed this paralysis from Whitehall with the frustration of an admiral watching a fleet disintegrate in a storm. Since the Restoration, he had attended meetings of the Admiralty Board with regularity, and between January 1674 and 1679 he was the most frequent participant among the commissioners. Those years had taught him to think in terms of clear hierarchies, signal flags, and the chain of command that ran from flagship to frigate with unquestioned efficiency. He understood the movement of resources against unrelenting forces; he had directed fleets against the Dutch in conditions of smoke and confusion not entirely unlike these burning streets. He attempted to impose this naval model upon London, issuing orders as if they were signals to a line of battle and expecting the Corporation to respond like a disciplined squadron executing a tack in formation.

But the City was not a fleet. It had no single captain, no uniform articles of war, no court martial to enforce obedience, no ability to jettison cargo to save the hull. The aldermen were not post-captains who would be broken for disobedience; they were property-holding citizens who answered to their own purses and to the Common Council. The King’s administrative genius, honed on the provisioning of warships and the allocation of naval stores, found no purchase in the labyrinthine self-government of the Square Mile, where every freeholder claimed sovereignty over his own threshold.

Beneath Bloodworth, the ward system that had governed London for centuries dissolved into its constituent atoms. Each alderman commanded theoretically the loyalty of his precinct, but as the flames approached their own doorsteps, these municipal officers abandoned their posts to supervise the evacuation of their personal households. The chain of command that should have run from Mayor to alderman to constable to householder snapped at every link.

In Bread Street Ward, the alderman organized the removal of his wine cellar while his constituents scrambled unaided. In Cordwainer Ward, the constable locked his own doors and left his beat. The Corporation’s strength had always lain in its distributed network of local knowledge and authority, but distributed authority required communication, and the smoke and noise severed the lines of contact. The Duke’s centralized military command, for all its energy, could not replicate this local intelligence; his soldiers did not know which courts connected which alleys, which houses stored gunpowder, which wells held water rather than seepage. They fought blind in a city that had gone dark.

The meteorological reality mocked every human effort. The easterly wind that had dried the thatch and timber through the long summer continued without faltering, carrying embers across firebreaks that would have stopped flames in still air. The soldiers calculated that twenty feet of cleared ground would halt the advance; the wind carried burning debris forty, fifty, sometimes eighty feet to ignite roofs untouched by the main conflagration.

The fire created its own weather, drawing air into the superheated core and expelling it in gusts that drove sparks upward and outward in unpredictable patterns. The Duke’s men watched in despair as houses they had cleared to the foundations ignited from above, straw and thatch falling from the sky like incendiary rain. The strategy of demolition assumed a two-dimensional battlefield where controlling the ground meant controlling the enemy, but fire occupied three dimensions, dancing across rooftops and sailing over barriers that would have stopped an army.

The three courtiers whom James had stationed at the perimeter posts brought with them the habits of court rather than command. They were men accustomed to managing estates through stewards, to giving orders that were obeyed because of their rank rather than because of immediate necessity. When they pointed to houses that must come down, they expected the gesture itself to accomplish the work, not understanding that demolition required physical labor, leverage, and time they did not possess.

Tuesday dawned. St Paul’s Cathedral stood in the path. The Duke’s soldiers had cleared some approaches. But the main body of flames moved westward. The institutional failure had allowed the conflagration to reach the heart. The cathedral’s stone walls faced the heat. The wooden scaffolding around it burned. The books stored in the crypt caught fire. The lead on the dome melted. It ran down the walls like water. The stones cracked from the heat. The cathedral burned from within. The symbol of London’s permanence proved temporary.

The fire continued to advance. It moved through the streets where Bloodworth had abandoned command. It consumed the houses that the Mayor could not compel his citizens to sacrifice. The flames paid no heed to the orders from Whitehall. The fire continues to advance, unchecked by the royal authority now nominally in charge.
