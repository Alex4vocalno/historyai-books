Chapter 26

A Monument of Warning and Memory

Seen from above in the spring of 1668, London was a city of two competing labors. The gaze descended through the haze toward the cleared scars of the fire, where new foundations were being laid. Dominating this panorama stood the stark ruin of St. Paul’s, its broken frame presiding over the bustling reconstruction below. This persistent silhouette above the renewal posed a silent question for the city: whether to build a monument to its trauma or simply build over it, to remember the fire or merely to replace it.

The descent continued, narrowing toward a particular vacant plot near the Thames. Fish Street Hill sloped down toward the river, a topography unchanged since the flames had raced up it on the first Sunday of September 1666. Here, where Pudding Lane met the hill, the ground lay empty. Thomas Farriner’s bakery was gone, rebuilt now farther along the lane under new management, though Farriner himself had survived to resume his trade as king’s baker. The original site remained unbuilt. Pudding Lane was a small street running between Eastcheap and Thames Street, historically used by butchers to transport offal to the river. Something else was planned for it.

The decision had already been made. Back in the anxious months of 1667, while the Fire Court sorted claims and the first new houses rose on their old foundations, the question of permanent commemoration had circulated through official channels. The Rebuilding Act of 1669 would formalize what Charles II had already determined. The statute stipulated that, the better to preserve the memory of this dreadful visitation, a column of either brass or stone should be set up on Fish Street Hill, on or near the site of Farynor’s bakery, where the fire began. This clause was not an afterthought appended to practical legislation. It occupied its own clause, its own purpose. The city would have its memorial whether its houses were finished or not.

The mechanism of this decision reveals how the state understood what had happened. The fire required explanation. The plague year of 1665, which had killed perhaps a fifth of London’s inhabitants, had needed no monument; it had left its marks on empty houses and altered streets, visible to anyone who cared to look. The fire was different. It had unfolded as a sequence of human choices: Farriner’s oven, Bloodworth’s refusal, the pulling down of houses that came too late, the wind that dropped finally on Wednesday afternoon. A natural disaster needed no interpretation. A civic catastrophe demanded one.

Christopher Wren received the commission as part of his accumulating authority over the city’s transformation. Surveyor General since 1669, he had already designed the first of the new churches, had drawn plans for St. Paul’s that would wait upon the clearing of its site, had become the indispensable instrument of royal will in the reconstruction. For the Monument, he collaborated with Robert Hooke, Curator of Experiments at the Royal Society and lately named City Surveyor. Their partnership joined architectural ambition to scientific precision. Hooke understood load and stress as mathematical problems. Wren understood column and proportion as classical language. Together they would build the tallest isolated stone column in the world.

Their design emerged through the winter of 1669 and the spring of 1670. A Doric column, plain and severe, rising from a stepped base to a flaming urn at its summit. The height was fixed at 202 feet, 61 and a half metres, corresponding exactly to the distance from the column’s base to the site of Farriner’s bakery. Hooke’s calculation was characteristic: the monument would be a measuring instrument, its own dimension encoding the geography of the disaster. The shaft would be built of Portland stone, that pale limestone already chosen for the new St. Paul’s, so that the memorial and the cathedral would speak the same material language. A spiral staircase within would permit ascent to a viewing platform beneath the urn. Londoners would climb to survey what the fire had done, and what they had built since.

The engineering presented difficulties that occupied Hooke through 1670 and into 1671. A column of such height, freestanding, required foundations adequate to prevent settling or sway. The ground at Fish Street Hill was made ground, built up over centuries of occupation and river silt, unstable where the fire had burned away whatever organic structure it possessed. Hooke supervised the digging of foundations that descended to solid bearing, the laying of a broad base that would distribute weight across the treacherous soil. The work proceeded slowly. Each course of stone had to settle before the next could be added. The column would take six years to complete, far longer than the houses rising around it.

Meanwhile, another construction proceeded in parallel. The inscriptions that would explain the monument’s meaning required drafting, revision, approval. The base would carry texts on its four faces: an account of the fire’s origin and progress, a description of how the monument was started and brought to perfection, a catalogue of mayors and monarchs under whose authority the work proceeded. These conventional elements were expected by the classical vocabulary Wren had chosen. But the inscription on the north side would carry a particular burden. It would name the fire’s cause.

The wording went through several versions. The committee charged with the text included men who remembered the panic of September 1666, when mobs had attacked foreigners and Catholics, when the French watchmaker Robert Hubert had confessed to starting the blaze and been hanged at Tyburn despite manifest evidence of his innocence. Hubert had claimed he threw a fireball through Farriner’s window, though he had arrived in London only after the fire began, though he was crippled and could hardly walk, though his confession shifted under questioning from Westminster to Pudding Lane as his interrogators supplied him with better information. The official narrative required his guilt. Without it, the fire had no human author, no point of moral fixity.

The inscription settled finally upon a formula that would be carved in 1681, after the column’s completion but authorized by decisions made in these earlier years. It named the treachery and malice of the Popish faction as the fire’s cause. The Latin version, composed for the educated eye, employed a phrase that rendered this as But the Popish frenzy which wrought such horrors. This was not remembrance but instruction: it taught future generations what to believe about an event they had not witnessed, fixing in stone an interpretation that judicial process had failed to secure.

The political context of this wording belongs to the years after 1678, when the Popish Plot would make anti-Catholic accusation once again central to English politics. But the impulse to such accusation was present from the first. Charles II’s government, in commissioning the monument, sought to provide what the chaos of 1666 had denied: a single, authoritative account of catastrophe. The city needed this account for practical reasons. Rebuilding required credit, investment, confidence that London would not burn again. A fire without cause was a fire that might recur at any moment. A fire caused by Catholic conspiracy was a fire that proper vigilance could prevent.

Wren and Hooke understood their commission in architectural terms. The column’s height, its classical severity, its position commanding the approach to London Bridge, all served to make visible the state’s power to commemorate and control. They did not design the inscriptions. Their responsibility ended where engineering met propaganda. Yet their design accommodated that propaganda. The urn of flame at the summit, gilded to catch the light, transformed the fire itself into an emblem of royal and civic survival. The viewing platform offered a prospect not of devastation but of recovery: the new streets, the new wharves, the cleared ground where St. Paul’s would rise again. The monument instructed its climbers to see what the state wished them to see.

The construction site at Fish Street Hill became a fixture of the cityscape through 1671 and 1672. Londoners passing between Thames Street and Eastcheap watched the column rise course by course, slower than the timber-framed houses that surrounded it, more permanent than anything else being built. The workmen were Hooke’s responsibility, their wages entered in his accounts, their progress reported to the Commission for Rebuilding. The foundation stone had been laid with ceremony appropriate to a royal project. Each subsequent stage attracted less attention, until the column became simply present, a fact of the street like the new brick buildings that enclosed it.

For Samuel Pepys, the monument entered awareness gradually, as one element among many in the transformed city. His diary, which he would abandon in 1669 before the column’s completion, recorded the early decisions without anticipating their result. He noted Wren’s appointment as Surveyor General, the progress of the new churches, the continuing difficulty of his own house repairs. The memorialization of the fire interested him less than its material consequences: the loss of his books, the cost of rebuilding, the altered geography of his daily routes. When he wrote of the fire’s cause, he recorded what he had seen (the panic against Catholics, the false confession of Hubert) without endorsing the official version. His diary preserved a private memory against the public instruction that the monument would eventually provide.

John Evelyn, whose diary continued through these years, attended more explicitly to the symbolism of reconstruction. He had walked through the burning city with Charles II, had described the ruin of St. Paul’s in terms borrowed from biblical lamentation. For Evelyn, the monument represented a proper piety, a recognition of divine judgment that the profane city required. Yet even he, when he recorded the laying of foundation stones and the progress of Wren’s designs, did not anticipate the anti-Catholic inscription that would eventually disfigure what he understood as a work of Christian commemoration. The monument he imagined was universal in its warning. The monument that rose was particular in its blame.

The contrast between these private records and the public text reveals the mechanism at work. The state required a memory that would serve present needs. Pepys and Evelyn, writing for themselves or for posterity, preserved complexity: doubt, contradiction, the sense that catastrophe exceeded any single explanation. The monument reduced this complexity to a single cause, a single moral. Its stone permanence would outlast the diaries, the Fire Court records, the testimony of those who had actually smelled the smoke and felt the heat. Future generations would encounter the fire first as inscription, only secondarily as history.

The engineering of this result involved multiple parties, each with distinct interests. The Crown sought stability and credit. The City Corporation sought to demonstrate its cooperation with royal authority while protecting its chartered liberties. Wren and Hooke sought architectural opportunity and professional advancement. The masons and laborers sought wages. The public, when it climbed the staircase or read the inscriptions, would seek understanding, consolation, perhaps warning. The monument had to satisfy all these demands, or at least appear to.

The height of 202 feet served multiple purposes. It made the column visible from the river, from the approaches to London Bridge, from the rebuilt streets that surrounded it. It permitted the encoding of distance as dimension. It challenged comparison with other columns ancient and modern, asserting London’s place in a classical tradition of commemorative architecture. And it provided employment for Hooke’s mathematical ingenuity, his capacity to calculate stress and stability in unprecedented proportions.

The Doric order carried its own meanings. Severe, unadorned, masculine in its proportions, it distinguished the monument from the Corinthian exuberance of some contemporary designs, from the Gothic survivals of the old St. Paul’s. Wren had used Doric elements in his early churches, establishing a vocabulary of classical restraint that the monument would extend. The order spoke of Roman virtue, of civic duty, of the kind of republican memory that Augustus had appropriated for imperial purposes. Charles II’s London claimed this inheritance while transforming it.

The urn of flame at the summit completed the symbolic program. Fire as destruction, fire as purification, fire as the city’s defining trial: all these meanings converged in the gilded metal that caught the morning and evening light. The flame was both what had happened and what had been survived. It marked the column as commemorative rather than functional, as belonging to the category of memorial rather than of lighthouse or watchtower, though its height might have served such practical purposes.

The inscription process revealed what political work architecture was required to perform. The committee drafts have not survived in sequence, but revision and compromise left their marks on every line of final text: accusation against Catholics explicit yet qualified; blame attributed to faction rather than religion; official condemnation placed above popular rage.

For those who remembered 1666, this instruction must have carried particular force. They had seen what happened when accusation escaped official control: the attacks on strangers in the streets, the summary executions of suspected looters, the eventual hanging of Robert Hubert as a scapegoat whose guilt satisfied no one who examined it closely. The monument’s text represented an institutionalization of this scapegoating, a fixing of blame that would survive the death of everyone who knew it to be false. The stone would lie more persuasively than the gallows.

The contrast between Hubert’s fate and the monument’s accusation illuminates the transformation underway. In 1666, the need for a human cause had produced a living victim, quickly tried and dispatched, his confession extracted and his body displayed as warning. By 1669, this mechanism had proved inadequate. Hubert’s innocence was too widely known, his execution too obviously theatrical. The monument provided a different kind of satisfaction: not the death of a guilty man but the permanent assertion of guilt itself. The Catholics would not be executed. They would be inscribed.

Wren’s involvement in this process remained professionally detached. His surviving papers contain no reflection on the inscription’s content, no protest against its falsification of events he had witnessed. His business was with stone and proportion, with the engineering problems that Hooke’s calculations made soluble. Yet his design accommodated the inscription, provided its physical setting, lent classical authority to partisan accusation. The separation of architectural from political responsibility served all parties. Wren could build without lying. The state could lie without building.

The construction proceeded through 1672 and beyond, the subject of periodic reports and inspections. The column rose above the surrounding rooftops, visible from increasing distances, a marker of location for ships on the river and travelers on the roads. Its meaning remained unstable during these years. Until the inscriptions were carved, it was simply a column, impressive in scale but ambiguous in purpose. Only with the completed text would it become fully what its commissioners intended: an instrument of public memory.

The delay between structural completion and inscription installation (some five years) suggests uncertainty about final wording, or perhaps political calculation about the moment of revelation. The Popish Plot of 1678 created the atmosphere in which explicit anti-Catholic accusation became once again expedient. The monument’s text rode this wave of sentiment, fixing in stone what might otherwise have remained provisional. When Londoners read the inscription after 1681, they encountered not the immediate aftermath of the fire but its reinterpretation through subsequent crisis.

For London’s identity this timing mattered more than any single clause could show: here stood no settled verdict on what happened in September 1666—only one version among many competing accounts—private diaries still whispered doubt; oral tradition still carried contradiction; contradictory testimony still survived in memory—but none could displace what now stood engraved where all could read it.

The physical experience of the monument reinforced this authority. To climb the spiral staircase was to submit to its geometry, to emerge at height into a prospect organized by its commanding position. The viewing platform directed attention outward and downward, toward the new city that had replaced what burned. The inscription waited at ground level, accessible to those who had not climbed, legible to those who passed by on daily business. Both experiences (elevated overview and grounded reading) served state purposes. The monument taught citizens to see their city as recovered, and to remember its trauma as caused by designated enemies.

The parallel with St. Paul’s, rising slowly on its own site through these same years, intensifies this reading. Both structures embodied royal authority over urban transformation. Both employed classical vocabulary to assert continuity with ancient civilization. Both would outlast their builders, speaking to generations unborn. Yet where St. Paul’s was devotional in purpose, the monument was didactic. Where the cathedral invited prayer, the column instructed memory. Together they composed a program of civic reconstruction that united sacred and secular, survival and warning.

By 1672, the shaft had risen high enough to be visible from the river approaches, a white finger against the city’s smoke-hazed sky. The work continued. Each course of stone locked into place with Hooke’s precision, each measurement checked against the calculations that would keep it standing through wind and weather and time. Below, at Fish Street Hill, pedestrians navigated the construction barriers that narrowed the lane, stepping around piles of stone and timber, past the hoardings that hid the foundations from casual view. The monument was becoming infrastructure, part of the daily geography of London even before its completion.

This was the mechanism in action: not a single decision but a sequence of choices, each made by particular people at particular moments, each contributing to an outcome that none had fully envisioned. Charles II had wanted a memorial. Wren and Hooke had wanted to build high. The committees had wanted to settle blame. The city had wanted to rebuild its credit and its confidence. These desires converged at Fish Street Hill, in a column that would encode them all in stone and height and text.

The digging of foundations for the Monument at Fish Street Hill established an official narrative in stone.
