Chapter 16

The Burning of the Booksellers

Seen from above, the whole map of the City showed the legal quarter’s flight as a stain spreading outward: the streets around Fleet Street choked with wagons and handcarts, men in black gowns arguing at crossroads about the ownership of chests of parchment. Monday had ended in that confusion. By Tuesday morning, the fire had found new country to the north and west. It moved now with the systematic appetite of a thing that had learned what fed it best. The wind still blew from the east, still drove the flames up the narrow lanes toward the cathedral and the dense warren of shops that clustered in its shadow.

The booksellers had watched the smoke since Sunday. They had seen the glow over the riverfront, heard the bells change from summons to alarm, watched the stream of refugees thicken along Cheapside. Most had done nothing. The trade required capital sunk in inventory; a bookseller who abandoned his stock abandoned his livelihood. Stationers’ Hall stood in Ave Maria Lane, a brick building that seemed, to men who measured risk against weekly receipts, a reasonable shelter. The company’s records show members storing goods in the hall’s crypt, in its upper rooms, in any space that could be cleared of its ordinary contents. They believed the stone walls would hold. They believed the fire would turn, or be turned, before it reached the Churchyard.

Paternoster Row ran parallel to the cathedral’s northern flank, a street of timber-framed houses whose ground floors opened directly to the street as shops, with living quarters and storage above. The booksellers had made this district the intellectual marketplace of the nation. Here a man could buy a Bible in Welsh or a pamphlet attacking the Dutch, a law text fresh from the press or a ballad about the latest murder.

The stock represented merchandise and something more: the physical substrate of public discourse. The Restoration had brought censorship, but it had also brought a hunger for news, for argument, for the printed word that circulated in coffee houses and was read aloud to the illiterate. This commerce depended on objects—reams of paper, stacks of stitched pamphlets, bound volumes shelved from floor to ceiling—concentrated in a space of perhaps four acres, packed with the efficiency of a warehouse and the combustibility of a tinderbox.

The fire reached the Row on Tuesday morning. The exact hour is uncertain; witnesses would later disagree about whether the first sparks fell before or after the cathedral itself caught. What is certain is the sequence of vulnerability. The buildings were old, their timber seasoned by decades of London summers. The stock within them—paper, parchment, the oakum used in binding, the spirits employed in sizing—burned faster than ordinary household goods. The booksellers had packed their most valuable items into Stationers’ Hall, believing the stone structure would resist. They had not considered that the hall’s roof was lead, that lead melts, that molten lead runs downward and ignites what it touches.

The loss was documented because the booksellers were organized. The Stationers’ Company maintained records; its members could write. In the months following the fire, they would submit petitions, compile inventories, attempt to calculate what had been destroyed. These documents survive in fragments, enough to show the scale of what burned. One bookseller, Andrew Crooke, had his warehouse in the Churchyard; he would later claim losses exceeding £3, 000 in printed books alone. Another, Thomas Dring, lost his shop and his dwelling together. The company’s common stock, held in trust for members, burned in the hall’s crypt. The total damage to the trade would eventually be estimated—cautiously, for many records were themselves consumed—at something approaching £100, 000, a sum that represented private wealth and the accumulated intellectual capital of a generation.

The mechanism of destruction followed a pattern that by Tuesday had become familiar. The wind drove sparks ahead of the main fire front; these settled on roofs and in upper windows, igniting separately from the main advance. The booksellers, like others before them, attempted to remove their stock. They loaded carts, hired porters, carried bundles to whatever open space seemed safe. But the density of the district worked against them. The lanes were narrow, the carts few, the time shorter than hope had suggested. Some saved portions of their stock; many saved nothing. The fire moved faster than human calculation could adjust.

The informational consequences were immediate and lasting. The London Gazette, the official newspaper of the government, had its printing house in the Blackfriars, outside the fire’s path, and continued publication. But the Gazette was a single voice, controlled, censored, appearing twice weekly. The broader ecosystem of print—the pamphlets that responded to events within days, the books that carried arguments too extended for periodical publication, the very paper on which these were printed—suffered a catastrophic contraction. For weeks after the fire, paper was scarce. For months, certain titles were simply unavailable, their plates and remaining copies consumed together. The market for ideas did not cease, but it operated under constraint, with voices eliminated that might otherwise have contributed to the understanding of what had happened and what should follow.

The booksellers’ loss had a distinctive character. The fire had already destroyed dwellings, warehouses, the administrative machinery of the City. These damages were severe, measurable in pounds and in lives disrupted. The burning of Paternoster Row went further: it destroyed a public good, thinned the medium through which a polity understood itself. The booksellers were not merely merchants; they were, in the vocabulary of the time, “stationers,” men licensed to produce and sell the written word, subject to the regulation of the Stationers’ Company and, through it, to the broader supervision of the state. Their shops were the points of distribution for the texts that shaped opinion, recorded law, preserved religious truth. When these shops burned, more than private property was consumed.

The fire’s path through the district can be traced in the claims submitted to the Fire Court in the years following. The court’s records show the same pattern repeated: shop and dwelling consumed together, stock destroyed, tools lost, the means of livelihood extinguished. The booksellers sought compensation for goods and for “utensils,” the presses and type and binding equipment that represented fixed capital. Some had insured their stock; many had not. The insurance market was new, imperfect, itself damaged by the fire’s scale. The court would struggle to separate losses caused by the flames themselves from losses caused by the measures taken to halt them: a warehouse burned by advancing fire versus one pulled down to create a firebreak. This distinction mattered for liability; it also mattered for understanding how the disaster had operated upon the city’s fabric.

The Stationers’ Hall itself illustrates the problem. The building survived the initial fire, its stone walls standing while timber structures around it fell. But the goods stored within burned nonetheless, destroyed by heat, by falling debris, by the lead that melted from the roof and ran through the upper rooms. Was this fire damage or structural damage? The court would eventually assign it to the former, but the question exposed the inadequacy of existing categories. The booksellers had acted reasonably, even prudently, in concentrating their valuables in what seemed the strongest building available. They had not foreseen that strength itself—stone walls, lead roof—could become instruments of destruction, containing and intensifying heat until it found other fuel.

The broader pattern of the fire’s advance is visible in the sequence of destruction. From the Guildhall, where the City’s records had burned on Monday, the flames moved northwest toward the cathedral and its surrounding district. The wind determined this path, but the wind had been blowing from the east since Sunday; the fire’s reach on Tuesday represented not a change in weather but a failure of containment. The Duke of York’s command post at Temple Bar, established late Monday, had stabilized the western front along Fleet Street. But the northern and northwestern sectors remained exposed, and the fire found in the booksellers’ quarter the same conditions that had fed it elsewhere: dense construction, combustible contents, inadequate water supply, confused authority.

The human experience of this destruction is harder to recover. The booksellers were not, by and large, men of sufficient standing to leave extensive diaries or correspondence. Their names appear in company records, in court petitions, in the lists of those granted relief by parish or corporation. We know that some fled early, saving what they could carry; that others stayed too long, attempting to salvage inventory that proved too heavy or too bulky; that at least one man died in the Churchyard, though the circumstances of his death are disputed. The fire’s casualties were fewer than its scale might suggest, but they were real, and they were concentrated among those who, like the booksellers, had reasons to delay their departure.

A telling fragment survives. The arch down into the lower church, and so all the goods burned. A very great loss. His father hath lost above £1000 in books. The speaker is unidentified, the context unclear: whether the “lower church” refers to a crypt, a cellar, some architectural feature of Stationers’ Hall or of St. Paul’s itself. The figure of £1000 suggests a substantial merchant, perhaps a wholesale distributor rather than a retail shopkeeper. The baldness of the statement—arch, goods, loss, the precise sum—conveys what elaboration cannot: the suddenness with which security became catastrophe, the inadequacy of stone against heat and falling lead.

The informational blackout that followed had political consequences. The fire occurred in a moment of heightened tension between England and the Dutch Republic; war had been declared in March, and the English fleet had suffered a humiliating defeat in June. Rumors of arson spread immediately, and the absence of competing sources of information allowed official versions to dominate. The London Gazette reported the fire’s progress under strict censorship; private newsletters, which might have carried alternative accounts, were interrupted by the destruction of their producers and distributors. The booksellers’ quarter had been particularly important for the circulation of manuscript news, the “separates” and “intelligences” that supplemented and sometimes contradicted official publication. This channel was now closed.

The recovery of the trade was slow. The Stationers’ Company met in temporary quarters; its members scattered to whatever premises they could find, many across the river in Southwark or in the suburbs that the fire had not reached. The rebuilding of Paternoster Row would wait upon the larger reconstruction of the city, and upon the resolution of property claims that kept sites in dispute for years. When the district rose again, it would be under new regulations, with wider streets and brick construction required. But the intellectual geography had changed. The concentration of the book trade in a single district had been, in part, a function of guild regulation, of the company’s control over who could print and where. The fire disrupted this control; the rebuilding, and the larger transformation of London’s economy in the decades following, would never fully restore it.

The cathedral itself stood exposed by this destruction. St. Paul’s had been undergoing repair since 1661, its medieval structure judged unsound, scaffolding covering much of its exterior. The booksellers had used its churchyard as a marketplace, a gathering space, a storage area for goods that seemed temporarily surplus. Now that churchyard was a waste of ash and cooling metal, the shops that had defined its perimeter collapsed into their cellars. The great building rose above this ruin, apparently intact, its stone walls seeming to promise what the Stationers’ Hall had falsely offered. But the fire had reached its base, and the materials of its repair—the timber scaffolding, the wooden sheds where workmen stored their tools, the temporary structures that had accumulated around a decade of construction—provided fuel that stone alone could not.

The wind continued. Tuesday was the day of greatest destruction not because the fire was largest—it had covered more ground by Wednesday—but because it reached on this day the concentrations of value that made its advance most consequential. The Guildhall had been administrative; the booksellers’ quarter was intellectual; the cathedral was symbolic. Each represented a different order of loss, and the sequence of their burning traced the fire’s capacity to find what a city most valued and least protected. The booksellers had believed themselves secure in their organization, in their stone hall, in the apparent solidity of their trade. They had not understood that security in one dimension—guild membership, licensed premises—could coexist with radical vulnerability in another: the physical concentration of combustible goods, the absence of effective fire prevention, the dependence on a water supply that failed when most needed.

The claims submitted to the Fire Court would eventually number in the thousands. The booksellers’ petitions stand out for their attention to intellectual property, to the loss of copies and plates and “copies” in the technical sense of manuscript authority. These were assets that existed only in the recognition of the trade, that could not be inventoried like bales of cloth or barrels of wine. The court struggled with such claims, as it struggled with all that exceeded the categories of existing law. What was the value of a text that existed in no physical form, that could be reprinted from memory or reconstructed from fragments? The booksellers asserted rights that the common law barely recognized; the court’s patience with these assertions reflected both the disorder of the moment and print’s emerging importance to national life.

The concentration of value in so small a space had made Paternoster Row irresistible to the fire’s advance, yet this same concentration had seemed, to the men who worked there, a source of collective security. The Stationers’ Company regulated entry to the trade, controlled the number of presses, policed the boundaries between printer, bookseller, and binder. This corporate structure had created a district where every adjacent shop dealt in related goods, where credit flowed through established channels, where reputation accumulated in visible premises. The fire exposed the hidden cost of this arrangement: when destruction came, it found the trade’s entire infrastructure gathered for convenient consumption. The booksellers had built a system designed to maximize the efficiency of intellectual commerce, never imagining that efficiency would translate so directly into vulnerability.

The specific contents of the shops amplified this structural peril. A merchant’s warehouse might hold textiles or hardware, goods valuable but relatively inert. The bookseller’s stock was itself a fire waiting to happen. Paper, whether loose or bound, offered enormous surface area to any spark; the sizing applied to make it receptive to ink contained starches and glues that burned readily. Parchment and vellum, though more durable, curled and blackened in heat that left stone unscathed. The inks—carbon-based blacks, iron-gall formulations, the occasional mineral pigment—contributed their own chemistry to the conflagration. Even the tools of the trade participated: the turpentine and linseed oil used in varnishing bindings, the alcohol-based spirits employed in cleaning type, the tallow candles that lit workshops before dawn and after dusk. The booksellers had surrounded themselves with the means of their craft, never calculating how thoroughly those means might be turned against them.

The wind’s persistence from the east created a phenomenon that witnesses would struggle to describe, the flames seeming to hunt through the district with deliberate intent. Sparks traveled farther than seemed possible, carried on currents generated by the fire’s own heat, rising and falling in patterns no one could predict.

A bookseller might watch the main fire front a street away, believe himself safe for the quarter-hour needed to load another bundle, and find his upper floor already burning from a shower of embers that had descended silently through a ventilation gap or around a chimney pot. This distributed ignition—fires starting not in sequence but in parallel, across a wide front—overwhelmed the capacity of any individual to save more than a fraction of his holding. The booksellers had planned for orderly retreat, for the prioritized removal of most valuable stock; they encountered instead a chaos of simultaneous emergencies, each demanding immediate attention, each diverting resources from the others.

The particular tragedy of Stationers’ Hall deserves careful attention, for it reveals how thoroughly conventional wisdom had misled those who followed it.

The company’s members had, in the hours before the fire’s arrival, conducted a kind of triage of their possessions. The most portable and immediately valuable items—cash, jewelry, certain manuscript authorities—went with individuals or into the hands of trusted servants dispatched to safety. The bulk of the stock, the heavy inventory that represented the trade’s working capital, they consolidated in what they believed to be the strongest available structure.

This decision was not panic but calculation, the product of collective deliberation among men who understood risk as a commercial category. They had observed, or heard described, the fire’s behavior elsewhere: its preference for timber over stone, its difficulty in penetrating thick walls. They had not observed, because no one had yet seen, what happened when stone contained rather than excluded intense heat, when the protective shell became a furnace.

The lead roof of Stationers’ Hall was, in ordinary circumstances, an asset. It resisted weather, required little maintenance, signaled the company’s prosperity and permanence. Under fire conditions, it became a mechanism of destruction.

By Tuesday afternoon, the smoke rising from Paternoster Row had merged with the larger pall that hung over the entire city. The booksellers who had escaped with their lives gathered where they could, in the open spaces to the north, in the suburbs, watching the glow that marked where their livelihoods burned. Some had saved enough to begin again; most faced years of debt and dependency. The company’s charitable fund, itself diminished by the fire, would distribute relief according to rules that favored established members over newcomers, the fully licensed over the marginal. The inequalities that had characterized the trade before the fire were reproduced in its aftermath, inscribed now in the record of who had lost how much and who could prove it.

The ash of paper and melted type metal in St. Paul’s Churchyard formed a landscape of intellectual ruin, over which the cathedral itself now stood exposed and doomed.
