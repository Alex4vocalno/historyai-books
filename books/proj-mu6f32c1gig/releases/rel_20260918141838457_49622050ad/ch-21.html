Chapter 21

The First Relief

The smoke had cleared enough by Saturday, 8 September 1666, for the scale of dependency to become visible. King Charles II, surveying from the river or from the higher ground at Whitehall, looked upon a city that had fed itself for centuries now unable to bake a loaf. The medieval street pattern was gone. The baking houses along Thames Street had burned, and with them the markets, the granaries, the fisheries. What remained was a population scattered in fields, possessing nothing but what they had carried on their backs, and that population was hungry.

The royal response came quickly. On 5 September, the King issued a proclamation ordering that ‘for supply of the distressed people left destitute by the late dreadful and dismal fire… great proportions of bread be brought daily’ to the former markets and to those lately ordained. Orders followed to set up public ovens in Moorfields and other open spaces beyond the walls. Bread would be baked and distributed to the destitute. The machinery of relief, such as it was, began to turn. Royal authority, which had spent the fire’s duration struggling to impose order on flames, now attempted to impose order on survival.

Yet the gap between command and condition yawned wide. In the same fields where the King’s ovens were ordered erected, diarists and letter-writers recorded a different reality. The encampments that had formed in Moorfields, in Finsbury Fields, in Islington and Highgate and as far as Hampstead, were not orderly distributions of charity. They were collections of desperation. People who had owned houses on Friday, 31 August, now slept in ditches. Those who had sold fish, or cloth, or legal counsel, now begged for bread. And among them moved others: traders who had escaped the fire with their stock, or who brought goods from the counties, or who simply recognized opportunity in catastrophe.

Royal proclamation met grassroots squalor. Intended generosity met profiteering. The King’s command assumed a population that would queue, receive, and disperse. The actual population of the fields was dense, mobile, and competing for scarce resources. Individual virtue had little to do with it. Institutional imagination had collided with physical reality.

The mechanism of relief, examined from its outcome backward, reveals how thin the administrative fabric had become. The City of London, which had governed its own affairs through the Corporation and its courts, had lost its physical seat. The Guildhall was burned. The Royal Exchange was gone. The halls of the great livery companies (Grocers, Drapers, Mercers) stood in ruins or in precarious partial survival. The men who had administered poor relief, maintained the markets, regulated prices, and judged disputes were themselves displaced, their records destroyed, their customary authority uprooted.

Into this vacuum stepped the Crown. Charles II had spent the fire’s duration in visible motion: riding through threatened streets, directing firebreaks, receiving reports. Now he extended that personal intervention into the economic sphere. The King’s order to bake bread carried a claim of prerogative in an area where royal authority had traditionally been limited. The City Corporation governed markets; the Crown did not. Yet here was the King commanding where bread should be made, and implicitly, to whom it should be given.

The City Council minutes, where they survive from these days, record the municipal response as both cooperative and defensive. The Corporation accepted the King’s aid while attempting to maintain its institutional dignity. On 6 September, two days before the baking order, the Council had already moved to establish substitute markets. The proclamation ordered markets in Bishopsgate Street, Tower Hill, Smithfield, and other locations outside the burned area. The logic was practical: the population needed food, the traditional markets were destroyed, new sites must be designated. But the logic was also political. The Corporation, by acting first, by designating its own market spaces, preserved the forms of its authority even as the substance crumbled.

The provincial response added another layer. Records from other towns and counties document donations flowing toward London. Exeter sent money. York organized collections. The corporate solidarity of England’s urban network, often competitive in ordinary times, activated in face of catastrophe. These contributions arrived without systematic coordination. They supplemented royal and municipal efforts without replacing them. The result was a patchwork: some refugees received aid from multiple sources, others fell between the administrative cracks.

The bread itself became a measure of the system’s reach. The ovens ordered for Moorfields and comparable sites were substantial constructions, requiring fuel, flour, and bakers to operate. The quantities involved (pounds of bread promised, numbers of ovens authorized) appear in the records as assertions of capacity. Yet capacity to bake was not capacity to distribute. The fields where the homeless gathered were not designed for queue management. There were no lists, no tickets, no established criteria of need. The strong received. The weak waited. The cunning traded.

Contemporary diaries capture this friction. Samuel Pepys, whose record of the fire’s progress has provided the narrative spine for these days, turned his attention in the aftermath to the conditions of survival. His entries for 7–10 September note the continuing disorder, the presence of the King and Duke in the streets, the beginnings of collection for the homeless. John Evelyn, writing from his house at Deptford and his visits to the ruins, recorded the “miserable inhabitants” in the fields, their “poor goods” scattered, their “lamentable” condition. Other letter-writers, less famous but equally precise, described the price of bread rising in the unburned suburbs, the farmers withholding grain in anticipation of higher returns, the city refugees competing with the local poor for charity.

The profiteering that these accounts document was not universal, but it was systematic enough to constitute a pattern. Some traders who brought goods to the emergency markets charged prices that reflected scarcity and transport risk. Others exploited information asymmetries: selling to desperate buyers who could not compare prices, who did not know what aid might be available without cost. The Crown attempted to suppress this through proclamation, fixing prices for bread and other necessities. Price-fixing in conditions of shortage, however, tends to produce evasion rather than compliance. The records of enforcement from these weeks are sparse; the evidence of continued complaint is not.

The relief effort’s structural flaw was its reliance on location. The homeless had to come to the bread, to the markets, to the distribution points. Those who were old, sick, wounded in the fire’s chaos, or simply too exhausted to travel fell outside the system’s reach. The fields became sorting mechanisms, separating the mobile from the immobile, the resourceful from the helpless. Deliberate cruelty played no part in this; it was what happened when a welfare system was improvised without a welfare infrastructure.

The Crown gained political capital from its visible role. Charles II appeared in the fields, directing, distributing, commanding; the Duke of York worked alongside him. Kingship became performance: the monarch touching his suffering subjects, the court descending from Whitehall to Moorfields. The propaganda value was considerable. Newsletters and gazettes circulating in these weeks emphasized royal activity, royal concern, royal command. The King’s person, rather than his institutions, became the guarantee of survival.

Yet this personalization carried risk. If relief failed — if bread ran short, if disease spread among the encamped population — responsibility attached directly to Charles himself rather than diffusing through institutions that might absorb blame or share credit for success.

The week from 8 to 14 September established patterns that would persist through the longer recovery. The tension between royal command and municipal administration, between centralized direction and local knowledge, between charitable impulse and economic calculation (these shaped every subsequent decision about rebuilding, about compensation, about justice). The relief effort was the first test of whether London could govern itself in ruins. The answer was qualified. It could govern, but not well. It could feed its people, but not all of them, not equally, not without friction and waste.

The physical conditions of the encampments worsened as the week progressed. September weather in England is variable, and rain fell on some of these nights. The refugees had little shelter. Tents were scarce; most people huddled under blankets, under improvised lean-tos, under the open sky. Sanitation was nonexistent. The fields that had served as military muster grounds, as pleasure gardens, as open common, became cities of filth. Contemporary observers noted the smell, the disease risk, the moral as well as physical disorder. Women and children, in particular, suffered conditions that violated the period’s standards of decent protection.

The charitable impulse that the fire aroused was genuine and substantial. Churches across England organized collections. Neighboring counties sent grain and livestock. Private individuals of means (some with London connections, some simply responding to the news) contributed money and goods. This was the traditional response to catastrophe in a society without insurance, without state welfare, without institutionalized relief. The parish, the county, the realm itself constituted a network of mutual obligation that activated in crisis.

Yet charity without coordination produces inequality. Some parishes sent their collections to specific individuals, to favored causes, to institutions they knew. Some of the homeless had powerful connections: merchants with country correspondents, lawyers with client networks, clergy with parishioners elsewhere. These connections could secure preferential access to relief. The truly isolated, the poor without patrons, the immigrants without parish membership, found themselves at the end of every queue.

The records of the Fire Court, established later to adjudicate disputes arising from the disaster, preserve fragments of these early relief experiences. Witnesses in property cases mention, in passing, where they slept in September 1666, how they ate, what they lost. These incidental details, collected for other purposes, confirm the diary evidence of disorganization. People moved between encampments, seeking better conditions. They traded their remaining goods for food. They entered into improvised arrangements: sharing shelter, pooling resources, hiring protection against theft.

Theft itself was a chronic problem. The militia patrols that had attempted to prevent looting during the fire continued their work in the ruins, but their numbers were insufficient, their authority disputed. The fields outside the walls were harder to police. Refugees robbed refugees. Professional thieves, attracted by the concentration of desperate people with portable property, operated in the margins. The Crown’s proclamations against looting continued, but enforcement depended on local magistrates who were themselves displaced, distracted, or overwhelmed.

The baking operation in Moorfields became, by the second week, a fixed point in this fluid landscape. The ovens, once erected, required continuous fuel and flour supply. The bakers, whether royal employees or impressed civilians, worked under pressure. The crowd that gathered each morning represented the relief system’s success and its limitation: success in producing bread, limitation in the numbers who could be served, the time consumed in distribution, the disorder of the process itself.

The quantities involved deserve attention. The King ordered bread baked for thousands. The exact figures vary in the sources: some records suggest tens of thousands of loaves distributed, others imply smaller numbers with larger per-person rations. The discrepancy itself is significant. No single authority controlled measurement. The royal household, the City Corporation, the parish vestries, private charities (all kept their own accounts, or failed to keep them). The total relief effort was the sum of partial, overlapping, sometimes competitive initiatives.

This was the “flawed but essential framework” that the chapter’s claim identifies. Essential, because without it thousands would have starved. Flawed, because it reflected no coherent principle of distribution, no established procedure for emergency welfare, no accountability for failure. The framework was improvised from available materials: royal authority, municipal tradition, charitable custom, economic necessity. It held together through the immediate crisis, but it established no precedent that could be easily repeated.

The Crown’s political initiative in relief had longer consequences. Charles II, by demonstrating that the monarch could feed the people when the Corporation could not, advanced a claim to broader intervention in London’s affairs. This claim would surface in the rebuilding legislation, in the disputes over property rights, in the eventual settlement of fire debts. The King had shown himself necessary. Necessity, in political argument, implies continuing obligation.

The Corporation recognized this danger. Its council minutes from mid-September show efforts to reassert municipal competence. The substitute markets, the poor relief collections, the correspondence with other cities (these were activities that preserved institutional presence). The Corporation could not match the Crown’s visibility, but it could match its range. Every function that the municipal structure performed, however imperfectly, was an argument against royal takeover.

The refugees themselves were not passive recipients of this institutional competition. They moved between jurisdictions, compared conditions, made choices. Some left London entirely, returning to ancestral villages or seeking opportunity in other towns. Others pressed against the walls, waiting for permission to re-enter the ruins, to salvage what they could, to re-establish claims. The fields were temporary, everyone understood. The question was what would replace them.

The answer, in these first days, was unclear. The Crown spoke of rebuilding, but had no plan. The Corporation spoke of recovery, but lacked resources. The charitable network provided immediate relief, but could not sustain indefinite support. The economic damage (lost stock, destroyed tools, interrupted credit) accumulated daily. The bread in Moorfields kept people alive. It did not restore their livelihoods.

By 14 September, the first week of organized relief was ending. The ovens still burned. The crowds still gathered. The rain still fell on improvised shelters. The quantities of bread distributed, whatever their precise measurement, represented a continuing commitment (royal, municipal, charitable) to the survival of a displaced population. But they also represented a dependency that had not existed three weeks before. Eighty thousand people, more or less, who had fed themselves through market exchange now required distribution. The mechanism that met this need, however improvised, however unequal, established the terms on which London would recover. The city would be rebuilt, in part, on the foundation of these field kitchens, these emergency markets, these contested distributions of charity and command.

The cost was not merely economic. The relief effort revealed, to all who cared to see, how thin the social fabric had been. The fire had destroyed not only buildings but assumptions: that the Corporation could protect its people, that the market would provide, that charity would suffice in emergency. What replaced these assumptions was a more explicit dependence on central authority, a more visible role for the Crown in daily survival. The Monarchy’s strength in September 1666 was not its resources, which were strained, but its capacity to act where other institutions had failed. This capacity, demonstrated in Moorfields, would shape the politics of rebuilding.

The bread continued. The ovens ordered by the King, the flour procured by the Corporation, the fuel contributed by neighboring counties (all converged on the fields where London’s displaced population waited). The quantities were substantial: thousands of loaves daily, tens of thousands weekly, the precise numbers disputed but the scale undeniable. This was the arithmetic of catastrophe, the measurement of a city’s reduced condition. The people who ate this bread, who queued for it in rain and mud, who competed for it with strangers and neighbors, were the human substance of the disaster. Their survival was the first reconstruction. Their dependency was its price.
