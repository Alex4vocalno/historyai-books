Chapter 27

The Phoenix City’s New Shape

Seen from above in the spring of 1669, London revealed a new shape, a cityscape transformed not by design but by the accumulated pressure of three years. Where the flames had passed, the medieval tangle had loosened; streets ran straighter now, or straighter in patches, with King Street and Queen Street cutting broad new corridors through the ruins. To the west, the great skeleton of St. Paul’s still dominated the skyline, its fate undecided, while north and east, the old city of timber houses and narrow lanes survived intact beyond the fire’s reach. From the tower of St. Mary-le-Bow, this was the view: a contradictory map of compromise, where uniform brick fronts suggested planning even as old boundaries clung stubbornly to the ground.

This was the Phoenix City. Not the rational metropolis that Christopher Wren and his fellow architects had proposed in the autumn of 1666, but something more contradictory and more revealing. The fire had engineered a modernization that was profound precisely because it was piecemeal, filtered through institutions that responded to emergency rather than vision. What emerged by 1669 was safer, more durable, commercially resilient, and still recognizably London: a city that had absorbed catastrophe and converted it into structural advantage without ever quite surrendering to the planners’ dreams.

John Evelyn, surveying the scene from his garden at Sayes Court, recorded his ambivalence in the diary he kept with characteristic diligence. The new streets pleased him, the broader and fairer ways that replaced the narrow, dark, and troublesome passages of his youth. Yet he mourned what had been lost, the antique character of the medieval city, its accidental beauties and accumulated human texture. He wrote that London was, but is no more, and the tense caught his meaning exactly: a continuous present interrupted, a place still becoming something else.

The rebuilding had proceeded faster than anyone had predicted in the dark days of September 1666.

But speed had its costs. The grand schemes for a city of piazzas and radiating avenues, for a London that might have rivaled the Baroque magnificence of Paris or Rome, had been abandoned within months of their proposal. Wren’s own plan, with its geometric clarity and its great cathedral at the center, had been rejected by a Common Council more concerned with preserving property lines than achieving architectural glory. The city chose to rebuild itself in its own image, modified but not transformed.

The mechanism of this choice lay in the interaction of regulation, economics, and topography. The Rebuilding Act of 1666 had established the framework: wider streets, brick construction, no wooden fronts. But the act’s enforcement depended on a machinery of surveyors, judges, and building inspectors that had to be created from nothing in the aftermath of disaster. The Fire Court, established to settle the impossible tangle of property disputes, had become by 1669 the effective government of the rebuilding zone. Its judges, led by Sir Matthew Hale and his successors, applied legal pressure that was simultaneously coercive and creative. They forced compromise where owners would not yield, ordered demolitions where safety demanded, and in thousands of individual cases established precedents that shaped the emerging city more decisively than any master plan.

Samuel Pepys, moving through this landscape in his official capacity as Clerk of the Acts to the Navy Board, noted the transformation in the practical terms that came naturally to him. The new buildings were very fine, he admitted, but he worried about the cost, the charge of brick and tile that fell heavily on owners and tenants alike. His own house in Seething Lane had survived the fire, a fact that shaped his perspective: he saw the rebuilding from outside, as a spectator with vested interests rather than as a participant in loss.

In March 1669, he walked through the new streets near the Exchange and counted the changes. The Royal Exchange itself was rising again, its replacement already funded by subscription among the merchants who needed its floor for their business. The surrounding lanes had been widened, their old names preserved but their dimensions altered. It was strange, he wrote, to see how the city is rebuilt, and yet not rebuilt; the same and yet not the same.

The strangeness Pepys perceived was the signature of a particular kind of modernization. The fire had created patterns of loss that revealed the hidden anatomy of the city and then reshaped it according to new pressures. What burned and what survived had not been random. The densest commercial districts, packed with warehouses and workshops, had suffered most. The great houses of the wealthy, with their stone walls and spacious courts, had often survived or been rebuilt quickly. The pattern of destruction and renewal thus reinforced existing hierarchies while modifying them: the wealthy retained their advantages, but the commercial classes gained new infrastructure, wider streets, and more durable buildings for their enterprises. The fire had been democratic in its violence but selective in its consequences.

The physical evidence of this process accumulated in the surveys and maps that multiplied in the years after 1666. William Morgan’s great survey of 1667, updated through 1669, recorded the transformation in the language of property: brick frontages measured in feet, street widths established by proclamation and enforced by threat of demolition.

The numbers told a story of partial success. King Street, cut through the heart of the burned district, achieved a width of forty feet, substantial by medieval standards, though far short of the grandeur Wren had envisioned. Queen Street matched it. Threadneedle Street, Lombard Street, Cornhill: all emerged broader than before, their curves straightened, their building lines regularized.

Yet the regularity was always local, always compromised. A street would run straight for two hundred yards, then jog suddenly to accommodate a property owner who had held out against compulsory purchase.

The new building code required brick, but it permitted wooden construction in the upper stories if the fronts were faced with tile or plaster. The result was a city of hybrid forms, safer than its predecessor but still vulnerable, modernized in patches while the old fabric survived nearby.

The contrast with Wren’s abandoned schemes was instructive. His plan of 1666, developed in collaboration with Robert Hooke and presented to the King in the weeks after the fire, had proposed nothing less than the rationalization of London’s entire urban form. Wide boulevards would radiate from key nodes. The riverfront would be opened to public access, its wharves regularized and its warehouses removed from direct contact with the water. St.

Paul’s would stand at the center of a great piazza, visible from multiple approaches, its dome commanding the city as St. Peter’s commanded Rome. The plan was beautiful, legible, and impossible. The legal and political machinery required to implement it did not exist. The cost of compensating property owners for their lost boundaries would have exceeded the resources of the Crown and the City combined. And the social resistance, thousands of Londoners defending their ancient rights of ownership against abstract planning, proved insurmountable.

Wren himself adapted to this reality with the flexibility that characterized his long career. By 1669, he had shifted his attention from the macroscopic to the particular: from the plan of the whole city to the design of individual churches, from the grand avenue to the parish boundary. The churches rose rapidly in these years: St. Mary-le-Bow, St. Bride’s, St. Stephen Walbrook, each a demonstration of what could be achieved within the constraints of existing property lines. Wren’s genius found expression not in the imposition of geometric order but in the negotiation of irregular sites, the transformation of awkward plots into luminous spaces. The dome of St. Paul’s, still unbuilt in 1669, remained his great ambition. But its realization would require another decade of political maneuvering, fund-raising, and compromise with the committees and commissioners who controlled the project’s purse strings.

The economic pressures shaping the rebuilt city were equally complex. The shortage of labor that had crippled early reconstruction efforts had eased by 1669, but the cost of materials remained high. Brickworks had multiplied in the Home Counties, their kilns smoking day and night to supply the insatiable demand. The price of timber, ironically, had fallen: the fire had released thousands of tons of salvageable wood into the market, and the new building codes restricted its use. The result was a building industry transformed by regulation, its practices standardized in ways that would have been impossible without the external pressure of the Rebuilding Acts. The medieval system of craft control, with its local variations and customary privileges, gave way to something more like a modern construction industry: specialized, mobile, responsive to price signals and legal requirements.

This transformation extended to the social geography of the city. The fire had caused, in the assessment of modern historians, the largest dislocation of London’s residential structure in its history until the Blitz. Thousands of households had been displaced, and their relocation shaped the emerging patterns of settlement.

The wealthy moved west, to the new developments in Covent Garden and St. James’s, or to the villages beyond the city wall that would soon become the West End. The poor crowded into the unburned districts to the north and east, or into the cheap new construction that filled the margins of the rebuilding zone.

The fire thus accelerated a process of social segregation that had begun before 1666 and would continue for generations. The rebuilt city center became increasingly commercial, its upper stories occupied by workshops and warehouses rather than families. The separation of living and working, of domestic and economic space, proceeded space by space, street by street, in the cumulative decisions of thousands of individual builders and tenants.

The riverfront illustrated these pressures with particular clarity. The Thames had been crucial to the fire’s spread and to its eventual containment.

The Rebuilding Act of 1666 had mandated open wharves along the entire riverside, with no houses obstructing access to the water. This provision, more than any other, reflected the lessons of September 1666, when the flames had leaped from warehouse to warehouse along the crowded waterfront.

Yet its implementation had been partial and contested. The great wharfingers, men like Sir William Warren, who controlled stretches of the riverside, resisted any reduction of their holding capacity. The Navy Board, Pepys’s own institution, needed warehouse space for naval stores and pressed for exemptions. By 1669, a compromise had emerged: wider streets running perpendicular to the river, breaking the continuous wall of buildings, but private wharves surviving in the interstices. The result was safer than the medieval arrangement but far from the open public waterfront that Wren and his colleagues had proposed.

The persistence of old property lines in unburned areas completed this picture of selective transformation. North of London Wall, east of the Tower, the fire had not reached, and the medieval city survived intact. The same narrow lanes, the same timber houses, the same risks that had made the Great Fire possible. The Rebuilding Acts applied only to the burned district, and the Corporation lacked the will or the legal authority to extend their provisions to the untouched areas. The result was a city of two textures: the regularized, brick-built center and the surviving medieval periphery. The boundary between them was visible in the street map, in the building materials, in the very air. Smoke patterns differed, fire risks differed, the experience of walking changed from one zone to the next.

This dual city posed problems that would persist for centuries. The regulations that made the rebuilt center safer did not apply to the areas that had escaped the flames. The social and economic pressures that had packed the medieval city with combustible materials continued to operate beyond the fire’s reach. The Great Fire had been a lesson, but it had been a lesson applied selectively, its moral force dissipated by the resistance of property and the limits of legal jurisdiction. The patterns of 1666 thus marked not only what had been lost but what had been spared, and the contrast between the two zones measured the incomplete nature of the city’s transformation.

The demographic impact of the fire compounded these physical changes. Contemporary estimates suggested that seventy thousand people had been made homeless in September 1666. By 1669, most had returned or been replaced, but the composition of the population had shifted. The plague of 1665 had already thinned the city’s ranks; the fire completed a process of demographic disruption that reshaped London’s social fabric. Newcomers arrived from the provinces and from abroad, drawn by the building boom and the economic opportunities of the rebuilt capital.

The old parish structures, with their intimate knowledge of local populations, struggled to keep track of the mobile, temporary workforce that flooded the construction sites. The city’s government, never strong in social welfare provision, found itself overwhelmed by the scale of need. Private charity and informal networks filled some of the gaps, but the fire’s human cost extended far beyond the immediate casualties into the disrupted lives of survivors and migrants alike.

The financial mechanisms of reconstruction revealed similar patterns of innovation and constraint. The Fire Court had developed procedures for valuing destroyed property and apportioning costs between owners and ground landlords that became, in effect, a new branch of English law. The valuations were cautious, the processes slow, but they established precedents for handling large-scale property disaster that would influence later development. The city’s credit, severely shaken in 1666, had recovered remarkably by 1669. The rebuilding taxes, resented but paid, had funded public works on an unprecedented scale. The private investment that flowed into brick and tile, into wharves and warehouses, testified to confidence in London’s commercial future. The fire had destroyed capital but also released it, redirecting investment from the old patterns of the medieval city into new forms that promised greater returns.

Christopher Wren, walking through this landscape in 1669, could measure the distance between his ambitions and the emerging reality. The great dome of St. Paul’s remained a paper project, its funding uncertain, its design still evolving. The churches he had built or was building demonstrated his skill but also his adaptability: each was a response to specific conditions, a negotiation between ideal form and practical constraint. The Monument, rising slowly at Fish Street Hill, would be his most visible contribution to the city’s memory of the fire, its column of Portland stone marking the site where Thomas Farriner’s bakehouse had stood.

But even this project had been compromised, its height reduced, its inscriptions modified by political pressure. The city was being rebuilt, but not according to any single vision. It was being rebuilt by thousands of individual choices, each responding to immediate pressures, each contributing to an outcome that no one had fully planned.

A cold tally awaited anyone who cared to make it. The percentage of new construction in brick, the feet of street widening, the number of churches completed or under way: these figures, stripped of sentiment, encapsulated a transformation that was both material and social. London had become safer, more durable, more commercially efficient. It had not become the rational metropolis of Wren’s dreams. The Phoenix City that emerged from the ashes was recognizably the descendant of the medieval city that had burned, modified by catastrophe but not created anew. The fire’s legacy was thus double: a demonstration of what planning could achieve and a record of its limits, inscribed in the very fabric of the streets and now awaiting the final accounting that only the official inquiry could provide.
