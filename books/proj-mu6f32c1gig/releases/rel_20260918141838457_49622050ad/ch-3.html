Chapter 3

A Baker on Pudding Lane

The coals still held their heat when Thomas Farriner raked them level across the oven floor. The final act of his Saturday, and the habit of twenty-seven years in the baking trade. Outside, Pudding Lane ran dark between Eastcheap and the Thames. The tide was low. The night smelled of river mud and the faint sweetness of burned flour that never quite washed from a baker’s clothes.

He covered the embers with ashes. The safeguard, the routine that made it possible to sleep above a furnace. The Baker’s Company taught its apprentices the method: bank the fire deep, let it smother slowly, and the oven would hold warmth for tomorrow’s first batch without flame. Farriner had performed this rite since 1637, when he entered the Company as a young man from somewhere north of London, precisely where the parish records do not say. By 1649 he kept his own shop. By the 1660s he supplied bread to the Royal Navy, a contract that required volume, regularity, and the confidence of naval pursers who could choose among a dozen bakers in the riverside ward. He was a well-known baker in the City of London, and a churchwarden in his parish.

The banking done, he climbed the narrow stair to the family rooms above. His daughter Hannah slept in the chamber they shared. A maidservant lay somewhere in the house, her name has not survived in the documents, though her fate would be remembered. The building was typical of the district: timber-framed, jettied forward over the street, the upper floors pressing toward their neighbors across a gap barely wide enough for a man to stretch his arms. The ground floor held the bakehouse with its two ovens. Flour stores filled corners. Fuel lay heaped in the yard behind, in any space that could be found in a building where every square foot earned its keep. Above, the living quarters. Everything stacked, everything touching, everything built for a city that had grown by accretion rather than plan.

Farriner was not a poor man. The hearth tax assessments placed him among the better-off tradesmen of the parish of St. Margaret, New Fish Street. He served as churchwarden, a position that required property, reputation, and the time to collect tithes and supervise the poor. His Navy contract brought him into contact with naval administrators, with victualling clerks, with the machinery of state that reached down from Whitehall into the warren of City lanes. He was, in the Restoration’s emerging vocabulary, respectable.

Respectability had its architecture. The Pudding Lane house was his place of business and his family dwelling combined. The ovens dominated the ground floor: arched brick chambers, mouth wide enough to slide the long-handled peels bearing dough, interior hot enough to turn water to steam the instant it touched the walls. Farriner burned faggots and sea coal in the firebox beneath. The fuel lay heaped in corners, in the yard behind, in any space that could be found. Flour filled bins, sacks, chests. The dust coated every surface, explosive in the right concentration, merely filthy in the ordinary run of things.

The inventory of risk went unremarked, though no one called it so. London had burned before. In 1633 a fire at the Bankside had consumed fifty houses. In 1661 a blaze at the Savoy had killed several. The memory of these events shaped behavior without altering structure. Bakers banked their fires. Households kept leather buckets. The watch called the hours and looked for flame. The system presumed that fire would be small, local, controllable. The gap between a spark and a catastrophe could be bridged by vigilance and routine.

Farriner’s routine failed. A short time after midnight on Sunday, 2 September 1666, a fire broke out at Thomas Farriner’s bakery in Pudding Lane. The occupants, save the family’s maidservant, who was too frightened to try, fled through an upstairs window to the house next door. When the house burned down, she became the fire’s first victim. The question of how the fire began became a matter of official inquiry, unofficial rumor, and enduring historical dispute.

The sequence is recoverable from later testimony, though the testimony contradicts. Farriner himself, examined after the disaster, maintained that he had extinguished all fire before retiring. His daughter Hannah, who survived, would carry the family account forward. The maidservant, who did not survive, left no statement. Neighbors would claim to have seen smoke, to have heard cries, to have noticed earlier signs of trouble that Saturday evening. The precise ignition point, whether a fallen ember, a stray spark, an oven door improperly sealed, disappeared in the general destruction.

What can be established is the physical circumstances that made any spark lethal. The Pudding Lane house stood where the street narrowed toward Thames Street, the ground falling away to the river. The buildings crowded together, their upper stories nearly touching, their roofs a continuous surface of thatch and tile. The materials were old timber, dried by a long spell of hot and dry weather through mid-1666, oiled by years of domestic cooking, varnished by the smoke of thousands of hearth fires. The Thames ran low. The wooden pipes that carried water to street cisterns were themselves vulnerable, and the pressure was never strong.

Farriner’s bakery concentrated these hazards. The ovens, even when banked, maintained temperatures sufficient to ignite dry fuel. The flour dust, suspended in air, could burn with explosive force. The building’s construction, timber frame, lath and plaster infill, wooden floors, provided fuel on every surface. Above, the sleeping chambers offered no fireproof separation from the bakehouse below. The single stairwell acted as a chimney, drawing flame upward the moment it found purchase.

The family woke to smoke. Farriner found his bedroom door sealed by it, the draft from the stairwell feeding the fire that had begun below. The window offered the only escape. He and Hannah climbed through, perhaps using a neighboring roof, perhaps helped by someone in the next house. The maidservant remained inside. Whether she was overcome by smoke, paralyzed by fear, or simply unable to find the window in darkness and confusion, the result was the same. She was the first recorded death of the Great Fire, though her name, like those of most who perished, was never entered in any official tally.

The escape took minutes. The alarm spread more slowly. London had no organized fire brigade. The parish constables, the watchmen, the neighbors themselves formed the first response. Someone raised the cry of “Fire!”, who first did so is another disputed point. The bells of St. Margaret’s began to ring, the conventional signal of danger. But the hour was late, the streets dark, the initial smoke visible only to those immediately adjacent. The fire had perhaps half an hour’s uncontested growth before any systematic countermeasure could begin.

This interval was critical. In half an hour, a fire in a timber building with ample fuel can reach temperatures that make close approach impossible. The radiated heat ignites adjacent structures before flame ever touches them. The smoke, thick and toxic, drives back would-be rescuers. The initial failure of response, whether attributed to confusion, inadequate alarm, or the physical difficulty of the site, allowed the fire to establish itself across multiple buildings before any serious attempt at containment.

Farriner, standing in the street in his nightclothes, watching his livelihood and home consume itself, was in no position to direct operations. His status as churchwarden, as Navy contractor, as respectable householder, meant nothing in the face of flame. The authority to tear down buildings, to create firebreaks, to command labor and materials, lay with the Lord Mayor. Sir Thomas Bloodworth, awakened at his house in Lombard Street, would ride to the scene, view the fire, pronounce it negligible, and return to bed. This sequence, so often cited as the defining failure of official response, had its roots in the same institutional caution that Chapter 2 examined: the fear of liability, the cost of destruction, the preference for established procedure over emergency improvisation.

But Bloodworth’s failure, decisive as it was, came after Farriner’s. The baker’s micro-decision, whether to bank the fire adequately, whether to check it a second time, whether to sleep above it at all, created the specific conditions without which the mayor’s error would have been irrelevant. The systemic vulnerabilities of London’s building stock, its water supply, its governance structures, required a particular local ignition to become catastrophic. Farriner provided that ignition.

The biographical sketch that the records permit shows a man positioned at the intersection of several networks that would conduct and amplify the disaster. His Navy contract linked him to the state apparatus preparing for war with the Dutch. His churchwardenship connected him to parish structures of relief and record-keeping that would be overwhelmed. His location on Pudding Lane placed him at the center of the most densely built, least accessible district of the City, where the street pattern dated to medieval times and the buildings had accreted without regard to modern fire risk.

His trade was essential and dangerous. Bread was the staple food of London, consumed at roughly a pound per person per day. The baking industry concentrated in riverside wards where water transport brought grain and fuel, where the poor could find employment as laborers, where the smell and smoke of industrial production were tolerated. Bakers worked through the night to have fresh bread for morning sale. Their ovens, maintained at operating temperature for hours, were permanent ignition sources in a combustible environment.

The specific layout of Farriner’s premises can be reconstructed from later surveys and the general patterns of the baking trade. The ground floor, as noted, held the ovens and stores. The first floor above contained the family living space: chambers for sleeping, perhaps a kitchen for the household’s own meals, storage for clothing and valuables. Higher floors, if any, would have been garrets for servants or additional storage. The building was probably three or four stories, the upper levels projecting over the street in the jettied fashion that maximized floor area within the medieval street line.

This projection created the conditions for rapid fire spread. When flame broke through an upper window, it touched the jetty of the opposite house. The gap between buildings, narrowed by centuries of encroachment, became a channel for radiated heat and falling embers. The thatched roofs, where they survived, offered no resistance. Even tiled roofs, when lapped by flame from below, would crack and allow fire into the roof space where timber rafters and lath supported the covering.

Farriner’s stock contributed to the intensity. Flour, when scattered and suspended in air, burns with explosive rapidity. The sacks and bins in his bakehouse, once ignited, became fuel loads sufficient to maintain the fire through any ordinary supply of water. The wooden implements of his trade, peels, troughs, bins, added to the combustible mass. Within minutes of the initial ignition, the bakehouse itself would have been unsurvivable.

The family’s escape through the upstairs window indicates the speed of this development. They did not attempt the stair, though it offered the direct route to street level. Smoke, or flame itself, had already made that passage impossible. The window, presumably opening onto a roof or a narrow gap between buildings, provided the alternative. The maidservant’s failure to follow suggests either that she was in a different part of the house, that she woke later, or that she lacked the physical capacity or presence of mind to climb through.

Her death, unrecorded in name, exemplifies the invisibility of domestic servants in the documentary record. She was present in the household, essential to its functioning, yet appears only in the account of the escape, as the one who “was too frightened to try.” Whether this judgment was fair, whether she had any realistic chance of escape, whether she was already overcome by smoke, these questions cannot be answered. Her fate entered the narrative as a minor detail, a casualty of the family’s disaster, rather than as an individual tragedy.

The house next door, which received Farriner and his daughter, was itself doomed. The fire spread laterally through the contiguous structures, following the line of the street, jumping gaps where they existed, consuming everything in its path. By the time any organized response began, multiple buildings were involved, the radiant heat was intense enough to prevent close approach, and the only effective countermeasure, the creation of firebreaks by demolition, would require decisions that the available authorities were unprepared to make.

Farriner’s subsequent role in the inquiry into the fire’s origin was necessarily defensive. He had every incentive to maintain that he had followed proper procedures, that the fire was an unforeseeable accident, that no negligence could be attributed to him. The official investigation, conducted by parliamentary committee and reported in the London Gazette, accepted this account in its essentials. No charges were brought against him. He resumed his trade, eventually rebuilding on the same site, continuing his Navy contract, living until 1670.

Official exoneration, however, did not settle the question. Alternative theories circulated immediately. Some blamed Catholic arsonists, a foreign plot, the Dutch enemy. Robert Hubert, the French watchmaker who falsely confessed to starting the fire at Farriner’s bakery, would be hanged at Tyburn on 27 October 1666, his body torn apart by a crowd seeking certainty where the official record offered only accident. It was hoped that with Hubert’s death, ‘the talk of plots and conspiracies might die with him.’ The persistence of these conspiracy theories, long after Hubert’s confession was discredited, suggests how unsatisfying the mundane explanation remained.

The mundane explanation was nevertheless correct. The fire began in a bakery, in the early hours of Sunday morning, in conditions of drought and crowding that made any spark potentially catastrophic. Farriner’s specific responsibility, whether he banked his fire properly, whether he checked it before sleeping, cannot be determined with certainty. The records permit contradictory readings. What they establish beyond dispute is that the fire originated in his premises, that it spread with devastating speed, and that the structures of response that should have contained it failed at multiple points.

The failure was not simply personal. Farriner operated within a system that normalized risk. The Baker’s Company’s safety rules, the parish watch’s fire precautions, the building regulations that theoretically governed construction, all existed, all were inadequately enforced, all presumed a level of vigilance and resource that the actual conditions of London life could not sustain. The baker who banked his fire inadequately was indistinguishable, until disaster struck, from the baker who banked it well.

The Pudding Lane house, in its physical particulars, embodied these systemic pressures. The need to maximize productive space in expensive central London real estate produced the jettied upper floors, the crowded interior, the mixing of industrial and domestic functions. The need to maintain oven temperatures through the night produced the banking practice, the sleeping above the fire, the acceptance of permanent ignition source as cost of doing business. The need to move naval supplies efficiently produced the concentration of bakeries in riverside wards, the wooden buildings, the inadequate water supply.

Farriner’s choices, examined in this context, appear less as individual moral failure than as adaptive responses to structural constraints. He slept above his bakery because that was where bakers slept. He banked his fire according to the accepted method. He built or occupied a timber-framed house because that was what the building stock offered and what the building regulations, such as they were, permitted. The catastrophe that resulted was overdetermined: any number of changes, in the weather, in the construction, in the response, might have prevented it, yet none were made because none appeared necessary until too late.

The hour before the fire found London in the peculiar stillness of a late summer night. The theatres were closed for the season. The court was at Whitehall, preparing for the autumn session. The Navy was mobilizing for war, its victualling contracts active, its bread supply dependent on men like Farriner working through the night. The city had survived plague the previous year, a trauma that had emptied streets and disrupted trade without altering the physical environment. Drought had continued through the summer, the wooden city drying toward its ignition point.

In this stillness, Farriner raked his coals and climbed his stair. The routine was complete. The risk was normalized. The spark that would find the tinder was already present, held in the embers beneath the ash, waiting for the draft that would revive it. The scene was set. The next movement was the spark itself and the immediate failure of response.
