Chapter 20

The Smoldering City

Seen from above, the hot ruins of the eastern wharves lay silent, a massive, unaccounted-for blow to London’s maritime economy that would shape the recovery in ways no parliamentary inquiry could yet measure. Across that whole map of ash, stretching from the Tower to the Temple, Thomas Farriner’s bakehouse on Pudding Lane — where a maidservant too frightened to flee had perished four days before — was now a single point.

On Thursday morning, 6 September 1666, the fire that had begun in the early hours of Sunday was finally exhausted.

What remained was not victory but vacancy: thirteen thousand houses destroyed, eighty-seven parish churches gutted, St Paul’s Cathedral collapsed into its own crypt, and the commercial heart of England reduced to a smoldering plain where the medieval street pattern had been erased entirely. The wind that had driven the flames so mercilessly had dropped. The Duke of York’s soldiers had stopped the westward advance at Pie Corner. The eastern demolitions, ordered in desperation, had finally contained the Thames-side burning.

But containment was not governance, and the absence of flame revealed a deeper crisis than the fire itself had exposed.

At Whitehall, King Charles II faced the administrative wreckage with the same pragmatism he had brought to the streets during the disaster’s peak. His first proclamation, issued on Thursday 6 September and printed in the London Gazette, attempted to impose order upon chaos through the only instruments available: paper and precedent. The proclamation commanded that all persons who had salvaged goods from the fire should carry them to the nearest parish churches, there to register their property under the supervision of constables and churchwardens. It established curfews for the ruins, forbade unauthorized entry into any house, and threatened imprisonment for looters. Most significantly, it ordered that no person should spread or publish any false news or reports concerning the fire’s cause, specifically prohibiting accusations against any particular person or persons until official inquiry could establish the truth.

The language of the proclamation revealed both the Crown’s anxiety and its limitations. Charles commanded obedience as monarch, but he addressed a population that had just watched monarchical authority prove insufficient to save their city. The prohibition against false news acknowledged what the proclamation could not control: that in the absence of official explanation, explanation would be invented. The requirement to register salvaged goods at parish churches assumed a parish structure that the fire had largely destroyed—eighty-seven of the City’s churches lay in ruins, their registers, their churchwardens, their very boundaries consumed. The proclamation was necessary. It was also, in immediate practical terms, nearly impossible to execute.

Three miles east, in the smoking wilderness of Thames Street, the gap between royal command and ground-level reality yawned wide enough to swallow any hope of quick restoration. A militia patrol, one of the Duke of York’s detachments retained for post-fire security, picked its way through rubble that retained enough heat to scorch leather soles. They were looking for looters. What they found was a coal merchant whose warehouse had stood near the Steelyard, now digging through the debris of what had been his own property. The merchant’s hands were burned.

His coat was torn. He had been working since dawn to recover the iron strongbox in which he kept his account books, knowing that without those records he could prove neither his debts nor his credits, could establish no claim to compensation, could not even demonstrate that he had existed as a merchant before the fire erased his material existence.

The soldiers stopped him. The proclamation required that all salvaged goods be taken to church registration. The merchant had no salvage yet, only the hope of it, and he refused to abandon his search. The confrontation that followed—recorded in the examinations taken by the Fire Court in subsequent months, when the merchant sought compensation for goods lost—illustrated the impossible position of both parties. The patrol was enforcing a proclamation designed to prevent anarchy. The merchant was attempting to preserve the documentary basis of his economic life.

The strongbox, when finally recovered, contained papers so scorched that their ink had run and their edges crumbled to ash. Some records were legible. Others were not. The patrol moved on, leaving the merchant to continue his excavation, and their commander to report to his superiors that the ruins contained not criminals so much as ghosts: citizens attempting to reconstruct their identities from cinders.

This collision—distant order, proximate desperation—repeated itself across the burned zone throughout Thursday and into Friday. The proclamations multiplied. On Friday 7 September, Charles issued a second order, this one establishing temporary markets at Bishopsgate and Smithfield for the sale of corn, meal, and other provisions, since the customary markets at Leadenhall and Gracechurch Street lay inside the destroyed area. The King authorized the importation of Dublin corn duty-free. He commanded that no victualler or other person should sell provisions at prices higher than those current before the fire. Each command addressed a real need. Each assumed an administrative capacity that the fire had degraded. The price controls required enforcement mechanisms that no longer existed. The temporary markets assumed merchants who still possessed goods to sell and customers who possessed money to buy. The duty-free corn assumed shipping infrastructure that the eastern wharf fires had partially destroyed.

The London Gazette, printed at Oxford since the fire had consumed the printing houses around St Paul’s, carried these proclamations to a readership scattered across refuge camps in the fields north of the City, in Southwark, in Westminster, in the villages of Middlesex and Surrey where Londoners had fled. The Gazette’s account of Thursday and Friday emphasized royal action, repeating the formula that His Majesty had been pleased to command, that His Majesty had given order, that His Majesty had appointed. The grammar of monarchy remained intact. The material basis of its effective exercise did not. The Gazette could print the King’s commands. It could not print bread for the refugees, nor locate the missing, nor establish what proportion of the City’s population had actually died.

The death toll remained, and remains, uncertain. The fire’s official duration—Sunday 2 September through Wednesday 5 September—suggested a contained catastrophe with limited human loss. Contemporary estimates ranged from a handful of verified deaths to several hundred presumed ones, the uncertainty itself testimony to the destruction of record-keeping structures and the social dislocation that followed. John Evelyn, writing on Thursday from his refuge in Deptford, noted the poor inhabitants who were all to be seen sitting like dejected creatures among the rubbish of their houses, but he recorded no count of dead. Samuel Pepys, whose diary provides the most detailed personal chronicle of the disaster, mentioned seeing poor wretches carrying their goods and poor people staying in their houses as long as till the very fire touched them, but he named no casualties.

The absence of bodies in the official record did not mean absence of death. It meant that death, like property, like employment, like the street pattern itself, had become difficult to document.

This documentary vacuum—the inability to establish basic facts about destruction, loss, and mortality—created conditions in which rumor flourished with the energy of suppressed fear. By Thursday evening, as Charles’s proclamations against false news circulated, the false news had already achieved a velocity that paper could not match. Witnesses in the ruins reported conversations heard, strangers observed, suspicious behavior noted in the final hours before particular structures caught fire. The pattern was consistent across disparate accounts: the fire had not been accident but design, the designers were foreign, and their foreignness was specifically Catholic.

The accusation drew upon deep reserves of English anxiety. The memory of the Gunpowder Plot, sixty-three years past, remained vivid in Protestant London. The recent Second Anglo-Dutch War, concluded only in 1665, had supplied fresh images of Dutch enemies capable of maritime destruction. The presence of French and Dutch residents in the City—traders, craftsmen, servants—provided visible targets for suspicion that required no evidence to activate. By Friday, 7 September, the London Gazette found it necessary to print a specific denial: His Majesty had also given command to the Lord Mayor and the justices of the peace, to examine strictly into the truth of any such reports, and to apprehend all such persons as should be found to spread or publish any false and scandalous reports.

The denial acknowledged what it could not prevent. That same Friday, a French watchmaker named Robert Hubert, recently arrived in London and wandering the ruins, found himself detained by citizens who took his foreign speech and his presence in the burned zone as evidence of guilt. Hubert’s subsequent confession—false, coerced, and ultimately fatal—lay weeks in the future. But the mechanism of his detention was already active: a population desperate for explanation, a vacuum of official information, and the ancient habit of finding in the alien the cause of catastrophe.

The rumors had specific content that revealed the social fracture lines beneath the universal loss. Foreigners, it was said, had been seen firing houses. Catholics had been observed with balls of fire and fireballs—the terminology itself unstable, suggesting both incendiary devices and supernatural agency. The French ambassador’s house in Northamptonshire was searched for evidence of conspiracy. Dutch residents in London reported threats and assaults. The accusations moved faster than the proclamations against them because they answered psychological needs that official information could not satisfy. They provided causation where the true causes—drought, wind, wooden construction, inadequate water supply, confused authority—were diffuse, systemic, and politically uncomfortable to name.

The militia patrols found themselves enforcing order against citizens who believed they were enforcing justice. On Thursday afternoon, near the remains of the Royal Exchange, a patrol intervened to stop a crowd that had surrounded a Dutch sailor attempting to recover goods from a burned warehouse. The sailor had papers proving his employment. The crowd had testimony—hearsay, third-hand, contradictory—that he had been seen with a fireball on Monday night. The patrol escorted him to temporary detention. The crowd dispersed, but not satisfied. The incident was recorded, briefly, in the examinations taken for the subsequent parliamentary inquiry, one of dozens of similar confrontations that established the post-fire security problem: not simply preventing theft, but preventing the transformation of grief and dislocation into collective violence.

The economic geography of loss became visible in these first two days as returning citizens traced the boundaries of their former lives. Thomas Farriner, the baker whose bakehouse had started the fire, appeared in the records of Thursday and Friday not as culprit but as claimant, seeking compensation like thousands of others. The irony was noted by contemporaries—Pepys recorded it with characteristic directness—and it would be exploited by subsequent polemicists. But in the immediate aftermath, Farriner’s position was structurally identical to that of the coal merchant and thousands of others: a man attempting to reconstruct his economic existence from the fragments that heat and water had spared.

The pattern of survival and destruction revealed the social anatomy of the medieval city with cruel clarity. Stone buildings, generally those of the wealthy and the institutional, had often survived with damage that was reparable. Wooden tenements, housing the poor, had vanished entirely. The guild halls, symbols of corporate identity, stood as burned shells or collapsed ruins. The warehouses of the great trading companies, their stone vaults and brick walls, had sometimes protected goods that wooden structures would have consumed. The fire had been democratic in its initial spread, opportunistic in its consumption of anything combustible. But its aftermath displayed inequality with the nakedness that destruction brings: those with stone walls had something to return to; those with wooden partitions had nothing.

This inequality shaped the refugee population that camped in the fields north of the City on Thursday and Friday. The London Gazette reported, with the optimistic vagueness of official communication, that great numbers of people had been relieved by the charity of well-disposed persons. The reality, recorded in private correspondence and in the petitions that would soon flood Whitehall, was more desperate.

Those with resources had removed themselves to lodgings in Westminster, in Southwark, in the villages beyond the walls. Those without resources—estimates ranged from tens of thousands to more than a hundred thousand—occupied makeshift shelters in Moorfields, in Finsbury Fields, along the roads leading north. The weather held fair, which was mercy. The absence of plague, which had emptied the City only the year before, was providence.

But the provisioning of this displaced population, the prevention of epidemic disease in crowded encampments, the maintenance of public order among people who had lost everything and received nothing certain in return—these problems exceeded the capacity of existing institutions.

Charles’s proclamations addressed some of these needs obliquely, through the mechanisms of market regulation and import authorization. But the scale of displacement demanded intervention that no seventeenth-century government had attempted. The poor rates, the parish-based system of relief, assumed a parish structure that the fire had destroyed. The charitable giving that the Gazette celebrated was voluntary, uneven, and insufficient. The first organized relief efforts—soup kitchens, temporary shelters, the distribution of bread and small coin—would emerge in the following days from churches, from guilds, from the initiative of individual magnates. But on Thursday and Friday, the relief was not yet organized. It was improvised, partial, and visibly inadequate to the need.

The Duke of York, who had commanded the firefighting effort with military efficiency, turned his attention to security. His soldiers, who had demolished houses with gunpowder to create firebreaks, now patrolled the ruins to prevent looting and violence. The transition was not seamless. The same men who had been authorized to destroy property for the public good were now expected to protect private property in its most vulnerable state. The same population that had assisted in the demolitions—sometimes willingly, sometimes under compulsion—now confronted armed patrols as they attempted to recover their own goods. The military presence prevented worse disorder. It also enforced a state of emergency that had no legal terminus, no mechanism for returning to normal governance.

The Parliamentary inquiry that would eventually investigate the fire’s causes had not yet been convened. The Fire Court that would adjudicate property disputes had not yet been established. These institutions, whose creation would occupy the coming months, represented attempts to supply after the fact the administrative capacity that had been lacking during the crisis. Their eventual forms—specialized tribunals with extraordinary powers, summary procedures, relaxed rules of evidence—acknowledged that normal legal process was inadequate to the scale of disruption. But on Thursday and Friday, the disruption was still raw, and the institutions that might address it were still merely prospective.

The whispered accusation in the ruins overshadowed the search for property, creating an urgent need for an official cause and for basic sustenance. Robert Hubert, still wandering, still foreign, still unexplained in his presence, would become the vessel for this need. But the larger movement was toward systematic response: the mapping of destruction, the enumeration of loss, the provision of relief, and the reconstruction of authority from the ground that fire had cleared. The city that emerged from these two days would not be the city that had entered them. The medieval street pattern, the wooden tenements, the dense commercial clustering that had made London vulnerable—all of these lay in ashes. What would rise in their place remained, on Friday evening, 7 September 1666, as uncertain as the death toll, as contested as the fire’s cause, and as urgent as the hunger of the refugees in Moorfields.
