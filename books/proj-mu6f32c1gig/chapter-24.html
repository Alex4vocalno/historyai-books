Chapter 24

The Fire Court in Session

Seen from above, London in December 1666 was a city of stark, open wounds. The great scar ran eastward to the river, a swath of blackened shells and skeletal timbers. Closer in, the pattern fractured into intimate ruin: lone chimney stacks, walls ending in nothing, cellars become cisterns of ash. Surveyors had mapped this ground with chain and rod, but every vanished wall marked a lost boundary, and every burned house a dissolved contract. The fire had consumed property and the paper and markers that defined ownership itself.

Parliament had seen this coming. In the weeks after the fire, as the king’s commission gathered testimony and the surveyors began their work, the legal problem had become obvious. The common law courts could not handle the volume. A dispute over a party wall might take three years to reach judgment. A question of inheritance from burned deeds might take seven. By then the city would be a field of permanent ruins, its merchants departed, its trade lost to Amsterdam or Hamburg. So the legislature did what emergencies sometimes permit: it suspended the normal rules and created something new.

The Fire of London Disputes Act, passed in late 1666, established a tribunal with powers that ordinary judges would have found shocking. The Fire Court could compel witnesses, examine parties on oath, and enter premises to view the damage. Most radically, it could issue decrees that bound the immediate litigants and their heirs and assigns forever, with no appeal to any other court. The commissioners were to be lawyers of standing, appointed by the Lord Chancellor, instructed to decide cases according to equity and good conscience rather than strict precedent. Speed was the object. A petition filed in the morning might be heard by afternoon. A decree issued on Tuesday could be executed on Wednesday.

The court opened its sessions in February 1667, though its authority ran back to the previous December. The first hearings were held at Clifford’s Inn, one of the Inns of Chancery that lined Fleet Street, its chamber large enough to accommodate the commissioners’ table, the clerks’ desks, and the growing piles of survey maps that arrived each week from the city. The building was old, its timbers dark with age, its windows small. The commissioners brought their own candles. They would need them. The days were short, and the business was not.

—-

The first case on the morning of 4 February 1667 concerned a house in Bread Street. The petitioner was a mercer who had leased the property from a vintner. The house was gone. The question was who would pay to rebuild it.

The mercer stood before the commissioners’ table with a copy of his lease, or what remained of it. The document had survived the fire because he had carried it with him when he fled. It specified a term of twenty-one years from 1660, with rent of forty pounds per year. The usual covenants applied: the tenant was to keep the premises in repair, the landlord was to maintain the structure. But the house had been timber-framed, with jettied upper stories that projected over the street. The Rebuilding Act of 1666 required new construction to be of brick or stone, with uniform frontages and no projections. The mercer could not simply rebuild what had burned. He would have to build something different, and more expensive.

The lease said nothing about this. It had been drafted in 1660, when timber was legal and brick was merely fashionable. The mercer argued that the obligation to repair did not extend to reconstruction at a higher standard than the original. The vintner argued that the covenant to repair implied rebuilding, and that the tenant must bear the cost of compliance with new regulations. Both men had surveyors’ reports. Both had estimates from builders. The figures differed by more than a hundred pounds.

The commissioners, a Chief Baron of the Exchequer recently appointed and two serjeants-at-law, heard the arguments without interruption. Then the Chief Baron asked a question that cut through the legal thicket: what had the parties understood in 1660? Would the mercer have taken the lease at forty pounds if he had known he might face this expense? Would the vintner have granted it if he had known the house might burn? The lease was silent because both parties had assumed the world would continue as it was. The fire had destroyed that assumption along with the house.

The decree, issued that afternoon, split the difference. The mercer would rebuild to the new standard, but the vintner would contribute twenty pounds toward the additional cost. The rent would be suspended for one year while construction proceeded. Both parties would have preferred total victory. Both would live with the compromise. The house would be rebuilt. The street would be restored. The city would have one more taxable property, one more trading address, one more piece of the fabric that made London worth living in.

The Fire Court did not enforce contracts; it rewrote them to fit circumstances that no contract could have anticipated. It did not vindicate rights; it created arrangements that made rights worth having again. The commissioners worked from the surveys that Hooke and his assistants had produced, but they added something the surveys could not provide: a determination of what fairness required when the old measures no longer applied.

—-

By March, the court was hearing twenty cases a week. The petitions followed certain shapes. Landlord-tenant disputes like the Bread Street case came often, each complicated by the particular terms of particular leases. Boundary cases arrived daily, where neighbors argued over the line of a vanished party wall or the width of a burned alley. Inheritance cases accumulated, where multiple claimants produced contradictory copies of the same destroyed deed. Mortgages generated their own queue: the security had burned and the debt remained. Apprentices appeared, their masters’ shops gone, their indentures’ value uncertain. Partners in joint ventures could not agree whether the fire had dissolved their association or merely suspended it.

The commissioners developed methods for each type. For boundary disputes, they relied heavily on the survey maps, but they also sent viewers to the site, sometimes walking through the ruins themselves with the parties and their surveyors, trying to reconstruct from memory and debris where one property had ended and another begun. For inheritance cases, they examined the claimant’s possession and reputation: who had collected the rents, who had paid the taxes, whose name appeared in the parish records. For commercial disputes, they looked to the course of dealing between the parties, the customs of the trade, the practical possibility of performance.

The records of these hearings survive in the Fire Court’s decree books, preserved now in the London Metropolitan Archives. They are not dramatic documents. The language is formulaic, the descriptions terse. A petition from a citizen and draper shewing that he is seised of a messuage in Watling Street, that the same was burnt in the late dreadful fire, that another man claimeth an interest therein. The response is equally spare: an order that the draper shall rebuild the messuage, and that the claimant shall permit him so to do. But beneath the formulae, the human situations are visible. The draper had a business to restore. The claimant had an interest to protect. The decree gave each man something, and required something of each. The alternative was paralysis.

—-

The court’s work was not universally welcomed. The common lawyers resented the intrusion on their jurisdiction. Some property owners complained that the commissioners were too ready to compromise, too willing to override clear contractual rights in the name of expediency. Others complained of the opposite: that the court moved too fast, that its decrees were issued before all the evidence could be gathered, that its lack of appeal left no remedy for error.

These criticisms were not without foundation. The Fire Court’s procedures sacrificed certain values—deliberation, precedent, the right of appeal—to achieve others: speed, finality, the resumption of economic life. Whether the trade was worth making depended on what one valued. For the mercer who needed to reopen his shop, for the carpenter who needed to know where to set his foundations, for the city that needed its tax base restored, the court’s haste was a benefit. For the landlord who believed his lease gave him absolute rights, for the heir who possessed a copy of a deed that the court found insufficient, the same haste was an injury.

The commissioners were aware of these tensions. In their more elaborate decrees, they sometimes explained their reasoning, as if anticipating challenge. One such case, heard in April 1667, involved a warehouse in Thames Street that had belonged to a merchant who died in the fire. His widow claimed the property as his administrator. His brother produced a will, allegedly executed three days before the fire, that left the warehouse to a charitable trust. The will had been in the brother’s possession; the original was burned; the witnesses were dead or dispersed.

The commissioners heard evidence for two days. They examined the brother’s testimony, the copies of the will, the circumstances of its alleged execution. They noted inconsistencies. They noted also that the widow had possessed the warehouse before the fire, had managed it, had paid the ground rent. In the end, they decreed for the widow, but with a proviso: if the brother could produce better evidence within two years, the decree could be reopened. A Solomonic judgment satisfied no one completely while allowing the warehouse to be rebuilt in the meantime. The charity, if it existed, would wait. The city could not.

—-

The physical setting of the court changed as its business grew. By May 1667, Clifford’s Inn could no longer contain the press of petitioners. The sessions moved to the Guildhall, where the City Corporation provided a larger chamber and more clerks. The move was symbolic as well as practical. The Fire Court was becoming an institution of the city itself, not merely a temporary expedient of the central government. The Corporation’s involvement increased. City officials sat as commissioners. City surveyors provided evidence. The rebuilding that the court made possible was, increasingly, the Corporation’s rebuilding, shaped by its interests and its vision of urban order.

The cases continued to multiply. The surveyors’ work had revealed how complex property holding in the city had become. A single house might involve a freeholder, a ground landlord, a head lessee, an underlessee, and a mortgagee. Each of these interests had been defined by documents, many of which were now ash. Each had been bounded by physical structures, many of which were now rubble. The Fire Court’s decrees had to reconstruct this architecture of ownership from fragments: surviving copies, oral testimony, the surveyors’ measurements, and the commissioners’ sense of what arrangement would best serve the city’s recovery.

One case from June 1667 illustrates the density of these relationships. The property was a corner house in Cheapside, one of the city’s principal commercial streets. The freehold belonged to a gentleman in Essex who had never seen it. The ground lease, for ninety-nine years from 1630, was held by a goldsmith who had sublet the premises to a haberdasher. The haberdasher had mortgaged his interest to a scrivener. All four parties appeared before the Fire Court, each with a different proposal for rebuilding. The freeholder wanted the most valuable structure possible, to maximize his reversionary interest. The goldsmith wanted to minimize his contribution, since his lease was more than half run. The haberdasher wanted to rebuild quickly, to resume trade. The scrivener wanted security for his debt, which was now unsecured.

The commissioners’ decree, issued after three days of hearing, was a masterpiece of distributed obligation. The freeholder would contribute to the foundations. The goldsmith would rebuild the walls to a specified height. The haberdasher would complete the interior and roof, with the scrivener advancing funds for that purpose. The mortgage would be extended, with interest reduced, until the haberdasher’s trade could support repayment. Each party gave something. Each received something. The house would rise. The street would be restored. The intricate web of credit and obligation that made commerce possible would be reconstituted, not exactly as before, but functionally equivalent.

—-

The Fire Court’s jurisdiction was not unlimited. It could not hear criminal cases, though many petitioners asked it to punish neighbors who had allegedly spread the fire or profited from the confusion. It could not hear cases against the crown, though some property owners argued that the government’s failure to suppress the fire had created their losses. It could not hear cases involving property outside the burned area, even when the same parties and the same uncertainties were involved. These boundaries frustrated some petitioners, but they preserved the court’s focus. Its mission was reconstruction, not retribution; the future, not the past.

Within its jurisdiction, however, the court was inventive. When parties to a dispute could not agree on a surveyor, the commissioners appointed one and bound both parties to accept his findings. When a tenant claimed that rebuilding was impossible because the ground landlord refused access, the court could order entry permitted under penalty of damages for refusal. When an heir appeared with a claim that might affect a property already decreed, security against future disturbance could be required as a condition of proceeding further at law or in equity elsewhere—extraordinary powers exercised without common-law safeguards yet justified by necessity.

The court’s work attracted attention beyond London. Visitors came from other cities, other countries, to observe its methods. Some were officials facing similar problems: the great fire of Edinburgh in 1700, the fires of Boston and Philadelphia in later decades, would lead to similar tribunals. Others were theorists of law, interested in how equity could supplement and correct the rigidities of formal rule. The Fire Court was a practical demonstration of legal innovation, shaped by necessity rather than philosophy, but no less instructive for that.

—-

By the autumn of 1667, the first phase of the court’s work was nearing completion. The immediate crisis of disputed ownership had largely been resolved. Hundreds of decrees had been issued, thousands of properties cleared for rebuilding. The surveyors’ maps, once provisional, had been confirmed by judicial notice. The new streets, specified in the Rebuilding Act, could be laid out across ground whose title was no longer uncertain.

But the court’s work was not finished. The Rebuilding Act of 1670 extended its authority for another term, recognizing that new disputes would arise as construction proceeded and that some of the original decrees would require modification. The court would sit, with interruptions, until 1676, hearing cases that grew more technical and more particular as the general patterns settled. Its later decrees concerned matters like the allocation of costs for sewer construction, the adjustment of frontages to accommodate the new street widths, the resolution of disputes between builders and their clients. These were the ordinary conflicts of urban development, no longer emergencies, but the court continued to handle them with the same expedition, the same preference for practical solution over formal right.

The cumulative effect was transformative. The Fire Court did not merely resolve individual disputes; it established a template for how the rebuilt city would work. Its decrees created precedents, not legally binding but practically influential, for how property owners should share the costs of common walls, how landlords and tenants should divide the burdens of compliance with new regulations, how neighbors should accommodate each other’s interests in a densely built environment. The law that emerged was not the common law of property, with its emphasis on absolute rights and clear boundaries. It was a law of accommodation, of shared obligation, of mutual adjustment to circumstances that no single owner controlled.

This was the Fire Court’s legacy. When Christopher Wren designed his new churches, when the Corporation laid out its widened streets, when the individual builders raised their brick houses to the new standard, they worked on ground that the court had cleared. The legal infrastructure of the rebuilt city, the predictable rules that made investment and construction possible, had been created not by legislation alone, but by the daily work of men sitting at tables, hearing testimony, issuing decrees, turning the facts of destruction into the possibilities of renewal.

The last case of the first session was heard on 20 December 1667. The petitioner was a widow from Pudding Lane, seeking confirmation of her title to a cellar that had survived the fire while the house above it burned. The commissioners examined the survey, heard her testimony, and issued their decree. She could rebuild. The ground landlord would contribute. The street where the fire had begun would have one more restored property, one more piece of the ordinary life that had been interrupted.

The clerks closed their books. The candles were extinguished. Outside, in the lanes that were still largely ruin, the winter darkness came early. But the foundations were being dug now, visible in the cleared ground. The legal order that made them possible had been established. What remained was the building itself, the slow, material work of raising walls, setting beams, fitting the city back together in brick and stone rather than timber and thatch. The Fire Court had done what law can do in a crisis: not restore what was lost, but make the loss bearable, the future imaginable, the next step possible.
