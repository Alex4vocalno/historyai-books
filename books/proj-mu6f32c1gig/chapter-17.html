Chapter 17

The Fall of a Cathedral (Tuesday Afternoon, 4 September 1666)

In 1666, the ash of paper and melted type metal in St. Paul’s Churchyard had settled into a gray paste over cobblestones that morning, but the cathedral itself still stood, its tower visible above the smoke, its bulk promising what the surrounding ruins could not. Londoners had watched timber houses vanish in minutes, had seen stone churches lose their roofs and towers, but St. Paul’s represented something else—a mass of masonry so vast that fire, however fierce, must surely exhaust itself against its bulk. A Puritan minister who witnessed the destruction recorded this common conviction: many believed the cathedral a safe refuge for their goods, and for themselves. They carried their possessions into its nave, stacked them against its pillars, and waited for the terror outside to pass.

This confidence was not irrational. The cathedral’s walls were stone, its pillars marble and Purbeck, its vaulting limestone. Wood appeared only in the roof, the doors, the furnishings—vulnerable elements, certainly, but separated from the exterior by thick masonry.

What the faithful could not see, or chose not to see, was the wooden skeleton that now shrouded the entire structure.

Christopher Wren had surveyed the cathedral in 1665 and found it in dangerous decay. The king had authorized repairs, and for months scaffolding had climbed the walls, filled the nave, and covered the roof. This wooden infrastructure—beams, planks, ladders, platforms, hoists—represented thousands of cubic feet of seasoned timber, dry as tinder, arranged in a lattice that touched every part of the building. The cathedral had become, in effect, a wooden structure wearing a stone mask. As the parliamentary inquiry later learned, the crypt had also been crammed full of rescued goods, and was filled with the tightly packed stocks of the printers and booksellers in adjoining Paternoster Row.

The fire reached it through the booksellers’ district. By Tuesday afternoon, the flames had consumed the shops of Little Britain and Ave Maria Lane, had crossed the churchyard, and now licked at the cathedral’s very walls. The heat was not ordinary combustion. John Evelyn, approaching from the west, described an incredible chaos of fire and ruine. The burning of so many books—paper compressed, inked, stacked—produced a thermal intensity that melted metal and cracked stone. The air itself seemed to burn.

Inside the cathedral, the refugees still waited. Some had climbed to the vaults, believing height meant safety. Others remained in the nave with their possessions. The lead roof began to soften. This was the first visible sign of catastrophe: the great sheets of lead, each one weighing hundreds of pounds, started to liquefy. The melting point of lead is 621 degrees Fahrenheit. The temperature above the burning booksellers’ shops far exceeded this. The molten metal ran down through the roof beams, poured through gaps in the vaulting, and fell into the nave in great drops, as one observer put it—silver streams turning to gray rain, then solidifying as they cooled, then melting again as the heat built.

The scaffolding caught. This was the decisive turn. The wooden platforms and ladders, saturated now with radiant heat from the surrounding inferno, ignited not from direct flame but from the sheer intensity of the air. Fire traveled the scaffolding as flame travels a fuse—rapidly, irresistibly, reaching into every corner of the structure. The wooden roof, already weakened by falling lead, became a burning lake. The stone walls, exposed to heat no masonry was designed to withstand, began to crack and spall. The marble pillars, heated unevenly, shed their facing in sheets.

Samuel Pepys, who had spent the morning carrying news and witnessing destruction, climbed to an elevated position to watch. His account, written that evening, captures the sequence with the precision of a man still trying to believe what he saw. First the lead roof melted and flowed down in great streams. Then the scaffolding burned through, and sections of roof fell into the nave. Then the stone walls, deprived of their wooden bracing and subjected to internal temperatures that expanded their fabric unevenly, began to collapse. The great tower, which had dominated London’s skyline for six centuries, fell down. The west front, with its elaborate screen and statuary, followed. The entire structure, stone and lead and wood together, became an incredible vehement fire.

The time was approximately six o’clock. The collapse took hours to complete, but the decisive failure—the breach of the walls, the fall of the tower—occurred in this window. Witnesses differ on details. The London Gazette, reporting four days later, emphasized the melting lead and the burning roof but said nothing of the scaffolding. The parliamentary inquiry, conducted in the months following, collected testimony that mentioned the scaffolding repeatedly, as if survivors needed to explain how stone could burn. The Puritan minister described the refugees trapped inside, some in the middle of the church, flying from one place to another for shelter, and finding none. The heat was such that none could escape once the roof fell. Their goods, their bodies, and the cathedral’s fabric fused in the same destruction.

Evelyn approached closer than prudence allowed. He had spent the morning at Deptford, supervising the removal of his own goods to safer ground, and now rode toward the disaster with a servant. What he saw at St. Paul’s exceeded his capacity for description, though he tried for pages. The dreadful fire, the melancholy ruins, the sickening smell of burning—his adjectives pile up against the reality. Most terrible was the collapse of the tower, which he placed at approximately eight o’clock, though this may represent the final fall rather than the initial breach. The tower’s destruction was audible across the city. Stones that had been lifted into place by medieval cranes, that had survived the Reformation’s iconoclasm and the Commonwealth’s purges, now flew apart with reports like cannon fire.

The scaffolding was Wren’s. This fact, obvious to contemporaries, carries a weight of irony that no narrative needs to underline. The architect who would rebuild London had, through his necessary repairs, prepared the way for its greatest loss. The cathedral had needed saving; the attempt to save it had destroyed it. This was the cinder signature of St. Paul’s—not the fire’s random strike, but the convergence of long neglect with urgent intervention, of stone’s apparent permanence with wood’s hidden vulnerability.

The psychological effect was immediate and profound. London had lost its churches before—eighty-seven parish churches would burn in these four days. It had lost its commercial heart, its legal center, its poorest alleys and its grandest houses. But St. Paul’s was different. It was the parish church of no single ward, the possession of no single interest. It was England’s church, the symbolic center of a Protestant nation, the burial place of its medieval heroes, the stage for its state ceremonies. Its fall meant that nothing was secure—not tradition, not sanctity, not the very stones that seemed to promise endurance.

Pepys understood this. After watching the cathedral burn, he walked through the city with his clerk, searching for a working boat to carry his own goods to safety. Everywhere he found the streets full of nothing but people and horses and carts loaden with goods. The panic had spread beyond the fire’s reach. People who had watched St. Paul’s fall now assumed that the entire city must burn, that the fire would continue until it reached the walls, the river, the open fields. Some loaded boats and rowed for the countryside. Others simply waited, goods packed, for the flames to find them.

The Duke of York had established his command post at Temple Bar, west of the burning zone. By Tuesday evening, this position, which had seemed secure that morning, faced direct threat. The fire had jumped the line of the Fleet River, which many had assumed would serve as a natural break. It now advanced along Fleet Street toward the Temple and the Inns of Court, the legal heart of England. James directed the creation of firebreaks with the desperate energy of a commander who understood that his authority was dissolving with every street lost. He had no legal power to order the destruction of private property; he assumed it, and his men obeyed, because the alternative was worse.

But the fall of St. Paul’s changed the calculus. Before that collapse, the fire had been a disaster of unprecedented scale, yet still comprehensible as a fire—something that consumed combustible materials and stopped at stone. Afterward, it became something else: a force that consumed stone itself, that mocked human effort and divine protection alike. The men working the firebreaks at Temple Bar worked now without the consolation of precedent. If St. Paul’s could fall, any building could fall. If the cathedral’s thick walls had not protected its refugees, no wall would protect anyone.

Evelyn recorded the specific losses within the church. The choir stalls, a most curious piece of workmanship, burned with the rest. The organ, recently rebuilt, melted. The great bell, Tom of Lincoln, fell and cracked. The library, which had survived the medieval fire of 1087 and the depredations of the Civil War, succumbed in an hour. The monuments—effigies of knights and bishops, memorial tablets accumulated across centuries—exploded or crumbled as their supporting masonry failed. The very graves opened in the heat, and bones that had rested undisturbed for hundreds of years were scattered in the rubble.

This desecration had a particular horror. The dead were supposed to be safe. Christian burial promised rest until resurrection; the church’s consecrated ground was meant to protect even the body from violation. Now fire accomplished what time and war had not, reducing distinguished remains to anonymous ash. Evelyn, whose piety was conventional but sincere, found this aspect almost unbearable. The sickening smell he mentioned was partly spoiled meat, partly burning cloth and wood, but partly also the distinctive odor of calcined bone.

The refugees inside the cathedral had chosen their refuge rationally, based on information available to them. They had seen timber houses burn; they had seen even stone churches lose their roofs. But St. Paul’s was larger than any parish church, its walls thicker, its construction more massive. The mistake was not in their reasoning but in their premises. They did not know about the scaffolding, or did not understand its significance. They assumed that stone meant safety, that scale meant endurance, that the house of God would be protected by its purpose. These assumptions were reasonable. They were also fatal.

The fire’s behavior at St. Paul’s revealed patterns that would guide the final desperate efforts to stop it. The lead roof, melting and flowing, had acted as a conductor of heat rather than a barrier. The stone walls, deprived of their roof’s protection and subjected to internal fire, had failed from within. The scaffolding, intended as temporary support, had become permanent fuel. These lessons were not abstract. Men watching the cathedral burn could see exactly how the fire had won, and from that observation could deduce what might yet stop it.

The most important deduction concerned firebreaks. The failure at St. Paul’s demonstrated that partial measures were worse than useless. The cathedral had been surrounded by burning buildings on three sides; the heat from these had accomplished what direct flame might not have done alone. To save what remained of London, the fire must be deprived of fuel not merely in its path but in its vicinity. Buildings must come down before the heat reached them, not after. The gap must be wide enough that radiant heat could not carry across it, not merely narrow enough that flame could not leap.

This was a counsel of absolute destruction. It meant tearing down houses that were not yet burning, that might never burn, in the hope of saving others. It meant destroying property without compensation, without legal process, without certainty of success. The Duke of York had been ordering such destruction since Monday; after St. Paul’s, the necessity became obvious even to those who had resisted. The king himself, who had spent Monday night in the streets with his brother, returned to the fire lines on Tuesday evening with renewed urgency. He understood, as his subjects did, that the fall of the cathedral marked a threshold. The fire was no longer a municipal emergency. It was a national crisis, a test of sovereignty itself.

The practical consequences extended through the night. At Temple Bar, at Fetter Lane, at Holborn, men worked with axes and gunpowder to create the gaps that might yet arrest the fire’s westward march. The gunpowder, which had been used sparingly on Monday for fear of uncontrolled damage, became the preferred method. A barrel properly placed could bring down a row of houses in minutes, where axes required hours. The risk of explosion, of fire spread by the blast itself, was accepted as the lesser danger.

Pepys, having arranged for his goods to be carried to Bethnal Green, returned to the river and found a boat to take him past the burning city. From the water, he saw the whole City in dreadful flames near the water-side. St. Paul’s was still visible as a mass of fire, its outline recognizable but its details lost in the glare. The river itself reflected the flames, so that water seemed to burn. He recorded the sight with the numb precision of a man who had exceeded his capacity for astonishment: it made him weep to see it.

The weeping was significant. Pepys was not a sentimental man. His diary recorded plague deaths, political crises, marital quarrels, with a detachment that sometimes reads as coldness. The burning of London moved him beyond that detachment. The fall of St. Paul’s, specifically, represented a loss that could not be calculated in pounds or measured in acres. It was the loss of a symbol, of a confidence, of the assumption that some things endure.

The booksellers had understood the cathedral’s vulnerability better than the refugees who sought shelter within it. For years, the stationers and printers of Paternoster Row had conducted their commerce in the shadow of St. Paul’s, their livelihoods physically and symbolically dependent on the great church. They had watched the scaffolding rise through 1665 and 1666, had grumbled at the dust and obstruction, had perhaps noticed how the wooden platforms created covered walkways where rain could not reach. They knew, in the practical way of tradesmen, that the cathedral was not stone alone but a system of elements—some permanent, some temporary, all necessary to its functioning. When the fire took their shops, they did not run toward the nave. They had no illusions about what the scaffolding meant.

This professional knowledge, scattered among men who were now themselves refugees, represented a kind of expertise that the broader population lacked. The booksellers understood buildings as processes, as assemblies of materials with varying properties and lifespans. They had seen timber rot and lead fatigue, had calculated the weight of paper and the strength of shelves. When they looked at St. Paul’s shrouded in its wooden exoskeleton, they saw not a fortress but a compound risk. The refugees saw what the cathedral had been; the booksellers saw what it had become.

The thermal environment that destroyed St. Paul’s was, by contemporary standards, literally incredible. Men who had spent their lives around fireplaces, forges, and occasional building fires had simply never encountered temperatures high enough to melt lead in quantity, to crack marble, to make stone walls behave like heated iron. The burning of compressed paper and vellum in the surrounding district created what modern fire science would recognize as a flashover condition—a sustained, uniform high temperature that exceeded the ignition points of most common materials simultaneously. The cathedral did not catch fire in the ordinary sense, with flames spreading from point to point. It was engulfed by an atmosphere that had itself become combustive.

This distinction mattered for what came after. Observers who described the cathedral as burning were not wrong, but they were imprecise in ways that shaped subsequent understanding. The parliamentary inquiry, gathering testimony through the autumn of 1666, struggled with this imprecision. Witnesses spoke of the lead melting, of the roof falling, of the walls collapsing, but could not agree on sequence or mechanism. Some insisted that the roof had fallen first, others that the walls had failed before the roof descended. The scaffolding appeared in testimony almost as an afterthought, a detail that survivors offered to explain what seemed to require explanation: how a stone building could be destroyed by fire. The inquiry’s final report acknowledged the scaffolding’s role but emphasized the “extraordinary violence” of the fire itself, as if the cathedral’s fall required both special pleading and supernatural agency.

The architectural irony extended beyond Wren’s involvement to encompass the very nature of the repairs that had created the vulnerability. The cathedral had been decaying for decades—perhaps for centuries—its medieval fabric stressed by settling foundations, by the weight of its own tower, by successive alterations that had weakened original structures. The 1665 survey had revealed cracks in the piers, leaks in the roof, a general “ruinous and decayed” condition that threatened collapse even without fire. The scaffolding was necessary because the building was failing; the building failed because the scaffolding was present. This circular causation, obvious in retrospect, was invisible to contemporaries who saw only the immediate disaster. The long history of deferred maintenance, of makeshift repairs, of institutional poverty that had left England’s greatest church dependent on temporary wooden structures, dissolved into the apparent suddenness of the catastrophe.

This was the psychological nadir. The fire would burn for another day, would consume more buildings, would threaten more lives. But the worst moment, in terms of morale and meaning, came with the cathedral’s collapse. Afterward, the city could only fight or flee; it could no longer hope for miraculous deliverance. The destruction of St. Paul’s proved that the fire was not a punishment for particular sins, not a visitation on particular neighborhoods, not a test that faith or courage could necessarily pass. It was simply a physical process, amenable to physical countermeasures and to nothing else.

The bent cross lying in the smoldering rubble of St. Paul’s nave marks the point of deepest despair, making the gamble of absolute, destructive action at the city’s edge not just possible but necessary.
