Chapter 19

The Fire’s Eastern Reach

In 1666, the line at Pie Corner was therefore more than a physical boundary: it was a political marker—the point at which one kind of authority had proved its capacity and established its claim. Yet six hundred yards to the east, along the riverfront where the Thames curved toward the Tower, another reality persisted. There the garrison commander at the White Tower had spent the morning issuing orders for immediate demolition of houses in the lanes approaching East Smithfield and preparing gunpowder charges to create a firebreak should the flames cross the narrow strip of ground still separating them from the royal fortress. The contrast was stark: in the west, a victory being celebrated; in the east, a desperate holding action against an enemy still very much alive.

This was Wednesday, 5 September 1666. The wind that had driven the fire through the heart of London had dropped the previous evening, and by morning the western front had stabilized. Samuel Pepys, climbing the steeple of Barking Church near the Tower, looked out over a panorama of ash and smoking timbers stretching from the river to the wall. The city lay in ruins. What caught his eye, what made him reach for his diary, was not the uniformity of destruction. It was its unevenness. The western suburbs had been saved. The eastern wharves had not.

The fire’s final assault had come by misdirection. All eyes had turned westward as St Paul’s collapsed on Tuesday afternoon, as the flames threatened to break into the Inns of Court and the wealthy precincts where the Strand met Fleet Street. Military resources had concentrated at that junction precisely because the symbolic stakes were highest there. A fire in Fleet Street meant a fire in Westminster, a fire at the seat of law and government and royal power. The eastern parishes, by contrast, offered no such dramatic possibilities. They were poorer, less documented, their inhabitants less likely to appear in the pages of gentlemen’s diaries. Their destruction would register as economic loss, not political catastrophe.

John Evelyn understood this distinction. Where Pepys climbed to see, Evelyn walked to comprehend. His diary entry for Wednesday records a circuit of the perimeter, a deliberate survey of what remained and what had vanished. He noted the dreadful ruins of the Exchange, the melting of lead roofs, the vast heaps of stone where churches had stood. But he also noted what others passed over: the continuation of the fire along Thames Street toward the Tower, the furious burning of the warehouses and wharves that made London the greatest port in England. The eastern reach had not stopped when the western front stabilized. It had simply continued, less observed, until it met the stone walls of the Tower itself.

The vulnerability of this district was structural, not accidental. Thames Street ran parallel to the river, a dense corridor of commerce where the materials of trade were stored in quantities that made fire probable as well as possible. Train oil, tar, hemp, timber, coal—these were the foundations of maritime prosperity, and they burned with an intensity that defeated ordinary measures. The houses here were older, more tightly packed, their construction less substantial than the merchant palaces of Cheapside. The parish records, where they survived, show a population of watermen, porters, small traders, the kind of people who left fewer traces in official documents and therefore commanded less attention when crisis came.

Why had the defensive effort concentrated so heavily in the west? The wind provided one answer. From Sunday through Tuesday, it had blown steadily from the east, driving the fire toward the heart of the city and then beyond. The logical place to make a stand was therefore on the western edge, where the flames were advancing fastest. But wind explains only part of the misallocation. The rest lies in perception: what the authorities could see, what they could imagine, what they believed themselves to be defending.

The military command at the Strand-Fleet Street junction was fighting for London’s place in the political order. A fire that reached Westminster threatened the Restoration settlement itself, the fragile balance of crown and city that had taken six years to construct. The eastern wharves, by contrast, were merely commercial. Their loss would be measured in pounds and pence, in the inconvenience of displaced trade, not in the survival of institutions. This was a miscalculation, but it was a miscalculation embedded in the social vision of the men who made decisions. They saw the city as a political entity first, an economic one second. The fire taught otherwise.

Evelyn’s walk took him past the evidence. The Custom House, where the records of England’s trade were stored, had burned on Monday night despite desperate efforts to save it. The water wheels under London Bridge, which supplied the city’s piped water, had caught fire on Sunday, eliminating a resource that might have made difference in the eastern districts. These were not symbolic losses. They were functional ones, damage to the machinery of daily life that would persist long after the political crisis had passed.

The Tower garrison’s preparations on Wednesday morning reveal how close the eastern threat had come. The fortress housed not merely the crown jewels and the royal mint but the largest concentration of gunpowder in England. A fire that reached the Tower’s magazines would have produced an explosion visible across the Home Counties, a catastrophe transcending anything the western suburbs might have suffered. The garrison commander had spent the night organizing bucket chains and wetting down the stone walls, but he knew these measures were insufficient. By dawn, he had moved to the final option: the systematic demolition of everything between the fire and the fortress walls.

This was the same strategy that had finally worked in the west, but applied here with less fanfare and less certainty of success. The houses in the lanes approaching East Smithfield were not stone-built merchants’ residences but timber-framed tenements, easier to pull down but more numerous, their occupants less able to remove goods before the soldiers arrived. The records of the Tower garrison show the commander requisitioning labor from the surrounding parishes, pressing men into service with the authority of martial law. They also show his anxiety about the gunpowder: repeated orders to move barrels to the inner Ward, to prepare boats for waterborne evacuation, to stand ready for the worst.

The fire never reached the Tower. The wind’s drop on Tuesday evening, combined with the garrison’s demolitions, created a buffer that held. But this was a narrow margin, and the cost of holding it was unevenly distributed. The western firebreaks had saved property worth thousands of pounds, the homes of men who would sit on the parliamentary inquiry and help shape the narrative of the disaster. The eastern demolitions had destroyed the homes of men who would appear in no inquiry, whose losses would be recorded only in the petitions of the poor, documents that rarely survived.

Evelyn noted this asymmetry without quite naming it. He recorded the poor inhabitants camping in the fields beyond the walls, the miserable objects carrying what they could save from the ruins. But his attention kept returning to the grand losses: the churches, the halls, the noble buildings that had made London famous. The eastern wharves, functional and unglamorous, received less of his concern. This was not cruelty on Evelyn’s part. It was the limitation of his perspective, the natural tendency of an educated man to see history in the destruction of institutions rather than in the displacement of working people.

The fire’s eastern reach thus exposes a pattern that would shape the aftermath. The parliamentary inquiry would focus on the origin of the fire, on the question of Catholic conspiracy, on the failures of the Lord Mayor in the first hours. It would not examine why the eastern districts had burned while the western suburbs had been saved, why the Tower had come so close to disaster while the western firebreaks had been held at such cost. These were questions without villains, without dramatic possibility, and therefore without official interest.

Yet the damage was real and lasting. The wharves between London Bridge and the Tower had been the engine of the city’s maritime trade, the point where goods from across the world entered the English economy. Their destruction meant not merely the loss of stored cargo but the disruption of the entire system of import and distribution. Ships that had ridden at anchor in the Thames, waiting to unload, now found no facilities to receive them. Merchants who had relied on the Thames Street warehouses for storage now faced ruin. The poor who had lived in the tenements behind the wharves now had nothing, not even the prospect of employment in a port that no longer functioned.

The gunpowder preparations on Wednesday morning thus mark a turning point not recognized at the time. The immediate crisis was averted. The fire stopped at the edge of the demolished zone, its fuel exhausted, its energy spent. But the conditions that had made the eastern reach so vulnerable remained: the concentration of combustible materials, the density of poor housing, the absence of permanent fire-fighting capacity, the reliance on ad hoc measures in moments of emergency. These were not addressed in the Rebuilding Act or in Christopher Wren’s plans. They were structural features of a maritime economy that could not function without risk, and that therefore accepted risk as the price of prosperity.

Evelyn ended his Wednesday walk at the edge of the ruins, looking back at what had been a city. His diary records a mood of sadness and horror, but also of determination to rebuild. What it does not record, what perhaps he did not see, was the particular character of the eastern destruction. The western front had been held by deliberate effort, by the concentration of resources and authority at a decisive point. The eastern front had been held by luck and improvisation, by the wind’s change and a soldier’s readiness to blow up his neighbors’ houses. Both had worked, in the sense that both had stopped the fire. But they had worked differently, and the difference mattered for what came after.

The pattern of survival and loss in the eastern parishes also reveals something the official record preferred to forget. The fire had moved through these districts not as a single wave but as a series of opportunistic advances, finding fuel in the narrow courts behind the main streets, jumping from warehouse to tenement to riverside shed. The destruction was therefore patchy, some blocks reduced to ash while adjacent ones stood damaged but intact. This irregularity would complicate the work of the Fire Court for years to come, as judges tried to establish boundaries of responsibility between landlords and tenants, between neighbors whose properties had burned and those whose had merely suffered water damage or structural weakening from the demolitions.

The maritime economy faced a particular crisis. The Port of London was not merely a collection of wharves but a coordinated system: customs inspection, warehousing, lighterage, porterage, each function depending on the others. The fire had struck at multiple points in this chain simultaneously. The Custom House records were gone, creating immediate uncertainty about duties owed and goods in bond. The warehouses were gone, leaving merchants without storage and ships without discharge. The watermen’s houses were gone, dispersing the labor force that moved cargo from ship to shore. Recovery would require not merely rebuilding but reconstruction of the entire network, and no single authority had the power to coordinate such an effort.

The crown’s response to this eastern crisis, such as it was, came piecemeal and late. While the Duke of York had commanded immediate attention to the western threat, the Tower garrison had operated with less guidance and less resource. The difference in treatment reflected the difference in perceived importance, but it also reflected a structural problem in urban governance. London had no fire department, no permanent emergency service, no established protocol for coordinating military and civilian authority. Each crisis was met with improvisation, and improvisation favored the visible over the essential, the politically urgent over the economically necessary.

The eastern wharves would eventually be rebuilt, of course. The port could not function without them, and the port was the foundation of English trade. But the rebuilding would follow the pattern of the fire’s uneven destruction: private, uncoordinated, responsive to immediate commercial pressure rather than to any comprehensive plan. The new wharves would be built of brick where the old had been timber, a material improvement won at the cost of the disaster. They would be built with narrower streets and less encroachment on the river, concessions to safety that also reduced capacity. And they would be built by the same economic logic that had made the old wharves vulnerable: the pressure to maximize storage and throughput, to pack valuable cargo into limited space, to house the labor force as cheaply as possible in structures that would burn again when the next fire came.

The Duke of York’s command post at Temple Bar, where Strand meets Fleet Street, had been established on Tuesday afternoon as the fire threatened to break through into the western suburbs. The concentration of military force there—soldiers, demolition teams, the personal authority of the King’s brother—represented the maximum effort the city could mount. It succeeded because the stakes were immediately comprehensible: a fire in Fleet Street meant a fire in Westminster, and a fire in Westminster threatened the political order itself. The eastern districts offered no such clarity. Their value was cumulative, systemic, dependent on the functioning of a commercial network that few of the decision-makers fully understood. This explains why, as the garrison commander at the White Tower prepared his desperate holding action on Wednesday morning, the official narrative of victory was already being written from the west.

This misalignment between political attention and economic function had deep roots in the Restoration settlement. The men who commanded the firefighting effort—courtiers, military officers, members of the Privy Council—saw London primarily as the seat of royal power. Their careers, their fortunes, their understanding of civic order derived from this perception. The port economy, by contrast, was managed by merchants, customs officials, and the great trading companies, a separate sphere of authority with its own hierarchies and interests. When crisis came, there was no institutional mechanism to coordinate these two worlds. The Tower garrison could requisition labor and prepare demolitions, but it could not command the resources of the port, could not direct the merchant fleet, could not even communicate effectively with the customs officers whose records were burning in the Custom House.

The wind’s behavior on Tuesday evening had thus created a temporal illusion that persisted into Wednesday morning. The drop in the easterly wind seemed to signal the fire’s defeat, a natural reprieve that allowed the western firebreaks to hold. But this same drop also meant that the flames along Thames Street were no longer being driven forward by a steady blast. They were smoldering, advancing fitfully, finding new fuel in the irregular pattern of warehouses and tenements.

The western front had been stabilized by human effort at a decisive moment. The eastern front was merely pausing, gathering strength, waiting for the next gust or the next opportunity. The garrison commander understood this distinction. His preparations on Wednesday morning were not the precautions of a man who believed the danger had passed. They were the measures of someone who knew the fire was still alive, still hungry, still capable of the catastrophic leap that would bring it to the Tower’s magazines.

The geography of the eastern districts made this threat particularly insidious. Thames Street ran close to the river, but the ground rose gradually toward the Tower, creating a series of lanes and courts that climbed away from the water. Fire moving inland would find itself in a tangle of narrow passages, timber-framed houses leaning toward one another across spaces barely wide enough for a cart. The garrison’s demolition orders targeted these approaches specifically: the lanes toward East Smithfield, the tenements along Little Tower Hill, the cramped housing that had grown up against the fortress walls over centuries of unplanned expansion. These were not the grand avenues of the western city. They were the organic product of maritime labor, housing built by and for the people who served the port economy, dense because land was scarce and expensive, combustible because building materials were cheap and regulation nonexistent.

The social composition of this district also shaped its vulnerability. The watermen of the Thames, the porters who moved cargo from ship to warehouse, the small traders who supplied the maritime workforce: these were not people with political connections, with access to the Duke of York’s command post, with the ability to petition for military assistance.

Evelyn’s Wednesday walk thus captured a moment of transition that the official narrative would obscure. The fire was stopping, or had stopped, or would stop within hours depending on the precise location. The wind had dropped. The gunpowder had not been needed at the Tower, though it had been ready. The immediate emergency was passing into memory, and memory was already selecting what to retain. The collapse of St Paul’s, the saving of Westminster, the heroism of the Duke of York at the western firebreaks: these would become the familiar story. The burning of the eastern wharves, the near-disaster at the Tower, the structural vulnerability of the port economy: these would fade from view, preserved only in scattered records and the unwritten experience of those who had lost everything.

The hot ruins of the eastern wharves, now silent but representing a massive, unaccounted-for blow to London’s maritime economy, would shape the recovery in ways that no parliamentary inquiry examined. The merchants who lost their warehouses would struggle to rebuild without capital. The watermen who lost their homes would drift to other parishes, other occupations, other cities. The port infrastructure that had made London the center of English trade would be reconstructed piecemeal, by private initiative rather than public planning, perpetuating the vulnerabilities that the fire had exposed. The western firebreaks were a political achievement. The eastern demolitions were a narrow escape. Between these two boundaries lay the full measure of what the fire had cost, and what the city had not yet learned to count.
