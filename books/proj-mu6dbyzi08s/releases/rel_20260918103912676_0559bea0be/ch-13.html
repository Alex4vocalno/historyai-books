Chapter 13

Landfall on a Barren Shore

Thomas Orde-Lees wrote in his diary on 15 April 1916 that he felt profound relief at touching solid ground for the first time in 497 days. Three hours later, Alexander Macklin recorded that the wind had risen to hurricane force, that driving snow reduced visibility to a few yards, and that the party had no shelter, no fresh water, and no idea whether the narrow beach on which they stood would survive the next tide. Both men were describing the same moment. Both were telling the truth.

The boats had brought them through. Shackleton had named the three craft after the principal backers of the expedition: the James Caird, the Dudley Docker, and the Stancomb Wills. Those names, painted on transom and bow, were the only connection these small vessels had to the world of patronage and planning that had launched the Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition two years before. The party had waited until 8 April 1916, when they finally took to the boats as the ice started to break up. Over a perilous period of seven days, they sailed and rowed through stormy seas and dangerous loose ice to reach Elephant Island on 15 April. Now they were ashore, and the boats were everything—carrying the expedition’s entire food supply, its few remaining navigational instruments, and the lives of twenty-eight men.

The landing at Elephant Island was not the landing Shackleton had planned. Clarence Island had been the original objective, a smaller landmass that might have offered easier access and a better position for signaling passing vessels. But the currents and winds had pushed the little flotilla westward, and by the morning of 14 April, it was clear that Elephant Island was their only option. The island was desolate and unvisited, a mountainous mass of rock and ice rising from the Southern Ocean. No ship stopped here. No whaling station existed on its shores. The men who landed on its beaches would be invisible to the world.

—-

The approach had begun in the early hours of 14 April. Worsley, navigating from the James Caird, had taken sightings through breaks in the cloud cover and calculated their position as best he could with a wet chronometer and a sextant soaked with spray. The margin for error was measured in miles. The three boats stayed close together, the larger James Caird leading, the smaller Dudley Docker and Stancomb Wills following in her wake. The men rowed in shifts, their hands blistered and raw, their bodies aching from cramped conditions. They had not slept properly in days. They had eaten nothing but cold rations—pemmican, biscuit, the occasional lump of seal meat saved from the ice camp. Water came from melting snow in the bottom of the boats, mixed with spray that broke over the gunwales.

Frank Hurley, the expedition’s photographer, recorded in his diary for 12 April that the conditions were beyond description. The seas broke over the boats with such force as to threaten to stove them in. The men sat huddled together, soaked to the skin, frozen, and utterly miserable. But Hurley also noted that Shackleton had moved from boat to boat during a brief calm, calling out encouragement, checking the lashings, making sure each craft was holding together. The Boss never showed doubt. His presence alone kept the party together.

By the afternoon of 14 April, the outline of Elephant Island had become visible through the murk. A cheer went up from the men in the Dudley Docker, taken up by the crews of the other boats. They had made landfall. Or so they thought. The coastline that emerged from the fog was a wall of ice cliffs and black rock, with no beach visible, no place where a boat could be brought ashore without being smashed. The swell rolled in from the open sea and exploded against the rocks. The boats would have to move west, following the shore, searching for a gap.

Night fell, and with it the temperature dropped. The men could not stop rowing. To stop was to drift backward, to lose the ground they had gained, to risk being blown out to sea again. They rowed through the darkness, guided by the faint outline of the coast and by Worsley’s occasional calls from the Caird. The Stancomb Wills, the smallest and most vulnerable of the three boats, fell behind. Shackleton ordered the Caird to heave to, and they waited until the Wills caught up. Then they pressed on.

Dawn on 15 April revealed a narrow spit of beach at the northwest corner of the island, a crescent of black sand and shingle hemmed in by glaciers and rock faces. The beach was perhaps fifty yards long and twenty yards deep at its widest point. Beyond it, the land rose steeply, ice-covered and impassable. A worse landing site would be hard to imagine. But it was the only one they had found, and the men had been rowing for hours, their strength nearly gone.

Shackleton gave the order to go in. The James Caird led, her bow turning toward the shore, her crew pulling hard against the surf. The boat grounded with a grinding sound, her keel striking the shingle, and the men tumbled out into waist-deep water. They dragged the boat up the beach, above the tide line, and turned to help the other craft. The Dudley Docker came next, then the Stancomb Wills. Within an hour, all three boats were ashore, and twenty-eight men stood on solid ground for the first time since 18 January 1915.

—-

Orde-Lees recorded his relief in his diary. The storekeeper had kept a careful account of the expedition’s provisions, and his mind turned immediately to inventory. They had saved the boats. They had saved most of the supplies. They had saved themselves. For a man who had spent months calculating rations and worrying about the next meal, the simple fact of standing on land that was not moving, that would not crack beneath them, that would not drift them further from rescue—this was a deliverance worth recording.

But Macklin’s diary told the other side of the story. The surgeon had watched the men stumble onto the beach, their legs weak from days of sitting, their faces hollow with exhaustion. He had seen the state of their hands and feet, the frostbite that had begun to form during the nights in the boats, the cuts and sores that had gone untreated. And he had looked at the beach itself, at the glacier that loomed above it, at the waves that broke just beyond the narrow strip of shingle, and he had understood that their ordeal was not over. It had merely changed form.

The wind that Macklin recorded was no ordinary gale. Elephant Island sits in the convergence zone of the Southern Ocean’s weather systems, where depressions sweep eastward around the globe without interruption. The wind that struck the beach that afternoon came from the southwest, funneled between the island’s peaks and the open sea, carrying snow that had been whipped from the glaciers. Within hours, the men were digging holes in the shingle, trying to create some shelter from the blast. They had no tents. They had no building materials. They had only the boats, the sails, and the few spars and oars they had brought with them.

Shackleton organized the party into work details. One group was sent to find fresh water. Another was told to gather stones for windbreaks. A third was set to unload the boats, bring supplies above the high-water mark, sort rations and assess what remained. The Boss moved among them, giving orders, offering encouragement, projecting the same calm confidence that had sustained the expedition through months of drift and days of open-boat voyaging. But his diary, written later that night in the dim light of a lantern, betrayed the reality of their situation. They were on land, he recorded, but they were not safe.

—-

The inventory that Orde-Lees compiled told its own story. They had saved approximately three weeks’ worth of food at full rations, perhaps six weeks if they reduced their intake. They had saved the Primus stoves, which meant they could cook, but they had very little fuel. They had saved some biscuits, pemmican, sugar and milk powder. They had saved a small quantity of seal meat taken from the ice floe, but it was spoiling. They had no fresh water source yet identified. They had no shelter. They had no medical supplies beyond what Macklin carried in his kit. And they had no way to signal for help.

The boats themselves were damaged. The James Caird, the largest and most seaworthy, had taken a beating in the surf. Her planking was sound, but her seams had been strained, and her canvas covering was torn in several places. The Dudley Docker had shipped water continuously during the voyage, and her hull showed signs of stress. The Stancomb Wills, the smallest and most fragile, had nearly been swamped more than once. All three craft would need repairs before they could put to sea again.

But putting to sea again was not a decision for that night. The men were exhausted beyond measure. Some collapsed on the shingle and fell asleep where they lay. Others huddled together in the holes they had dug, trying to share body heat, trying to stay out of the wind. Shackleton posted a watch, two men at a time, to keep an eye on the boats and to watch for any change in the weather. The rest were told to sleep.

Sleep did not come easily. The wind howled across the beach, driving snow into every crevice, finding every gap in the inadequate clothing the men wore. The temperature had dropped below freezing, and the dampness that had soaked into their garments during the boat journey now turned to ice. They lay shivering, cramped together, dreaming of warmth and food and safety, waking to find that the storm had not abated.

The next morning, 16 April, revealed the full extent of their predicament. The beach on which they had landed was narrower than they had thought. The high tide had come within feet of the boats, and the next spring tide would likely cover the entire spit. The glacier above them showed signs of instability; cracks ran through its face, and the sound of ice shifting could be heard in the brief lulls between gusts. The wind had not diminished. If anything, it had strengthened during the night.

—-

Shackleton called a meeting. The men gathered around him, huddled against the wind, their faces drawn and gray. He did not sugarcoat the situation. They were on Elephant Island, he said. The island was uninhabited, unvisited, and unlikely to be visited by any ship in the foreseeable future. Their supplies were limited. Their position was precarious. If they stayed where they were, they would die.

But there was a possibility, he continued. South Georgia lay approximately 800 miles to the northeast. It was a whaling station, with ships and men and supplies. If a small party could make the voyage in one of the boats, they could bring back help for the rest. It would be a desperate undertaking. The boat would have to cross the wildest stretch of ocean in the world, in winter, with no guarantee of making landfall. But it was the only chance they had.

The men listened in silence. They knew what the Boss was proposing. They knew that the boat that went would face the worst the Southern Ocean could offer. They knew that those who stayed would face an ordeal of a different order: waiting on a barren shore, through an Antarctic winter, with limited food and no shelter, hoping that the boat had made it, hoping that rescue would come before their supplies ran out.

Shackleton did not ask for volunteers. He told them what he needed. The James Caird would be the boat. She was the strongest, the most seaworthy. He would lead the party. Worsley would navigate. He needed four more men to crew her. The rest would stay, under the command of Wild, and they would make the best shelter they could. They would ration their food. They would wait. And they would trust that the Boss would bring them through.

—-

The diary entries from those first days on Elephant Island reveal the psychological strain that the landing imposed. Hurley wrote of the crushing disappointment of finding the island so hostile, so utterly without resource. They had hoped for some relief, some sign that their ordeal was ending. Instead, they found that it had only begun. Orde-Lees, ever the pragmatist, listed the supplies and calculated how long they would last. His figures were grim. At full rations, they had less than a month. At reduced rations, perhaps six weeks. After that, they would have to hunt, and there was no guarantee that the island would provide.

Macklin’s medical notes painted the most immediate picture of their condition. The men were suffering from exposure, from exhaustion, from the cumulative effects of months of inadequate nutrition and constant cold. Frostbite had affected several of them, particularly on their hands and feet. Their clothes were rotting off their bodies, saturated with salt water and worn thin by use. Their boots were disintegrating. Their physical state was such that even the simple tasks of gathering stones and digging shelters left them exhausted.

And yet, they worked. They worked because the alternative was to sit and wait for death. They worked because Shackleton told them to work, and his authority was absolute. They worked because the discipline of the expedition, the habits of obedience and routine that had been established on the ship and maintained on the ice, now transferred itself to this barren shore. The ship of men had lost its vessel, but it had not lost its structure.

The boats were hauled higher up the beach, their lashings checked, their covers repaired. The supplies were sorted and stored in piles protected by stones. A perimeter was established, a rough circle of rocks that marked the limits of their camp. Watches were set against both weather and any sign of wildlife that might provide food. The men took turns at every task, sharing the labor as they had shared everything since the expedition began.

—-

But the landing site, which would later be named Cape Valentine after the date of their arrival, was clearly untenable. The beach was too narrow, too exposed. The wind that swept across it had nowhere to go; it funneled between the glacier and the sea, accelerating as it passed. The spray from the waves broke over the shingle, soaking everything within reach. The position was indefensible.

On the second day, Shackleton sent a party to explore the coastline to the west. If there was a better site, they needed to find it before the weather worsened further. The party, led by Wild, clambered over the rocks and ice, fighting the wind at every step. They returned hours later with news of a small point of land, a mile or so along the coast, where the beach was wider and a low cliff offered some protection from the prevailing wind. It was not ideal, but it was better than where they were.

The decision was made to move. The boats would have to be relaunched, rowed along the coast, and landed again. The supplies would have to be carried overland, or stowed in the boats and transported by sea. It would be a major operation, requiring coordination and effort from men who had barely recovered from the voyage that had brought them here. But there was no choice.

The move began on 17 April. The James Caird was launched first, her crew rowing her slowly along the coast while the rest of the party watched from the shore. The boat rounded the point and disappeared from view. An hour later, she returned, having found the new site and confirmed that landing was possible. The Dudley Docker went next, carrying supplies and men. The Stancomb Wills followed.

By evening, the entire party had relocated to the new site. The beach was wider, perhaps a hundred yards long, with a low cliff at its back that broke the force of the wind. There was room to spread out, to establish a proper camp, to create some semblance of shelter. The men set to work immediately, digging into the shingle, building walls of stone, rigging the boat sails as windbreaks. They worked through the night, driven by the knowledge that the storm could return at any moment.

The site would later be named Point Wild, after Frank Wild, the second-in-command who would lead the shore party during the long wait for rescue. But in those first days it had no name—only a place where they had stopped and would try to survive.

Shackleton’s diary recorded the move with characteristic brevity. Relocated to new beach, he wrote. Better position. Still no water source. Still no shelter worthy of the name. But better. The entry for that day ended with a single observation: the boat must go soon.

—-

The boat was the James Caird, and the voyage she would undertake had already begun to take shape in Shackleton’s mind. He had consulted with Worsley, with Crean, with the other veterans of the boat journey. The consensus was that the attempt was possible, but only just. The distance was immense, the conditions were terrible, and the margin for error was nonexistent. But staying on Elephant Island meant certain death for all of them. Going meant that some might survive.

The party that would crew the Caird was selected. Shackleton would lead. Worsley would navigate. Crean, the reliable Irishman who had served with Shackleton on the Discovery expedition and had proven his worth a dozen times over, would be third. Three more men were chosen: McNish, the carpenter, whose skills would be essential if the boat needed repairs; McCarthy, a seaman with experience in small boats; and Vincent, another sailor. Six men in a twenty-two-foot boat, crossing eight hundred miles of the worst ocean in the world.

But before the Caird could leave, the shore party had to be established. The men who stayed would need shelter, food, and a command structure that could maintain order through the long months ahead. Wild was the natural choice to lead. He had been with Shackleton since the Nimrod expedition, and his quiet authority was respected by every man. He would have the difficult task of keeping twenty-two men alive on a barren shore, through an Antarctic winter, with no certainty of rescue.

The days following the relocation were a frenzy of activity. The boats were hauled up the beach and secured with stones and ropes. The supplies were sorted and stored. A proper shelter was constructed, using the two remaining boats—the Dudley Docker and the Stancomb Wills—upended and lashed together, their sails stretched across to create a roof. The structure was crude, but it offered some protection from the wind and snow. The men crowded inside, twenty-two of them, sleeping in rows, their bodies pressed together for warmth.

Water remained the critical shortage. The island had no obvious streams or lakes; ice from the glacier above them was their only source. Parties were sent to chip away at its base, carrying blocks back to camp to be melted—dangerous work on an unstable face where cracking ice sounded constantly overhead.

Food, too, was a constant concern. Orde-Lees calculated that at current rations, they had approximately six weeks of supplies. After that, they would have to find local sources. The island had penguins, and the men watched the shoreline for any sign of them. Seals were a possibility, though none had been seen since the landing. The sea might provide fish, but they had no lines or hooks. They would have to improvise, as they had improvised everything else.

—-

The psychological strain of those first days on Elephant Island was recorded in the diaries with varying degrees of candor. Hurley wrote of the crushing weight of their situation, of the sense that they had escaped the ice only to find themselves trapped in a new and more terrible prison. Macklin noted the quiet desperation that settled over the camp, the way the men moved through their tasks without speaking, the way they avoided each other’s eyes. Orde-Lees, ever the optimist, tried to focus on the practical. They were alive. That was something. The rest they could manage.

But even Orde-Lees, with his storekeeper’s eye for inventory, could not ignore the reality of their position. The supplies were limited. The shelter was inadequate. The boats were damaged. And the weather showed no sign of improving. The wind blew constantly, driving snow and spray across the beach, finding every gap in their defenses. The temperature remained below freezing, and the men huddled together for warmth, their clothes stiff with ice, their hands and feet numb.

Shackleton moved among them, offering words of encouragement, checking on their condition, making sure that the work continued. His presence was a constant, a fixed point in the chaos. The men looked to him for direction, and he gave it. But in his private moments, the Boss felt the weight of their situation. His diary entries from those days are terse, almost cryptic. Must get the boat ready, he wrote on 18 April. No time to lose. And the next day: weather holding. For now. Must go soon.

The James Caird was prepared for her voyage. McNish, the carpenter, worked on her hull, strengthening the planking, caulking the seams, making her as seaworthy as possible. The mast was stepped, the sails checked and repaired. Provisions were loaded: pemmican, biscuits, sugar, milk powder, and a small quantity of water. The boat was low in the water, heavy with supplies and men. She would ride even lower once the six crew members were aboard.

The departure date was set for 24 April. Shackleton wanted to leave sooner, but the weather refused to cooperate. The wind continued to blow from the southwest, exactly the direction they needed to go. To launch in those conditions would be to risk being driven back onto the rocks, or blown out to sea without making any progress. They would have to wait for a shift in the wind, a window of opportunity.

The waiting was its own kind of torture. The men who would stay watched the preparations with mixed emotions. They knew that the boat was their only chance, but they also knew that the men who went would face horrors they could barely imagine. Some felt relief that they had not been chosen. Others felt guilt. All felt fear.

—-

The shore party’s position was still precarious. Point Wild was better than Cape Valentine, but it was still exposed, still vulnerable. The shelter they had built was barely adequate. The supplies they had been left with would last, at best, a few months. If the James Caird did not return by then, they would die.

Shackleton addressed the shore party on 22 April, two days before the scheduled departure. He stood before them, his face weathered by months of exposure, his voice steady despite the wind that whipped at his words. He told them that he would bring help. He told them to hold on, to maintain discipline, to trust in Wild’s leadership. He told them that they would survive, that they would see home again. The men listened, and they believed him. They had no choice.

The final preparations were made. The James Caird was loaded with ballast to improve her stability in the heavy seas they expected to encounter—stones gathered from the beach and stowed in the bottom of the boat. It was an improvisation, like everything else, but it gave the craft a better chance of surviving the waves.

The logbook of the Endurance had been lost with the ship, but Worsley had kept his navigation notes, and these were brought aboard. The chronometer, the sextant, the almanac—all the tools of celestial navigation—were packed with care. Worsley knew that his calculations would determine whether they lived or died. A mistake of a few miles could mean missing South Georgia entirely, and missing South Georgia meant death.

The night before departure, Shackleton wrote a letter to his wife, Emily. He entrusted it to Wild, with instructions that it be sent if the Caird did not return. The letter was never published, but its contents can be imagined. It was a farewell, a testament, a final expression of love and duty.

—-

The morning of 24 April dawned clear, with a wind that had shifted to the northwest. It was not ideal, but it was better than they had had in days. Shackleton made the decision to go. The James Caird was launched, her crew scrambling aboard as she floated free. The shore party stood on the beach, watching as the boat rowed away from the shore, her sail catching the wind, her hull disappearing into the swell.

The party’s fragile, exposed hold on Cape Valentine, with hurricane winds threatening to blow them or their boats back into the sea, had been the opening act of their landfall. Now, as the James Caird vanished over the horizon, the shore party faced a different kind of exposure: the knowledge that their fate was no longer in their own hands.
