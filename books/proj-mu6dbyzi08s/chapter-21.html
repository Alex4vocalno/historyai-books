Chapter 21

“Take Me to My Men”

Seen from above, the whole white expanse between them resolves into a single held breath: seventy miles of fog and loose pack ice, and on either side of it the same pressure, the pressure of Wild’s order to prepare the Stancomb Wills for a final, suicidal voyage if no rescue came by spring, hanging over the camp like a cloud. Zoom out, and that pressure existed in two places at once on 30 August 1916.

Down on the water, aboard the Chilean naval tug Yelcho, Captain Luis Pardo stood on the bridge calculating his position by dead reckoning, his charts showing a coastline he could not see through the murk. Across the ice, in the Snuggery at Point Wild, Frank Wild sat with a scrap of paper, working out how many days remained before he would have to order his men into the Stancomb Wills and point the bow toward the open sea.

The Yelcho had no radio. She had no double hull. She was a small steel tug, borrowed from the Chilean government and pressed into service for a rescue that three better-equipped vessels had already failed to complete. Pardo knew the coordinates of Elephant Island from Shackleton’s briefing, but coordinates were only numbers until a man could put his eyes on the land. The fog pressed against the bridge windows. The loose pack ice grated along the hull, a sound that men who had survived the Endurance knew too well. Pardo had orders to turn back if the ice closed in. He held his course.

Frank Wild’s calculation was simpler and more terrible. The twenty-two men under his command had been living on Elephant Island for 137 days. They had survived the winter in a shelter built from the two remaining boats, overturned and reinforced with stones and snow. They had eaten penguin and seal, boiled down to broth and stretched with whatever they could find. Wild had set a deadline in his own mind: if rescue did not come by early October, when the days grew longer and the sea ice might open, he would launch the Stancomb Wills. The boat could carry only a portion of the party. The others would have to wait, with reduced rations, for a second trip that might never happen.

Wild had not written this down. He had not spoken it aloud. But the men knew. They watched his face when he stood at the water’s edge, scanning the horizon. They saw how he portioned the food, how he counted the days.

The two calculations—Pardo’s navigation and Wild’s deadline—converged on a single point: a narrow strip of beach beneath a glacier’s snout, where twenty-two men waited for a sound that might be the wind or might be an engine.

The Yelcho had sailed from Punta Arenas on 25 August, four days earlier, with Shackleton aboard. The expedition leader had tried three times to reach his men, his first three attempts foiled by sea ice which blocked the approaches to the island, as recorded in the expedition’s official chronology. The Southern Sky, a steel trawler, had been stopped by ice sixty miles from Elephant Island in May. The Instituto de Pesca No. 1, a Uruguayan steamer, had turned back in June when the pack closed around her. The Emma, an old sealer borrowed from a British merchant in Punta Arenas, had made it within sight of the island in July before engine trouble forced her to retreat. Each failure had cost time. Each failure had narrowed the window before winter closed in again.

Shackleton had watched the Emma’s engineer wrestle with the damaged engine, knowing that on Elephant Island, his men were eating the last of their stores. He had appealed to the Chilean government and was offered the use of the Yelcho, a small seagoing tug from the Chilean Navy, as documented in the expedition records.

She was not a proper ice vessel. She was a harbor tug, built for quiet waters and short runs. But she was available, and she was steel, and her captain had agreed to try. Pardo had studied the charts and listened to Shackleton’s description of the island’s approaches. He had taken on extra coal and provisions, understanding that this voyage might be his only chance. The Yelcho had no wireless to call for help if something went wrong. She had no reinforced bow to smash through heavy ice. She had Pardo’s navigation and Shackleton’s will, and on the morning of 30 August, those two things were all that stood between twenty-two men and another winter on the beach.

The fog began to lift at half past ten. Pardo had been steering by compass and guesswork, adjusting his course whenever a glimpse of the sun showed through the overcast. Shackleton stood beside him on the bridge, binoculars ready, watching for the first sign of land. The pack ice had thinned overnight, giving way to loose floes and open water. The Yelcho pushed through, her engine laboring against the cold. At eleven o’clock, the fog parted, and a dark shape rose from the sea ahead.

Elephant Island.

Shackleton lowered his binoculars. He had not seen this coast since April, when the James Caird had pulled away into the open sea, leaving twenty-two men on the beach. He remembered their faces as the boat pulled clear—the forced cheer, the raised hands, the knowledge that some of them might never meet again. He had promised to return. He had promised that no man would be left behind. Now the island stood before him, and somewhere on that shore, his men were waiting.

The Yelcho rounded Cape Valentine and entered the channel between the island and the mainland ice. The water was clear of pack, a miracle in itself for late August. Pardo steered toward a point on the western coast, following Shackleton’s directions. The beach came into view: a narrow strip of shingle beneath a towering wall of ice, where the glacier met the sea. And on that beach, a dark smudge that resolved, as they drew closer, into a hut.

The lookout called out. Figures were moving on the beach. Men were running toward the water, waving their arms. Shackleton raised his binoculars and counted. One, two, five, ten. He kept counting until he reached twenty-two. All of them. All alive.

The relief that flooded through him was not joy. It was something deeper and quieter, a release of tension that he had carried for four months. He had stood on the bridge of three ships before this one, watching the ice close in, knowing that his men were waiting for a rescue that he could not deliver. He had written letters to their families, explaining what had happened, what he was trying to do. He had walked the docks at Punta Arenas, looking for vessels, talking to captains, begging for coal and crews. The weight of those months—the failures, the delays, the knowledge that every day he spent trying again was a day his men went hungry—had settled into his bones. Now, at last, he could see them. They were thin and ragged and their clothes hung from their frames, but they were alive.

The Yelcho dropped anchor a quarter-mile from the beach. Shackleton ordered the whaleboat lowered. He climbed down the ladder and took his place at the oars, pulling toward the shore with a crew of Chilean sailors. The men on the beach had launched their own boat—one of the two remaining whaleboats, pulled up on the shingle all winter and now pushed into the water for the first time in months. The two boats met in the shallows. Shackleton stood up and called out to Wild.

“Are you all well?”

Wild’s voice carried back across the water. “We are all well.”

The words were simple, and they were enough. The whaleboat touched the shingle, and Shackleton stepped ashore. The men surrounded him, pressing forward to shake his hand, to clap his shoulder, to ask questions all at once. He moved through them, taking each man by the hand, looking into his face. They were thinner than he remembered. Their skin was weathered and their eyes had the hollow look of men who had lived on inadequate rations for too long. But they were alive. Every one of them.

The camp that Wild had built stood at the base of a rocky outcrop, sheltered from the wind by the glacier’s snout. The two boats had been turned upside down and reinforced with stones, canvas, and snow to create a long, low shelter. The men had called it the Snuggery. Inside, the space was cramped and dark, lit by a single blubber stove. The smell of seal oil and unwashed bodies hung in the air. Shackleton ducked through the entrance and looked around. The bunks were built from stones and canvas, lined with sleeping bags that had not been dry in months. Provisions were stacked against the walls: tins of Bovril, bags of biscuit, piles of penguin meat. A calendar on the wall showed the days crossed off one by one, a record of the waiting.

The men had been living here for four and a half months. They had come ashore from the three boats in April, exhausted and frozen, after a week of rowing through pack ice and open sea. They had dragged the boats up the beach, built their shelter, and settled in to wait. Wild had taken command in Shackleton’s absence, maintaining the discipline and routine that had kept the party together through the long months on the ice. He had organized hunting parties, cooking rotations, and watch schedules. He had kept the men busy, knowing that idleness was the enemy of morale. And he had made them believe that rescue would come.

The belief had been hard to sustain. The first month passed, then the second. The sun disappeared entirely for weeks, leaving them in a twilight lit only by the moon and the aurora. The temperature dropped below freezing and stayed there. The food stores shrank, and the hunting parties had to go farther from camp to find penguins and seals. The men’s clothes rotted on their bodies, eaten by salt and damp. Their boots fell apart, and they wrapped their feet in canvas and rope. They passed the time with games, with songs, with arguments that flared up and died away. They waited.

Wild had set a deadline in his own mind, though he had not shared it with the men. If rescue did not come by early October, he would launch the Stancomb Wills. The boat could carry only ten men, so they would have to draw lots. The chosen ones would row for the South Shetland Islands, six hundred miles away, in the hope of reaching a whaling station. The odds were not good. The boat was small, the sea was cold, and the navigation would be by dead reckoning. But staying on Elephant Island through another winter was not an option. The food would not last. The shelter would not hold. Wild had watched the days pass on the calendar, calculating how much time remained before he would have to give the order.

Now, standing on the beach beside Shackleton, he could set that calculation aside. The rescue had come. The Yelcho waited in the channel, her engines thrumming, her crew ready to take them off. Wild turned to his men and gave the order: strike camp. Load everything. They would leave within the hour.

The evacuation was chaos, but it was controlled chaos. The men scrambled through the Snuggery, grabbing personal effects and essential supplies. They rolled up their sleeping bags, packed the remaining food, and hauled the provisions down to the beach. The two whaleboats were righted and launched, and the men rowed out to the Yelcho in relays. Shackleton stood on the beach, directing the operation, making sure that no man was left behind. He had promised to return, and he had kept that promise. Now he would keep the other one: to bring them all home.

The physical state of the men told the story of their ordeal. Their faces were gaunt, their cheekbones sharp beneath weathered skin. Their clothes were stiff with salt and grease, patched and repatched until the original fabric was barely visible. Their hands were cracked and swollen, scarred from handling ice and rope and blubber. Some of them could barely walk; they had spent months in the cramped shelter, their muscles atrophying, their joints stiffening. They helped each other down to the beach, leaning on arms and shoulders, laughing at their own weakness.

The emotional state was harder to read. They laughed and joked as they loaded the boats, calling out to each other, shouting across the water to the Yelcho. But beneath the noise, there was a kind of shock. They had lived for so long with the possibility of death that the reality of rescue had not yet sunk in. They moved like men in a dream, going through the motions, waiting to wake up. Only later, when the Yelcho was underway and Elephant Island was shrinking behind them, would the full weight of their deliverance settle in.

Shackleton watched them from the deck of the Yelcho, counting heads as they came aboard. Twenty-two men, all present, all alive. The number matched the count he had carried in his head since April, when he had left them on the beach and turned the James Caird toward South Georgia. He had promised to return. He had promised that no man would be left behind. Now, standing on the deck of a Chilean tug, watching his men help each other below, he could finally let go of the weight he had carried.

Captain Pardo approached him on the bridge. The Chilean had done what three other captains had failed to do. He had navigated through fog and ice, found an island that was barely marked on the charts, and brought his ship to the right place at the right time. Shackleton thanked him. Pardo nodded, accepting the thanks without ceremony. He had his orders, and he had carried them out. The rescue was a matter of professional duty.

The Yelcho weighed anchor and turned toward the open sea. The men lined the rails, watching Elephant Island shrink into the mist. Some of them cheered. Some of them were silent. The island had been their prison and their refuge, the place where they had waited and worked and wondered if they would ever leave. Now they were leaving. The beach where they had built the Snuggery was empty. The two boats were gone, hauled aboard the Yelcho and lashed to the deck. The only sign that anyone had ever been there was the pile of stones where the shelter had stood, and the bones of the seals and penguins they had eaten.

Frank Wild stood beside Shackleton at the rail. The two men had known each other for years, serving together on the Nimrod expedition and now on this one. Wild had been Shackleton’s second-in-command, the man he trusted to hold the party together while he went for help. That trust had been justified. Wild had kept them alive, kept them disciplined, kept them believing that rescue would come. Now he stood on the deck of the rescue ship, watching the island disappear, and let out a breath he felt he had been holding for four months.

Shackleton turned to him. “I thought you’d be thinner,” he said.

Wild smiled. “I thought you’d be taller.”

The joke was an old one, a bit of banter that went back to their days on the Nimrod. It broke the tension, and the two men laughed. Then Shackleton grew serious again. He had a question he needed to ask, though he already knew the answer. He had counted the men as they came aboard. He had seen their faces, their bodies, their eyes. He knew that all twenty-two were alive. But he needed to hear Wild say it.

“How many did we lose?”

Wild looked at him. “None,” he said. “We lost none.”

The words were simple, and they were the words that Shackleton had traveled eight hundred miles in the James Caird to hear. He had crossed the Southern Ocean in an open boat, climbed the mountains of South Georgia, and tried three times to reach Elephant Island in borrowed ships. He had begged for coal and crews, negotiated with governments and whaling companies, and watched the ice close in around vessel after vessel. Through all of it, he had carried the knowledge that twenty-two men were waiting for him on a beach at the edge of the world. If he failed, they would die. If he succeeded, they would live. Now, standing on the deck of the Yelcho, he could finally say that he had succeeded.

The voyage back to Punta Arenas took four days. The men crowded into the Yelcho’s small hold, sleeping on the deck and on each other, eating the food that the Chilean crew provided. They were weak and stiff, but they were alive. Shackleton moved among them, talking to each man, asking about his experience, making sure that everyone was accounted for. He was the captain again, the leader who had brought them through. But there was something different in his manner now. The urgency was gone. The desperate drive that had pushed him across the Southern Ocean and up the mountains of South Georgia had been replaced by a kind of quiet satisfaction. He had done what he set out to do. He had brought his men home.

When the Yelcho docked at Punta Arenas on 3 September, the town turned out to meet them. The news had spread by wireless that the rescue had succeeded, and the crowds lined the wharf to see the men who had survived the impossible. The Chilean government hosted a reception. The British residents gave speeches. The press sent dispatches around the world. Shackleton stood at the center of it all, accepting the congratulations, thanking the people who had helped.

But his mind was already moving on. There were still men unaccounted for—the Ross Sea party, on the other side of the continent, had been laying depots for a crossing that never came. They did not know that the Endurance had been crushed, that the Weddell Sea party had been stranded, that Shackleton had crossed the ocean in an open boat. They might still be waiting, or they might be dead. The rescue of the Elephant Island party was not the end of the expedition. It was only the end of one chapter.

A journalist pushed through the crowd and asked Shackleton for a statement. The expedition leader thought for a moment, then turned to look at his men. They were gathered in a group behind him, watching, waiting to hear what he would say. He had led them through the ice, across the ocean, over the mountains, and back again. He had kept them alive when everything had gone wrong. Now he had brought them home.

“Take me to my men,” he said.

The journalist did not understand. Shackleton gestured toward the group behind him. “These are my men,” he said. “Take me to them.”

He walked away from the cameras and the crowds, toward the twenty-two men who had waited for him on Elephant Island. They had crossed the ice together, rowed the boats together, lived through the winter together. Now they stood on a wharf at the edge of the world, alive because they had refused to give up, alive because their leader had refused to leave them behind.

The Yelcho waited at anchor in the harbor, her engines cold, her crew resting after the voyage. She had done what she was built to do: she had brought her passengers safely home. The empty beach on Elephant Island was already disappearing under the snow, the pile of stones that marked the Snuggery buried by the next storm. The island had no monument, no marker, no sign that twenty-two men had once lived there. But the men were gone, and the ship that had rescued them was gone, and the story of their survival was already spreading around the world.

On the other side of Antarctica, the Ross Sea party did not know that the Weddell Sea party had been saved.
