Chapter 28

A Defeat That Was a Victory

Seen from above, the metropolis spread out in a grey patchwork of chimneys and rooftops, the Thames winding through its center like a bruised ribbon. London in 1921 was a city of diminished expectations, its streets crowded with men who had returned from the Great War to find that the victory they had been promised had somehow failed to deliver—men who understood, as the crew of the Quest had come to understand on the long steam north from Elephant Island, that the geography of maybe does not end at the ice edge but follows a man home. The war had cost Britain dearly, leaving the nation’s treasury emptied and its confidence shaken, its appetite for glory significantly diminished.

The financial district operated at a pace that felt frantic and hollow at once. Demobilized soldiers stood on corners in civilian clothes, waiting for work that was not there, waiting for a future that seemed to have been used up. In this atmosphere of strained recovery and collective weariness, the figure of the polar explorer cut a strange and anachronistic silhouette—a man whose greatest achievements had come in the years before the world had broken apart.

The office at 14 Bedford Street was small and cluttered, its windows looking out onto a street that had seen better days. Sir Ernest Shackleton sat at a desk covered with unpaid bills, promotional pamphlets, and correspondence that grew more urgent with each passing week. The letterhead on the stationery announced the Shackleton-Rowett Expedition, and the promises made in the text below spoke of scientific discovery, of geographic achievement, of a return to the southern seas that would restore British prestige in the arena of polar exploration.

But the papers on the desk told a different story. The creditors were pressing. The backers were cautious. The ship that had been purchased—a small, elderly sealer renamed the Quest—was not the vessel that a man would choose if he had the luxury of choice. She was available, and she was affordable, and those two qualities had become the determining factors in what had once been an enterprise driven by ambition and imagination.

The years since the rescue of the Endurance party had not been kind to Shackleton.

He had returned to a country that was preoccupied with war, a country that had little attention to spare for the remarkable story of survival that had played out on the other side of the world. The publication of his book South had brought in some revenue, but not enough to clear the debts that had accumulated during the years of the expedition and its aftermath. The lecture tours had been grueling and often unrewarding, audiences distracted by the news from the front, their minds elsewhere even as they sat in the halls and listened to the tale of the ice and the boats and the journey across the mountains of South Georgia.

He had attempted other ventures—a plan to transport troops to Russia, a scheme for mineral extraction in the Arctic—but none had succeeded in providing the financial stability that he needed, and none had satisfied the restlessness that seemed to have taken up permanent residence in his chest.

The organizing of the Quest expedition was not, as the promotional materials would have it, a bold new chapter in the history of exploration. It was an act of compulsion—a return to the only arena in which Shackleton had ever felt fully himself.

The Antarctic had broken him, had crushed his ship, had forced him to watch as his dreams of continental crossing dissolved into the ice. But it had also made him. The very failure that had defined the Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition had become, in the strange alchemy of public memory, a kind of triumph—a story of leadership and survival that had captured the imagination of those who had followed it from afar.

Roald Amundsen, the man who had reached the South Pole first, had written in a letter to the Royal Geographical Society’s secretary John Scott Keltie that the English nation had by Shackleton’s deed won a victory which never could be surpassed. The irony was sharp: Shackleton had failed in his stated objective, had accomplished none of the goals he had set for himself, and yet had somehow produced from that failure a narrative that rivaled, in the public estimation, the achievements of those who had actually reached their destinations.

But public admiration did not pay bills, and it did not fill the emptiness that had settled into Shackleton’s frame during the years of waiting and wandering since his return from the south. The war had taken something from him, as it had from everyone, and the years of inactivity that followed had only deepened the sense that his real life lay somewhere behind him, in the ice that had held him and then released him. The Quest was not a fresh start. It was a pilgrimage.

The ship herself was modest, a hundred and twenty feet of Norwegian-built timber, designed for sealing work in the northern waters, now pressed into service for a voyage that would take her to the opposite end of the globe.

She was not the Endurance. She was not even the Nimrod. She was what was available, and what could be afforded, and Shackleton had convinced himself that she would be sufficient for the work that lay ahead.

The work itself was vaguely defined—a circumnavigation of the Antarctic continent, scientific observations, perhaps a search for certain islands that were rumored to exist in the waters below Cape Horn. The expedition had been funded by John Rowett, a businessman from the West Country who had known Shackleton in earlier years and who had agreed, after some persuasion, to provide the capital necessary to fit out the vessel and pay the crew. It was not the kind of backing that Shackleton had commanded in earlier days, but it was enough, and enough had become the standard by which all things were now measured.

The crew that assembled at the London docks in September 1921 was a mixture of old hands and new faces. Frank Wild, who had been with Shackleton through the entire Endurance saga, who had held the men together on Elephant Island through the long months of waiting, had agreed to come along as second-in-command. There was something almost inevitable about Wild’s presence—he had followed Shackleton before, and would follow him again, the loyalty between the two men having been forged in circumstances that no civilian could fully understand.

Others from the earlier expedition had also signed on: Alexander Macklin, the surgeon who had tended the men through the ordeal on the ice; Leonard Hussey, whose banjo had provided the only music in the camp at Elephant Island; James McIlroy, another of the doctors who had served under Shackleton’s command.

Their presence was not merely professional. It was personal. They had been through something together, and the bond that had formed in the ice had not been broken by the return to civilization.

The departure from London came on September 17, 1921, the Quest steaming down the Thames and out into the English Channel, past the white cliffs of Dover and into the open sea. The crowds that had gathered to see the ship off were smaller than those that had lined the docks for the departure of the Endurance seven years earlier. The war had changed things. The public appetite for adventure had been diminished by the realities of the trenches, by the millions of dead, by the sense that enough young men had already been sent to die in distant places. But there were still those who came to watch, who stood at the railings and waved as the ship moved away from the shore, who felt something stir in their chests at the sight of a vessel setting out for the unknown.

Shackleton stood on the bridge, watching the coastline recede. He was forty-seven years old, and his hair had gone grey, and his face bore the marks of the years that had passed since he had last steamed south. But his eyes were fixed on the horizon, on the direction that would take him back to the ice, back to the continent that had consumed so much of his life and had given him, in return, a fame that had not brought him peace.

The voyage south was uneventful at first, the Quest making her way down the eastern Atlantic, stopping at Madeira and then at Cape Verde, the crew settling into the routines of shipboard life. But there were signs, for those who knew how to look, that something was not right. Shackleton’s health had been a concern even before the expedition departed. He had suffered from what he called attacks in the years since the war—episodes of pain and breathlessness that he had dismissed as the result of overwork and strain. He had been examined by doctors, who had warned him about the state of his heart, who had advised him to rest, to avoid exertion, to live quietly. He had ignored them. The expedition was his life, and he would see it through, whatever the cost.

The ship reached Rio de Janeiro in late November, and it was there that the first serious crisis occurred. Shackleton collapsed, the pain in his chest so severe that he could not stand. The ship’s doctor, Alexander Macklin, examined him and found a pulse that was irregular, a heart that was struggling to maintain its rhythm. Macklin advised rest, advised a return to England, advised against continuing the voyage. Shackleton refused. He had come too far, had worked too hard to assemble this expedition, to turn back now. The Quest would continue south, and he would be aboard her, whatever the consequences.

South Georgia appeared on the horizon on January 4, 1922, the mountains of the island rising from the sea like the walls of a fortress. It was a place that Shackleton knew well, a place that held memories of triumph and of terror. He had stood on these shores six years earlier, after the crossing of the island that had saved his men, after the journey that had entered into legend. He had returned to South Georgia once before, during the rescue attempts that had finally brought the men of Elephant Island home. But this was different. This was a return not as a rescuer, but as a pilgrim, coming back to the place where he had achieved the impossible, where he had walked out of the wilderness and found salvation.

The Quest anchored in the harbor at Grytviken on the evening of January 4, the lights of the whaling station reflecting on the dark water. Shackleton came on deck to look at the shore, to see the buildings where he had found help, the paths that he had walked. He was moved, according to those who were with him, by the sight, his eyes filling with tears as he recalled what had happened here, what this place had meant to him and to the men whose lives had depended on his ability to find a way across the mountains and back again.

That night, Shackleton wrote in his diary. The entry was brief, almost cryptic, noting the wonder of the evening and the sight of a lone star hovering above the bay. It was the last entry he would ever make.

January 5, 1922, began like any other day aboard the Quest. The crew rose to their duties, the ship’s routines proceeding as they had throughout the voyage. Shackleton remained in his cabin, still weak from the journey, still struggling with the condition that had plagued him for years. Macklin visited him in the morning, checking his pulse, listening to his heart, noting the irregularities that had become familiar. They spoke about the expedition, about the plans for the days ahead, about the work that remained to be done.

At some point during the conversation, Shackleton asked Macklin about his own health, about how the doctor thought he was doing. Macklin was honest. He told Shackleton that he needed rest, that he needed to take better care of himself, that the strain of the expedition was taking a toll that his body could not indefinitely sustain.

Shackleton listened, and then he spoke. The words that Macklin would remember for the rest of his life were a question: what did the doctor want him to give up now? It was a question that contained within it the entire history of Shackleton’s life—the refusal to surrender, the insistence on continuing, the belief that there was always one more expedition, one more voyage, one more chance to prove himself against the ice.

He had given up so much already. He had given up his fortune, his health, his years. He had given up the chance at a normal life, a settled existence, the comforts that came with staying home. What more could anyone ask him to give?

The conversation ended, and Macklin left the cabin to attend to his other duties. It was the last time he would speak to Shackleton alive.

In the early hours of January 5, a noise was heard coming from Shackleton’s cabin. Members of the crew rushed to investigate, and they found him on the floor, unconscious, his breathing labored and irregular. Macklin was summoned, and he did what he could, but it was clear that the situation was beyond the help that was available on the ship. The heart that had carried Shackleton through the ice, through the boats, through the mountains, through the years of struggle and effort, had finally given out. At 2: 50 in the morning, Ernest Shackleton was pronounced dead. He was forty-seven years old.

The news spread through the ship with the speed that such news always travels, passing from mouth to mouth in whispers that grew into a general understanding. The Boss was gone. The man who had led them through the impossible, who had brought his men home from the edge of the world, had died in his cabin, within sight of the shores that had witnessed his greatest triumph. It was an ending that no one had expected, and yet, in retrospect, it seemed almost inevitable. The ice had held him, and then it had released him, and now it had claimed him after all.

The decision about what to do with Shackleton’s body was not one that could be made by the crew alone. A message was sent to London, to the expedition’s backers and to Shackleton’s family, asking for instructions. The response came back several days later, after a consultation with Lady Shackleton. She requested that her husband be buried on South Georgia, the island that had been the scene of his greatest achievement, the place where he had completed the journey that had saved his men. It was a decision that transformed the ending of Shackleton’s life into a kind of permanent return, an anchoring of his legacy to the landscape that had defined him.

The funeral was held on March 5, 1922, after the Quest had returned to South Georgia from a brief continuation of the expedition under Wild’s command. The service was simple, attended by the crew of the ship and by the men who worked at the whaling station, who had seen Shackleton pass through six years earlier and who understood what his return meant. The coffin was carried to the cemetery at Grytviken, a small plot of ground that looked out over the harbor and the mountains beyond. The service was read, the words spoken, and then the body of Sir Ernest Shackleton was lowered into the ground, there to remain.

The epitaph that was chosen came from the poem that Shackleton had quoted throughout his life, the lines that had sustained him through the darkest moments of the Endurance saga: that a man should strive to the last breath, from the moment of his birth to the moment of his death. It was a statement of belief, a credo that had guided every decision he had made, every risk he had taken, every expedition he had launched. And now it marked the place where he lay, a permanent testimony to the life he had lived.

The death of Shackleton transformed the narrative of the Endurance expedition in ways that were not immediately apparent. In the months and years that followed, the story of the ice and the boats and the mountains took on a quality that it had not possessed while its protagonist was still alive. The survival of the twenty-eight men, remarkable as it was, became part of a larger arc that now included the death of their leader on the shores of South Georgia. The tale of rescue became a tale of sacrifice. The victory that had been won against the ice became a victory that had been purchased at a cost that was still being calculated.

Frank Wild, who had taken command of the Quest after Shackleton’s death, completed the voyage as best he could, fulfilling what he understood to be the intentions of his fallen leader. The ship returned to England in 1922, its cargo of scientific observations and geographic data a modest return on the investment that had been made. But the real cargo was the story itself, the narrative that had now reached its conclusion in the cemetery at Grytviken. Wild would never return to the Antarctic. He had given enough, and the ice had taken enough, and there was nothing left to prove.

The public reception of Shackleton’s death was muted, in part because the world had moved on from the era of heroic exploration. The war had changed the calculus of heroism, had raised questions about the value of individual sacrifice that had not been asked so insistently before. The men who had died in the trenches had died in numbers that made the losses of polar exploration seem almost modest by comparison. And yet there was something about Shackleton’s end that resonated, that spoke to a quality of leadership and determination that had not been entirely extinguished by the cynicism of the postwar years.

The newspapers reported the death with respect, noting the achievements of the explorer’s career and the circumstances of his passing. The Times of London observed that a striking personality had been removed from the roll of British explorers. The Manchester Guardian, which had covered the Endurance saga extensively, noted that the story of his last expedition would be read with melancholy interest. There was no hysteria, no outpouring of national grief comparable to what had followed the death of Scott and his companions a decade earlier. The world had changed too much for that.

But the story did not end with the funeral. In the years that followed, the narrative of the Endurance expedition continued to grow, to take on new meanings, to speak to new generations in ways that Shackleton himself could not have anticipated. The survival of the twenty-eight men became a touchstone for discussions of leadership, of crisis management, of the human capacity to endure. The decisions that Shackleton had made—the abandonment of the ship, the march across the ice, the voyage of the James Caird, the crossing of South Georgia—were analyzed and dissected, held up as examples of what a leader should do when everything is at stake.

The counter-argument persisted, as counter-arguments do. There were those who maintained that Shackleton’s leadership had been brilliant, that his instinctive decisions had saved his men, that his force of personality had bent fortune and nature to his will. And there was truth in this. But there was also truth in the larger picture: that the survival of the party had been a collective achievement, a victory of the social organism that the expedition had become, a triumph of cooperation and discipline over circumstances that should have destroyed them all. Shackleton had led, but he had not led alone. The men had followed, and in following, they had made the leadership possible.

The record showed that Shackleton had, by his own admission, broken down during the Nimrod expedition of 1907–1909, that he had been unable to carry out his share of the work on the return journey, that he had been in a severely weakened state and had required assistance from his companions. He had denied Scott’s claim, in The Voyage of the Discovery, that he had been carried on the sledge. But the weakness had been real. The strain had been genuine. The leader who had saved his men on the Endurance expedition was also the man who had been saved by his companions on an earlier journey. The relationship between leader and led was not a one-way street. It was a reciprocal arrangement, a mutual dependence that became, in the crucible of the ice, the difference between life and death.

The legacy of the Endurance expedition, then, was not simply a story of heroic leadership. It was a story of human endurance, of the capacity of a group of men to survive together what none of them could have survived alone. The ice had held them, and in holding them, it had forced them to become something more than twenty-eight individuals. It had forced them to become a single organism, a body that thought and moved and suffered as one. The leadership that Shackleton provided was essential to this process, but it was not sufficient. The men had to follow. They had to trust. They had to give themselves over to the collective enterprise, to surrender their individual fates to the judgment of the group. And they did.

The death of Shackleton on South Georgia, within sight of the shores he had struggled to reach six years earlier, completed the arc of the expedition in a way that no fiction could have improved upon. The man who had brought his men home from the edge of the world had come back to the place where the rescue had been launched, and there he had died, his heart giving out in the harbor that had witnessed both his greatest triumph and his final defeat. The failure of the Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition—the fact that the crossing had never been accomplished, that the continent had never been traversed, that the stated objective of the voyage had been beyond reach—this failure had been transformed, through the survival of the men and the death of their leader, into something else entirely.

The cemetery at Grytviken overlooks the harbor, and the mountains rise behind it, and the waters that stretch out toward the horizon are the same waters that Shackleton crossed in the James Caird, the same waters that he navigated by the stars and by the force of his own determination. The grave is marked by a simple headstone, and the epitaph speaks of striving to the last breath.

The leader who had failed in his stated objective was laid to rest in the ground that he had crossed in the moment of his greatest triumph. The survival of the twenty-eight men was a victory of the social organism, of the collective will, of the bonds that had formed in the crucible of extremity. Shackleton had led, but the men had followed, and together they had achieved something that neither could have achieved alone. The ice had held them. And in the end, it held their leader as well.
