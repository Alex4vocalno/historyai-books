Chapter 6

The World Contracted to a Ship’s Beam

The men did not know how long the drift would last. But by the first week of February 1915, they knew that it would not end soon. On 17 February, the sun had dipped below the horizon at midnight, showing the end of the Antarctic summer. On 24 February, Shackleton realized that they would be held in the ice throughout the winter and ordered the ship’s routine abandoned, with regular watches cancelled and the Endurance now functioning as a shore station.

The wardroom of the Endurance had been transformed into something between a lecture hall and a music hall. The long table, where the officers had taken their meals since departing South Georgia, now served as a stage. On the evening of 6 February, the entire ship’s company—officers, scientists, seamen, and stokers—crowded into the space to hear what Shackleton had called, with deliberate formality, the first “Saturday Night Concert.” The gramophone played. Reginald James, the physicist, gave a talk on stars. The carpenter, McNish, told a story in his thick Scots brogue that drew laughter even from men who could barely understand him. The evening ended with the entire company singing “The Flag of Britain” while Frank Hurley, the photographer, snapped photographs by flash powder. The scene had the air of a village fête held in a wooden box suspended in white silence.

Shackleton had ordered the concert series with purpose. The ship was beset. The engines were silent. The boilers had been extinguished on 17 January, committing the vessel to drift with the ice until released naturally or destroyed by pressure. There would be no landing at Vahsel Bay, no march across the continent, no rendezvous with the Ross Sea party on the other side. The Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition had become, for the present, an experiment in stationary living. The Boss, as the men called him, understood what the men themselves did not yet fully grasp: that the enemy now was not the ice but time, and that time would corrode discipline and hope if given nothing to work upon.

The concert on 6 February was the visible manifestation of a system already taking shape. Work details had been assigned. Watch schedules had been established. Scientific observations were being recorded at fixed hours. Meals were served at fixed times. The ship’s routine, which had governed life under sail and steam, now governed life in stillness. The world had contracted to a ship’s beam, and within that contracted world, Shackleton was building a society.

The scientific program provided the skeleton of daily existence. Every morning at eight, the meteorologist climbed to the bridge to read the instruments and record the temperature, wind speed, and barometric pressure. The observations were routine, the kind of work that could be performed by habit, and that was precisely their value.

The readings gave the day a structure. At noon, the navigator, Worsley, or his deputy, took a sight with the sextant to determine the ship’s position—if the sun was visible, which it often was not. The results were posted in the wardroom. The men read them with interest, tracking the drift north or south, east or west, as if the numbers meant something.

In truth, the numbers meant nothing. The ship went where the ice took her, and the ice took her where the Weddell Sea’s gyre dictated. But the act of measurement, of recording, of posting, gave the illusion of progress, of purpose, of control.

The other scientists found their own rhythms. James worked on his magnetic observations, taking readings from the instruments housed in a specially constructed hut on the ice nearby. The biologist dissected specimens and preserved them in jars, though the supply of specimens had dwindled since the ship stopped moving. The geologist sorted rock samples and wrote up his notes from the landings at South Georgia and the brief stops before besetment. There was little geological work to do on a ship locked in pack ice, but the geologist found tasks to fill his hours, and his quiet industry set an example.

The seamen and stokers, men accustomed to physical labor, were assigned work that would have seemed make-believe if the consequences had not been so real. Each morning, a party went onto the ice to exercise the dogs. The forty-nine dogs, housed in kennels built on the ice alongside the ship, needed to be fed, watered, and run. The dog-handling teams competed with each other, informally at first and then in organized trials that Shackleton encouraged. The competitions gave the men something to talk about, something to bet on, something to care about beyond the walls of the ship. The dogs themselves became personalities in the small world of the expedition. Their names—Shakespeare, Samson, Soldier, Sue—were known to every man aboard. When a dog died, and dogs did die, the loss was felt.

Other work details addressed the practical necessities of indefinite besetment. The carpenter, McNish, and his assistants built structures on the ice: a storehouse for supplies, a shelter for the magnetic instruments, a kennel for the dogs. The men hauled stores from the hold and stacked them on the floes, redistributing weight in case the ship had to be abandoned quickly. They dug ice from the forepeak and stowed it for fresh water. They repaired sledging gear and tested it on short excursions across the pack. The work was real enough, but it was also a kind of theater, a performance of purpose in a situation where purpose had been stripped away.

Shackleton’s own movements through this miniature society were deliberate. He made a point of appearing at the morning muster, of walking through the mess deck where the ratings ate, of speaking to men by name. He asked after their families, their homes, their hopes. He listened to their complaints and offered small concessions where he could. When a dispute arose between two of the seamen, he settled it quickly and privately, before it could fester. When a man showed signs of despondency, Shackleton found a task for him, a responsibility, a reason to get out of his bunk in the morning. The Boss was everywhere, and his presence was the presence of authority, but it was also the presence of care.

The contrast between Shackleton’s public manner and his private understanding was lost on no one who observed him closely. In his own account, written later, he described the early weeks of the drift as a period of preparation, of making the best of things. But the diaries of the men tell a more complicated story. Frank Worsley, the captain, noted in his journal that Shackleton had accepted the failure of the original plan with characteristic resilience, but also that he had been seen standing at the rail for long periods, staring out at the ice, saying nothing. The Boss was thinking, and what he was thinking was that the expedition had become a waiting game, and that waiting games were the hardest kind to win.

The Saturday night concerts continued. On 13 February, the second concert featured a performance on the banjo by the meteorologist, who played with more enthusiasm than skill, and a recitation by Thomas Orde-Lees, the storekeeper, of a comic monologue he had memorized from a magazine. Orde-Lees was an odd figure, a former Royal Marine with a taste for practical jokes and a habit of complaining in his diary about the food, the cold, the other men, and Shackleton. His entry for 13 February noted that his performance had gone well enough, though he thought the banjo playing was rather rough. The Boss seemed pleased, which was the main thing. Orde-Lees’s grievances, petty and persistent, were themselves a kind of stability. In a world where nothing changed, his complaints were a reliable feature of the landscape.

Frank Hurley’s diary told a different story. The photographer, a man of artistic temperament and relentless energy, found beauty in the ice and purpose in recording it. His entries for February 1915 described the play of light on the floes, the forms of pressure ridges, the colors of the sky at sunset. Hurley spent his days on the ice, hauling his heavy plate camera, setting up shots, waiting for the light. He photographed the ship from every angle. His images, developed in the darkroom he had set up in the ship’s hold, were shown to the men and discussed with the same attention given to the noon position readings. The photographs gave the drift a history, a narrative, a meaning.

The ship’s position on 22 February was 76° 58′S, the most southerly latitude she would reach. Thereafter, the drift carried her northward, away from the continent, away from Vahsel Bay, away from the planned landing. The men noted the change in the posted positions and understood what it meant. They would not break free this season. They would winter in the ice. On 24 February, Shackleton called the officers together and informed them, formally, that the ship would be held throughout the winter. The winter would be passed in the pack. The trans-continental crossing would not take place. The expedition’s objectives had been defeated by the Weddell Sea.

The announcement was not a surprise. The men had watched the drift, had seen the ice thicken around them, had felt the cold deepen. But the formal statement, delivered by the leader in the wardroom, marked a turning point. The period of suspended anxiety, of hoping for a break-up, was over. A new phase had begun. The ship’s routine, which had been a temporary expedient, would now become a permanent way of life.

Shackleton ordered the ship’s routine reorganized for winter. The watch schedule was adjusted. The work details were reassigned. The scientific program was expanded, with observations taken at more frequent intervals. The Saturday night concerts were made a permanent fixture, with a roster of performers and a schedule of events. The dog competitions were formalized, with prizes awarded for speed, obedience, and endurance. The men were encouraged to take exercise on the ice, to play football matches on the floes, to keep their bodies and minds active.

The reorganization was a feat of social engineering. Shackleton understood that the greatest threat to the expedition was not cold, not hunger, not the ice itself, but the erosion of morale. A ship’s company, confined for months in a small space, with no prospect of relief, would inevitably develop tensions, grievances, factions. The discipline that held them together would fray. The hope that sustained them would dim. His task, as leader, was to slow that erosion, to manage the decline, to keep the social fabric intact until the ice released them or until something else happened.

The something else was the ice itself. The pack was not a passive prison. It moved, it shifted, it pressed. Pressure ridges formed and reformed. Floes cracked and split. The sounds of the ice—the groans, the cracks, the explosions like artillery—were a constant accompaniment to life aboard. The men grew accustomed to them, or thought they did. But every sound was a reminder that the ice was alive, that it could turn at any moment.

On 17 February, the sun had dipped below the horizon for the first time since the ship entered the pack. The long Antarctic night was beginning, though the full darkness was still months away. The twilight would linger, the days would shorten, and then the sun would disappear entirely. The men watched the light change and felt the cold deepen. The temperature dropped. The ice thickened. The world contracted further.

The dogs felt the change. They grew thicker coats, huddled in their kennels, moved less. The men, exercising them on the ice, noticed the difference and reported it in their diaries. The dog-handling competitions continued, but the tone had changed. The men were not just exercising the dogs; they were preparing them, and themselves, for harder times.

The supply situation was stable but finite. The stores brought aboard at South Georgia had been calculated for a trans-continental march, not for an indefinite drift. Shackleton ordered a review of the provisions, and Orde-Lees, as storekeeper, conducted it with meticulous care. The results were posted in the wardroom: flour, biscuits, pemmican, tea, sugar, dried vegetables, canned meat. The quantities were sufficient for many months if husbanded carefully. But they were not infinite. The expedition was living on capital, and the capital would eventually run out.

The rationing began. Portions were reduced. Meals were simplified. The men complained, some of them, in their diaries. Orde-Lees, who had a talent for complaint, noted that the tea was weaker, the biscuits fewer, the meat tougher. But he also noted that no one went hungry, and that the Boss was seen eating the same rations as everyone else. The fairness of the distribution was itself a form of management. Shackleton made sure that the officers received no more than the men, and that he himself received no more than the officers. The discipline of the table matched the discipline of the watch.

The entertainment program expanded. In addition to the Saturday concerts, there were lectures, debates, reading groups. The ship’s library, a collection of books donated by supporters and purchased before departure, was opened to all hands. The men read novels, histories, travel accounts. They discussed what they read. The physicist James gave a series of lectures on astronomy, illustrated with diagrams drawn on the wardroom blackboard. The biologist lectured on penguins, drawing on his observations from the brief landings. The geologist spoke on the formation of glaciers, a subject of immediate relevance.

The lectures were popular, but the concerts remained the highlight of the week. The third concert, on 20 February, featured a skit written by the surgeon Alexander Macklin, in which several of the officers pretended to be explorers encountering a polar bear. The bear was played by the second officer, Tom Crean, in a costume made of blankets and furs. The men laughed until they were weak. The laughter was a release, a moment of genuine pleasure in a world of monotony. Shackleton watched from the back of the wardroom, smiling, and later told Macklin that it was the best medicine they had.

The diaries of the men reveal the texture of life in these weeks. Hurley wrote of beauty, Orde-Lees of grievance, Macklin of the dogs he tended as part of his duties. The seamen, whose diaries are fewer, wrote of work and weather and the small incidents of shipboard life. The cook, Charles Green, recorded the meals he prepared and the challenges of cooking in a cold ship with limited supplies. The engineer, Lewis Rickinson, noted the state of the engines, which were silent but needed to be maintained in case of release. Each man had his perspective, his preoccupations, his way of passing the time.

What emerges from these records is a picture of a society functioning, barely, under pressure. The pressure was not yet extreme. The ship was intact, the stores were adequate, the cold was bearable. But the pressure was there, in the background, in the knowledge that the ice might never release them, that the supplies might run out, that something might break. The men did not speak of these fears, most of them, but the fears were present, and they shaped the way the men lived.

Shackleton’s leadership in this period was defined by his attention to detail. He did not make grand speeches or issue dramatic orders. He managed. He walked the ship, talked to the men, observed the dogs, checked the stores, attended the concerts, laughed at the jokes, and kept his own counsel. His diary entries for February 1915 are brief, factual, almost impersonal. The brevity was deliberate. The Boss was not a man to commit his fears to paper.

The men responded to his leadership in different ways. Some, like Worsley and Crean, were loyal without question. Others, like Orde-Lees, were loyal but critical. Still others, like McNish, were loyal but independent, men who would do their jobs but who reserved the right to complain. The mix of personalities was a potential source of conflict, and Shackleton managed it by giving each man a role, a place in the system. The system was the thing—the daily rhythm of work and rest, of observation and record, of concert and lecture—that held the men together when the ice held them apart from the world.

The first of March brought no change. The ship drifted northward, slowly, at the pace of the pack. The temperature continued to drop. The light continued to fade. The routine continued, day after day, as it would for weeks and months to come. The men rose, worked, ate, exercised, attended lectures and concerts, wrote in their diaries, and slept. The ship was a world, and the world was a routine.

But the routine was fragile. It depended on the ice remaining stable, on the ship remaining intact, on the supplies holding out, on the men remaining disciplined. Any of these could fail, and if one failed, the others would follow. Shackleton knew this, and he watched for signs of failure with the attention of a man monitoring a slow leak. The pressure ridges that formed in the distance, the cracks that appeared in the floes, the sounds that echoed through the hull at night—these were the signs he read, and the signs told him that the ice was not passive, that the prison was alive, that the drift was not a waiting game but a slow-motion crisis.

The crisis would come. The ice would turn. The ship would be tested. But that was in the future, and in the present, there was the routine, the concerts, the lectures, the dog competitions, the meals, the watches, the diaries. There was the ship of men, floating in the white silence, holding together by habit and discipline and the will of the Boss.

On 1 March, Shackleton wrote in his diary a brief entry noting that all was well, that routine was established, that men were in good spirits. The man who wrote it was thinking of the months ahead, of the winter, of the spring, of the ice. He was thinking of the Ross Sea party on the other side of the continent, laying depots for a crossing that would never happen. He was thinking of the families of the men, waiting for news that would not come. He was thinking of the expedition’s sponsors, of the Royal Geographical Society, of the Admiralty, of the commitments he had made and the promises he might have to break. He was thinking of failure, and of survival, and of the difference between them.

The difference was the men. The twenty-eight men on the ship, and the ten men on the other side of the continent, and the men who had turned back, and the men who had never sailed. The expedition was a social organism, and its survival depended on the strength of the organism, not on the strength of any individual. Shackleton understood this, and he acted on it. The routine, the concerts, the lectures, the dog competitions—these were not entertainment. They were maintenance. They were the means by which the organism kept itself alive.

The ice held them. The ice would hold them for ten months, through the winter and into the spring, through the darkness and into the light, through the cold and into the colder. The ice would hold them until it decided to release them or destroy them. And in the meantime, they would hold themselves, by routine and discipline and the will to endure.

The pattern of life was established on a fragile premise: that the ice would remain a passive prison. This managed stability awaited its violent test.
