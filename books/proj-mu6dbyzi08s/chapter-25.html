Chapter 25

A Question of Gold and Coal

Seen from above, the metropolis still presented its familiar shape: the Thames winding past the docks and wharves, the dome of St. Paul’s rising above rooftops blackened by soot, the railway lines threading outward toward suburbs and beyond. This aerial view, however, concealed the specific weariness of a city three years into a war that now seemed endless. From the street level, London in the autumn of 1917 wore the grey exhaustion of that unending conflict. The effort showed in every drawn face, in every queue, and in every ration card. Women in mourning stood at tram stops; recruitment posters flapped from hoardings with desperate exhortations; shops carried less stock at higher costs unimagined before August 1914. The city was still functioning, still feeding itself, still moving its millions through the streets each day.

In a solicitor’s office off the Strand, a ledger lay open on a desk. The book was thick, its pages densely filled with figures in neat columns, and the man who had been writing in it had paused to look out the window at the street below. The ledger belonged to the Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition. Its pages recorded not glory but coal bills, insurance premiums, unpaid wages, and the mounting costs of a venture that had captured the world’s imagination and nearly bankrupted everyone involved. The solicitor turned back to his work. There were letters to write, creditors to placate, and assets to value. The men had come home, but the expedition’s debts remained.

The contrast between the public story and the private accounts could hardly have been starker. In the newspapers, Shackleton’s name still carried the lustre of the miraculous rescue. Twenty-eight men saved from the Weddell Sea pack ice, a small boat voyage of eight hundred miles across the wildest ocean on earth, a crossing of South Georgia’s unmapped interior, and finally, after four attempts, the rescue of the men left behind on Elephant Island. The tale had everything: courage, endurance, leadership against impossible odds. The Illustrated London News had devoted pages to Hurley’s photographs. Lectures had drawn crowds. The King had received the explorer at Buckingham Palace. But the applause had faded, the lecture fees had been collected and spent, and what remained was the ledger.

The expedition’s finances had never been robust. Shackleton had raised money for years before departure, cajoling donors, securing loans, mortgaging his own future earnings against the hope of success. The outbreak of war had complicated everything. Funds that might have flowed more freely in peacetime now had to compete with the demands of the war effort. The Admiralty had taken back the men they had released. The government had other priorities. And when the ships were lost—Endurance crushed in the Weddell Sea ice on 21 November 1915, Aurora torn from its moorings in May 1915 and drifting helplessly north for over six months—the expedition’s assets dwindled to almost nothing. The ships themselves were gone. The stores and equipment were lost. What remained was a mountain of debt and a collection of photographs and film that might, if sold properly, recoup something of what had been spent.

The creditors had begun to present themselves almost as soon as the men were safe. Coal merchants who had supplied the ships. Outfitters who had provided clothing and equipment. Shipping companies who had transported men and materials to South America and New Zealand. The families of the Ross Sea party men who had died—Spencer-Smith, Victor Hayward, Aeneas Mackintosh—wanted answers and compensation. The expedition had promised wages, and those wages had never been paid in full. Now the men were back, some of them already serving at the front, and the expedition’s accounts showed a deficit that no amount of public applause could erase.

Shackleton himself had returned to London in May 1917, not as a conquering hero but as a debtor. He walked into offices where he had once been received as a celebrity and now faced men who wanted to know when they would see their money. The war had changed everything. The mood of the country had shifted. The appetite for tales of polar exploration had dimmed in the face of daily casualty lists from the Western Front. Shackleton found himself explaining the same things over and over: how the ice had taken the ship, how the men had survived, how every penny had been spent on the rescue. The story that had thrilled the public now had to be repackaged for creditors who wanted figures, not adventures.

The Royal Geographical Society had its own questions. The Society had supported the expedition, lending its prestige and some of its funds to Shackleton’s venture. Now the council wanted a proper accounting. The minutes of their meetings record a mixture of admiration and frustration. The rescue had been remarkable, yes. The survival of the entire Weddell Sea party was evidence of Shackleton’s leadership. But the expedition had failed in its primary objective. The crossing of the Antarctic continent had not been accomplished. The Ross Sea party had laid depots across the ice shelf for a journey that never happened, and three men had died in the process. The Society’s secretary, Arthur Hinks, had the unenviable task of sorting through the correspondence, the claims, and the complaints. He wrote memos. He filed reports. He tried to keep track of who owed what to whom.

The ledger told its own story. The coal bills alone were substantial. The expedition had needed fuel for the ships, for the motor sledges, for the stoves that kept the men alive through the Antarctic winter. The suppliers wanted payment. The insurance companies wanted premiums. The men wanted their back pay. And the expedition’s funds were nearly exhausted. What money remained had to be stretched to cover as many obligations as possible, and there was no agreement on which debts should take priority.

The sale of Hurley’s photographs and film became a matter of urgent necessity. Frank Hurley had risked his life to capture the images of the Endurance trapped in the ice, the ship’s final moments, the men on the floes, the boat journey to Elephant Island. He had dived into the freezing water to rescue his glass plates from the flooded hold. He had lugged his heavy camera equipment across the ice and into the boats. His images were the expedition’s most valuable remaining asset, and Shackleton needed to monetize them quickly. Negotiations began with publishers and film distributors. The rights to the photographs, the rights to the film footage—everything was for sale. The money would go to the creditors, to the unpaid wages, to the costs of winding up the expedition’s affairs.

But even this was not straightforward. Hurley had his own claims. He had not been fully paid for his work. He believed, with some justification, that the photographs were his creation and that he deserved a share of whatever they brought. The negotiations dragged on. The war made everything harder. Paper was rationed. Film stock was hard to come by. The public’s attention was elsewhere. The photographs that would later become iconic images of polar exploration were, in 1917, simply assets to be liquidated, and the liquidation was proceeding far more slowly than anyone had hoped.

Meanwhile, the Ross Sea party’s survivors had their own accounts to settle. The Aurora had been torn from its moorings in May 1915, leaving ten men stranded on shore with insufficient supplies. They had laid the depots anyway, walking hundreds of miles across the ice, hauling sledges through blizzards, starving and freezing. Spencer-Smith had died on the march. Mackintosh and Hayward had disappeared trying to cross the sea ice. The others had survived by a combination of determination, improvisation, and luck. When the Aurora, under Captain John King Davis, finally returned to Cape Evans on 10 January 1917, the survivors were astonished to see Shackleton approaching them; they then learned for the first time the futility of their labours. They came back to find that the expedition that had sent them south was effectively bankrupt.

The families of the dead men wanted more than financial settlement. They wanted explanations. They wanted to know why their husbands and sons had been left without adequate support, why the ship had been lost, why no rescue had come sooner. The correspondence in the expedition’s files shows the strain. Letters from widows, from parents, from solicitors acting on behalf of grieving families. Each letter demanded answers. Each answer led to more questions. The official inquiries had cleared Shackleton of negligence, but the private grief of the families could not be so easily assuaged. They had lost their men to an expedition that had failed in its purpose, and now they were being asked to accept financial settlements that seemed inadequate to the magnitude of their loss.

The Admiralty had its own concerns. The Royal Navy had released men for the expedition, expecting them to return within a reasonable time. The war had intervened. The men had been stranded far longer than anyone anticipated. Now they were coming back, some of them wounded in body or spirit, and the Navy needed to know what had happened to its personnel. The inquiries were polite but persistent. The Admiralty wanted reports, statements, documentation. They wanted to understand how the ships had been lost and whether any negligence had been involved. The fact that Shackleton had saved his men counted in his favor, but it did not exempt him from the requirement to account for the expedition’s failures.

The financial committee of the expedition met in London through the autumn of 1917, trying to bring some order to the chaos. The committee members were men of business, accustomed to balance sheets and bottom lines. They looked at the expedition’s accounts and saw what the public applause had obscured: a venture that had spent more than it raised, lost more than it saved, and accomplished less than it had promised. The rescue of the men was a remarkable feat, yes, but it was not what the donors had funded. They had funded a crossing of the Antarctic continent, a feat of exploration that would have secured Britain’s reputation in the polar regions. Instead, the expedition had lost two ships, three men, and nearly all its equipment. The ledger showed the result in cold figures.

Davis was a veteran of Mawson’s recent Australasian expedition, and had turned down Shackleton’s offers in 1914 of the command of either Endurance or Aurora. As a gesture, Shackleton was permitted to sail as a supernumerary officer when the ship left on its relief voyage from New Zealand on 20 December 1916. Now Davis found himself drawn into the financial wrangling as well, his experience with Australasian expeditions making him a useful intermediary with the New Zealand authorities and creditors.

The relief expedition’s costs had added their own layer to the debt. The Aurora had needed repairs, refitting, crew wages—funds that proved difficult to raise as the Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition was completely out of funds and private subscribers were hard to find.

Finally, the governments of Australia, New Zealand and Great Britain agreed jointly to fund the refit. The money had to come from somewhere, and the somewhere was increasingly the expedition’s overdrawn accounts.

The sale of expedition assets proceeded through the winter. Equipment that had survived—sledges, tents, scientific instruments—was auctioned or sold privately. The photographs and film rights were finally disposed of, though the returns were disappointing. The war had depressed the market for such things. Publishers were cautious. The public was preoccupied. The images that would later sell thousands of books and fill lecture halls for decades were sold for a fraction of their eventual value. The money went to creditors, but it was not enough. Some debts had to be written off. Some creditors accepted partial payment. Some claims were disputed and remained unresolved.

Shackleton’s own position was difficult. He had personally guaranteed some of the expedition’s debts. His future earnings were encumbered by obligations that would take years to satisfy. The war had taken his brother Francis, killed in action in 1916. The world he had known before 1914 was gone. The polar exploration that had been his life’s work was now a thing of the past. The great age of Antarctic discovery had ended with Scott’s death in 1912, and the war had buried whatever remained. Shackleton would find other work, other expeditions, but the Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition was finished, and its financial legacy was a burden he would carry for years.

The men who had served under him had their own burdens. Some had returned to find their families struggling. Some had been wounded in the war that followed their Antarctic ordeal. Some found that the skills that had kept them alive on the ice were of little use in a world transformed by mechanized warfare. The expedition had been a ship of men, a social organism held together by discipline and mutual dependence, and that organism had been dissolved by the return to civilization. The men scattered. Some kept in touch. Others disappeared into the war or into their own lives. The bonds forged in the ice did not translate easily into peacetime.

The legal formalities of dissolution took months. The expedition had been organized as a corporate entity, with shareholders and directors and formal obligations. Unwinding that entity required lawyers, meetings, filings with the appropriate authorities. The shareholders had expected returns on their investment. They received instead notices of loss and requests for patience. The expedition’s solicitors sent out letters explaining the situation, requesting more time, pleading for understanding. Some creditors were sympathetic. Others were not. The correspondence in the files shows the range of human response to disappointment: some wrote gracious letters accepting partial payment, others fired off angry demands for immediate settlement in full.

The Royal Geographical Society’s council reviewed the expedition’s final accounts with a mixture of resignation and regret. The Society had backed Shackleton when others doubted. They had provided funds, legitimacy, institutional support. They had hoped for a triumph that would rival or exceed Amundsen’s conquest of the Pole. Instead, they received a remarkable survival story but no crossing, no scientific triumph, no territorial claim. The Society’s secretary recorded the proceedings in his neat hand. The council thanked Shackleton for his efforts. They noted the loss of life with appropriate solemnity. They accepted the financial accounting with what grace they could muster. The ledger was closed.

But the closing was not complete. Some claims remained unresolved. Some creditors refused to accept the settlements offered. Some families of the dead continued to press for more information, more compensation, more acknowledgment of their loss. The expedition’s files filled with correspondence that would continue for years. The legal entity would linger on paper long after the men had gone their separate ways. The financial aftermath of the Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition would prove almost as protracted as the ordeal itself.

In December 1917, the solicitor in the office off the Strand prepared his final report. The ledger was balanced as well as it could be. The assets had been sold. The creditors had been paid what was available. The remaining debts were documented and filed. The expedition’s corporate existence would be wound up in due course. He wrote a covering letter to the expedition’s committee, summarizing what had been done and what remained undone. The letter was polite, professional, and utterly devoid of the drama that had characterized the expedition itself. The ice had held the men for nearly two years. The ledger would hold them longer.

The war, of course, continued. The men who had survived the Antarctic found themselves in a world where survival was a daily question for millions. The hardships they had endured on the ice now seemed to belong to a different era, a different kind of struggle. The public that had thrilled to Shackleton’s story had newer, more urgent concerns. The expedition’s accounts would be settled, eventually, but the settlement would be a footnote to a larger history.

Shackleton stood in the solicitor’s office on a December afternoon, looking at the documents spread across the desk. The final accounts. The letters to creditors. The notices of dissolution. The expedition’s corporate existence was ending, but its debts would follow him for years. He picked up his pen and signed the papers. The signature was firm, unhurried. He had faced worse than creditors. He had brought his men home from the ice, and no ledger could take that away from him. But the ledger had its own claims, its own persistence. The expedition was over. The bills remained.
