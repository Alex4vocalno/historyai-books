Chapter 5

The First Embrace of the Floes

Shackleton did not yet know how it would change, or what form the change would take. He understood only that the pack had found them, and that the weeks ahead would reveal whether the Endurance could endure what the Weddell Sea intended.

The sound came first. It began on 20 January 1915, a low grinding that vibrated through the deck planking and into the soles of men’s boots. Thomas Orde-Lees, the expedition’s storekeeper, noted it in his diary that evening: a persistent rasping against the hull, like some enormous beast dragging its flank along the ship’s side. The sound had been absent the day before. Now it accompanied every hour, and it carried with it a new and unsettling intimacy. The ice was no longer something the Endurance pushed through; it was something that pressed back. The ship had entered the pack at 5: 00 p. m. on 18 January, after a gale had blown the ice away and then returned it, thicker and softer than before.

The ship’s log recorded the position at noon: 76° 34’ South, 31° 10’ West. This was farther south than any vessel had penetrated in the Weddell Sea since James Clark Ross himself, seventy years earlier. But the latitude was no longer a measure of progress. It had become a marker of isolation. The Endurance had entered waters from which no ship had ever returned under its own power, and the pack was closing around her with methodical, inexorable patience.

Frank Worsley, the captain, stood on the bridge through the long Antarctic twilight, watching the pressure ridges form where floes collided. He was a New Zealander, forty-two years old, with a seaman’s instinct for wind and water. He had taken this command knowing the risks. But watching the ice now, he understood that the risks had shifted. The danger was no longer a storm that might pass or a shoal that could be avoided. The danger was the environment itself, vast and patient, and the ship was trapped within it like a fly in amber.

Shackleton came to the bridge in the early hours of 21 January. He had been below, consulting the charts, calculating distances to Vahsel Bay. The goal remained the same: reach the coast, land the shore party, and begin the transcontinental crossing before the winter closed in. But the charts showed a coastline that existed only in fragments, surveyed by earlier expeditions from a distance, and the ice between the ship and that coast was thickening by the hour.

Worsley gestured toward the horizon. The pack stretched in every direction, a white plain broken by pressure ridges and the occasional open lead of dark water. The wind had shifted to the southeast, driving the floes together, closing the gaps through which the Endurance might have steamed.

The pack was tightening. Every hour they waited, the leads grew smaller.

Shackleton nodded. He had seen pack ice before, on his southern journey with Scott a dozen years earlier, and on his own Nimrod expedition more recently. He knew the patterns: the way ice could open suddenly, offering a passage, then close again without warning. The trick was to be ready, to seize the moment when it came.

He ordered steam kept up. They might get a chance yet.

But the chance did not come. On 22 January, the log recorded the position again: 76° 39’ South, 31° 17’ West. The ship had drifted south with the pack, gaining five miles of latitude while losing any ability to choose her course. The ice held her now, not with the dramatic grip of a vise, but with the steady, accumulating pressure of a medium that had simply absorbed her.

In the engine room, the chief engineer tended the boilers with careful attention. Coal was finite. Every hour the furnaces burned consumed fuel that might be needed later, in circumstances no one could predict. The order to keep steam up was a gamble: burn coal now in the hope of a lead, or save it for an emergency that might never come? The engineer did not make the decision. He only tended the fires, watched the pressure gauges, and waited.

The crew watched too. There were twenty-seven men aboard, not counting Shackleton himself. They came from different backgrounds and different stations: sailors, scientists, a photographer, a carpenter, two surgeons. They had signed on for different reasons—some for the adventure, some for the pay, some for the chance to see a part of the world no human had ever seen. Now they watched the ice, and they waited to see what their commander would do.

Orde-Lees wrote in his diary on the evening of 22 January. The entry was private, not meant for the official record, and it carried a note of anxiety that the ship’s log did not capture. The pack seemed to be closing in on all sides. They were making no headway whatever. The Boss remained cheerful, but Orde-Lees could not help feeling that their situation was growing more precarious with each passing day.

The contrast between the official record and the private diaries would become a pattern. The log, kept by Worsley or his officers, recorded positions, temperatures, wind directions—the factual skeleton of each day. It did not record fear. It did not record doubt. But the diaries, those private repositories of thought, began to carry a different burden. Men wrote when they could not speak, and they wrote what they would not say aloud.

On 23 January, the pressure increased. The grinding sound grew louder, and the ship’s timbers groaned under the strain. The Endurance was built for ice navigation, her hull reinforced with greenheart and oak, designed to withstand the squeezing of the pack. But the pack had time. It could wait. And the pressure was building.

Shackleton walked the deck that morning, studying the ice. He was looking for something—a lead, a crack, a sign that the pack might open and offer a passage south. The sun was bright, the sky clear, and the light reflected off the white surface with an intensity that hurt the eyes. Frank Hurley, the expedition’s photographer, had brought his glass-plate negatives on deck to capture the scene. The ice, he wrote later, had a beauty that was almost cruel. It glittered and shone, a landscape of perfect stillness, and the ship sat at its center like a toy.

But Shackleton was not looking for beauty. He was looking for a way out.

A dark line of water showed between two floes to the east, perhaps half a mile distant. It might lead somewhere. It might lead nowhere. The only way to know was to try.

Worsley raised his binoculars. The lead was there, but it was narrowing.

Shackleton hesitated. Every movement through the ice burned coal. Every mile of open water might close behind them, trapping them in a new position no better than the old. But staying still meant accepting the grip of the pack, and Shackleton had not come this far to accept anything.

He gave the order. They would try for it.

The engines thrummed to life. The propeller turned. The Endurance began to move, pushing through the slush and broken ice toward that dark line of open water. For a moment, it seemed they might make it. The lead widened as they approached, and Worsley steered the ship’s bow into the gap.

Then the ice shifted. The wind, steady from the southeast, drove the floes together with sudden force. The lead narrowed. The ship’s hull scraped against the ice on both sides, and the grinding sound became a roar. Worsley fought the wheel, trying to keep the Endurance in the center of the channel, but the channel was disappearing.

Shackleton ordered full ahead.

The engines surged. The ship pushed forward, her bow rising as she rammed the ice ahead. But the ice would not yield. It pressed in from both sides, and the ship shuddered to a stop, her momentum spent, her bow embedded in a floe that stretched to the horizon.

The lead was gone. The opportunity had lasted less than twenty minutes.

Shackleton stood on the bridge, watching the ice close around them. The Endurance was held now, truly held, in a way she had not been before. She had steamed into that lead under her own power, making a choice, taking a risk. Now she was trapped, and the trap had closed by accident, by the simple movement of wind and ice.

He gave the order quietly. Bank the fires. They would conserve coal.

The order was practical. It was also symbolic. Banking the fires meant accepting that the engines would not be needed, at least not now. It meant acknowledging that the ship’s movement was no longer under human control. The Endurance would go where the ice took her, and the men aboard would have to wait.

In the mess room that evening, the crew ate their dinner in a subdued atmosphere. The food was the same as always—tinned meat, biscuits, tea—but the conversation was different. The usual jokes and stories seemed out of place. The ship was beset now, truly beset, and everyone aboard understood what that meant.

Alexander Macklin, one of the surgeons, wrote in his diary that night. They were in the hands of Providence now. The ice had them, and there was nothing to do but wait and see what it intended.

It was a doctor’s observation, clinical and detached. But other men wrote differently. Orde-Lees, who had been anxious before, was more so now. The Boss had given the order to bank fires. This was a grave development. They were entirely at the mercy of the elements.

The irony was that the ship’s log recorded nothing dramatic. On 23 January 1915, the entry noted the position, the weather, the fact of being beset. It did not record the decision to steam for the lead, the failure of that attempt, or the order to bank fires. The log was a record of positions, not of choices. But the choices were what mattered.

Shackleton understood this. He had made a choice—to try for the lead—and the choice had failed. Now he had to make another choice: how to lead men who were trapped, how to maintain hope when there was no clear path forward, how to prepare for a winter that might last eight months or longer.

He walked the ship that night, speaking to the men, asking about their work, their thoughts. He did not offer false reassurance. He did not pretend that the situation was anything other than what it was. But his presence, his calm, his visible attention to their welfare—these things mattered. The men saw that their commander was not defeated, and they took heart from that.

The ship of men, as a social vessel, was already beginning to take shape. The physical ship was trapped, her engines silent, her course surrendered. But the social vessel—the expedition as a community, a hierarchy, a disciplined organism—was now the primary means of survival. Shackleton had built that vessel through his choices of men, through the routines he established, through the example he set. Now it would be tested.

On 24 January, the position had shifted again. The log recorded 76° 41’ South, 31° 20’ West. The drift continued, slow and inexorable, carrying the ship and the ice together toward the north. The Weddell Sea gyre, that vast circular current that trapped ice for years before releasing it, had them now. They were part of its system, caught in its slow rotation, and no amount of human effort could change that.

The temperature was falling. The thermometer read 15 degrees Fahrenheit, cold enough to freeze the spray on the rigging, cold enough to remind men that summer was ending and winter was coming. The days were growing shorter too. The sun, which had circled the horizon without setting in December, now dipped below the ice for a few hours each night. Darkness was returning to the Antarctic.

Shackleton called a meeting of the officers that afternoon. They gathered in the wardroom—Worsley, the navigator; Wild, the second-in-command; Hurley, the photographer; Macklin and McIlroy, the surgeons; James, the geologist; Hussey, the meteorologist; Clark, the biologist; Greenstreet, the chief officer. They were the leadership of the expedition, the men Shackleton relied on for advice and support.

He stated the fact without preamble. They were beset. The ice had them, and they would likely be there through the winter.

He did not ask for opinions. He did not invite debate. He outlined what would happen next. The ship would be winter quarters. The men would continue their routines. The scientific work would proceed as planned—the dredging, the meteorological observations, the biological sampling. Life would go on, even if the ship did not move.

They had provisions for a year, more if they were careful. They had coal, they had equipment, they had the best men he could find. They would spend the winter there, and when the ice opened in the spring, they would make for Vahsel Bay and proceed with the crossing.

It was a plan. It might not work—the ice might not open, the ship might be crushed, the provisions might run out—but it was a plan, and having a plan was better than having none.

Worsley asked about the drift. How far north would they go? Where would they be when the ice released them?

Shackleton admitted he did not know. But he knew this: they would deal with whatever came. They had faced worse than this.

It was not true, not exactly. They had not faced worse than this. The Nimrod expedition had been difficult, but the ship had never been trapped. The Discovery expedition with Scott had been trapped, but they had known that help would come. Here, there was no help coming. They were alone, and they would remain alone until they saved themselves.

But Shackleton’s confidence, even if it was performed, had its effect. The officers nodded. They returned to their duties. They told the men what the Boss had said, and the men, hearing it, took heart.

Hope, in such circumstances, was a rationed resource. It could not be spent all at once. It had to be conserved, distributed carefully, renewed when possible. Shackleton understood this intuitively. He did not promise immediate release. He did not claim that the ice would open tomorrow. He offered instead a framework for endurance: winter would pass, spring would come, and they would be ready.

The days that followed settled into a pattern. The ship was beset, but life aboard continued. The men rose at 7: 00 a. m., ate breakfast, and began their duties. Some tended the dogs, kept on deck in kennels built for the voyage. Others worked in the laboratories, processing samples, recording data. Still others maintained the ship, checking the hull for damage, keeping the pumps ready.

The ice, for its part, was not idle. It moved constantly, grinding against the hull, shifting and settling. The pressure ridges rose and fell like waves in slow motion. The floes cracked and reformed, opening leads that filled with slush and then froze again. The Weddell Sea pack was not a static prison. It was a dynamic system, and the Endurance was caught in its currents.

On 26 January, the log recorded a significant event: the ship had reached her most southerly position of the entire drift. The latitude was 76° 58’ South. She would never go farther south than this. From this point on, the drift would carry her north, away from Vahsel Bay, away from the planned landing site, away from the continent itself.

Shackleton noted the position without comment. He had hoped to reach the coast, to begin the crossing, to achieve the goal he had announced to the world. But the ice had other plans. The Weddell Sea was not a highway; it was a trap, and the trap was closing.

The crew did not know that this was the farthest south. They only knew that the drift continued, that the ice held them, that the days grew shorter and colder. They adapted. They found routines. They discovered that life aboard a beset ship was not so different from life aboard a moving ship, except for one thing: they were going nowhere.

Orde-Lees wrote on 27 January. They were settling into a routine now. The Boss had ordered regular watches and duties. There was work for everyone. The time passed, though slowly. He found himself watching the ice for hours, looking for some sign of a lead, but there was nothing. They were trapped.

The diarist’s anxiety had not abated, but it had found a new expression. He was not alone. Other men wrote, in letters home that would not be delivered for months, of their frustration, their fear, their hope. The ice was beautiful, yes, but it was also cruel. It offered nothing but the certainty of waiting.

Shackleton, meanwhile, maintained his public calm. He moved among the men, asking about their work, their health, their families. He played cards with the officers in the evening. He consulted with Worsley about navigation, with Hurley about photography, with Macklin about the dogs. He was present, always present, and his presence was its own form of leadership.

The ship of men was taking shape. The routines, the hierarchy, the discipline—all of these were becoming the primary means of survival. The physical ship was important, but the social vessel was essential. If the men lost hope, if they turned against each other, if discipline broke down, then no amount of provisions or equipment would save them.

Shackleton understood this. He had read the accounts of earlier expeditions, the ones that had failed. He knew how quickly things could fall apart. He was determined that this expedition would not fail in that way. Whatever else happened, the men would remain united, disciplined, hopeful.

On 30 January, the temperature dropped sharply. The log recorded minus 10 degrees Fahrenheit, the coldest reading since the ship had entered the pack. The ice groaned and cracked, and the men felt the vibration through the deck. The pressure was building again.

Shackleton ordered a check of the hull. The carpenter, McNish, went over every inch of the ship’s timbers, looking for damage. He found none. The Endurance was built for this, and she was holding. But the pressure would continue, and the ship would have to withstand it for months.

The nights were long now. The sun rose late and set early, and the hours of darkness stretched out. The men lit lamps in the wardroom and the mess deck, playing cards, reading books, talking. The dogs howled sometimes, restless in their kennels, and the sound echoed across the ice.

It was, in its way, a kind of peace. The ship was safe, for now. The men were fed, for now. The ice held them, but it had not harmed them. They were alive, and they were together, and that was something.

But Shackleton knew that the peace was temporary. The ice would not hold forever. The pressure would build, the floes would shift, and the ship would face a test she might not pass. He did not speak of this to the men. He spoke instead of routine, of duty, of the work that needed to be done.

On 1 February, the log recorded a new position: 76° 50’ South. The drift north had begun. The ship was moving, but not under her own power. She was being carried, slowly, steadily, away from the continent and toward the open sea that lay thousands of miles away.

The men did not know how long the drift would last. They did not know where they would be when the ice released them. They did not know if the ship would survive the pressure, or if they would have to take to the ice and walk. They knew only that they were beset, and that the winter was coming.

Shackleton stood on the deck that evening, watching the sun set over the ice. The sky was clear, the air cold, and the light golden on the white expanse. It was beautiful, in a way that hurt to look at. The ice stretched to the horizon, and beyond the horizon, and beyond that, until it reached the continent itself, the continent they had hoped to cross.

He had failed, in a sense. The crossing would not happen, not this year. The plan had called for a landing at Vahsel Bay, a march across the pole, a rendezvous with the Ross Sea party on the other side. None of that would happen now. The ice had seen to that.

But failure was not the end. Failure was a condition, a circumstance, a fact to be dealt with. Shackleton had failed before—at the pole with Scott, at the pole on his own expedition—and he had survived. He would survive this too. And he would bring his men through, whatever it took.

The ship was now a drifting piece of the landscape; her engines were silent, her course surrendered to the whims of the Weddell Sea.
