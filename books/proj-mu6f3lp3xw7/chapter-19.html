Chapter 19

The Admiralty’s Silent Knowledge

Two documents lay on separate desks in Whitehall on the morning of 6 May 1915. One was a decrypted German naval signal, freshly logged in the Admiralty’s cryptographic section, giving the grid position of a U-boat transiting south along the Irish coast toward the shipping lane. The other was a wireless broadcast, typed in the signal office and sent to all merchant vessels in the Irish Sea, advising them that submarines had been sighted in the area and recommending caution. One document named a coordinate. The other offered a generalities. The gap between them, measured in nautical miles and in operational consequence, would not become public for years. But it sat there, on that morning, as the Lusitania steamed eastward toward the point where the two documents would converge.

The cryptographic section occupied a small room in the Admiralty’s Old Building. It had been assembled in the opening weeks of the conflict, after British naval forces recovered German signal books from three sources: the light cruiser Magdeburg, run aground in the Baltic in August 1914; a merchant marine code seized from a captured steamer; and the main German naval code, reconstructed from fragments by a team led by Commander Alexander Kirk. By the spring of 1915, the section was reading German naval wireless traffic with a regularity that the Imperial German Navy did not suspect. Intercepted signals arrived through a chain of coastal wireless stations, were decoded, translated, and logged, often within hours of transmission. The work was secret even within the Admiralty. A narrow circle of senior officers knew the full scope of what the room could read.

The Director of the Intelligence Division, Rear Admiral Thomas Jackson, received the decrypted material. The First Lord, Winston Churchill, and the First Sea Lord, Admiral Lord Fisher, were briefed on significant intercepts. The War Staff, the operational planning body, received summaries. What happened to those summaries, who acted on them, who did not, and what decisions followed, was the question that would later sit beneath every line of the Mersey inquiry’s transcript. The room held a handful of officers, civilian linguists, and clerks. Its work was the Admiralty’s most closely guarded secret.

The intercepts concerning U-20 began on 1 May 1915, the day the Lusitania left New York. German naval wireless stations transmitted operational orders to boats at sea. The cryptographic section decoded these signals. On 2 May, an intercept placed U-20 in the North Sea, heading west. On 4 May, a further signal placed her off the north coast of Scotland, having transited the dangerous waters between the Orkneys and the mainland. By 5 May, decoded traffic placed U-20 in the Atlantic, proceeding south toward the Irish coast. The grid references were specific. The bearing was toward the approaches to Liverpool, the lane that every transatlantic liner, including the Lusitania, was bound to use.

The Admiralty knew this route. It had prescribed it. The North Channel, between Ireland and Scotland, was the recognized approach for merchant shipping entering the Irish Sea. Captain Turner’s course, plotted under Admiralty instructions, would bring the Lusitania past the Old Head of Kinsale and into the channel. The route was known, published in sailing directions, and obvious to any commander with a chart. It was also the route that U-20, according to the intercepts, was positioned to intercept.

On 5 May, the cryptographic section logged a signal giving U-20’s operational area. The boat had been assigned a patrol zone off the southern Irish coast, covering the approaches to St. George’s Channel and the waters around the Fastnet Rock. This was not a guess. A German naval order had been decoded and translated. The patrol zone overlapped with the Lusitania’s track.

What the Admiralty did with this intelligence is the question that the official record does not answer directly. The War Staff’s minutes for this period are incomplete. The surviving files contain summaries of intercepts but not, in every case, a record of who read them, when they were read, or what operational response was ordered. What the files do contain is the text of the warnings that were sent.

On 5 May, the Admiralty broadcast a general warning to merchant shipping. The message advised vessels that submarines had been sighted in the southern Irish Sea and on the approaches to Liverpool. It recommended that ships maintain high speed, keep well offshore, and avoid headlands. Addressed to all ships, the message named no specific vessel. No coordinates for any particular submarine appeared in it. No escort was offered.

The Lusitania, then in mid-Atlantic and receiving her wireless messages via the Marconi station at Valentia, would have received this general warning. Captain Turner later testified that he had received Admiralty wireless messages about submarines in the area. But the messages he received were the same vague advisories that every ship received. None of the specific intelligence extracted from the German signals appeared in them. No message said that a submarine was at these coordinates, on this bearing, assigned to patrol this exact zone. They said, in effect: be careful.

On 6 May, the intercepts continued. Further decoded signals placed U-20 in the waters south of Ireland, moving eastward along the shipping lane. The position was consistent with a boat patrolling the approaches to the North Channel. The Lusitania, making her way east at reduced speed, Turner having dropped to 21 knots to conserve coal and to time his arrival at the Mersey Bar for high tide, was on a converging course. The intercepts made this convergence visible, in theory, to anyone in the Admiralty who was reading them.

A second Admiralty warning went out that same day. Slightly more specific than the first, the message reported submarines active off the south coast of Ireland and recommended that ships take a mid-channel course. The Lusitania was not named. Turner was not advised to alter course, to reverse his direction, to put in at Queenstown, or to wait for a naval escort. The grid reference decoded that morning was not passed to him.

The question of why the Admiralty did not provide Turner with the specific intelligence has been asked since the first postwar revelations about the cryptographic section. Several explanations have been offered. None is fully satisfying. Each, however, illuminates the institutional logic of the Admiralty in the spring of 1915.

The first explanation is secrecy. The room’s existence was the Admiralty’s most closely guarded secret. If the Germans learned that their naval codes had been broken, they would change them, and the flow of intelligence would stop. This fear was real and, in the context of a war that would last years, rational. But it raised a question of proportion. Was the risk of compromising the intelligence source greater than the risk of losing a passenger liner with nearly two thousand people aboard? The Admiralty never answered this question in any document that survives. It may not have been asked.

The second explanation is operational doctrine. The anti-submarine strategy in the spring of 1915 was still in formation. The navy had too few destroyers to escort every merchant ship. The War Staff had to prioritize naval vessels for military use, troop transports, munitions ships, naval auxiliaries. A passenger liner, even one as famous as the Lusitania, was classified as a merchant vessel. Under the doctrine of the time, merchant vessels were expected to rely on speed and route avoidance, not on naval protection. The Lusitania was faster than any submarine. In theory, she could outrun any threat. In theory, a general warning was sufficient.

But theory had a flaw. The Lusitania was fast, but she was not fast enough if she did not know where the threat was. A submarine on the surface could make perhaps 14 knots. The Lusitania could make 21. But a submarine lying in wait, submerged at a point on the liner’s track, did not need to be fast. It needed only to be in the right place. The intercepts told the Admiralty that U-20 was in the right place. The general warning did not tell Turner where that place was.

The third explanation is darker. The historian Patrick Beesly, whose study of British naval intelligence in the First World War remains the most detailed account of the cryptographic section’s operations, concluded that the evidence, while circumstantial, pointed toward a deliberate decision to expose the Lusitania to risk. His reasoning was not that the Admiralty wanted the ship sunk, but that it may have calculated that even an unsuccessful attack on a British passenger liner carrying American civilians would draw the United States closer to war. Beesly wrote that he was reluctantly driven to the conclusion that a conspiracy existed deliberately to put the Lusitania at risk in the hope that even an abortive attack on her would bring the United States into the war. He added that such a conspiracy could not have been put into effect without Winston Churchill’s express permission and approval.

This is a severe judgment. The documentary record does not contain a smoking gun, a memorandum, an order, a minute sheet, that proves it. What the record contains is something more unsettling: a pattern of knowledge followed by inaction, of specific intelligence followed by general warnings, of a map being filled in detail in one room while the man who needed it most received nothing he could use.

The contrast was not merely between knowledge and ignorance. It was between two kinds of knowledge held by the same institution. The Admiralty knew where U-20 was. It knew the Lusitania’s route. It knew the liner’s speed and her expected position. From the intercepts, it knew that the patrol zone and the shipping lane overlapped. And it knew that the warnings it had sent did not convey any of this. The Admiralty held both sides of the equation, the threat and the target, and it did not bring them together in any operational order that survives.

On the morning of 7 May, the day of the sinking, no further intercept arrived to place U-20 precisely. The last decoded signal was from the previous day. But the last known position was enough. U-20 had been moving east along the south Irish coast, toward the shipping lane. Schwieger’s own war diary, later recovered and translated, confirmed that he had sighted no targets on 6 May and had continued southward, then eastward, surfacing at night to charge batteries. His track, reconstructed from the diary, placed him on the morning of 7 May in the waters off the Old Head of Kinsale. This was the same position the intercepts had indicated. The Admiralty’s intelligence had been accurate.

At ten past two that afternoon, the Lusitania was struck. The torpedo from U-20 hit the starboard side, below the bridge. A second explosion followed, larger and more destructive, its origin debated to this day. The ship sank in eighteen minutes.

In the hours and days that followed, the Admiralty’s public posture was one of shock. Churchill issued a statement calling the sinking a spectacle of murder. The press, briefed by official channels, treated the attack as an unprovoked act of German barbarism against a defenseless civilian ship. The diplomatic notes that Washington drafted were based on this understanding: that the sinking was a surprise, an unforeseeable atrocity, an act of terror that no reasonable person could have anticipated.

But in the cryptographic section, and in the offices of the War Staff, the files told a different story. The intercepts of 2, 4, 5, and 6 May had tracked U-20 from the North Sea to the Irish coast. The warnings sent to merchant shipping had been general. No specific warning had been sent to the Lusitania. No escort had been dispatched. Whether these omissions were the product of doctrine, secrecy, or calculation was not a question the Admiralty would answer in public. The Mersey inquiry would not ask it.

The inquiry, convened under Lord Mersey, focused on Captain Turner’s actions. It examined his speed, his course, his response to the warnings he had received. The warnings he had not received were not examined. No witnesses from the cryptographic section were called. The intercepts were not discussed. The Admiralty’s operational decisions were not scrutinized. The ship’s navigation, the boats, the cargo, and the conduct of the crew were the subjects. It was during the closed hearings that the Admiralty tried to lay the blame on Captain William Thomas Turner, their intended line being that Turner had been negligent. Turner was ultimately cleared. The blame was placed, officially and formally, on the German government and on Schwieger.

This was not wrong. Schwieger had fired the torpedo. The German government had declared the waters around Britain a war zone and had warned passengers not to travel on British ships. The German embassy in Washington had placed its advertisement in the newspapers beside the Lusitania’s sailing notice. These facts were not in dispute. A German commander had deliberately attacked a passenger liner, and the act violated the laws of war as they stood in 1915.

But the German act did not exhaust the question of responsibility. The Admiralty had possessed intelligence that could have prevented the disaster. It had chosen, or failed, to act on that intelligence in a way that would have given Turner the information he needed. The distinction between what the Admiralty knew and what it communicated was not a matter of hindsight. It was a matter of record. The intercepts were dated, logged, and filed. The warnings were dated, broadcast, and recorded. The gap between them was measurable. It was the gap between a grid reference and a general advisory, between a specific threat and a vague caution, between a map filled in and a map left blank.

The Admiralty’s files held this gap. Through the inquiry, through the diplomatic exchanges, through the public mourning and the press campaigns, the files stayed locked. They held it while the Americans drafted notes based on outrage and the Germans drafted responses based on justification. They held it while the dead were laid out in the sheds at Queenstown and the survivors told their stories to reporters and to the inquiry. The files held the silent knowledge that the disaster, while caused by a German torpedo, had been preceded by a British failure, a failure of communication, of doctrine, or of something worse.

The strongest counter-explanation is simple and must be answered directly. A German commander, operating under clear orders, fired a torpedo at a civilian passenger vessel without warning. This is true. Schwieger fired. He fired at a ship he knew was a passenger liner. His government had announced a policy of unrestricted submarine warfare in advance. No warning was given to the ship. No opportunity was offered for passengers to disembark. He acted within the operational logic of his orders and the tactical logic of his position. The moral responsibility for the deaths of 1, 199 people rests heavily, and legitimately, on him and on the government that sent him.

But this truth does not foreclose the other truth. The Admiralty had the intelligence to know that a submarine was on the Lusitania’s track. The capability to warn Turner specifically existed. The capability to divert the ship or to provide an escort existed. None of these things was done. The reasons may have been institutional, doctrinal, or strategic. They may have been, as Beesly argued, something more deliberate. But the effect was the same: the Lusitania entered the zone where U-20 waited without the knowledge that the Admiralty possessed and she did not. The passengers who died had no access to the intercepts. Turner had no access. Only the Admiralty did. And the Admiralty, holding the full map, let the ship sail into it.

The Lusitania was, in this sense, sunk twice. Once by Schwieger’s torpedo. Once by the silence that preceded it. The first sinking was an act of war. The second was an act of institutional choice, whether driven by secrecy, doctrine, or calculation, and the result was the same. The Admiralty’s files, locked in the cryptographic section and in the War Staff’s cabinets, contained the knowledge that the public narrative of surprise could not accommodate. The coordinates, the dates, the decoded orders, and the warnings that were sent instead, all of it sat in those files. The evidence that the sinking was not merely a German atrocity but the catastrophic intersection of German aggression and British inaction, the point where a submarine’s torpedo and a command’s silence met, with eighteen minutes of consequence between them, was there.

The files would not be opened for years. When they were, the map they contained would be nearly complete. U-20’s track from the North Sea to the Old Head would be visible. The Lusitania’s course from New York to the same point would be visible. The intercepts, the warnings, the gap between them, all would be visible. And in the white space where an order should have been written, the absence that defined the Admiralty’s role would be visible too. The silent map, drawn in code and locked in a drawer, was the document that the inquiry never saw and the public never knew. It showed, with terrible precision, how the disaster had been tracked before it happened.

Three years later, in 1918, the American cartoonist Winsor McCay released The Sinking of the Lusitania, a silent animated short film re-creating the never-photographed sinking of the British liner. At twelve minutes, it was called the longest animated film of its time. A work of propaganda, made to stir American outrage and to support the war effort that the sinking had helped to bring about, it drew the ship, the torpedo, the passengers in the water. McCay drew the horror as the public imagined it. What he could not draw, because no one had told him, was the map that had been sitting in a locked room in Whitehall three years before, the map that showed the torpedo’s path before the torpedo was fired, and the silence that let it reach its target.

The Admiralty’s files held their silence through the inquiry, through the diplomatic crisis, through the years of war that followed. While the Americans protested and the Germans defended and the dead were buried, the files stayed locked. The truth they contained, that the disaster had been foreseen in its details, if not in its precise hour, and that the knowledge had not been passed to the man who needed it, was not a truth that any government wanted told. It complicated the narrative. It spread the blame. It turned a simple story of German barbarism into something harder and more painful: a story in which the victim’s own government had possessed the means to prevent the crime and had not used them.

The questions raised by those files did not disappear. They migrated, slowly, into other investigations, into the cargo manifests, the hold inventories, the lists of what the Lusitania had been carrying besides passengers. The questions followed the evidence downward, into the ship’s own belly, where another kind of silence waited.
