Chapter 17

The Sheds at Queenstown

The living were being led to the Royal Hotel for tea and brandy. The dead were being carried into the market sheds. Two processions moved through the same evening streets of Queenstown on 7 May 1915, and the contrast between them was not planned by anyone. No one in the port had prepared for both receptions at the same hour. The town’s ad-hoc systems were now forced to serve survivors and casualties alike, with the same insufficient staff and the same inadequate buildings, in the same failing light.

Queenstown was a town of perhaps three thousand souls, a naval station and ferry port on the southern coast of Ireland, accustomed to the comings and goings of Cunard liners stopping for mail and passengers on the Liverpool-to-New York run. Its normal rhythms were modest. A liner would anchor in the harbor, tenders would ferry out mail and passengers, and the town would go about its business.

But on the evening of 7 May 1915, the town received not a liner but the wreckage of one, delivered in fragments across the decks of fishing trawlers, naval patrol boats, and the steamers that had gone out to the Old Head of Kinsale when the distress signals were heard.

The living came first, in the greatest numbers, and they filled every available space. The Seamen’s Institute took women and children. The railway hotel opened its rooms. Private houses on the waterfront took in survivors who had no one else. The naval barracks made space.

Cunard’s local agent, a man named P.S. Barrett, worked through the evening to find beds and food and clothing for people who had lost everything they carried, which for many was everything they owned.

But even as Barrett and the town’s residents were settling survivors, the fishing boats and patrol vessels were bringing in the dead. The Heron, a steam tug, had gone out to the scene and returned with bodies recovered from the water. Other boats followed. The dead were brought ashore at the Cunard wharf and at other landings, and the question immediately presented itself: where do you put them? Queenstown had no morgue adequate for a catastrophe of this scale. The town’s ordinary mortuary facilities could handle the routine deaths of a small port—a fisherman drowned, an elderly resident, the occasional accident. What was arriving exceeded them.

The market sheds were commandeered first. These were open structures, roofed and walled, used on market days for the sale of livestock and produce. Stone floors, open stalls, the smell of hay and animal dung still in the air. Bodies were carried in and laid on the ground in rows, and as the rows lengthened through the night and into the morning, the sheds filled, and additional spaces had to be found. The Cork Curling Rink, an unlikely building for the purpose, was pressed into service. Freight warehouses near the docks were opened. Every available covered space within reach of the wharf became a potential resting place for the dead, and by the morning of 8 May, the town had become a single, dispersed morgue.

Coroner John Hogan arrived to take charge of what the law required. His jurisdiction was the Queenstown district, and the dead now lying in the market sheds were, legally, his responsibility. An inquest would have to be held. But first, the bodies had to be identified, or the attempt made. Hogan began the process of organizing what was, in effect, a mass identification line. Relatives and friends who had heard the first confused reports of the sinking were already arriving from Cork and from farther afield, traveling by train and by car to Queenstown to search for people they loved among the living and the dead. Some found survivors in the hotels. Others found only the sheds.

The scene in the market sheds was, by every account, grim. Bodies were laid in rows on the floor, on tarpaulins, on whatever surfaces could be found. Many of the dead had been in the water for hours. Some were identifiable by their faces. Others were not. Personal effects were collected from clothing—watches, rings, letters, papers, anything that might establish a name. Lists were begun. Each body was assigned a number. Each number was matched, where possible, to a description: height, hair color, clothing, distinguishing marks, the contents of pockets. The process was methodical not because anyone had rehearsed it but because method was the only alternative to chaos. Out of necessity, a bureaucracy of the dead was being improvised on the stone floors of a market shed.

The handwritten inventories that emerged from this process became the first official record of the sinking’s human cost. Neither diplomatic documents nor naval signals, neither newspaper dispatches nor wireless messages, these lists were compiled by a coroner and his assistants in a town that had never expected to need such a thing. They carried a weight that no subsequent official account could set aside. The numbers on those lists would be revised upward as more bodies were recovered. The descriptions would be cross-referenced against passenger manifests. The personal effects would be catalogued and, eventually, claimed or stored. But the essential work was done in those first hours, in the sheds, by people who had no training for mass death and who did it anyway.

Each body in the sheds was a man, a woman, or a child. Their physical presence in the market sheds and the curling rink and the warehouses of Queenstown constituted an archive that no government could suppress and no inquiry could ignore. The disaster had produced not only survivors with stories but remains with names, and those remains were now lying in rows in a small Irish port, waiting to be counted.

The Cunard Line had operated the Lusitania on the New York-to-Liverpool route since 1907, and in peacetime she had been one of the fastest and most luxurious liners on the Atlantic. But the war had changed the economics of passenger travel. Many of the large liners were laid up from 1914 to 2015, due in part to falling demand for passenger travel across the Atlantic, and in part to protect them from damage due to mines or other dangers. Some of these liners were eventually used as troop transports, while others remained idle in port.

The Lusitania had not been laid up. She continued to sail, though with reduced passenger loads and under conditions that no peacetime captain had ever had to consider. The decision to keep her in service was Cunard’s, and it was made in the context of a war that had not yet touched the passenger trade directly.

Now, in the market sheds of Queenstown, that decision’s consequences were being laid out on stone floors.

The Admiralty had known, through Room 40’s intercepts, that U-boats were operating off the southern coast of Ireland in the days before the Lusitania’s arrival. The specific intelligence about U-20’s position had been decoded and circulated within the Admiralty’s operational intelligence structure. What was done with that intelligence, and what was not done, would become a central question in the inquiries that followed. But on the evening of 7 May, in Queenstown, the intelligence failures were not yet visible. What was visible was the result: bodies in rows, survivors in hotels, a town overwhelmed by the physical reality of a disaster that had occurred eighteen miles off the Old Head of Kinsale.

The rescue boats that had gone out in the afternoon returned in ones and twos through the evening, each carrying its cargo. Some brought living survivors, half-dressed, suffering from exposure and shock. Others brought only the dead. The distinction between the two categories was not always clear at the moment of recovery. People pulled from the cold water of the Celtic Sea in May might appear lifeless and yet survive. People who appeared uninjured might die hours later of exposure or shock. The rescue boats’ crews did what they could. They wrapped the living in blankets. They laid the dead in whatever space was available on deck. They steamed back to Queenstown and delivered their passengers, living and dead, to the wharf, and the town took them in.

The process of identification was, by its nature, partial. Many bodies were never recovered. The Lusitania had carried 1, 959 people, including passengers and crew. The final death toll would reach 1, 197, or 1, 195, depending on which accounting was used, and of those, many remained at the bottom of the Celtic Sea. The bodies that were recovered were a fraction of the total, and they were not necessarily a representative fraction. The water had taken some and returned others, according to currents and buoyancy and the weight of clothing and the position of the body. The people in the market sheds were not all the dead. They were the dead who had been found.

But the dead who had been found were enough. Enough to fill the market sheds and the curling rink and the warehouses. Enough to require Coroner Hogan’s attention and the attention of every official in Queenstown who could be pressed into service. Enough to draw grieving relatives from Cork and from Dublin and from London, traveling through the night to walk among the rows and look at faces and search for a husband, a wife, a child, a parent, a friend. Enough to make the sinking of the Lusitania not a headline or a diplomatic note but a physical fact, undeniable, present, lying on a stone floor in a small Irish port.

The Admiralty’s conduct in the hours before the sinking, the Room 40 intercepts that tracked U-20’s movements, the failure to provide the Lusitania with a naval escort, the absence of specific warnings delivered to Captain Turner—all of these would be examined in the inquiries that followed. But none of them could be examined, none of them could even be formulated as questions, until the physical evidence of the disaster had been assembled and documented. The market sheds of Queenstown were, in this sense, the first hearing room. The bodies laid out in rows were the first witnesses. And the lists compiled by Coroner Hogan and his assistants were the first testimony.

The cargo of rifle cartridges that the Lusitania had carried in her hold—4, 200 cases of Remington. 303 caliber cartridges, documented on the manifest and declared to customs in New York—would later become a point of contention in the diplomatic and legal arguments over the sinking. The German government would cite the cargo as evidence that the Lusitania was not a purely civilian vessel but an auxiliary cruiser carrying munitions. The British government would argue that the cargo was legitimate and that its presence did not justify the torpedoing of a passenger liner without warning. These arguments would unfold over months, in diplomatic notes and in the inquiry chambers of the Wreck Commissioner’s Court. But in the market sheds of Queenstown, on the night of 7 May and the morning of 8 May, none of this had yet been articulated. What had been articulated was a list of the dead.

The survivors who had been brought ashore and dispersed to the hotels and the institute and the private houses of Queenstown were, in their own way, also part of the record. Their accounts of the sinking—the torpedo’s impact, the second explosion, the list to starboard, the difficulty of launching the boats, the eighteen minutes between the impact and the ship’s disappearance—would be collected in the following days by journalists, by Cunard officials, and by the naval authorities. These accounts would later be given formally at the Mersey inquiry.

But the survivors’ accounts were, by their nature, subjective and variable. They contradicted each other on points of detail. Some reported a single explosion. Others reported two. Some said the ship listed immediately to starboard. Others described a more gradual heel. The survivors’ testimony would be sifted and compared and weighed, and it would produce a picture of the sinking that was detailed but not entirely consistent.

The dead produced no such contradictions. A body in a row on a market shed floor did not argue. It did not misremember. It did not exaggerate or minimize. It simply lay there, and its presence on the list was a fact that required no corroboration and admitted no dispute. The dead were the most reliable witnesses the disaster had produced, and they had been gathered, without anyone intending it, into the first archive of the sinking.

Coroner Hogan’s inquest would eventually be held, and it would produce findings. But the inquest was, in a sense, a formality. The real work of establishing what had happened—the human cost of it, the scale of it—had been done in the sheds, in the hours and days after the sinking, by the people who laid out the bodies and made the lists and walked the grieving relatives through the rows.

This work was not forensic in the modern sense. No one was examining the bodies for blast patterns or metal fragments or the chemical residues of a torpedo warhead. The identification process was practical, not scientific. It was designed to put names to the dead and to account for them, not to determine the cause of the sinking. But the lists it produced, and the physical reality of the bodies themselves, would hang over every subsequent inquiry like a weight that could not be lifted.

The Mersey inquiry, presided over by Wreck Commissioner John Bigham, Lord Mersey, would convene in June 1915 in London. Testimony would come from survivors, from Cunard officers, from naval witnesses, and from government officials. The cargo manifest, the wireless logs, the Admiralty’s instructions to the Lusitania, and the movements of U-20 would all be examined. A report would clear Captain Turner and Cunard of negligence and place the blame squarely on the German government. But the inquiry would also have to contend with the physical evidence assembled in Queenstown—the rows of dead, the catalogued effects, the lists of names—and with the fact that this evidence had been compiled not by a court or a commission but by a coroner and a town’s residents in the hours after the disaster, before any official narrative had been constructed.

The German perspective on the sinking was, at this point, a matter of public record. Walther Schwieger, commanding U-20, had fired a single torpedo at the Lusitania from a submerged position off the Old Head of Kinsale. His war diary recorded the attack in clinical terms: the track of the torpedo, the impact, the second explosion, the rapid listing and sinking of the vessel. Schwieger had acted under the doctrine of unrestricted submarine warfare, declared by Germany in February 1915, which designated the waters around the British Isles as a war zone in which Allied ships could be sunk without warning. The German embassy in Washington had placed advertisements in New York newspapers warning passengers that they sailed into the war zone at their own risk.

Schwieger was responsible for U-20 sinking the passenger liner RMS Lusitania, leading to the deaths of 1, 199 people, an event that played a role in the United States’ later entry into World War I. He also torpedoed other vessels during the same patrol. The German government would defend the attack as a legitimate act of war against a vessel carrying munitions in a declared war zone. The British government would call it mass murder.

Both characterizations would be argued in the diplomatic notes and inquiry chambers that followed. But in the market sheds of Queenstown, the argument had not yet been joined. What existed, on the night of 7 May and the morning of 8 May, was a physical fact: the dead, laid in rows, identified or unidentified, documented on lists that would be typed up and copied and transmitted to Cunard’s offices in London and to the Admiralty and, eventually, to the American embassy and the State Department in Washington. These inventories were the first draft of a record that would grow to include inquiry testimony, diplomatic correspondence, and the war diary of U-20 itself. But the lists came first. The lists were the foundation.

The town of Queenstown, in the days that followed, would continue to receive bodies as they were recovered from the water. The fishing boats and patrol vessels went out each morning and returned each evening, and the process of identification and documentation continued. Some bodies were claimed by relatives and taken away for burial. Others were buried in the local cemetery at Old Church, above the harbor, in graves that would be visited for decades. The market sheds and the curling rink and the warehouses were used for as long as bodies continued to arrive. The ad-hoc system that had been improvised on the evening of 7 May became, through repetition and necessity, a routine. The routine was grim, but it functioned.

The functioning of the routine was itself a kind of judgment. A town of three thousand people had been able, through improvisation and effort, to process the physical wreckage of a disaster that had killed nearly twelve hundred. The town had not been prepared. No plan existed for this. No morgue adequate to the task had been built. No trained forensic personnel were on hand. No system for mass identification had ever been devised. And yet the work was done.

Bodies were laid out. Lists were made. Relatives were conducted through the rows. The dead were accounted for, as many as could be found, and the living were sheltered and fed and clothed. The town’s response was a testament not to planning but to human capacity under pressure, and it stood in contrast to the failures of planning and intelligence that had occurred at a higher level, in the Admiralty’s offices in London, where the information that might have prevented the disaster had been available and had not been acted upon.

The contrast between Queenstown’s improvisation and the Admiralty’s inaction was not yet visible to the public. It would emerge in the inquiries and investigations that followed, as the Room 40 intercepts were examined and the Admiralty’s instructions to the Lusitania were reviewed. But the physical evidence assembled in the market sheds—the bodies, the lists, the catalogued effects—would be present throughout those inquiries, a silent and immovable fact that no amount of diplomatic argument or legal maneuvering could set aside. The dead were in the sheds. The dead had been counted. The dead had been named, as many as could be named. And the count, and the names, would travel from Queenstown to London to Washington, where they would become the foundation of a diplomatic crisis that would reshape the relationship between the United States and Germany and would, eventually, contribute to the American entry into the war.

The morning light came through the open sides of the market sheds and fell on the rows. Hogan’s lists, in his careful hand, lay on a table near the door. The names and numbers would be typed and copied and sent by cable to offices where the dead were not yet abstractions but people with heights and hair colors and the contents of their pockets recorded on a page. The silent, documented rows of dead in the market sheds under morning light present an undeniable physical archive awaiting translation into news and diplomatic fact.
