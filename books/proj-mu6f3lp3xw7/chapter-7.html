Chapter 7

The Torpedo’s Track (2:10 p.m., 7 May 1915)

The crosshairs bisected the liner’s hull. Through U-20’s periscope, raised just above the surface, Kapitänleutnant Walther Schwieger could see the target clearly now. The haze that had shielded the ship on its approach had thinned. What had first appeared as a confusion of funnels and masts, so many that it looked like several vessels, had resolved into one large steamer making for the Old Head of Kinsale. Four funnels. Two masts. The silhouette was unmistakable to any sailor who knew the North Atlantic traffic.

Schwieger had been running submerged since early afternoon, conserving battery power, holding position in the lane where coastwise traffic converged toward Liverpool. His fuel was low. His patrol was near its end. The decision before him was narrow and technical. The ship was crossing his field of view at a range that his torpedo could cover in under a minute. He had one shot that carried a reasonable prospect of striking, and the window was closing as the liner moved steadily past his firing position.

His war diary would record the sequence with the brevity of a technical report. At 14: 10, with the target at approximately 700 metres, he ordered one gyroscopic torpedo fired from the bow tube, set to run at a depth of two fathoms. The torpedo left the tube with a compressed-air impulse that shuddered through the submarine’s hull. Through the periscope, Schwieger watched the track of compressed air bubbles foaming toward the ship. In his diary, he noted that he did not know the identity of the vessel before firing. He knew only that it was a large passenger steamer, proceeding on a steady course, close enough to engage. The torpedo ran at forty knots. The liner was making eighteen. The geometry was simple, and the torpedo did not deviate.

The entry in his diary for that day is a combat document. It records the time, the bearing, the range, and the observation of impact. It does not record hesitation. It does not record any attempt to identify the ship by name before firing. It records the act. The torpedo was a weapon. The ship was a target. The periscope was lowered after the shot, and U-20 began to reposition. The act was clinical. That is the word the diary demands. Clinical.

What Schwieger could see through the periscope lens was a vessel of approximately 31, 500 tons, 785 feet in length, carrying 1, 264 passengers, three stowaways, and a crew of 693. These figures were beyond his knowledge. But the size of the ship was visible. The lines of a Cunard express liner were visible, the four funnels that had been the most recognizable silhouette on the Atlantic since 1907. If the visibility was as excellent as his diary claimed, the deck arrangements and the passenger promenades were visible too.

What remained invisible were the 4, 200 cases of rifle cartridges stowed in the hold, part of a cargo of around 173 tons of war supplies. A woman in the first-class lounge was invisible. A man reading on the promenade deck was invisible. So was the child being walked by a nurse. The ship was visible. The people were not.

That is the nature of periscope warfare. The lens flattens the world into a target and a range.

The torpedo ran.

On the surface, the RMS Lusitania was finishing lunch. The dining saloon on D Deck was clearing. Waiters moved between tables with the practiced efficiency of a Cunard crew on the final day of a transatlantic crossing. Passengers who had eaten early were already on the promenade decks, taking the air, watching the Irish coast slide past on the port side. The sea was calm. The visibility was variable, haze in patches, then clear stretches, but the ship was close enough to land that passengers could see the green of the Old Head of Kinsale.

Some had gone below to pack for Liverpool. Some were in the lounge. Some were sleeping. The ship was making eighteen knots, a reduced speed. Captain William Thomas Turner had ordered the slower pace in response to fog encountered earlier in the crossing. He was now on the bridge, or near it, navigating by the land he could see.

The Lusitania was nearing the end of her 202nd crossing, bound for Liverpool from New York, and was scheduled to dock at the Prince’s Landing Stage later that afternoon. The voyage had been routine, slowed by fog but otherwise unremarkable. The German embassy’s warning, printed in New York newspapers before departure, had caused some anxiety among passengers. But the days at sea had eroded that anxiety. The Atlantic had been calm. The ship had performed as she always performed. The passengers had settled into the rhythms of a liner crossing: the meals, the walks, the deck games, the reading, the naps. By the seventh of May, with land in sight, the voyage felt nearly over. It was Friday afternoon. They would be in Liverpool by evening.

Among the passengers were people whose names would later be entered into the record of the disaster. Charles Lauriat, the American publisher, was on deck. Sir Hugh Lane, the art collector and nationalist, was aboard. Socialite Theodate Pope was traveling with her companion Edwin Friend. These names would survive because the survivors carried them. Most of the 1, 264 passengers were not notable. They were families, businessmen, immigrants, musicians, nurses, children. They had paid for passage on a Cunard liner because Cunard was the standard. The Lusitania was the ship that had won the Blue Riband. She was fast, reliable, and British. In May 1915, that was enough.

The torpedo covered 700 metres in roughly forty seconds. At forty knots, it ran just below the surface, its depth set to two fathoms. The compressed air that propelled it left a trail of disturbed water, a streak of foam, a wake, that was visible from the deck of a ship if anyone was looking in the right direction at the right moment.

Someone was.

The first sightings of the torpedo track came from passengers and crew on the starboard side. The accounts would later vary in their specifics. Some said the track was visible for a full minute. Others said it appeared suddenly. But they converged on the same essential observation. A streak of foam. A line of disturbed water, moving rapidly toward the ship. One survivor described it as a wake. Another described it as a streak of bubbles. The descriptions were consistent enough to be credible and varied enough to be honest. No two people see the same thing from the same angle at the same speed. But they all saw the same thing: a torpedo, running on the surface, aimed at the Lusitania’s starboard side.

The warnings came too late. Some were shouted. Some were silent, the hand pointing, the face turning. There was no time. The torpedo was moving at forty knots. The ship was moving at eighteen. The closing speed was the sum of their vectors. Even if the helmsman had seen the track the moment it became visible, even if the wheel had been put over instantly, the Lusitania could not have turned in time. She was 785 feet long. Her rudder was enormous. Her turning circle was measured in hundreds of yards. The torpedo was already inside that circle.

The impact came at 2: 10 in the afternoon. The torpedo struck the Lusitania on the starboard side, just forward of the bridge. The location matters. Forward of the bridge means the strike came in the area of the bow, below the waterline, in a section of the hull that contained cargo holds and lower deck spaces. The torpedo’s warhead detonated on contact with the hull plating. The explosion drove inward. Steel plating buckled and tore. Rivets sheared. The sea entered.

The sound reached the bridge as a deep concussion, a shudder that traveled through the hull and the deckplates and the superstructure. Passengers below felt it more directly. Those in the dining saloon felt the deck lurch. Those on the promenade decks felt the ship heel to port, then begin to right herself, then heel again. The first impression for many was not of an explosion but of a collision, as though the Lusitania had struck something. Some thought she had hit a rock. Some thought she had been in a collision with another vessel. The idea of a torpedo was not the first thought. It was, for many, not the second.

Schwieger’s diary records the impact as observed through the periscope before he lowered it. The torpedo struck. He saw the detonation. He saw, or believed he saw, a second explosion following the first, a larger, more violent eruption that sent debris and smoke upward from the ship’s hull. The diary notes this second explosion without explanation. He did not claim to know what caused it. He recorded that he observed it.

The second explosion would become the central controversy of the Lusitania disaster. But in the first seconds after the torpedo’s impact, there was no controversy. There was only the fact of the wound. The hull was open to the sea. The water was entering. The ship was beginning to list.

The Lusitania was designed with longitudinal bulkheads, internal walls running the length of the ship, intended to contain flooding in the event of damage. These bulkheads were a standard feature of Cunard’s express liners. They were meant to keep the ship afloat if one compartment was breached. But the torpedo’s impact had not breached one compartment. The explosion had torn a hole in the hull plating forward of the bridge, and the damage extended across multiple sections. The longitudinal bulkheads, rather than containing the flood, may have contributed to the list by trapping water on the starboard side. The ship began to heel to starboard almost immediately. The angle increased. The deck tilted. Tables slid. Chairs moved. Glasses and cups fell from surfaces that were no longer level.

On the bridge, Turner felt the impact and knew at once that his ship had been struck. The question of whether he knew it was a torpedo or suspected a mine would later be debated in testimony. What is not debated is that the Lusitania was wounded. The course that Turner had been holding, a steady line past the Old Head of Kinsale, close to land, was now irrelevant. The ship was no longer under full control. Her speed was dropping. Her bow was settling. Her list was increasing. The eighteen knots that had been a routine cruising speed became, within seconds, a memory. The engines would lose power as the flooding reached the boiler rooms. The ship would slow, and then she would stop, and then she would begin the final phase of her sinking.

The torpedo had made the hole. The hole was below the waterline. The sea was entering at a rate that the ship’s pumps could not match. The Lusitania was 785 feet long and 31, 500 tons. She was one of the largest vessels afloat. But a hole below the waterline is a hole below the waterline, and the sea does not negotiate. The water entered the cargo holds. It entered the lower deck spaces. It rose through the ship’s internal structure, finding passages and openings that the designers had not anticipated. The list increased. The deck angle reached a point where moving across it required effort. Then it reached a point where moving across it required hands and knees. Then it reached a point where moving was not possible.

The passengers who had been finishing lunch, who had been reading on the promenade deck, who had been watching the coast, who had been packing for Liverpool, were now standing on a deck that was tilting beneath them. The ship was wounded. The list was growing. The sea was visible where it should not have been, rising along the hull, covering the portholes, reaching the promenade deck. The Lusitania was settling by the bow and heeling to starboard.

The angle of heel was already enough to make the port-side lifeboats difficult to swing out. The starboard-side boats were on the high side, hanging away from the hull, difficult to lower. The ship’s crew had not drilled for this. The passengers had not been instructed. The boats were the standard Cunard lifeboats, wooden, heavy, designed to be lowered by davits that required the ship to be level. The ship was not level.

The torpedo had done its work. The weapon had been aimed, fired, and detonated as designed. The gyroscopic mechanism had held the torpedo on its course. The depth setting of two fathoms had placed the warhead at the correct depth to strike the hull below the waterline. The compressed-air engine had driven it at forty knots across 700 metres of open water. The warhead had detonated on contact. The hole had been made. The sea had entered. Everything that followed, the second explosion, the list, the boats that could not be launched, the eighteen minutes, the dead in the Queenstown sheds, the inquiry, the diplomatic crisis, flowed from this act. A torpedo had been fired at a passenger liner. The torpedo had struck. The ship was sinking.

Schwieger’s diary does not record remorse. It records the technical details of the engagement and the observation of the result. The diary is a combat document, written by a naval officer in the field, describing an action that fell within his orders. The German declaration of 4 February 1915 had established a U-boat blockade and war zone around the British Isles. Any vessel in that zone was, by the terms of the declaration, a legitimate target. The Lusitania was in that zone. As a British-registered vessel flying the British flag, she was, under the rules of unrestricted submarine warfare as the German Admiralty defined them, a target. Schwieger fired. The torpedo ran. The ship was struck.

The disconnect between the two tracks is the thing that holds this moment. On U-20, a man at a periscope made a calculation. Range, bearing, speed, angle. He ordered a torpedo fired. The weapon left the tube. He observed the impact. He lowered the periscope. The act was naval warfare, conducted under orders, within a declared war zone, against an enemy vessel. On the Lusitania, 1, 264 passengers and 693 crew members were finishing lunch. Books were being read on the promenade decks. Families were watching the coast of Ireland from the rail. Twenty kilometres separated them from Queenstown. Hours separated them from Liverpool. The deck was level. The sea was calm. The day was Friday. The time was 2: 10 in the afternoon.

The torpedo crossed the distance between these two worlds in less than a minute. In that minute, the Lusitania stopped being a passenger liner and became a casualty of war. The transformation was not gradual. It was instantaneous. The detonation was the hinge. The hole in the hull was the point through which all the subsequent events flowed. The sea entered. The ship listed. The boats could not be launched. The crew could not reach their stations. The passengers could not reach the boats. The ship sank in eighteen minutes. The dead were counted in Queenstown. The diplomatic notes were drafted in Washington and Berlin. The inquiry was convened in London. All of it began with a torpedo fired from a submerged position 700 metres to starboard, at a ship that was finishing lunch.

The wound was forward of the bridge, below the waterline, on the starboard side. The sea came through it. The ship began to list. The list would not stop.
