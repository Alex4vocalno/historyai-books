Chapter 17

The Editor’s Demand

The number remained in circulation, detached from its caveats, traveling through memoranda and newspaper columns and parliamentary answers, acquiring the solidity of repeated use. But in the days when it was still being born—when the Ministry of Health’s statisticians were still adding columns and the undertakers were still turning families away—a different kind of reckoning had already begun.

On the morning of Monday, 15 December 1952, Fleet Street maintained its customary posture: the great presses of the national dailies already thundering, the Times composing room setting type for the late edition, the evening papers calculating what their morning rivals had missed. The smog had lifted four days before. The wind had returned on Tuesday, 9 December, and by Wednesday the sky over London showed its ordinary December grey, the color of washed slate, which passed for clarity. The cattle at the Smithfield Show had been buried or rendered. The hospitals had begun emptying their corridors of the temporary beds. And the newspapers, which had reported the fog as weather, as inconvenience, as the annual nuisance of a London December, now faced the first arriving evidence that something else had occurred.

The Times that Monday carried a brief item on page six, below the fold, noting that the London County Council had recorded a considerable increase in deaths during the week of the fog. The figure mentioned was 1, 085 excess deaths for the week ending 6 December, with the council’s medical officer of health adding, cautiously, that the full count was not yet complete. The Daily Mirror, with its larger circulation and its different audience, gave the story more room: a photograph of a bus conductor with a flare, held from the previous week’s coverage, accompanied by a paragraph noting that the fog’s death toll was now believed to have passed 1, 000.

Neither paper used the word smog. Neither suggested that the deaths constituted anything other than a natural disaster, the unfortunate consequence of unusual weather upon the elderly and the infirm. The editorial pages were silent on the matter. The leaders that day addressed the Egyptian situation, the steel nationalization debate, the cost of living. The fog was weather. Weather was not politics.

Yet this particular event was part of a long and lethal pattern: since the second half of the 19th century, severe smog episodes had become a common occurrence in London, with industrialization causing an increase in air pollution that often killed hundreds of the city’s inhabitants when it amassed in great quantities during the winter months. The Great Smog of 1952 was not an anomaly; it was an escalation.

But the machinery of Fleet Street was already in motion, and its motion was driven by something more than the official pronouncements that arrived each morning in the lobby of the Press Association. The newspapers had received, in the days since the fog lifted, an unanticipated volume of correspondence. Readers wrote not to complain of delayed trains or cancelled football matches, but to describe what they had witnessed: the walk home with a hand on the wall, the relative who had seemed merely tired and was found dead in the morning, the persistent cough that would not clear.

The letters pages of the provincial papers had been quicker to reflect this shift. The Manchester Guardian, with its distance from London and its tradition of independent judgment, had already published on Saturday, 13 December, a brief note connecting the pea-souper to reports of increased mortality. The Yorkshire Post, the Birmingham Gazette, the Glasgow Herald—all carried, in their London correspondence columns, the first hints that the official story was incomplete.

The transformation did not occur simultaneously across all titles. It proceeded by editorial decision, by the accumulation of evidence that could not be ignored, by the competitive pressure of a market in which no newspaper wished to be seen as suppressing a story that its rivals were pursuing.

The Evening Standard, whose evening deadline allowed it to incorporate morning developments, was among the first to shift its frame. On Tuesday, 16 December, its front page carried a report from its own medical correspondent, noting that hospital admissions for bronchitis and pneumonia had risen by forty percent during the fog week and quoting, without attribution, a senior physician at the London Hospital who called the event the worst public health disaster in his professional experience. The physician was not named. The correspondent had obtained the quote by telephone, the physician speaking on condition that his name be withheld. But the words were in print, and they could not be unprinted.

The Daily Herald, voice of the trade union movement and the Labor left, moved more slowly. Its editorial line in the immediate aftermath had emphasized the hardship of working people, the dockers who had lost wages when the Thames froze to navigation, the bus crews who had worked double shifts in impossible conditions. The death toll, when it appeared in the Herald’s pages, was framed as a class issue: the poor suffered most because they lived in the most polluted districts, burned the cheapest coal, inhabited the housing where damp and cold already weakened their lungs.

This was true, as far as it went, but it was not yet the full truth. The Herald’s editor, Percy Cudlipp, was a cautious man who had survived the political turbulence of the 1930s and had no desire to be accused of sensationalism. He held the story back for two days while his reporters sought confirmation that the government itself acknowledged the scale of mortality.

The confirmation, when it came, arrived not from the Ministry of Health but from the London County Council. On Wednesday, 17 December, the council’s public health committee released a preliminary tabulation showing 2, 062 excess deaths for the two-week period encompassing the fog. The figure was not comparable to the Ministry’s later estimate of four thousand; it measured a different period, used a different baseline, and included no attempt to distinguish between deaths caused by the fog and deaths associated with it.

But it was a government number, issued by a government body, and it could be quoted without fear of libel. The Evening News led its late edition with the council’s figure. The Star, the last of the London evening papers to maintain a separate editorial staff, gave it a banner headline: 2, 000 DEAD IN LONDON FOG. The morning papers, committed to their earlier editions, could only follow in their second editions or the following day.

The Times, confronted with this development, faced a characteristic dilemma. Its readership expected sobriety; its tradition demanded that it lead on matters of confirmed fact rather than preliminary report.

The council’s figure appeared on page eight, in a brief item noting that the recent fog had been followed by an increase in mortality. The leader page remained silent.

But the correspondence columns, which the editor controlled less directly, began to receive letters that could not be ignored. A physician from St Bartholomew’s Hospital wrote to describe the admission ward during the fog’s final day. He observed that they received patients who were not ill but merely old, whose respiratory function had been adequate for ordinary London air but proved inadequate for what the air had become. They died, many of them, not of pneumonia but of suffocation, their blood so poorly oxygenated that consciousness could not be maintained. The letter was published on Thursday, 18 December, with a cautionary note from the editor observing that individual experience, however vividly described, cannot establish general conclusions.

The physician’s letter was followed by others. A statistician from University College London wrote to point out that the council’s figure of 2, 062 excess deaths, if projected across the full population at risk, implied a mortality rate that exceeds any recorded natural disaster in British history, including the influenza pandemic of 1918-19 when measured against population. This was, the statistician acknowledged, a rough calculation; the final figures might be lower, or they might be higher. But the order of magnitude was established. The fog had not killed hundreds. It had killed thousands. And the killing had occurred not in a moment, as in a bombing raid or a railway accident, but over days, while the city continued its ordinary business, while the government issued no warnings and took no precautions, while the coal burned and the power stations maintained their output and the air grew thick enough to taste.

The Daily Mirror, with its instinct for the human story, found its angle in the undertakers.

Its reporters visited funeral directors in the East End, in South London, in the industrial suburbs where the fog had been thickest. They returned with accounts that the paper published on Friday, 19 December, under the headline COFFINS RUN OUT IN FOG DEATHS. The story was specific: W. English & Son, undertakers of Bow, had been forced to borrow coffins from three neighbouring firms; Harrison & Sons of Camberwell had erected a temporary chapel of rest in their garage; the cremation authorities at Golders Green and Streatham had extended their operating hours to midnight and still could not clear the backlog. The Mirror’s reporter had obtained, from a cooperative funeral director, a figure: 340 burials in the week of the fog, against a normal weekly average of 180.

The paper did not attempt to extrapolate this across London’s hundred-odd funeral establishments, but the implication was clear. The council’s figure of 2, 062 was not an aberration. It was, if anything, an understatement.

The Mirror’s story forced the hand of its rivals. The Daily Express, which had given the fog minimal coverage during its duration and had treated the aftermath as a weather story, now assigned two reporters to investigate the mortality figures. The Express’s editor, Arthur Christiansen, was a showman who understood that a story ignored was a circulation opportunity surrendered. He demanded the real number by Monday’s edition. His reporters worked through the weekend, telephoning coroners’ offices, hospital mortuaries, the registrar general’s department. They found what the Mirror had found: local systems overwhelmed, temporary mortuaries established in schools and church halls, burial delays of two weeks or more.

The Express led Monday, 22 December with a composite figure of up to 3, 000 dead, attributed to official sources, and accompanied by a photograph of a queue outside a funeral director’s premises in Poplar. The photograph showed women in headscarves, men in work clothes, the ordinary costumery of working-class London, waiting on a pavement that still bore the black residue of the fog. The image needed no caption to communicate its meaning.

The accumulation of journalistic evidence began to exert pressure on the official narrative. The Ministry of Health, which had maintained since 9 December that no special measures had been required or contemplated, now found itself responding to questions that arrived not only from the opposition benches but from the press lobby. The ministry’s press officer, a civil servant named H.J.B. Marshall who had served in the same capacity through the war and the post-war austerity, adopted a strategy of partial acknowledgment.

Yes, mortality had increased. No, the final figures were not yet available. The ministry was studying the matter. The minister would make a statement in due course.

These formulations, which had served to deflect inquiry during the war years, now appeared in print as evidence of evasion. The Daily Telegraph, traditionally sympathetic to Conservative governments, published on 23 December an editorial noting that the Ministry of Health’s reluctance to provide figures has itself become a story, and asking whether the public interest is served by official reticence regarding a public disaster.

The Telegraph’s editorial marked a significant shift. The paper’s readership included the professional and administrative classes from which the government drew its support; its criticism could not be dismissed as partisan opposition. More importantly, the Telegraph had access to sources within the ministries that the popular papers could not reach. Its lobby correspondent, reporting on 24 December, noted that ministers are understood to be divided on whether to establish a formal inquiry, with the Minister of Housing and Local Government, Harold Macmillan, opposing any measure that would prejudice the coal industry’s position and the Minister of Health, Iain Macleod, inclined to favour a limited technical investigation. The sourcing was vague—ministers are understood—but the information was accurate, and its publication made the division public knowledge.

The press coverage had by Christmas Eve established a new framework for understanding the event. It was no longer the fog. It was the smog, a term that appeared with increasing frequency in the popular papers, or the Great Smog, the formulation that the Daily Mail employed in its Christmas Eve leader. The shift in terminology carried a shift in attribution. Fog was weather, natural, blameless. Smog was manufactured, the product of human activity, the consequence of choices that could have been made differently. The Great Smog was an event, with a date and a death toll and a demand for accounting.

The holiday interposed a partial pause. The papers published reduced editions on Christmas Day and Boxing Day, and the ministries operated with skeleton staffs. But the machinery of journalism continued to turn. The Sunday Pictorial, preparing its 28 December edition, assigned a reporter to investigate the government’s coal policy. The reporter discovered, or was directed to, the continuing sale of nutty slack, the low-grade coal dust that produced maximum smoke and minimum heat, which the National Coal Board distributed through government-subsidized merchants at prices the poor could afford. The Pictorial’s story, published under the headline THE COAL THAT KILLS, connected the specific fuel to the specific deaths. In the streets where the fog was thickest, the smoke was thickest, and the smoke was thickest where nutty slack was burned. The story named no official and proposed no policy. It simply established the connection, and let the connection speak.

The connection was taken up by the letters pages. A reader from Deptford wrote to the News Chronicle to describe her family’s fuel allotment. She explained that they were given no choice. The merchant brings what the Board sends. This winter it has been nutty slack, which burns badly and smokes constantly. Her husband was a docker, and his chest was not good. During the fog he could not breathe in their own front room. They kept the fire low to reduce the smoke, and froze. A reader from Camberwell, responding in the same paper, noted that the government which cannot prevent fog might at least refrain from selling the fuel that makes it deadly. The letters accumulated, each one a small contribution to a growing structure of public accusation.

The new year brought no relief to the government’s position. The Sunday Times, in its first edition of 1954, published a lengthy analysis by its medical correspondent, summarizing the evidence that had accumulated in the press and adding new material from a source identified only as a senior official of the Ministry of Health. The official, speaking anonymously, confirmed that the ministry’s internal estimate of excess deaths now stood at approximately 4, 000, with the possibility that further analysis will reveal a higher figure. The official added, in a sentence that the correspondent quoted directly, that the meteorological conditions were unprecedented, but the atmospheric pollution was not. This was the first acknowledgment, from any government source, that the disaster had been preventable.

The Sunday Times analysis was read in the ministries on Monday morning, 4 January 1953, with the recognition that the press had established a narrative that the government could no longer control. The figure of four thousand, which the ministry had hoped to release in its own time and with its own framing, was now in public circulation. Worse, it was accompanied by the interpretation that the ministry had sought to suppress: that the deaths were not a natural disaster but a policy failure, the consequence of prioritizing cheap coal over clean air, industrial output over public health, the convenience of the state over the lives of its citizens.

The press coverage had achieved this transformation through a method that was not itself new, but that had not been applied to environmental disaster in British experience. The newspapers had treated the smog not as a single event to be reported once and filed, but as a developing story to be pursued over weeks, with each day’s revelations building upon the last. They had used the official figures, when available, but had not waited for them, constructing their own estimates from local sources, from funeral directors and hospital administrators and coroners’ officers who had no collective interest in concealment. They had given space to individual testimony, the letters from readers that accumulated into a collective witness, and they had connected this testimony to structural analysis, the policy decisions regarding coal quality and power station location that had made individual suffering systemic.

The result was a new kind of public knowledge. The smog was no longer a matter of weather, to be discussed in the past tense and forgotten with the season. It was a political fact, a charge against the government that required response. The newspapers had not themselves proposed solutions; that was not their function. They had established that solutions were required, that the present state of affairs was intolerable, that the cost of inaction had been measured in thousands of deaths and would be measured again if nothing changed.

The pressure that the press had generated now converged upon the point where governmental decisions were made. The journalists who had pursued the story through the Christmas season, who had telephoned undertakers and transcribed letters from dockers’ wives, had constructed a demand that could not be ignored. The number that traveled through their columns—four thousand dead, perhaps more—had acquired a different solidity than the number that traveled through the ministry’s memoranda. It was a public number, a number that belonged to the city and to the nation, a number that required explanation and required remedy. The newspapers had done what the official tally alone could not do. They had made the smog belong to everyone who breathed, and everyone who breathed now waited to see what would be done.
