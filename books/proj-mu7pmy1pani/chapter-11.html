Chapter 11

The Undertaker’s Inventory

The telephone began ringing before the coal fire in the front office had warmed the room, and the undertaker understood before he reached for the receiver that the rhythm of his day had already been shattered. At the family firm in Lambeth, established when Victoria still had decades to reign, he had learned to measure mortality by seasons and ceremonies, by the predictable surge of winter deaths among the elderly and the quiet months when his workshop stood idle.

The voice on the line asked if he could attend to a death in a house three streets away, an elderly woman found by her neighbour. He made the note in his ledger, the same leather-bound book his father had used, and reached to replace the receiver when it rang again. A hospital mortuary was calling, asking when he could collect. Then another hospital. Then a private address in Kennington where the doctor had signed the certificate an hour ago and the family were waiting.

By nine o’clock he had stopped writing names and begun tallying numbers. The hearse, a Daimler he had purchased in 1948 after years of saving, sat in the cobbled yard with its engine cold. His assistant, a nephew who had returned from national service the previous spring, reported that the vehicle would not start. The corrosive fog had settled into the carburettor, into the electrical connections, into every mechanical cavity that required air to function. They pushed the hearse to the street and tried to bump-start it down the gentle slope toward the Thames, but the engine caught only to die again at the corner, and they had left it blocking the pavement while they returned for the older vehicle, the pre-war Austin that his father had kept for emergencies and that he had considered selling for scrap.

The Austin started, barely, and they drove to their first call with the headlights on though it was mid-morning, the beams reaching no further than the bonnet and dissolving into the grey that pressed against the windscreen. The undertaker had driven through the Blitz, through blackouts, through fogs that had seemed at the time to represent some natural limit of visibility. This was different. This was not darkness but erasure, the world reduced to what his headlights could touch and no more. He found the house by counting doorways from the corner, by the feel of the kerb under his wheels, by the faint yellow rectangle that materialised where a window tried to hold back the grey.

The body was ready, the certificate signed, the family assembled in the front room with the curtains drawn against a morning that had brought no light. He performed the measurements with his tape, noted them in his book, and explained that the coffin would be delivered the following day. The daughter asked if it could be sooner. He did not tell her that he had four other collections waiting, that his workshop held only two finished coffins, that the suppliers he had telephoned that morning had reported their own shortages and backlogs. He said only that he would do what he could, and he meant it, and the meaning of it had already begun to shift under the weight of the day.

By noon the undertaker had collected seven bodies. The Austin’s rear compartment, designed for one coffin or two if they were small, held a temporary arrangement of blankets and canvas sheets that he had improvised from the workshop stores. He drove with the windows open despite the cold, despite the burning in his throat and eyes, because the closed vehicle filled with the sweet, dense smell of the fog and something else, something he did not name even to himself.

At the mortuary of St. Thomas’s Hospital, where he had made deliveries for twenty years, the attendant met him at the service entrance with an expression the undertaker had not seen before, not in 1940, not in 1944, not in the worst weeks of any crisis he could remember. The mortuary was full. The bodies he had brought could not be received. The hospital had begun using the coal cellar, the corridor outside the pathology laboratory, any space that could be secured and cooled. The attendant gave him an address in Southwark, a temporary facility established in a disused warehouse, and told him that the London County Council was coordinating collections now, that the normal procedures no longer applied, that he should telephone a number that the attendant wrote on a scrap of paper with fingers that shook slightly.

This was how the disaster moved through the city: from atmosphere to lung, from lung to ward, from ward to mortuary, from mortuary to the earth that would receive what the air had claimed.

The undertaker did not telephone. He drove to the warehouse because he could not bear to return to his parlour with the bodies still in his charge, because the motion of driving was the only response he could find to the accumulation of death that his ledger recorded. The warehouse was not difficult to locate; a line of vehicles stretched along the street, hearses from three other firms, a municipal ambulance, a private van whose driver stood smoking in the grey murk with the indifference of someone who had long since passed through surprise.

They waited for two hours. The undertaker did not leave his vehicle. He watched the fog move in the light of the streetlamps, which had been burning since morning, a slow circulation that brought no clearing, no change, only the persistence of conditions that his body had begun to interpret as normal.

His eyes burned. His chest ached with a tightness he attributed to tension, to the unfamiliar posture of waiting, to anything but the air he was breathing.

When his turn came, the warehouse revealed itself as a provisional solution already overwhelmed. Men in warehouse coats and rubber aprons directed the transfer of bodies to metal racks that had been installed in rows, each shelf numbered in chalk, each body tagged with a paper label tied to the wrist or ankle. The undertaker helped carry his own burdens, something he had not done since his apprenticeship, lifting the canvas-wrapped forms from his vehicle to the waiting shelf, reading the numbers aloud for the clerk who recorded them in a ledger larger than any he had seen. The clerk was young, perhaps twenty, employed by the council for purposes that had nothing to do with death. He wrote the numbers without looking at what they described, and the undertaker recognised in this a mercy that he had not expected to find.

He returned to his parlour at four in the afternoon, the Austin empty, his clothes smelling of the warehouse and something else that would not wash out. His wife met him at the door with tea that he could not drink, and with the news that four more calls had come while he was out, that two families had visited in person to ask why he had not come, that the telephone was ringing again even as she spoke.

He went to his workshop, a brick outbuilding that had been a stable in his grandfather’s time, and counted his coffins. Two finished, oak veneer, ordered by a family who had prepaid for a funeral next week. One half-assembled, the shell complete but the lining not yet tacked. Three rough boxes in pine, kept for cases where the family could not afford better, or where the circumstances required something simple.

He had never in his career begun a day with so few finished coffins in his possession.

The suppliers he telephoned reported the same conditions. The largest manufacturer, a firm in Camden that had supplied his family for three generations, had ceased taking orders. Their workshop was operating at capacity, their stock depleted, their workforce struggling to reach the factory through air that made breathing a labor. Smaller firms, the one-man operations that filled gaps in the market, had simply closed, their proprietors ill or their premises unreachable. The undertaker who answered one call, a competitor in Walworth whose territory he had respected for fifteen years, spoke with the flatness of exhaustion and suggested that they might coordinate, might share resources, might find some way to meet demand that neither could satisfy alone.

He worked through the night in his workshop, his nephew assisting with the tasks that required two sets of hands, his wife bringing food that went cold on the bench. They assembled the rough boxes first, the pine shells that could be finished in hours rather than days, that could be lined with the white cotton sheeting his wife cut from a bolt purchased for curtains she would now never make. By morning they had four coffins ready, and the undertaker drove again to the warehouse to collect bodies for which families had made arrangements, for which services had been scheduled, for which the normal sequence of death and burial had somehow continued despite everything that had interrupted it.

The warehouse had changed in the hours of darkness. The racks had extended to fill another room, the chalk numbers running into four digits, the clerks working in shifts that followed no pattern the undertaker could discern. He presented his list of numbers, the identifiers that connected his ledger to the warehouse’s inventory, and waited while a boy not yet sixteen searched the rows with a torch whose beam carved small cylinders of visibility in the grey that seemed to have penetrated even here, even into this space that should have been sealed against the world outside.

The boy found three of his four. The fourth, an elderly man collected from a house in Vauxhall, had been moved, misnumbered, or simply lost in the accumulation that had exceeded any system of tracking.

The undertaker stood in the warehouse aisle, breathing air that tasted of concrete and formaldehyde and the particular density of many bodies in confined space, and understood that the fourth body would not be found in time for the funeral he had promised. He took the three he could identify and drove back through streets that had become familiar in their strangeness, the landmarks that emerged from grey and dissolved again, the pedestrians who materialised at his bumper and vanished into the murk behind.

The funeral took place at eleven, in a church whose interior seemed impossibly bright after the days outside, the candles and electric lights creating a space of visibility that felt almost violent in its clarity. The family had not asked about the fourth coffin, the body that would not be present, and the undertaker did not volunteer the information. He performed his role with the movements that his grandfather had taught his father, that his father had taught him, the choreography of grief that had not changed in its essentials since the firm was founded.

But he noticed, as he had never noticed before, the gaps in the congregation, the empty pews where neighbours should have sat, the absence of elderly relatives who had been unable to travel through air that would have killed them before they reached the church doors. The funeral was complete and incomplete, a ritual performed in the knowledge of all that it could not include.

By afternoon the undertaker had exhausted his supply of coffins again. He drove to the Camden manufacturer, not telephoning this time but presenting himself in person, demanding to speak with the manager, invoking relationships that had sustained his family through decades of mutual dependence.

The manager received him in an office that overlooked the workshop floor, where men worked at speed that suggested panic, where the normal distinctions between grades of timber and finish had collapsed into the single imperative of production. The manager was apologetic and absolute. No new orders could be accepted. Existing orders would be fulfilled by priority of date, not of need, and the undertaker’s firm was far down a list that grew longer with every hour.

The manager suggested alternatives, firms in Essex and Kent that might still have stock, that might be reachable by roads that had not yet been closed by visibility, that might be willing to sell to a London undertaker at prices that would absorb any profit from the funeral itself.

The undertaker drove to Essex. The fog thinned as he crossed the river at Woolwich, not clearing but becoming penetrable, allowing him to see perhaps thirty feet where before he had seen ten. He found the Essex firm by luck and persistence, arriving at closing time to find the proprietor locking his workshop with the haste of a man who had worked beyond his normal hours and wished only to return home.

The undertaker made his appeal, not to commerce but to solidarity, to the shared condition of men who handled death for a living and who recognised in the current crisis something that exceeded commercial opportunity. The Essex man sold him six coffins, finished but unlined, at a price that would require explanation to his accountant and perhaps to his conscience.

They loaded them into the Austin with straps and blankets, a precarious tower that reduced his visibility to nothing and forced him to drive by memory and touch, feeling the road through his wheels, correcting the drift of the load by the pressure of his hands on the wheel.

He reached Lambeth at nine in the evening, having been twelve hours in motion for a journey that should have taken three. His nephew helped unload, and they stored the coffins in the workshop where his grandfather had once kept horses, in the space that had never held so many finished containers for death. The telephone was ringing.

His wife had stopped answering it. He made himself lift the receiver and heard a voice he did not recognise, a man identifying himself as the secretary of the London Association of Funeral Directors, calling to inform him of an emergency meeting scheduled for the following morning, to discuss the crisis in burial services, to coordinate a response to the medical officers who were finally acknowledging what the undertakers had known for days.

The secretary’s voice carried the particular tone of official communication, the translation of emergency into procedure, and the undertaker recognised in it the first indication that the crisis might produce not only death but record, not only suffering but account.

He slept for three hours on the couch in his office, waking to the sound of his nephew starting the Austin’s engine, preparing for the day’s collections. The meeting was in Whitehall, in a room that the undertaker would not have found without assistance, in a building whose corridors spoke of authority that had nothing to do with the handling of bodies. He arrived late, having collected two deaths on the way, having left his nephew to manage three more that had come in overnight. The room held fourteen men, representatives of firms larger than his own, of municipal services, of the religious organisations that controlled many of London’s cemeteries. They sat in chairs that had been arranged in a semicircle, facing a table where a medical officer from the London County Council waited with papers that he shuffled but did not consult.

The meeting began with statement of fact. The council’s preliminary assessment suggested that deaths in the county had increased by approximately three hundred per day since the smog’s onset. This figure was provisional, subject to revision, almost certainly an underestimate. The normal capacity of the burial system, calculated on the basis of pre-crisis mortality, was approximately two hundred deaths per day. The gap between supply and demand was being managed through temporary storage, through extended hours at crematoria, through the emergency measures that the council had authorised and that the funeral industry was implementing. The medical officer spoke with the calm of someone who had rehearsed these sentences, who had translated the visible crisis into language that could be recorded in minutes and filed in archives.

The undertakers responded with particulars. The shortage of coffins, which the largest manufacturers had reported to their trade association three days before and which had only now reached official attention. The failure of transport, vehicles disabled by air that corroded metal and choked engines. The exhaustion of staff, men working eighteen-hour days in conditions that were themselves hazardous to health. The impossibility of maintaining standards, of providing to grieving families the service that the occasion required and that the profession demanded. One man, representing a firm in Kensington that buried the wealthy and the titled, spoke of clients who had offered any price for priority, for immediate service, for the normal sequence of death and dignity that money had always been able to secure. He had refused them. He was not certain, he said, that all his colleagues had done the same.

The meeting continued for two hours, producing resolutions that the undertaker recognised as necessary and insufficient. A central coordination office would be established. Coffin manufacturers would be requisitioned, their production directed to emergency priorities. Temporary mortuary facilities would be expanded, their operation standardised. The council would request assistance from surrounding counties, from the armed forces, from any source that could provide vehicles, personnel, space. The medical officer recorded these decisions with the satisfaction of a man who had transformed chaos into procedure, who had given to the unmanageable the appearance of management.

The undertaker returned to Lambeth in the afternoon, having missed the morning’s collections, having entrusted his nephew with responsibilities that the young man had not been trained to bear. He found the parlour in disorder, the telephone ringing unanswered, a woman waiting in the front room who had been there for an hour and whose grief had begun to curdle into anger. He attended to her, learned that her husband had died in the night, that she had been unable to reach a doctor until morning, that she had walked to his firm because no vehicle would come to her call. He measured the body where it lay, in the bedroom of a house three streets away, and promised a coffin by evening, a funeral within two days, services that he could not guarantee and that he would somehow provide.

The coffin came from his own workshop, one of the Essex pine boxes lined with his wife’s curtain material, finished with handles that he had removed from a coffin ordered by a family who would not now receive it. He drove to the warehouse himself, presented his number, waited while the clerks searched their ledgers for a body that had been recorded three days before and that might have been moved, misfiled, or simply absorbed into the mass that exceeded their system of accounting. They found it. He loaded it into the hearse, the Daimler that his nephew had finally coaxed into operation, and drove to the cemetery where the grave had been dug by men working in fog that reduced their efficiency by half and their visibility to nothing.

The burial was brief. The minister, an elderly man who had conducted the service with his handkerchief pressed to his mouth, departed immediately afterward, unable to remain in air that made each breath a conscious effort. The undertaker remained while the grave was filled, watching the mechanical operation of the digger that had replaced the manual labor of his grandfather’s time, that had transformed the final service into something that could be accomplished without the presence of mourners, without the witness of grief. He thought of the bodies in the warehouse, the numbered shelves, the clerks who recorded without looking, the system that had been improvised to manage what the normal procedures could not contain. He thought of the meeting in Whitehall, the medical officer’s careful language, the resolutions that would produce forms and files and eventually, perhaps, explanation.

The digger finished its work. The undertaker returned to his vehicle and found that the Daimler would not start again, that the fog had completed the corrosion that had begun three days before. He walked back to his parlour through streets that had become familiar in their strangeness, counting his steps, feeling the kerb with his feet, breathing air that he no longer expected to clear.

His nephew met him at the door with the news that seven more calls had come, that two hospitals had telephoned with urgent collections, that the council’s coordination office had sent a messenger with forms that required immediate completion. He sat at his desk, in the office where his grandfather had sat, where his father had sat, and began to write the names in his ledger, the same leather-bound book that had recorded deaths for seventy years, that had never held so many entries in so few days.

The forms from the council asked for numbers. He provided them, translating the particular into the statistical, the named into the counted. The image of an official communication about funeral service overload hands off the undeniable fact of mass death and raises the urgent question of its ongoing source.
