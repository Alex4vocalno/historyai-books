Chapter 11

The Lamb's First Stand

The barley was safe today, but the water was still rising.

Nell stood at the edge of the long field as the light began to fail, watching the dark line of the ditch through the mist. The repaired bank had held through another day, though she could see where the water pressed against it—an insistent weight, patient as a bruise. Her pocket hung heavy with the Board’s letter and the removed notice she’d worked free from the gatepost days ago, before the frost. She’d used the bright new nail to pry it loose, the metal still cold in her pocket beside the folded papers. She had not looked at either since.

Behind her, the farmhouse windows glowed. Kitty would be moving through her evening routine now—checking locks, counting the plates in the drying rack, settling into the patterns that held her world together. Three circles around the rim of a cup. Pause. Three more. The sight of that steady light loosened something in Nell’s chest, though it did not ease the tightness entirely.

She turned away from the ditch and walked toward the pasture where the flock gathered in the fading light. The ewes were due any day now. She had been watching them all week, noting the swell of flanks, the heavy stillness that came before lambing. In a spring this wet, every lambing carried extra risk. The cold seeped up through the ground, and the damp found its way into everything—wool, straw, the shallow folds of newborn skin.

The flock stood in a loose cluster near the old withy bed, their breath rising in soft clouds. Most of them looked ready to settle for the night, heads low, bodies still. But one ewe stood apart, near the edge of the drainage ditch that bordered the pasture. Her head was raised, her flanks twitching.

Nell stopped. The ewe turned to look at her, then lowered her head and strained.

The drizzle began again—a fine, cold mist that settled on Nell’s face and hands like a second skin. The light was going fast. She moved toward the ewe slowly, keeping her steps soft on the sodden ground, and saw the wet muzzle of a lamb already emerging, translucent hooves first.

The ewe strained again, and the lamb slipped out onto the wet grass. It was small—the smallest Nell had seen this season. Its head lolled against the ground, and it made no move to rise or shake the membrane from its face.

Nell knelt in the grass, her knees sinking into the cold mud, and cleared the lamb’s nose with her fingers. The lamb drew a thin breath, then another. But it did not stir. Its body lay limp against the earth, pale wet wool already soaking through with the mist.

“Come on,” Nell said, her voice low. She rubbed the lamb’s ribs with the heel of her hand, feeling the fragile cage of bones beneath the skin. “Come on now.”

The ewe turned and began to lick the lamb’s face, her tongue rough against the membrane. The lamb’s eyes flickered open—milky, unfocused—and closed again.

Footsteps behind her. She did not turn.

“Pass me the sack,” she said. “The one in my coat pocket.”

Amos was there before she finished speaking. He had come from the barn, drawn by the same instinct that had brought her out here. He pressed the burlap sack into her hand without a word, and Nell wrapped the lamb in it, lifting the small body clear of the wet ground.

The lamb’s head fell back against her wrist. Its breathing was shallow, irregular.

“The barn,” Nell said. She was already moving, cradling the lamb against her chest. “I need warm water and the feeding bottle.”

Amos did not answer. He was already walking toward the barn, his pace steady but quick.

Nell followed, her boots sucking at the mud with every step. The cold had settled into her fingers, and she pressed them against the lamb’s body through the sack, trying to share what warmth she had. The lamb’s heartbeat fluttered against her palm—a thin, rapid thing, like a moth beating against glass.

The barn rose dark against the grey sky. Amos had the lantern lit by the time Nell stepped inside, and the warm glow caught the dust motes drifting in the air. He had set out the feeding bottle on a shelf near the straw-strewn pen, and a bucket of warm water stood beside it, steam rising.

Nell knelt in the straw and unwrapped the lamb. It lay on its side, breathing in short gasps. Its legs were splayed, limp, and its mouth hung slightly open.

“Hand me the bottle,” she said.

Amos passed it to her. The milk inside was still warm—he must have prepared it while she was still in the field. Nell held the lamb’s head with one hand and pressed the rubber teat to its mouth. The lamb’s lips did not close around it.

Nell squeezed a drop of milk onto the lamb’s tongue and waited. The lamb swallowed—a small, convulsive movement—but did not suck.

“Again,” Amos said. His voice was quiet, watching from the shadows beyond the lantern’s reach.

Nell tried again. This time the lamb’s mouth moved, a weak attempt at drawing. A little milk disappeared, then the lamb’s head fell back.

“She’s too cold,” Nell said. “She needs warmth before she can feed.”

Amos moved without being asked. He crossed to a corner of the barn where a pile of clean sacking lay and brought it back, spreading it over the straw. Then he knelt beside Nell and took the lamb gently from her hands.

Nell watched as he settled the lamb against his chest, tucking the small body inside his coat. His large hands cradled the lamb’s head, steadying it. He began to rub the lamb’s side with slow, rhythmic strokes—nothing hurried, nothing forced.

Nell picked up the bottle again and waited.

The minutes stretched. Outside, the drizzle tapped against the barn’s wooden walls. The lantern hissed softly. Nell could hear the lamb’s breathing—still shallow, but steadier now.

Amos looked at her and nodded.

Nell moved closer and pressed the teat to the lamb’s mouth again. This time, the lamb’s lips closed around it. A weak suck, then another. Milk disappeared from the bottle.

“Keep going,” Amos said. “Don’t let her stop.”

Nell held the bottle steady, her free hand supporting the lamb’s jaw. The lamb sucked in earnest now—a halting rhythm, but real. The lamb’s eyes opened, clearer this time, and focused on Nell’s face.

She felt something shift in her chest—a loosening, a breath she hadn’t known she was holding.

When the bottle was empty, Amos loosened his coat and set the lamb on the straw. The lamb’s legs twitched. It tried to rise, collapsed, tried again. Amos placed his palm beneath its chest and lifted gently. The lamb’s hindquarters wavered, then steadied.

It stood.

The legs splayed slightly, and the lamb swayed, but it stood. Its head turned toward Nell, and it made a small sound—a thin bleat that seemed too large for its body.

Nell exhaled. Her hands were shaking, though she hadn’t noticed the cold until now.

“She’ll need to go back to the ewe,” Amos said. “Once she’s steady.”

Nell nodded. “Not tonight. She’s too weak. We’ll keep her here, keep her warm. I’ll bring the ewe in.”

“I’ll get her,” Amos said. He rose and moved toward the door, his steps silent on the straw.

Nell stayed where she was, watching the lamb. It had lain down again, but its head was up now, its eyes tracking her movements. The trembling had stopped. The lamb’s flanks rose and fell in a steady rhythm.

She thought of the letters in her pocket. The Board’s notice had named a date for the hearing—three weeks from now. Three weeks to gather evidence, to find arguments, to stand before men who had already made up their minds. Three weeks while the water kept rising.

The lamb made another sound, softer this time, and Nell reached out to stroke its head. The wool was still damp, but warmer now. She could feel the heartbeat beneath her fingers—still fast, but stronger.

Amos returned with the ewe. She came willingly, her eyes on the lamb, and lowered her head to nudge the small body. The lamb stirred and tried to rise again. This time it made it to its feet, swaying, and found the ewe’s udder.

Nell watched the lamb nurse. The ewe stood patient, her body angled to shield the lamb from the draft.

“She’ll make it,” Amos said.

Nell did not answer. She stayed where she was, watching the lamb feed, until the small body began to lose its unsteadiness. The lamb’s tail flicked—a sign of contentment, of life asserting itself.

She rose slowly, her knees stiff from the cold ground. Amos had moved back to the shadows, giving her space, but she could feel his presence there—the quiet reliability of him.

“Thank you,” she said.

He did not answer, but she heard him move toward the door, checking the latch.

Nell stayed a moment longer. The lamb had finished nursing and was standing beside the ewe, its legs planted more firmly now. Its eyes were bright. It would live.

She walked out of the barn into the misting dark. The farmhouse windows still glowed, and she could see Kitty’s silhouette moving behind the glass. The sight steadied her.

But as she crossed the farmyard, her eyes went to the ditch that ran along the edge of the long field. Even in the darkness, she could see the water level—higher than it should be, pressing against the repaired bank. The rain had been steady for days, and the fen was full, the water tables rising everywhere. The Board’s proposed dyke would cut through the long field, draining the water away from her land and into the drainage system that served the larger farms downstream. That was what they called “improvement.”

Nell’s hand went to her pocket. The letters were still there, folded together. She had not shown them to Kitty. She had not told her daughter the date of the hearing, or what it might mean. There was no need to add that weight to the weight Kitty already carried.

But the weight was there, whether she named it or not.

She reached the farmhouse door and paused with her hand on the latch. Behind her, the barn stood dark and silent. Inside, the lamb was warm, fed, standing on its own legs. A small victory. A life held in her hands and coaxed into staying.

Tomorrow, the water would still be rising.

Nell opened the door and stepped into the warmth and light.

Kitty was at the table, her hands wrapped around a cup of tea. She looked up as Nell entered, and her eyes went to Nell’s muddy boots, her damp coat.

“You were out long,” Kitty said.

Nell closed the door behind her and began to unbutton her coat. “Ewe was lambing. The lamb was small. Needed help.”

Kitty’s gaze stayed on her face. “Is it all right?”

“It is now.” Nell hung her coat on the hook by the door. “Amos is settling them in the barn for the night.”

Kitty nodded. She returned to her tea, her fingers tracing the rim of the cup in a slow, even circle. Nell watched her for a moment, noting the rhythm—three circles, pause, three circles. The counting calmed her. It was how she made sense of a world that shifted too often, too suddenly.

Nell crossed to the table and sat across from her daughter. She did not mention the letters in her pocket, or the hearing date, or the water rising in the ditches. There would be time for that later. There had to be time.

“The lamb stood,” Nell said. “Took milk from the bottle, then found the ewe. She’ll make it.”

Kitty looked up. Something in her face softened, the tight lines around her mouth easing. “Good,” she said. “That’s good.”

Nell reached across the table and covered Kitty’s hand with her own. The fingers beneath hers were cold, and she pressed warmth into them.

“We should eat,” Nell said. “Have you had anything?”

“Soup. From last night.” Kitty’s voice was steady now, the earlier tremor gone. “I kept some warm for you.”

Nell rose and went to the range, where the pot sat at the back, still faintly warm. She ladled soup into a bowl and brought it back to the table. The broth was thin, flavored with the last of the winter vegetables, but it was hot, and it filled the hollow that had opened in her chest.

She ate in silence, Kitty across from her, the house quiet around them. Outside, the drizzle continued, a soft sound against the windows. The lamp between them cast a steady glow, and in its light, Nell watched her daughter’s face—the pale skin, the hair parted precisely in the middle, the cardigan buttoned to the throat even here, in the warmth.

Kitty had screamed when she saw the notice nailed to the gatepost. The sound had torn through the morning, and Nell had come running to find her daughter standing rigid, her hands clenched at her sides, her face white with terror. The notice had broken something in Kitty’s world—a fixed point suddenly altered, a boundary violated. It had taken an hour to calm her, and longer still for Kitty to find her way back to speech.

But then something had shifted. Kitty had looked at the gatepost—at the small nail hole, the lighter patch of wood where the notice had been—and her face had changed. The terror had burned away into something harder, something fierce.

“Take it down,” she had said. “We take it down, and we don’t let them put it back.”

Nell had never seen that look in her daughter’s face before. It was the look of someone who had found something worth fighting for.

She finished her soup and set the bowl aside. Kitty had finished her tea and was watching her now, her fingers still now, resting on the table.

“Will the lamb be warm enough?” Kitty asked.

“I’ll check her again before I sleep,” Nell said. “Amos put down fresh straw. She’ll be fine.”

Kitty nodded. She rose and began to clear the table, her movements precise, each dish placed in its proper position in the drying rack. Nell watched her for a moment, then stood and reached for her coat.

“I’ll be back,” she said.

Kitty did not look up. “Take the lantern.”

Nell took the lantern from the hook by the door and stepped out into the night. The drizzle had eased, but the air was heavy with moisture, the ground soft beneath her boots. She crossed the yard toward the barn, her breath misting in the lantern’s glow.

Inside, the barn was warm with the smell of straw and animals. The ewe lay in the pen with the lamb tucked against her side, both of them breathing steadily. Nell hung the lantern on a hook and knelt beside the pen.

The lamb’s eyes were closed, but its ears twitched at the sound of her voice. Nell reached out and touched the small head, feeling the warmth of the wool, the steady heartbeat beneath.

She stayed there for a long moment, her hand resting on the lamb’s body. The night was quiet around her, broken only by the soft sounds of the animals, the distant drip of water from the eaves.

Tomorrow, she would need to check the ditch again. The bank was holding, but the water was rising, and the rain was not done. And tomorrow, she knew, she would see them—the wooden stakes the Board’s surveyor had placed, marking the path of the new dyke in a straight line through the long field. If they came to dig, they would destroy the best soil she had—the rich peat that her grandfather had drained, that her husband had worked, that she had fought to keep.

The letters in her pocket were a weight she could not set down. The hearing date was fixed. The process would move forward whether she was ready or not.

But here, in this moment, the lamb was warm and alive. The ewe lay beside her, breathing steadily. The barn held them both, sheltered from the wet dark outside.

Nell rose and took the lantern. She checked the latch on the door and stepped out into the night.

The farmhouse windows glowed across the yard, steady and bright. Kitty was inside, moving through her patterns, holding the world together one counted step at a time.

Nell walked toward that light. The ground was soft beneath her feet, and the water was rising, but the lamb had stood. She had held a life in her hands and refused to let it go.

Tomorrow, the fight would continue. But tonight, there was warmth, and shelter, and a small victory won against the dark.

She opened the door and stepped inside.
