Chapter 1

The Measure

The rain had stopped sometime in the night, leaving the yard slick and black. Nell Askew stepped out the back door into air that tasted of iron and wet earth. The cold went through her shawl like a hand. The pump handle gleamed with moisture; she crossed to it, pressed her palm flat against the metal, and let the chill settle into the bones of her wrist before she began to prime it. Three strokes to draw the water, the leather washer complaining with each pull. That sound was part of her now, woven into her mornings like the calling of birds from the reed beds.

Amos Reed was already in the lower field when she reached the gate. His figure bent over the drainage ditch, shovel cutting into the black peat, and the sight steadied her the way a level floor steadies a table. Two years since Thomas died, and the land still drained because she saw to it, because Amos saw to it when she could not. She pulled her shawl tighter against the wind that carried the smell of the fen—rotting vegetation, standing water, the sour sweetness of peat.

The gate latch stuck. She worked it with her thumb, the cold iron stubborn against her grip, and when it finally gave, her eyes caught on something that had no business being there. A rectangle of cream-colored paper, nailed to the oak post at eye level. The nail head was bright, new, not yet touched by the rust that crept over every other piece of metal on this farm.

She stopped. The wind fluttered the paper’s corner.

Nell stepped closer. The words were printed in black ink, precise and unyielding. NOTICE OF PROPOSED DRAINAGE WORKS. Beneath the title, smaller text arranged itself into paragraphs of such careful legal phrasing that she had to read them twice before the meaning settled into her. The Little Ouse Drainage Board intended to cut a new dyke through the long field. The work would commence upon completion of the survey. Objections were to be lodged in writing within fourteen days.

The date at the bottom was three days old.

“Mother?”

Kitty’s voice came from somewhere behind her. Nell did not turn. Her hand had found the edge of the paper, her fingers tracing the sharp crease where the wind had bent it against itself.

“What’s that on the gate?”

“Nothing you need to worry over.” The words came out before she could think them through. She pulled her hand back, tucked it into her apron pocket. “The hens need seeing to.”

Kitty appeared at her elbow, her face pale beneath the grey sky. She was twelve years old, thin as a reed, with her father’s dark hair and her mother’s stubborn jaw. Her hands clutched the empty feed bucket against her chest, knuckles white.

“He didn’t say good morning.”

“Who?”

“The man. The one with the sticks. He walked right past me like I wasn’t there.”

Nell’s head came up. She looked past Kitty, past the gate, out toward the long field that stretched away from the farmhouse in a flat dark expanse. A figure was moving across the distant black soil, stopping at intervals, bending to drive something into the ground.

“Inside,” Nell said. “Feed the hens and stay inside.”

“But—”

“Now, Kitty.”

The girl’s mouth set in a line that mirrored her mother’s. She turned and walked back toward the house, the bucket swinging against her leg, her shoulders hunched against the wind. At the door she paused, looked back, but Nell was already moving through the gate, her boots sinking into the soft earth with each step.

The long field had been Thomas’s pride. Forty acres of black peat, rich as cake, drained by ditches his father had dug and his grandfather before that. The crop rotation was written in his careful hand, pinned to the wall beside the kitchen table—potatoes, then wheat, then fallow. The long field had never failed them. Even in the wet spring of’09, when the pumps broke and the lower fields flooded, the long field had held.

Now a stranger walked across it, driving stakes into the soil.

Nell’s anger built slowly, a warmth spreading through her chest. She did not run. Running would admit something she was not ready to concede. Instead she walked, her pace steady, her eyes fixed on the figure ahead. The ground grew wetter as she approached the field’s center, the peat yielding beneath her weight with a sound like a sigh. She could hear the thud of the mallet now, see the wooden stakes standing in a line that cut straight across the furrows.

The man was younger than she had expected. Thirty, perhaps, with fair hair beneath a flat cap and spectacles that caught the flat light. He wore a coat of good wool, the sort that cost more than a week’s wages for a man like Amos, and his boots were sturdy but clean—the boots of someone who measured land rather than worked it. A leather case sat open beside him, brass instruments gleaming inside.

He did not notice her approach until she was close enough to see the notebook in his hand, the careful columns of numbers marching across the page.

“Good morning.” His voice was polite, measured. He closed the notebook and inclined his head. “I do apologize for intruding upon your land. I should have presented myself at the house.”

“You’ve nailed a notice to my gate.”

“I am aware. The board requires formal notification before works commence.”

“Requires.” Nell let the word sit between them. “And if I require you to remove it?”

The man—Philip Danvers, she assumed, though he had not offered his name—adjusted his spectacles with one finger. His expression did not change, but something in his posture shifted, a slight straightening that suggested he had not expected resistance quite so direct.

“The board has authority under the Act of 1894 to undertake necessary drainage improvements. The notice is a courtesy.”

“Courtesy.” She looked past him to the line of stakes, then back to his face. “You’ve walked my field. You’ve driven your markers into ground my husband’s family has worked for three generations. And you call it courtesy.”

“Mrs. Askew.” He said her name carefully, as if he had learned it from a document. “The proposed dyke will improve drainage for the entire district. The board’s engineers have determined that the current system is inadequate to handle the spring runoff. The new cut will relieve pressure on the main channel and prevent flooding in the lower fields—your lower fields included.”

“The lower fields flood because the pump needs seeing to before dusk.”

He blinked. The statement seemed to catch him off guard, though his expression remained polite.

“I see. The board’s assessment did not—”

“Your assessment walked past my daughter without speaking to her. Your assessment nailed a notice to my gate and called it courtesy. And now your assessment tells me that cutting my best field in two will save me from water I already know how to manage.”

Danvers was silent for a moment. His gaze moved past her, toward the farmhouse, then back to her face. She saw him notice the lines around her eyes, the hands that had wrapped potatoes and mended fences and hauled water for two years without a husband to share the weight.

“The water table,” he said finally, “has risen eight inches since 1905. The board’s measurements are quite precise.”

“Eight inches.”

“Across the district, yes. The fen is settling. The peat shrinks as it drains, and the water has nowhere to go. The board’s solution is the new dyke.”

Nell looked down at the black soil beneath her boots. Thomas had told her about the settling, years ago, when they were first married. The land sinks, he had said. It’s the price of draining it. We take the water out, and the land falls. His hand had been warm on her shoulder, his voice certain. We’ll manage. We always have.

“Where I stand,” she said, “the water table is not your eight inches. Where I stand, it’s the height of my boot heel after a wet night. It’s the smell of the ditch behind the house when the pump runs slow. It’s the way the lower field floods when the wind blows from the east.” She raised her eyes to meet his. “Your measurements don’t tell you that.”

Danvers absorbed this. He was quiet, his notebook held against his chest, his gaze moving across the field in a way that suggested he was seeing something different now—not the abstract lines of a map, but the particular weight of a place lived in.

“Perhaps,” he said, “the board’s survey could benefit from local knowledge.”

“Perhaps.”

The word hung between them. Nell heard her own voice in it, the flatness that passed for courtesy in a world where women were expected to yield.

“I will note your objections in my report.” Danvers opened his notebook again, produced a pencil from his coat pocket. “The board will consider them before work proceeds.”

“Will they.”

“They are required to. Under the Act.”

She watched him write, his hand moving in precise strokes. The wind picked up, fluttering the pages, and he pressed his palm flat to hold them still. In the distance, she could see Amos walking toward them, his shovel over his shoulder, his pace unhurried but watchful.

“Mrs. Askew.” Danvers closed the notebook and met her eyes. “I am not your enemy.”

“No.” She turned away, looking back toward the farmhouse, where smoke rose from the chimney—Kitty, stoking the fire, doing what she knew to do. “You’re only the man with the sticks.”

She walked back across the field without waiting for his response. The stakes stood in a line behind her, pale wood against black peat, and she counted them as she passed—seven, eight, nine—each one a marker of something that had not yet been taken but was no longer entirely hers.

Amos fell into step beside her. He did not speak, but his presence was solid, reliable as the pump handle, as the ditches he kept clear. They reached the gate together and stopped.

“Peat’s sinking faster this side,” he said, looking toward the stakes.

“So he tells me.”

“Board won’t change their mind.”

“I know.”

He shifted his weight, the shovel still resting on his shoulder. His face was unreadable, weathered by years of work and something else—something from before he came to this farm, something he never spoke of.

“You’ll need to write to them,” he said. “Objections. Within the fortnight.”

“I’ll write.”

They stood together in the grey light, the damp seeping through Nell’s boots, the notice still nailed to the gatepost beside her. The wind had died down, leaving the air still and heavy, pressing against her like a hand.

“Kitty’s frightened,” Amos said.

“I know.”

“The hens are fed.”

“Thank you.”

He nodded once and turned toward the barn. Nell watched him go, then reached out and pressed her palm flat against the oak post, feeling the grain of the wood, the roughness of the bark, the cold that had seeped into it overnight. The notice fluttered against her wrist.

She did not tear it down. That would come later, perhaps, or not at all—depending on what the board decided, depending on what she could find to say in a letter that might or might not be read. But she stood there a moment longer, her hand on the wood, her eyes on the line of stakes that cut across the dark field.

The farm was forty acres. The long field was twelve. If the dyke went through, she would lose the best of it—split in two, cut off from itself, the careful rotation Thomas had planned reduced to fragments.

Kitty’s voice drifted from the house, calling her name. Nell turned away from the gate and walked back toward the kitchen, where the fire was burning and her daughter waited, and the morning stretched ahead of her with all its ordinary demands.

Behind her, the Board’s notice remained nailed to the post, bright against the rain-darkened wood.
