Chapter 5

The Surveyor's Mark

The gate stood where she had left it, but the figure beyond was new. Nell Askew stopped at the edge of the long field, her boots sinking into the saturated ground, and watched the stranger move across her soil with the deliberate care of a man who had never had to wrest a living from peat. He wore a suit the color of wet stone, a hat set square on his head, and he carried himself as though the mist belonged to him.

The letter in her pocket had warned her this would happen. She had read it enough times to know its phrases by heart: necessary improvements, district benefit, compensation to be determined. Words that slid past each other without friction, leaving no mark. But seeing the man himself was different. The letter was paper. This was a body walking her furrows, stepping over the drainage cuts as though they were minor inconveniences rather than the work of decades.

She moved forward. The cold seeped through her gloves where she gripped the hammer at her belt. She had come out meaning to check the gate posts, to see whether the overnight rain had softened the earth around the hinges, but the sight of the figure had stopped that thought cold. Now she walked toward him without quite deciding to, her body carrying her forward while her mind hung back.

He had set up an instrument on a tripod, some brass-bound device with lenses and adjustments, and was peering through it toward the far hedge. A leather case lay open at his feet, wooden stakes bundled inside like kindling. As she watched, he straightened, made a note in a small book, and then reached for one of the stakes.

The first thunk carried across the field. The sound was not loud, but it had a finality to it, a note that seemed to cut through the damp air and settle into the ground itself. She felt it in her knees, in the pit of her stomach. Another thunk. Another. He was marking a line, she realized. A line that would become a dyke, and the dyke would become a wound through the middle of her best field.

“Good morning.”

His voice was polite, neutral, the voice of a man who had learned to speak to farmers as though they were children who needed managing. She had not realized she was close enough to hear. He had turned, catching her approach, and now stood with his mallet in one hand and a stake in the other, the picture of professional courtesy.

“Mrs. Askew, I presume.” It was not a question. “Philip Danvers, from the Drainage Board. I believe you received our notice.”

She stopped a few paces from him. Close enough to see the pale hairs on his wrists where his cuffs had ridden up, the small stain on his lapel that might have been tea or might have been ink. Close enough to smell the wool of his suit and, beneath it, something sharp and chemical, like the quills she used for marking lambs.

“I did.”

Her voice came out flat, harder than she had intended. She saw him register the tone, saw the slight shift in his posture as he adjusted to it.

“Excellent. I’m here to complete the preliminary survey for the new cut. I apologise for the early hour, but the light is best in the mornings, you understand. Fewer shadows to interfere with the readings.”

He gestured toward the theodolite as though inviting her to admire it. She looked at the instrument instead, at the brass gleaming dully in the grey light, and thought of all the mornings she had spent in this field with nothing but a spade and her own two eyes. No instrument had ever told her what this land needed. She had learned that from the way the water pooled after rain, from the smell of the soil when she turned it, from the quality of the celery that came up sweet or bitter depending on where she planted.

“Through the middle.”

It was not quite a question. He tilted his head, considering.

“The most efficient path, yes. The board’s engineers determined that a straight cut from the western boundary to the eastern drain will provide optimal flow with minimal excavation. It will lower the water table across the entire section.”

“Through my celery.”

“Through the long field, yes. I understand this may cause some disruption to your current planting. The board will, of course, consider appropriate compensation.”

He said it the way a man might speak of weather, as though compensation were a simple matter of arithmetic, as though the word could encompass the years she had spent building up this soil, the careful rotations, the knowledge she carried in her hands of which plots drained fast and which held water too long. As though any sum of money could replace what it meant to walk out her door each morning and know, with a certainty that ran deeper than thought, that the land beneath her feet was hers.

“Compensation.”

“The board’s surveyors will assess the value of the affected acreage and any improvements you’ve made. You’ll receive a fair price, Mrs. Askew. The board is not in the business of impoverishing landholders.”

She looked past him to the line of stakes he had already driven. Four of them, spaced at regular intervals, marching toward the hedge. They looked like grave markers. She thought of the letter in her pocket, its creases worn soft from folding and unfolding, and felt the weight of it against her hip.

“And if I don’t want to sell.”

The question hung in the air between them. His expression did not change, but something in his eyes shifted, a calculation so quick she might have imagined it.

“The board has the authority to proceed with necessary drainage improvements under the Act of 1879. I’m sure you’re familiar with the provisions. The compensation is mandatory. The sale is not a matter of wanting.”

He delivered the words gently, the way one might tell a child that winter would come regardless of preference. There was no cruelty in it. That was what struck her most. He was not a cruel man. He was a man doing a job, following a procedure, checking boxes in a notebook. The cruelty was in the procedure itself, in the way it reduced a field to a measurement and a woman’s life to a compensation figure.

“I see.”

She did not know what else to say. The words she had carried out here with her seemed to have dissolved in the damp air, leaving only the cold weight in her chest and the steady drip of water from the hem of her skirt.

“I’ll need to continue the line to the eastern boundary. If you’d prefer not to observe, I understand. These matters can be… difficult.”

He was offering her an exit. A kindness, of sorts. She could walk back to the farmhouse, pour herself a cup of tea, and pretend this was not happening. She could let him finish his work in peace and deal with the consequences later, when the letters came and the men arrived with their machines.

But she stayed.

“Go ahead.”

He blinked once, then nodded. “Very well.”

He turned back to his theodolite, adjusting the screws with practiced fingers, and she watched him work. Each measurement was precise. He would sight through the lens, make a note, move the instrument, sight again. Then he would take a stake from the bundle, position it with care, and drive it into the ground with three sharp blows.

Thunk. Thunk. Thunk.

She counted the stakes as they went in. Five. Six. Seven. The line was taking shape, a scar across the field that would not heal. She thought of the celery seedlings in the seed frames behind the farmhouse, their pale shoots just beginning to uncurl. She had planned to set them out in the long field next month, in the rich soil that held moisture without drowning, in the ground she had worked since before Kitty was born.

Thunk. Thunk. Thunk.

Eight. Nine. Ten.

The mist had begun to lift, and she could see the farmhouse now, the kitchen window catching what little light there was. A pale shape moved behind the glass. Kitty, watching. She would have seen the stranger arrive, would have seen Nell walk out to meet him. She would be standing at the window now with her cup of tea growing cold, waiting for some sign of what was happening.

Nell did not wave. She did not turn. She kept her eyes on the surveyor and his steady, methodical work, as though by watching she could somehow undo what was being done.

“Mrs. Askew.”

She looked up. He had paused, mallet in hand, and was looking at her with an expression she could not quite read.

“You have questions, perhaps. About the process, the timeline. I’m happy to explain what I can.”

She considered this. The letter had been thin on details, full of phrases like “in due course” and “subject to approval.” She had spent three days turning it over in her mind, wondering what came next, when the machines would arrive, whether there was any room to argue.

“When.”

“Excuse me?”

“When will they come. The diggers.”

He set down the mallet and pulled a small notebook from his pocket, flipping through pages with his thumb. “The board will review my survey and issue a final determination. That usually takes six to eight weeks. After that, the work contracts go to tender. You’re looking at late summer, early autumn, assuming no delays.”

Late summer. The celery would be in the ground by then, the plants established, the work of the season already done. They would cut the dyke right through the standing crop, churning soil and stalks into mud.

“And if I contest it.”

He closed the notebook and met her eyes. For a moment, she thought she saw something there, a flicker of discomfort or perhaps just the reflection of the grey sky.

“You can submit an objection to the board. There’s a process. Forms to fill, hearings to attend. I can provide the documentation if you wish.”

“Does it ever work. The objections.”

He was quiet for a long moment. The wind stirred the hem of his coat, and somewhere beyond the hedge, a lapwing called, its cry thin and lonely in the damp air.

“Rarely,” he said. “The board’s authority is quite broad when it comes to drainage improvements. The law was written to address the flooding problems of the seventies, you understand. Whole districts underwater, crops rotting in the fields. The intention was to give the board power to act quickly, without getting bogged down in disputes.”

“And now.”

“Now they use that power for improvements that aren’t strictly necessary. But the law hasn’t changed. The power remains.”

He said it simply, without judgment. A statement of fact, like the weather or the price of grain. She felt something shift in her chest, a hardening that had begun days ago and was now settling into place like sediment in still water.

“Thank you for your honesty.”

He inclined his head, a small bow that might have been respect or might have been relief. “I’ll finish the line and be on my way. You have my sympathies, Mrs. Askew. Truly. But this is the work.”

He turned back to his stakes, and she turned away. She walked back across the field, following the line of wooden markers he had left behind. Each one a wound. Each one a promise of what was to come.

She was halfway to the farmhouse when she heard footsteps behind her, the squelch of boots in wet ground. She did not need to turn to know who it was. Amos had a way of moving that was different from anyone else—a steady, economical stride that covered ground without seeming to hurry.

She stopped and let him catch up. He stood beside her, his spade resting against his shoulder, and looked out at the line of stakes.

“So.”

“So.”

They stood together in silence. The wind had picked up, carrying the smell of damp earth and dying reeds from the fen beyond the hedge. She thought he might speak, might offer some word of comfort or protest, but he said nothing. He simply stood there, a presence at her shoulder, solid and unmoving.

After a while, he walked forward to the nearest stake and crouched beside it. He reached out and rested his hand on the wood, his fingers wrapping around it as though testing its strength. She watched him from the corner of her eye, noting the way his jaw tightened, the way his shoulders drew together.

“It’s good wood,” he said. “Oak. They don’t skimp on the materials.”

“Is that meant to comfort me.”

“No.” He straightened, brushing dirt from his knees. “Just an observation.”

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw in his face what she had not allowed herself to feel. The weight of it. The wrongness. He had worked this land beside her for years, knew its moods and its hidden places, had helped her dig the drainage cuts and repair the gate posts and bring in the harvest when her husband grew too weak to lift a basket. This was his loss too, in its way.

“What will you do?”

The question was gentle, but she heard what lay beneath it. What can you do? What is left?

“Contest it. Write letters. Go to hearings.” She heard her own voice as though it belonged to someone else, thin and tired in the damp air. “Whatever the process allows.”

“And if that fails.”

She turned to look at him. His face was weathered, lined from years of outdoor work, but his eyes were clear and steady. He was not asking because he doubted her. He was asking because he needed to know what came next, for both their sakes.

“Then we’ll see.”

He held her gaze for a long moment, and something passed between them, a recognition that went deeper than words. They were standing on ground that might not be theirs much longer, in a world that was shifting beneath their feet, and all they had was each other and the work.

He nodded once, a small dip of his chin, and turned to walk back toward the farmyard. She watched him go, his figure receding into the mist, and then she looked back at the line of stakes. They stood straight and pale against the dark soil, marking a path that would cut through everything she had built.

In her pocket, the letter pressed against her hip, its creases worn soft. She thought of the words it contained, the phrases that slid past each other without friction, and she felt the cold resolve settle deeper into her bones.

The gate stood where she had left it. The stakes stood in a line across her field. And somewhere beyond the horizon, the machines were waiting.
