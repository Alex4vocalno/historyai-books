Chapter 14

Nells Stand on the Brink

The lamb still shivered in Kitty’s arms when the engine cut through the mist. Nell stood at the kitchen window, hands flat on the damp cloth of her trousers, and watched the black car nose along the farm track. Too fast for the ruts. It rocked side to side, and each jolt went through her like a hand shoved against her sternum. Another person who did not know how to move across this land.

Behind her, Kitty sat at the scarred table, the lamb bundled in a towel against her chest, her face pale above the wool.

The car stopped in the yard. A door slammed. Nell did not turn from the window. The engine ticked in the cold air. Boots crunched gravel, then paused—and she knew he was looking at the mud on her threshold, the pile of boots by the door, the way the farm showed itself to anyone who cared to see.

Three sharp raps. The space between each one precise and measured.

“Come,” Nell said.

Arthur Askew stepped into the kitchen. Her husband’s younger brother. A man who had left the fen before his voice broke and returned only for funerals. Dark wool coat, pressed and clean. Leather boots unstained by the mud that crept up Nell’s own legs. He carried a briefcase in one hand and set it on the floor beside the door as if the flagstones might soil the leather.

“Nell.” He looked at her, then past her to Kitty. “And Kitty. I heard about the lamb. I came as soon as I could.”

“The lamb’s fine,” Nell said. “Warm now. Eating.”

Arthur nodded. His gaze moved around the kitchen—the range, the stew pot, the bucket by the sink, the ceiling that needed whitewashing. Nell watched him count what he saw. She had seen that look before, on the faces of men from the Board, from the bank, from anywhere that was not here.

“Sit,” she said. “Tea’s hot.”

“I won’t stay long.” He moved to the table, pulled out a chair, sat across from Kitty. He did not look at the lamb. He looked at Kitty’s face—her hair plaited and precise, the cardigan buttoned to her throat. “How are you, Kitty? Truly?”

Kitty’s hand tightened on the towel. The lamb made a small sound, a bleat that was barely a breath.

“I’m well,” Kitty said. Quiet. Measured. “We saved the lamb.”

“You did.” Arthur’s voice was warm, but his eyes stayed on Kitty’s face. “Your mother tells me you were the one who found it. That’s brave work for a girl.”

Kitty did not answer. She looked down at the lamb, her fingers moving in its wool, counting the curls, one after another.

Nell turned from the window. She crossed to the range and lifted the kettle. The water was still hot, the tea in the pot dark and strong. She poured a cup and set it on the table in front of Arthur.

“Drink,” she said. “Then tell me why you’ve come.”

Arthur picked up the cup. He did not drink. He held it in both hands, the steam rising between them.

“I’ve come because I’m worried,” he said. “About you. About Kitty. About this place.”

“This place is fine.”

“Is it?” He set the cup down. “Nell, I’ve seen the notices. The Drainage Board has been here. They’ve marked your land.”

“Their stakes are in my field. That’s all.”

“That’s not all.” His voice was still gentle, but there was something beneath it now, a current that pulled. “I’ve spoken to the surveyor. Danvers. He told me about the compulsory purchase. About the hearing.”

Nell’s hand went to her pocket. The folded paper there—the notice she had pulled from her gate, the letter that had come before it. The edges were soft now, worn by her fingers.

“The hearing’s not decided,” she said.

“It’s a formality.” Arthur leaned forward. “Danvers explained the process. The Board has authority. They’ve already approved the dyke. Your land is in the path. They’ll pay you fair value.”

“Fair value.” Her own voice, flat and hard. “And what value is that? For thirty acres? For the house my husband built? For the ground his father turned before him?”

“You can’t farm what’s underwater.” Patient, as if he spoke to a child. “The fen is changing, Nell. The drainage isn’t working. The Board’s own engineers say the old cuts are failing. This new dyke is the only way to protect the rest.”

“Protect.” Nell moved to the table. She stood at the end, her hand on the back of a chair. “Protect what? The land they’ve already sold? The farms that went under when they changed the course five years back?”

“That was a different matter.”

“It’s the same matter.” Her fingers tightened on the chair. “They take what they want. They call it improvement. They call it necessary. And the people who’ve worked this land for generations, they’re meant to step aside.”

Arthur looked at her. His face was open, honest, concerned. He believed what he said. That was the worst of it. He believed.

“Nell.” He spoke carefully. “I’m not here for the Board. I’m here for you. For Kitty.”

Kitty’s head lifted. Her eyes moved from the lamb to Arthur, then to Nell. She said nothing, but her hand had stopped moving in the wool.

“What do you mean?” Nell asked.

“I mean there’s an offer.” Arthur reached for his briefcase. He opened it and took out a folder, thin and neat. “I’ve been in touch with a solicitor in Ely. There’s a buyer for the house, the land. The Board’s offer is fair, but with the right negotiation, you could do better. Enough to buy something in town. A proper house. Near the school. Near the shops.”

Nell did not touch the folder. She looked at it, lying on the scarred wood of her table, and felt the weight of it.

“You’ve spoken to a solicitor,” she said. “Without asking me.”

“I wanted to have options. Before it’s too late.”

“Too late for what?”

Arthur’s hand rested on the folder. “Nell, look at this place. The mud. The water. You spent yesterday digging a drain that will fail again next week. You’re fighting a battle you can’t win.”

“I’ve been fighting it for fifteen years.”

“And what has it cost you?” His voice softened. “Tom is gone. You’re alone here. Kitty is alone here. What happens when the next flood comes? When the dyke breaks? When the Board sends men to take what they’ve already marked?”

Nell’s breath caught. The cold in her knees, the ache that had settled there after hours in the ditch. The lamb shivered in Kitty’s arms, a small sound, a thread of fear.

“The Board can be fought,” Nell said. “There’s a process. A hearing. I can speak. I can show them what this land means.”

“The hearing is a formality,” Arthur repeated. “The decision is made. The only question is how much they pay, and whether you walk away with something or nothing.”

“And if I walk away?” Nell asked. “What then? A house in Ely? A life in a town where I know no one? Where Kitty knows no one?”

“Kitty would adapt.” Arthur turned to look at his niece. “Wouldn’t you, Kitty? There are girls your age in Ely. Schools. Shops. A proper life, not this.”

Kitty’s face was still. Her eyes were on the lamb, but Nell saw the tension in her shoulders, the way her fingers had curled into the towel.

“I like it here,” Kitty said. Small, but it did not waver. “I know the steps to the barn. I know how many boards are in the floor. I know where the lambs are born.”

Arthur’s expression flickered. “Kitty, you don’t know what you’re saying. This isn’t a life for a girl. It’s hard. It’s cold. It’s lonely.”

“I’m not lonely.”

“You don’t know what lonely is.” Still gentle, but an edge now. “You’ve never seen anything else. You’ve never had the chance.”

Kitty’s hand tightened on the lamb. The animal shifted, its small hooves scraping against the towel. Nell saw her daughter’s face, the struggle there, the need to count and order and hold.

“I know the numbers,” Kitty said. “I know the field has three hundred and twelve furrows. I know the barn has forty-seven beams. I know the ditch has sixty-two willows along the bank. I know because I counted them. I count them every day.”

Arthur looked at her. “And what happens when the Board cuts a dyke through your counting? When they take the field and the willows and the furrows?”

Kitty did not answer. Her lips pressed together, and her eyes went to the window, to the gray light beyond.

“That’s enough.” Nell’s voice was low, controlled. “Arthur, you’ve said your piece. You’ve shown me your folder. Now hear mine.”

Arthur turned back to her. “Nell, please. I’m trying to help.”

“You’re trying to help yourself.” The words built in her chest, a pressure she had held for months, for years. “You left this place. You chose something else. That was your right. But don’t come back now and tell me what’s best for my daughter. Don’t stand in my kitchen with your clean boots and your briefcase and pretend you know what it costs to stay.”

“I know what it cost Tom.” His voice was sharp now. “I know what it cost my brother. I was at the funeral, Nell. I saw what this place did to him.”

“This place didn’t kill him.” Nell’s hand came down on the table. The cups rattled. “His heart failed. That’s not the fen. That’s not the farm.”

“His heart failed because he worked himself to death trying to hold back water that was never going to stop.” Arthur stood. His chair scraped against the flagstones. “You’re doing the same thing. You’re digging drains that don’t work. You’re fighting a Board that’s already won. And you’re taking Kitty down with you.”

“Sit down.” Quiet, but it carried.

Arthur did not sit. He stood at the end of the table, his hands flat on the wood, and looked at her.

“I’m asking you,” he said, “as your brother-in-law, as someone who loved Tom, as someone who cares about Kitty. Sell the farm. Take the money. Start again somewhere safe.”

“Safe.” The word landed like a stone in her throat. “There’s no safe. Not in Ely. Not anywhere. The world changes, Arthur. The ground shifts. You think a town house will save you? You think a solicitor’s contract will hold back the water?”

“I think it gives Kitty a chance.”

Kitty looked up. Her face was pale, her eyes wide. The lamb had gone still in her arms, its breathing slow and shallow.

“Mama,” Kitty said. Her voice cracked. “What’s going to happen?”

Nell turned to her daughter. The fear there, the counting that had stopped, the order that was breaking. Arthur’s argument sank into her, a hook that caught and pulled.

“Nothing’s going to happen,” Nell said. “Not today. Not tomorrow. We’re staying here. This is our home.”

Kitty’s eyes searched her face. “But the stakes. In the field.”

“I’ll deal with the stakes.”

“But Arthur said—“

“Arthur doesn’t decide.” Firm. “I decide. This is my land. My house. And I say we stay.”

Arthur let out a breath. He picked up his briefcase, closed it, fastened the latches. The sound was loud in the quiet kitchen.

“You’re making a mistake,” he said. “And when the Board comes, when the water rises, when Kitty is left with nothing, that mistake will be yours to carry.”

Nell did not answer. She stood by the table, her hand on the back of the chair, and watched him.

Her brother-in-law moved to the door. He paused, his hand on the latch, and looked back.

“The offer stands,” he said. “The solicitor will wait. But not forever. The Board won’t wait at all.”

“Goodbye, Arthur.”

He opened the door. Cold air rushed in, carrying the smell of wet earth, of mist, of the fen that waited beyond the threshold. The door closed behind him. His footsteps crossed the yard, the gravel crunching, then the car door, the engine turning, the sound fading down the track.

Nell stood still. The kitchen was quiet except for the ticking of the clock, the lamb’s breathing, the distant call of a bird from the withy beds.

Kitty’s voice came small. “Mama, is he right?”

Nell turned. She crossed to the table and knelt beside her daughter. Her knees ached on the flagstones. Her hand found Kitty’s, and she held it.

“He’s not right,” Nell said. “But he believes he is. That’s harder.”

“What if the Board comes?”

“They’ll come. And I’ll be here. And you’ll be here. And we’ll face it together.”

Kitty’s eyes searched her face. The lamb stirred in her arms, and she looked down at it, her fingers beginning their counting again, one curl, two curls, three.

“I’m scared,” Kitty said.

Nell felt the words land. The hook in her chest, the doubt Arthur had planted. The fear in her daughter’s face, the need for order, for pattern, for something that would hold.

“I know,” Nell said. “But fear isn’t the end of it. We keep going. We keep working. That’s what we do.”

Kitty nodded, but her eyes did not meet Nell’s. She looked at the lamb, at the window, at the gray light that pressed against the glass.

Nell rose. Her knees protested. She went to the window and looked out at the yard, at the track where Arthur’s car had gone, at the long field beyond, where the white stakes stood in the wet ground.

The notice was still in her pocket. Its weight, the paper soft and worn. The Board’s words, their authority, their claim. Arthur’s folder on the table, his solicitor, his safe house in Ely.

None of it was safe. She knew that. The fen had taught her. The ground shifted. The water rose. The only thing that held was what you built yourself, with your own hands, on the land you knew.

But Kitty’s face. The fear there. The counting that had stopped.

Nell turned back to the kitchen. The stew pot sat on the range, the smell of it beginning to sharpen, the vegetables overcooking. She crossed to the stove and lifted the pot, set it aside, stirred the contents with a wooden spoon.

“Mama?” Kitty’s voice was small.

“Yes, love.”

“Can we count the willows tomorrow? Along the ditch?”

Nell’s hand stilled on the spoon. She looked at her daughter, at the lamb in her arms, at the face that was still pale but had lost some of its strain.

“Yes,” Nell said. “We can count them.”

Kitty nodded. She looked down at the lamb, and her fingers began to move again, one curl, two curls, three.

Nell turned back to the window. The light was fading, the afternoon sliding toward evening. The mist hung over the long field, softening the stakes, blurring the edges of the world.

Arthur’s words stayed with her. His folder on the table. His solicitor in Ely. His safe house, his proper life.

She had refused him. The words had come clear and hard, and she had meant them. But beneath the refusal, something else had taken root. A question. A doubt. Not about the land, not about the farm, but about Kitty, about the fear in her daughter’s face, about the counting that had stopped.

Nell looked out at the fen. The ground was soft. The water was rising. The stakes stood in her field, white and sharp, marking what the Board would take.

She had fought before. She would fight again. But the fight was different now. It was not just about the land. It was about what the land cost, and who paid the price.

The lamb made a small sound. Kitty’s voice murmured to it, soft and low. Nell turned from the window and went back to the table. She sat across from her daughter, and she watched her count the curls in the wool, one after another, a pattern that held.

Outside, the fen waited. The water rose. The ground shifted under everything she had built.

But here, in this kitchen, at this table, her daughter’s hands moved in the wool, and the lamb breathed, and the clock ticked, and the world was still, for now, what Nell had made of it.
