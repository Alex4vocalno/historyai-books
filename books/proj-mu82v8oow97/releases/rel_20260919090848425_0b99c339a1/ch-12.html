Chapter 12

Mud, Time, and Tomorrow

────────────────────────────────────────

The suck and pull of this ground would take the strength from her arms before noon. Nell stood knee-deep in black mud that had no bottom, only layers of older mud beneath, compacted peat giving way to silt giving way to something that moved when she pressed her weight against the spade. The long field stretched before her, forty acres of celery stubble and dark earth, flat as a table under a sky the color of old pewter. Overnight rain had swollen the ditches to their lips, and now the main drain at the field’s edge ran sluggish, its surface thick with the sheen of stagnant water.

She had come out before first light, leaving Kitty at the kitchen table with a cup of tea gone cold and the promise that she would not venture past the farmyard gate. The gatepost still bore its wound—a bright circle of exposed wood where the notice had hung, a pale scar against weathered grain. Nell had pried the thing off with her knife, folded it into her pocket with the original letter, and felt the weight of both papers against her thigh like stones. But the gatepost remained. The nail hole remained. Something had been altered that could not be smoothed over.

The drain was not running. That was the first thing she had seen when she walked the field’s perimeter, testing the banks with her boots. The water should have been moving toward the pump, should have been channeled away from the root systems of the winter celery, should have been doing what drains were made to do. Instead it sat in a black sheet, reflecting the grey sky, touching the stems of the young plants where they struggled in their rows.

She had waded in before she thought to wait for Amos. The mud closed over her boots with a sound like a slow inhalation, and she felt the cold seep through the leather, a wetness that would stay with her all day. The spade struck something solid—not the hard bottom she expected but a mass of silt and reed debris that had settled overnight, choking the channel where it bent near the withy bed. She drove the blade in again, felt it catch, and pulled. The mud resisted. It held the spade like a hand closing round the shaft, and she had to lean her whole weight back to wrench it free.

There was no use cursing. The fen did not care for curses. It had been here before her grandfather’s grandfather had cut the first ditch, before the first pump had drawn breath, and it would be here long after she and her kind had gone to the churchyard on the ridge. What it understood was work—steady, grinding, unglamorous work—and that was what she gave it.

The first spadeful came up black and heavy, trailing roots and the smell of decay. She threw it to the bank and drove the blade in again. The second spadeful was worse, thicker, more reluctant to leave its place. By the fifth, her shoulders had begun to ache, and by the tenth, the ache had settled into her lower back like a second spine. Sweat ran down her temples despite the cold, and her breath came in short, hard bursts that hung in the air as white vapor.

She had cleared perhaps three feet of channel when she heard Amos’s boots on the bank behind her. She did not turn. There was no need. He would have seen the water level, seen the way it pooled instead of running, seen her already at work with her back to the sky. He would know what needed doing.

“Main drain’s blocked.”

His voice came low and flat, not a question but an observation. She heard him step down into the mud behind her, felt the ground shift as his weight joined hers in the channel.

“Has been since before dawn.”

He did not ask why she had not waited. He did not ask what she had seen when she walked the field’s perimeter, whether the surveyor’s stakes still stood at the far edge where the new dyke would run, whether the notice had been replaced or the gatepost remained bare. He simply took his place beside her, found his rhythm, and began to dig.

They worked in silence for the better part of an hour. The mud fought them with every stroke, releasing its grip only reluctantly, sliding back into the channel as fast as they could clear it. Nell’s arms burned, then numbed, then burned again. Her boots had filled with water that had found its way over the tops, and her stockings were soaked through, her feet aching with a cold that would turn to pain later, when she sat by the kitchen range and tried to coax warmth back into her bones.

Amos worked with a methodical efficiency that she had learned to trust. He did not hurry. He did not waste motion. Each stroke of his spade was measured, each lift calculated to move the maximum weight with the minimum strain. She had watched him work this land since before her husband had died, had learned his rhythms the way she had learned the rhythms of the pump and the seasons, and she knew that he would not stop until the job was done, no matter how long it took.

The channel deepened inch by inch. The water level began to drop, slowly at first, then with more purpose as the blockage cleared. Nell could feel the current tugging at her boots, a gentle pressure that meant the drain was beginning to breathe again. But the worst of the silt still lay ahead—a solid mass that had accumulated at the bend where the drain curved around the withy bed, held in place by the roots of the willows that crowded the bank.

“Here.” She drove her spade into the mass and felt it hit something that did not yield. “The roots have caught it.”

Amos moved up beside her, his shoulder brushing hers in the narrow channel. He probed the silt with the edge of his blade, testing the depth, then grunted.

“Reed mat. Must have broken loose upstream.”

“Can we pull it?”

“Maybe. Or cut it.”

He handed her his spade and reached for the knife at his belt. The water rose to his thighs as he knelt, disappearing into the black murk, and she heard him cursing under his breath as he felt for the edge of the mat. The willow roots had grown through the woven reeds, binding them to the bank, and every tug sent vibrations through the channel that threatened to loosen more silt from the sides.

Nell held the light, such as it was, and tried to keep her footing. The mud had grown more treacherous as they worked, the banks slick with the slime of disturbed peat, and she could feel her boots sliding with every shift of weight. She planted her feet wider and braced herself against the side of the channel, watching Amos’s hands disappear to the wrists as he worked the blade through the tangled mass.

“Got it.” He straightened, water streaming from his sleeves, and hauled a section of the mat free. It came up trailing roots and black ooze, heavier than it looked, and he had to swing it to the bank in two motions, grunting with the effort. “There’s more.”

They worked at the mat in sections, cutting and pulling, cutting and pulling, until the channel began to run clear. The water darkened as the silt washed downstream, carrying the smell of the fen with it—the smell of peat and decay and something older, something that had been buried for centuries and now rose to the surface in the churn. Nell’s hands were numb, her fingers clumsy around the shaft of the spade, but she kept working, kept digging, kept pulling at the silt until she could feel the current running strong against her legs.

A sound from the bank made her look up. Amos had paused, one hand on the spade, the other raised to shade his eyes against the grey light. He was looking toward the far end of the field, toward the line of stakes that marked the proposed path of the new dyke.

“Board’s stakes are still standing.”

She followed his gaze. The wooden stakes rose from the mud at regular intervals, their tops painted white, marking a line that cut straight through the long field. They had been placed by Philip Danvers during his survey, precise and impersonal, and they stood like sentinels against the grey horizon.

“They’ll send someone to check.”

“Likely.”

“They’ll see the gatepost.”

“Likely.”

He did not look at her. His face was a mask of mud and exhaustion, his eyes dark in a face that had seen too many mornings like this one. She wondered what he was thinking, whether he had already calculated the odds, whether he had already made his peace with what was coming. But his face gave nothing away.

“Best get this finished.”

She turned back to the channel. The water was running now, fast and clear, carrying the silt toward the pump and the main drain beyond. The worst of the blockage was cleared, but the banks were weakened from the digging, and she could see places where the peat had begun to slump, threatening to slide back into the channel and undo the morning’s work.

“Need to brace the bend.”

Amos nodded. He climbed out of the channel and disappeared toward the withy bed, returning with an armload of willow whips cut from the thicket at the field’s edge. They were young and pliable, green beneath the grey bark, and he began to weave them into the bank where the roots had been disturbed, threading them through the existing growth to hold the peat in place.

Nell watched him work. It was an old technique, one her grandfather had used, one that had been passed down through generations of fenmen who understood that the land needed more than drainage—it needed something to hold it together. The willow roots would grow, would sink into the peat, would bind the bank in a way that bare earth could never manage. But it would take time. And time was something they might not have.

She climbed out of the channel and began to pack the peat around the woven whips, pressing it into place with her boots, tamping it down until it held. The mud was everywhere—in her hair, on her face, soaking through her clothes—and she could feel the weight of it in her bones, a heaviness that had nothing to do with exhaustion and everything to do with the knowledge that this was only one drain, one field, one morning’s work in a fight that would never end.

The sky had begun to lighten by the time they finished. The grey had thinned to a pale yellow at the horizon, and she could see the mist rising from the land, the fen exhaling after the night’s rain. The water in the channel ran clear and steady, the banks held, the willow whips were in place. They had won this round.

But the stakes still stood at the far end of the field. And the gatepost still bore its scar. And the letter and the notice still weighed in her pocket, two pieces of paper that carried the weight of everything she had built and everything she stood to lose.

Amos leaned on his spade, his breath coming in white clouds. He looked at the channel, then at the stakes, then at her. His face was unreadable, but his eyes held something she had not seen before—not defeat, exactly, but something close to it. A recognition of limits. A knowledge of what it cost to fight a battle that could not be won.

“That should hold.”

“For now.”

“For now.” He wiped his forehead with the back of his hand, leaving a streak of mud across his brow. “Rain coming by evening.”

“I know.”

“Ground won’t take it.”

“I know.”

He did not say what they both knew—that the ground was already saturated, that the water table had risen to within inches of the surface, that another heavy rain would undo everything they had done and more. He did not need to. The fen had its own language, and they both spoke it fluently.

He shouldered his spade and turned toward the farmyard. She watched him go, his silhouette dark against the lightening sky, his step heavy with the weight of the morning’s labor. He would go back to his cottage, clean up, eat something, and return for the afternoon’s work. He would not mention the stakes or the notice or the gatepost. He would not ask what she meant to do about the hearing, whether she had a plan, whether she thought they had any chance of stopping what was coming. That was not his way.

She stood alone in the field, the channel running at her feet, the mud hardening on her clothes. The cold was beginning to seep through her, now that she had stopped moving, and she could feel the ache in her shoulders settling into something deeper, a weariness that went beyond the physical. She thought of Kitty at the kitchen table, her tea gone cold, her hands wrapped around a cup that offered no warmth. She thought of the gatepost and its pale scar. She thought of the folded papers in her pocket—the letter and the notice—the words she had read so many times that they had lost their meaning, the formal language of compulsory powers and public benefit and fair compensation.

The fen stretched out around her, flat and black and patient. It had seen generations of farmers come and go, had watched them cut their ditches and build their banks and fight their losing battles against the water. It would watch her go the same way, in time. But not today. Today the drain ran clear. Today the banks held. Today the willow whips were in place, and the water was moving, and the field was still hers.

She pulled the spade from the mud and began the long walk back to the farmhouse. The mist rose around her, swallowing the stakes at the far end of the field, softening the edges of the world until it seemed that nothing existed but the ground beneath her feet and the sky above her head. Somewhere in the distance, a curlew called, its note falling through the grey air like a question. She did not know the answer. But she kept walking.
