Chapter 15

The Rising Ditch

────────────────────────────────────────

The rain had stopped an hour ago, but the sky stayed the color of bruised iron, and the water kept rising.

Nell stood at the lip of the lower ditch with her boots sinking into the peat, watching the dark surface creep up the gauge stick she had driven into the bank the week before. An inch since dawn. Another inch while she watched. The overflow had already licked across the low ground, a flat tongue of it reaching for the young barley, and the barley stood in it up to its first leaves, too young to hold, too young to drown.

The downpour had come in the small hours. A drumming on the roof that pulled her from sleep and sent her to the window, her palm flat against the cold glass, her breath fogging the pane. She had known what she would find at first light. The fen held water like a grudge, and the lower ditch was where it gathered its strength.

She waded in. The cold ran up her calves and soaked through her trousers before she had taken three steps, and the current pressed against her legs, slow and insistent, leaning toward the field. Somewhere downstream the drain was choked, a tangle of reed and silt that had slid from the bank in the night. She could feel it in the way the water moved — not flowing but shouldering, like a crowd that had lost its exit.

Amos came down the path from his cottage with his spade over his shoulder, his face set in the flat expression of a man who had already measured the work and found it wanting. He did not ask what had happened. He could see the water. He could see where it was going.

“Lower than yesterday,” he said.

“Lower than an hour ago,” Nell replied.

He stepped into the ditch beside her, the water rising to his thighs, and began to dig without another word. His spade cut the silt with a wet sucking sound, each stroke pulling free a load of black mud that he threw up onto the bank. Nell moved downstream, feeling for the blockage with her feet. The ground gave under her boots, not the honest give of peat but something looser, the fen turning liquid beneath its own skin. She had worked this land for twenty years. Wet seasons and dry, floods that came up to the farmhouse steps and droughts that cracked the clay like old glaze. She knew its moods. This was something different. The ground was not fighting her. It was surrendering.

She found the blockage where the ditch curved — a mass of reed and sod that had slumped from the bank and wedged against the culvert. The water pooled behind it, dark and still, rising toward the lip. She drove her spade into the mass. It gave, then caught, then held. The reeds had tangled around something solid, a length of old fencing or a root mass, and it would have to come free by hand.

“Amos.”

He came down the ditch toward her, the water parting around his chest, his movements slow and heavy against the current. The hollows under his eyes were dark as thumbprints. He had been here since before dawn, she realized. He had heard the rain on his own roof and come out to check the levels, had seen the water rising and known what it meant. He had already been working when she arrived.

“Blockage at the bend,” she said.

“I see it.”

He waded past her, his shoulder brushing hers in the narrow channel, and began to pull at the mass with his hands. Nell joined him. Her fingers found the slick stems of reed, the cold slime of sod, the hard resistance of wood underneath. They worked in silence, pulling and cutting, and the water climbed their bodies as the blockage held. Nell’s breath came hard in her chest. The cold seeped through her clothes and her fingers went numb and clumsy, and all the while the fen pressed against them, the weight of all that water, all that land, all that patience.

The blockage shifted. Amos felt it at the same moment she did — a loosening in the mass, a change in the pressure against their hands. He pulled hard, his arm straining, and a section of the tangle came free. The water surged past them, dark and fast, pulling at their legs, and for a moment Nell thought it would work, that the channel would clear and the level would drop.

The surge only revealed how deep the problem ran. The culvert itself was half-collapsed, the brickwork giving way under the pressure, and the water that should have flowed through was spreading out underneath, eating at the bank from below. Amos stood back, his chest heaving, and looked at the damage. His jaw worked once, side to side, the way it did when he was doing sums he did not like.

“Pump,” he said.

Nell was already moving. She climbed the bank, her boots slipping in the mud, and ran for the pump-house at the edge of the field. The engine was old, a single-cylinder diesel that had been there since before she inherited the farm, but it had always been enough. She had maintained it herself. She knew its sounds and its silences, the particular knock in the valve train and the way it smoked on cold mornings.

She pulled the starter cord. The engine caught, coughed, died. She tried again, her arms aching with the effort, and this time it caught and held, a rough knocking that smoothed into a steady rhythm. The pump began to draw. The pipes shuddered as the water moved through them. For a moment, standing in the doorway with the rain-washed air on her face, Nell allowed herself to believe it would be enough.

Then the knock returned, louder than before, and the engine gave a shuddering cough and stopped. She tried the starter again, and again, her hands raw on the cord, but the engine only turned over without catching. Smoke rose from the housing, thin and grey, and the sharp smell of burnt oil came off it like a warning. She put her hand on the casing. Too hot. She knew, the way she knew the land, that it was finished. The pump was done.

She stood in the doorway with her hands hanging at her sides and looked out at the field. The water was still rising, spreading across the low ground in a dark sheet, and the young barley stood in it up to the first leaves. Below, in the ditch, Amos was a dark shape against the water, already digging again. He had heard the engine stop. He had known what it meant. He had not stopped working.

She walked back down, her legs heavy, her clothes soaked and cold against her skin. The spade was where she had left it, half-buried in the mud. She picked it up and stepped back into the water. Amos did not look at her. He did not ask about the pump. He simply moved aside to make room, and they began to dig together, two figures in a rising flood, cutting a new channel by hand.

The work was brutal. Each stroke pulled free a load of mud and water, and each load was replaced by more, the ditch sliding back into itself as fast as they could clear it. Nell’s back ached. Her shoulders burned. The blisters she had raised the day before opened inside her gloves and she felt the wet sting of them with every stroke. The water rose to her waist, cold and heavy, and the current pulled at her legs and the ground shifted under her feet. She thought of Kitty in the farmhouse, going through her morning routine, counting the cups on the shelf and the spoons in the drawer, and she dug harder.

They worked without speaking. The only sounds were the splash of their spades, the suck of mud, the heavy rhythm of their breathing. Time lost its shape. Nell could not have said how long they worked, only that the light changed overhead, the grey turning paler, and still they dug. Amos worked beside her, his movements steady and precise, each stroke placed exactly where it needed to be. She had seen him work a hundred times, had trusted him with every task on the farm, but she had never seen him work like this — with a kind of desperate economy, as if each stroke might be the last one he had in him.

The breach was near the culvert, where the water had eaten through the bank and was spilling into the field. Nell drove her spade into the mud and felt it hit something solid, a layer of clay that might hold if they could pack it tight. She called to Amos. He came over, his face grey with exhaustion, his eyes dark.

“Clay here,” she said.

“Deep?”

“Enough.”

They began to cut sod from the bank, heavy squares of grass and earth, and pack them into the breach. The water pushed against their work, finding every gap, every weakness, but they packed it tight, building a wall of earth and sod that rose inch by inch. Nell’s arms screamed with the effort, her back bent double, her face inches from the water. She could feel the fen watching her, waiting for her to fail.

The wall held. The water still seeped through, finding cracks and channels, but the main flow was stopped. Nell stood back, her chest heaving, and looked at what they had made. It was not much. A rough barrier of mud and sod, barely waist-high, already beginning to soften in the water. But it was enough. For now, it was enough.

Amos leaned on his spade, his head bowed, his breathing harsh in the silence. Nell looked at him, really looked, and saw what the morning had cost. His face was grey. His hands shook against the handle, a fine tremor he could not stop, and he watched them shake as though they belonged to someone else. He had given everything he had, and she knew, with a certainty that settled in her chest, that he could not do it again tomorrow.

“It’ll hold,” she said.

He did not answer. He only nodded, a small movement, and began to climb the bank toward the path. Nell watched him go, his figure receding into the mist, and then she turned back to the field.

The water had stopped rising, but it had not fallen. The young barley stood in a dark sheet of flood, its pale leaves just visible above the surface. It looked wrong, too still, too quiet, as if the water had already begun to work on the roots below. She did not know if it would survive. She did not know if the barrier would hold through the night, if the rain would come again, if the ground would keep on surrendering.

She stood alone in the ditch, the water around her waist, and let the weight of the morning settle into her bones. The pump was dead. The drain was blocked. The field was flooded. And against her thigh, folded in her pocket, the letter from the Board pressed its corner into her leg, a splinter she could not remove. They wanted to drain this land. They wanted to cut their dyke through her fields and send the water somewhere else, make this ground solid and dry for men who had never worked it, never loved it, never stood knee-deep in its mud and felt it shift beneath their feet.

She pulled herself up the bank, her clothes heavy with water, and stood at the edge of the field. The mist was lifting. A pale sun broke through the grey, and in the distance the farmhouse windows caught the light. Kitty would be at the table now, her hands wrapped around a cup of tea, counting the minutes until Nell came home.

She began to walk, each step an effort, her boots squelching in the mud. The fen stretched out around her, flat and endless, its horizons lost in the haze. She had fought for this land for twenty years, had given it everything she had, and still it was not enough. The water kept rising. The ground kept sinking. And the men with their stakes and their notices kept coming, measuring and planning, drawing lines on maps that would turn this ground into something else entirely.

But not today. Today the barrier held. Today the barley still stood, even if it stood in water. Today she had worked beside Amos in the cold and the dark, and they had done what needed to be done, even if it was only enough for now.

She reached the farmyard and stopped, her hand on the gate. The white-painted stake was still there, marking the line of the proposed dyke, its bright wood incongruous against the grey sky. She had meant to pull it up. She had told herself she would. It stood in her field like a claim, a promise, a threat.

She left it where it was and walked toward the house.
