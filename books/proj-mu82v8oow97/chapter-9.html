Chapter 9

Saving Lamb, Losing Ground

────────────────────────────────────────

The barn held its breath in the grey light of early morning, the air thick with the sweet rot of old hay and the mineral tang of wet stone. Shafts of pale illumination fell through the gaps between boards, cutting diagonally across the packed earth floor where straw had been scattered and scattered again, each layer pressing down into the one beneath.

A water bucket stood near the far wall, its surface skimmed with a thin skin of ice that had not yet yielded to the day’s weak warmth. Along the beam above, swallows’ nests sat empty, their mud-caked rims dried to the color of old blood, the birds long gone to warmer skies.

The smell of the place was particular and dense: the ammonias of animal urine soaked deep into the ground over decades, the green smell of alfalfa, the dusty dryness of seed grain stored in wooden bins that had themselves begun to rot at the base. In the corner where the ewes were kept, a single lantern had burned low, its oil nearly spent, its flame reduced to a blue flutter that threw more shadow than light. The animals stood quiet in their stalls, their breath rising in slow clouds that caught what little light there was.

The ewe in the nearest pen had not moved from her lying-down position since the night before, her flanks still, her eyes fixed on something in the middle distance that no human could see. Beside her, in the straw, a shape too small and too still had been arranged with care, as though someone had placed it there and then stepped away, unable to look any longer.

Nell knelt in the straw, her knees pressed into the cold damp of it. The weight in her pocket pressed against her thigh—letter and notice together, deferred until there was time to face them properly. After last night, after Kitty’s transformation from terror to something harder and brighter, Nell had expected the morning to feel different. It did not. The light was the same thin grey, the cold the same deep ache in her joints, the farm the same collection of small urgencies that would not wait. She thought of the papers in her pocket, the Board’s neat script and formal threats. There would be time for that fight later, once the lamb was seen to, once the morning’s work was done. She could not carry both at once.

The lamb drew another shallow breath. Nell watched the tiny ribs rise and fall, rise and fall, each movement so slight it might have been imagination. The ewe had done all she could, pushing the lamb into the world sometime in the dark hours, but the creature had arrived wrong somehow, too weak to stand, too thin to thrive. Nell had wrapped it in a piece of old blanket, had tried to guide it to the teat, had even milked the ewe herself and attempted to feed the lamb from her cupped palm. The milk had run down its chin more than its throat.

“Come on,” she said, her voice low and rough. “You have to try.”

The lamb’s eyes were half-open, showing the thin blue-white line of the membrane beneath. It made no sound.

From somewhere behind her, footsteps approached across the packed earth. Nell did not turn. She knew the rhythm of those steps, the particular weight of them, the way Amos Reed moved through the world as though apologising for taking up space.

“Nell.” He stopped at the edge of the straw, his shadow falling across the lamb. “There’s trouble at the lower ditch.”

She closed her eyes for a moment, then opened them again. “What kind of trouble?”

“Silting up faster than I can clear. The water’s backing up into the long field. If we don’t get it moving soon, the whole section goes soft.”

Nell looked down at the lamb, at its faint breathing, at the way its head rested in the straw as though it had simply decided to stop holding itself up. She thought of the ditch, the endless ditch, the work that never stayed done, the water that never stopped pressing. She thought of the letter in her pocket and the lighter patch of wood on the gatepost where the notice had been.

“Can you hold it for an hour?” she asked. “Two?”

“Maybe. Maybe not. The ground’s giving way at the edges. One good rain and the whole bank could go.”

Nell set down the cold tea and stood. Her knees protested, her back ached from a night spent on a chair beside the barn door, and her hands were stiff with cold. She looked at the lamb one more time, then turned to face Amos.

His face was weathered in a way that made his age hard to guess, his eyes pale and watchful in the lantern’s dying light. He held his cap in his hands, turning it over and over, a gesture she had seen him make a hundred times when the news he carried was worse than he wanted to say.

“Show me,” she said.

They walked together across the farmyard, the mud sucking at their boots with each step. The sky had lightened to a flat grey that promised neither rain nor sun, only the endless damp that had defined the season. Nell could feel the letter pressing against her leg with every stride, a reminder of battles fought and battles yet to come.

Kitty appeared at the kitchen door as they passed, her cardigan buttoned to the throat, her face pale but composed. She held a cup of something that steamed in the cold air.

“Mother,” she said. “Is it the lamb?”

Nell stopped. She looked at her daughter, at the careful way Kitty held herself, as though she might crack if she moved too quickly. The transformation of the night before still hung in the air between them, unspoken but present. Kitty had screamed at the notice on the gate, had demanded it be removed, had stood shaking with a fury that Nell had never seen in her before. And now here she was, quiet again, contained again, but with something new in her eyes.

“The lamb’s weak,” Nell said. “I have to check the ditch. Will you sit with it?”

Kitty looked past her toward the barn, her expression unreadable. Then she nodded, once, and moved toward the door without another word.

Nell watched her go, then followed Amos toward the long field.

The ditch was worse than she had feared. The water had risen overnight, backing up from a blockage somewhere downstream, and the banks were already beginning to slump into the channel. Amos had been right to come for her. Another day, another rain, and the whole thing would overflow into the field, drowning the celery seedlings that had barely begun to show above the mud.

They worked in silence for the first hour, Nell taking the lower section where the water was deepest, Amos working upstream where the silt had built up into a thick brown mass. The cold seeped through Nell’s boots and into her bones, and her arms ached from the repeated motion of digging and lifting, digging and lifting. The mud clung to the spade and to her gloves, each shovelful requiring more effort than the last.

By midmorning, her breath came hard and her back screamed with every bend. She straightened and pressed a hand against her spine, feeling the knotted muscle beneath her palm.

Amos looked up from his section. He did not speak, but his eyes moved across her face, taking in the strain, the exhaustion, the way she held herself too carefully.

“I can take the lower section for a while,” he said.

“I can manage.”

“You’re favouring your left side.”

Nell looked at him sharply, then looked away. “It’s nothing.”

“It’s not nothing.” He drove his spade into the bank and left it standing. “You’ve been at this since before light. The lamb, then this. When did you last eat?”

She could not remember. The cold tea in the barn, maybe. Nothing since.

“There’s no time,” she said.

“There’s no time for you to collapse either.” He pulled off his gloves and tucked them into his belt. “I’ll take the lower section. You check the upper drainage. Then we both rest.”

Nell wanted to argue, but the words would not come. She was too tired to argue. She was too tired for many things.

“Fine,” she said. “The upper drainage. Then rest.”

She climbed out of the ditch, her boots slipping on the wet bank, and made her way toward the upper field. The ground here was softer than she liked, the grass springy under her feet, the kind of give that meant the water was close to the surface. She had known this land for forty years, had walked every foot of it in every season, and she could feel the change in it. The fen was winning. It had always been winning, slowly, patiently, the way water always won in the end.

The upper drainage was clear, at least. The channel ran clean, the water moving with some purpose, the banks holding. She stood for a moment and watched it, the brown water sliding past, carrying away the day’s work into the larger channels that fed eventually into the river.

When she looked up, the sun had moved higher in the grey sky. It was past midday. She had been working for hours.

Nell walked back toward the lower ditch, her body moving through the motions of walking while her mind drifted. She thought of the letter in her pocket, the notice she had torn down, the hearing that waited weeks away. She thought of Kitty’s face in the lantern light, the way her voice had cracked and then steadied. She thought of the lamb, alone in the barn, breathing its shallow breaths.

Amos was leaning on his spade when she reached him, his face drawn, his breathing heavy. He had cleared the blockage, or most of it. The water was moving again, sluggish but moving.

“It’ll hold for now,” he said. “But the next rain…”

“I know.”

He looked at her, really looked, the way he sometimes did when he was deciding whether to speak or stay silent. “You should go check on the lamb.”

Nell felt something cold settle in her chest, unrelated to the damp or the wind. She had known, somewhere beneath the work, that she might be too late. She had known when she left the barn that morning that the lamb might not wait for her.

“Will you finish here?” she asked.

“I’ll finish. Go.”

She went.

The walk back to the barn seemed longer than it should have been, each step pulling at her tired legs, the mud catching at her boots. She passed through the farmyard without stopping, passed the kitchen door where she might have stopped for water, passed the gatepost with its small nail hole and lighter patch of wood. The barn door stood half-open, just as she had left it.

Inside, the lantern had burned out completely. The grey light from the gaps between boards was enough to see by, barely. The animals stood or lay in their usual places, quiet, patient, waiting for whatever came next.

Kitty sat in the straw beside the ewe’s pen, her knees drawn up, her cardigan wrapped tight around her. She was not looking at the lamb. She was looking at her own hands, folded in her lap.

Nell stopped at the edge of the straw. She did not need to ask.

“It stopped breathing,” Kitty said, her voice flat and measured. “About an hour ago. I was here. I saw.”

The lamb lay where Nell had left it, wrapped in the old blanket, its small body still now, its ribs no longer rising. It looked smaller in death than it had in life, shrunken somehow, as though the effort of living had been taking up space inside it.

Nell knelt beside it. She did not pick it up. She only looked at it, at the closed eyes, at the faint curve of its mouth, at the way its legs were folded beneath it. It had never stood. It had never taken a step. It had never even lifted its head.

She thought of the ditch, the hours spent clearing it, the water now moving sluggishly toward the river. She thought of the lamb, alone in the barn, breathing its last while she shovelled mud. She thought of the choice she had made without thinking about it, the calculation that ran beneath everything she did: the lamb might survive, the ditch will not wait.

She had been wrong.

Kitty’s hand touched her shoulder, light and brief. Nell looked up at her daughter, at the pale face and the precise parting of her hair, at the way her mouth was set in a line that trembled only slightly.

“It’s not your fault,” Kitty said.

Nell wanted to say something, to explain, to offer the logic that had driven her out of the barn and into the ditch. But the words would not come, and when she tried to speak, her throat closed around nothing.

They sat together in the straw, mother and daughter, while the ewe watched them with her slow, uncomprehending eyes. Outside, the grey day moved toward evening, the light failing by degrees, the cold settling deeper into the bones of the barn. Somewhere in the distance, a rook called once and fell silent.

Nell reached into her pocket and felt the folded paper there, the letter and the notice pressed together. She thought of the hearing, weeks away, and the land that would not stop draining, and the water that would not stop rising. She thought of all the small losses that added up to a life, the lambs that did not make it, the banks that would not hold, the gates that stood only until someone came to tear them down.

The lamb lay still in its blanket. The ewe breathed slow and steady beside it. And Nell knelt in the straw, her hands empty, her body aching, the weight in her pocket heavier than it had been before.
