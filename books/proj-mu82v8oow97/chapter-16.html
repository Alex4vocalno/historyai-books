Chapter 16

Nells Wrench, Amoss Doubt

The ditch held, for now, but the young barley stood in suspiciously quiet water, and Nell knew what that silence meant before she reached the engine shed. The pump’s usual rhythmic chug had become something else, a stuttering cough that dropped out and caught again, irregular as a failing heart. She wiped her hands on her soaked trousers and pushed through the shed door into air thick with hot oil and the sharp mineral smell of heated metal. Amos stood at the engine’s side with his back to her, one palm flat against the housing, reading the vibration through his skin the way a doctor might read a pulse.

She crossed to him without ceremony. The engine shuddered, caught, shuddered again.

“What is it?”

Amos did not turn. “Governor valve. Worn past bearing.”

The engine gave a violent lurch, metal screaming against metal, and then settled into a rhythm so slow and labored that each revolution seemed a separate act of will. Nell watched the great belt quiver on its pulley. The shed filled with a sound like breathing that had forgotten how to breathe.

“Can you fix it?”

“Maybe.” He reached for a spanner hanging on the wall. “Need to see inside first.”

She stepped closer. The heat coming off the engine block pressed against her face. Oil had blackened the concrete floor in spreading stains, and somewhere above them a bulb swung on its cord, throwing shadows that circled like weary birds. Amos worked in silence, his hands moving with the kind of certainty that came from decades of repetition. He loosened a bolt, then another. The engine continued its ragged song.

“Will you need me to hold something?”

He paused. His hands stayed where they were, wrapped around the spanner’s handle. She saw the calculation move through him, the old reluctance to let anyone into the private space of his work. Then he reached behind him without looking and pulled the lamp from its hook.

“Here.” He held it out to her. “The housing. I need to see the valve seat.”

Nell took the lamp. Its metal casing was warm from the shed’s trapped heat. She moved to the opposite side of the engine, positioning herself where the light would fall across the housing Amos was preparing to open. The bulb threw a yellow cone that caught the floating dust, the grease on Amos’s knuckles, the scored surface of the metal beneath his hands. He worked the last bolt free and lifted the housing cover away.

The governor valve sat in a bath of dark oil, its brass surface scored with deep grooves where metal had ground against metal for too long. Amos leaned close. His breath fogged the air in front of his face. He reached in with one finger and pressed against the valve’s stem, testing the play, and the valve tilted sideways in its seat, loose as a tooth in a rotten gum.

“See that?” His voice was low, almost gentle. “The stem’s worn half through. Shouldn’t move like that.”

“Can it be mended?”

“Not mended. Not properly.” He straightened and wiped his hands on a rag that hung from his belt. The rag came away black and shining. “But we can buy time. Maybe.”

The engine gave another lurching cough, and for one terrible moment the rhythm stopped entirely before catching again. Nell felt the silence in her chest. Outside, she knew, the water was rising in the ditches, pressing against the banks, seeking every weakness. The barley could not wait for a new part to be ordered and shipped. The barley did not care about proper repairs.

“What do you need?”

Amos crossed to the workbench against the far wall. He opened a drawer and rummaged through its contents, his movements quick and precise. She heard the clink of metal against metal, the scrape of a tin lid being pried open. When he returned, he held a flat piece of tin between his fingers, no larger than a postage stamp, and a small ball of soldering wire.

“Shim,” he said. “We shim the seat. Tighten the fit enough to hold the rhythm.”

He knelt beside the engine housing, and the movement seemed to draw all the remaining light in the shed toward him. Nell adjusted her position, angling the lamp so that its beam fell directly onto the valve chamber. The oil’s surface gleamed like black glass. She could smell it now, the thick organic reek of it, mixed with the sharper tang of heated iron and the musty undertone of peat dust that had settled into every corner of the shed over years of fenland farming.

The engine slowed again, and this time it seemed to gather itself for a final effort before resuming its labored pace. Amos did not look up. His hands moved into the light, and she watched them work, those thick fingers performing an operation as delicate as surgery.

Here, in the oily gloom of the engine shed, time seemed to thicken and slow. The lamp’s yellow glow carved a small world out of the darkness, a world that contained only the engine’s failing heart and Amos’s hands moving inside it. Nell held the light steady and felt the weight of the moment press against her. The cold damp of her clothes had seeped through to her skin, but she did not move. The heat from the engine block reached her in waves, and she could taste oil on her tongue, could feel it coating the inside of her mouth.

Amos cut a thin strip from the tin shim with a small knife. His hands were steady, but she noticed a tremor in his forearm, the kind of tremor that came from hours of digging and lifting, from the accumulated exhaustion of fighting a losing battle against water that never stopped rising. He pressed the shim into position beside the valve stem, working it down into the seat with the tip of his knife.

“Hold the lamp closer.”

She stepped forward and brought the light down until it almost touched the housing’s rim. The beam fell across the valve chamber, and she could see the shim’s edge gleaming against the dark oil. Amos reached for the soldering wire. He touched it to a spot beside the shim and then produced a small metal tool from his pocket, a heating iron that must have been warming against the engine block. When he touched the iron to the wire, a thin curl of smoke rose and the smell of hot metal filled the air, sharp and acrid. The solder bloomed silver and spread across the shim’s edge, fixing it in place.

The engine coughed, and this time the cough seemed to go on longer, a grinding rattle that shook the whole machine. The belt slapped against its pulley. The shadows swung wildly as the bulb swayed on its cord.

“Steady,” Amos said. He did not look up. His hands continued their work, pressing a second shim into position, soldering it with the same careful precision.

Nell’s arm ached from holding the lamp. The heat from the engine and the shed’s close air pressed against her face. Sweat ran down her temple and into the collar of her shirt. She could feel her heart beating in her chest, quick and shallow, matching the engine’s failing rhythm. The smell of oil and solder and heated metal filled her nose and throat. She thought of the barley standing in its quiet water, of the white stakes marking the dyke’s path through her land, of the letter in her pocket that she had not shown to Kitty.

Amos sat back on his heels. He wiped his hands again on the rag and then reached for the housing cover.

“Light.”

She angled the beam toward him. He fitted the cover back into place and began to tighten the bolts, working in a circle around the housing, each turn of the spanner a small and deliberate act. When the last bolt was secure, he stood and wiped his palms against his thighs.

“Now we see.”

He crossed to the engine’s starter and pulled the lever. The engine turned over once, twice, a grinding sound that seemed to come from somewhere deep in its metal bones. Then it caught. The rhythm began, slow at first, then steadying, finding its pace. The belt hummed on its pulley. The governor valve, shimmed and soldered, held its position.

The sound changed. The engine’s laboring cough smoothed into a deep and regular thrum, a sound that seemed to come from the earth itself, from the water moving through the ditches and the peat settling under its own weight. The pump was working. The water was rising.

Nell lowered the lamp. Her arm ached. She had not realized how tightly she had been holding her breath until she let it go.

“How long?”

Amos was watching the engine, his eyes on the belt, the housing, the valve. “A week. Maybe two. The solder will hold, but the shim’s only tin. It’ll wear through.”

“And then?”

“Then we need a new valve. A proper one.” He turned to look at her, and his face in the dim light looked older than she remembered, the lines around his eyes cut deep by years of fenland work. “Or we need a new engine.”

The engine thrummed on. The sound filled the shed, a steady heartbeat that pushed water through the ditches and kept the barley from drowning. Nell listened to it and felt the weight of the letter in her pocket, the weight of the notice she had pulled from her own gate. The Board’s formal notice. The compulsory purchase. The surveyor’s white stakes cutting through her land like a scar.

“A week,” she said.

“Maybe two.”

“That’s not long.”

Amos turned back to the engine. He placed one hand against the housing again, feeling the vibration, reading the machine the way he read the land. His face was calm, but she saw the exhaustion in the set of his shoulders, the way his weight rested too heavily on his feet.

“No,” he said. “It’s not.”

They stood together in the shed’s oily warmth, listening to the engine’s steady rhythm. Outside, the mist was thickening, swallowing the outlines of the barns and the distant shape of the farmhouse. The water was rising in the ditches, pressing against the banks, testing every weakness. The white stakes stood in the long field, marking the path of the new dyke that would cut Nell’s land in two.

She thought of Kitty in the farmhouse kitchen, her face pale, her hands wrapped around a cup of tea. She thought of Amos returning to his cottage, alone with his knowledge of the fen. She thought of the letter she carried, its creases worn soft from folding and unfolding.

“Thank you,” she said.

Amos did not look at her. He reached for a rag and began to wipe the oil from his hands, moving slowly, methodically, as if the gesture itself were a kind of rest.

“It’s only land,” he said. “It doesn’t feel anything.”

She recognized her own words, thrown back at her. She watched him clean his hands and felt something shift between them, a recognition that passed without being named. They were both tired. They were both fighting a battle that they could not win. But the engine was running, and the pump was working, and the barley had one more week to stand.

Nell turned toward the door. The mist outside had swallowed everything beyond the farmyard, and she could see only the faint shape of the farmhouse windows, glowing yellow through the white. She thought of warmth, of tea, of Kitty waiting in the kitchen with her cardigan buttoned to the throat.

“I’ll see you at first light,” she said.

“First light,” Amos agreed.

She stepped out of the shed and into the mist. The cold air hit her like a hand, closing around her throat and pressing against her chest. Her clothes were still damp, her boots still heavy with mud. The engine’s rhythm followed her across the farmyard, a steady thrum that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, the heartbeat of a farm that was still alive, still fighting, still holding on.

The farmhouse door opened before she reached it. Kitty stood in the doorway, her silhouette sharp against the lamplight behind her. She did not speak, but she moved aside to let Nell enter, and when Nell stepped into the warmth of the kitchen, Kitty closed the door behind her and turned the lock.

The range was glowing. The table was cleared. A cup of tea sat on the counter, still steaming, and Nell realized that Kitty must have been watching for her through the window, waiting, preparing.

“The pump?” Kitty’s voice was quiet, measured.

“Fixed. For now.”

Kitty nodded. She moved to the range and took a kettle from the hob, pouring more hot water into the cup on the counter. She handed it to Nell without a word, and Nell took it, feeling the warmth seep into her cold fingers.

They stood together in the kitchen, listening to the muffled thrum of the engine through the walls. The letter in Nell’s pocket pressed against her hip, a weight she had been carrying for too long. She thought of the white stakes in the long field, of the notice she had pulled from the gate, of Amos’s hands moving in the lamplight.

“Kitty.” She set the cup down on the table. “There’s something I need to show you.”

Kitty’s face was pale, but her eyes were steady. She did not move, did not speak, just waited.

Nell reached into her pocket and drew out the folded papers. The original letter from the Board, its edges soft and worn. The notice she had pulled from her gate, its bright nail still embedded in the wood at its corner. She spread them on the table, smoothing the creases with her palm.

Kitty looked down at them. Her hands, hanging at her sides, began to move, her fingers counting something invisible, a pattern only she could see.

“They want to take the long field,” Nell said.

“I know.” Kitty’s voice was calm, but there was something new in it, something that had not been there before. “I’ve seen the stakes. I’ve counted them.”

Nell felt the words settle into her chest. She had been carrying this alone, had thought she was protecting Kitty by keeping the weight of it to herself. But Kitty had been watching, counting, seeing what Nell had tried to hide.

“We’ll fight them,” Kitty said. Her voice cracked on the last word, then steadied. “We’ll fight them the way you fight the water. One day at a time.”

Nell looked at her daughter, standing in the kitchen’s yellow light with her cardigan buttoned to the throat and her hands still moving through their invisible patterns. The engine thrummed through the walls. The tea grew cold on the table. Outside, the mist pressed against the windows, white and impenetrable, swallowing everything.

“All right,” Nell said. “One day at a time.”
