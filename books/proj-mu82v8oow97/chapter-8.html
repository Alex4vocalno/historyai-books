Chapter 8

Kitty's Broken Routine

The repaired bank had held through the night, but the whole field still felt like a sponge beneath Nell’s boots, and she knew that holding was not the same as healed. She stood at the kitchen window with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea gone cold, watching the yard, waiting for the light to strengthen enough to show her what work the dark hours had done to her land. Behind her, the range gave off its steady warmth. The water bucket sat in its corner, half-full, the surface trembling each time the wind pressed against the farmhouse walls.

Kitty had been awake since before dawn. Nell had heard her moving in the room above—the soft tread of feet finding their familiar path from bed to washstand to window and back again. Three times the floorboards had creaked in that particular sequence, and each time Nell had counted the steps, knowing Kitty was counting too, measuring the morning against the pattern of all the mornings before it.

The tea in Nell’s cup had stopped steaming. She set it on the scarred table and reached for her gloves, working her fingers into the leather while her eyes stayed fixed on the gate at the end of the yard. The gate that led to the long field. The gate where Philip Danvers had stood with his clipboard and his measured voice, explaining what the Board would do and why she could not stop it.

She had the letter still. Folded into her pocket, worn thin at the creases from how many times she had unfolded it, read it, refolded it. The words had long since stopped being words and had become instead a kind of weight, pressing against her hip bone when she walked, reminding her with every step that time was moving and the Board’s patience was not infinite.

The stairs creaked. Nell turned to see Kitty descend, one hand on the banister, her face pale in the grey morning light. She wore her cardigan buttoned to the throat, her hair parted precisely in the middle and plaited, the same way she had worn it since she was a girl. Nell had stopped suggesting she might try something different. Change was not something Kitty bore easily.

“Tea’s in the pot,” Nell said. “Still warm.”

Kitty nodded but did not move toward the table. Her gaze had already drifted past Nell, through the window, toward the yard and the gate beyond. This was her ritual, the one she performed without fail. She would walk to that gate, count her steps, touch the post, and return. Some mornings Nell went with her. Most mornings she simply watched from the window, understanding that this small pilgrimage gave Kitty something the rest of the world could not.

“I’ll go with you today,” Nell said.

Kitty’s head turned. “You don’t need to.”

“I want to.”

“It’s early.”

“I’m awake.”

The words settled between them. Kitty’s hands hung at her sides, her fingers moving through a sequence Nell recognized—touching the seam of her cardigan, the edge of her sleeve, the fold of fabric at her wrist. A pattern. A counting. A way of making the world stay in its proper shape.

“Alright,” Kitty said.

They went out together. The morning air was thick with the smell of wet earth and the faint sweetness of decaying reeds from the fen beyond. Nell’s boots found the familiar ruts in the path, and she matched her pace to Kitty’s, letting her daughter lead. One step, two, three. Kitty counted as she walked, her lips moving without sound. Nell had learned not to ask what number she was counting toward. The question had upset Kitty once, long ago, and Nell had never asked again.

The gate stood at the boundary between the farmyard and the long field, its posts sunk deep into ground that had once been firm. Now the earth around the bases was soft, and Nell could see where the wood had begun to darken with moisture that never quite dried. The gate itself hung crooked, one hinge lower than the other, though it still swung and latched. She had meant to fix it. There had always been something more urgent.

Kitty stopped three paces from the gate. Her counting ceased. Nell saw her daughter’s shoulders rise and fall once, twice—a steadying that was more habit than necessity. Then Kitty stepped forward, reached out, and touched the gatepost with her fingertips.

The scream came before Nell could draw breath.

Kitty’s hand recoiled from the wood as if she had touched a coal. She stumbled backward, her heel catching on the rutted path, and Nell moved without thinking, her arm catching Kitty around the waist before she could fall. The sound that came from Kitty’s throat was not a word, not a cry for help, but something raw and formless. Her whole body shook.

“Kitty. Kitty, what is it?”

But Nell’s eyes had already followed her daughter’s gaze to the gatepost, and she saw what Kitty had seen. There, nailed to the weathered wood at the height of a man’s chest, was a sheet of paper. White, crisp, new. The ink still dark against the page. A notice from the Drainage Board, formal and impersonal, announcing that survey work would commence on the proposed dyke path through the long field, and that all affected landholders were required to provide access.

Nell’s stomach dropped. She had known this would come. She had known since Danvers had stood in this very yard and explained the process, the notices, the timelines. She had planned to reach the gate first, to spare Kitty. But the morning had slipped—she had thought there would be time, at least another day. She had underestimated how soon they would move, and now here they were, Kitty shaking in her arms, and that white paper flapping in the wind like a flag of surrender.

“Take it down,” Kitty said. Her voice cracked on the words. “Take it off.”

“I will.”

“Now. Take it off now.”

Nell moved toward the gate. Her hand reached for the paper, her fingers brushing the edge of the Board’s official seal, but before she could pull the nail free, Kitty’s voice rose again.

“No. No, don’t touch it.”

Nell turned. Kitty had pressed herself against the yard’s fence, both hands gripping the railings until her knuckles went white. Her breath came in short, shallow gasps that fogged in the cold air.

“Kitty, I need to take it down. It’s upsetting you.”

“You can’t. You can’t take it. That’s worse.”

“It’s just a piece of paper.”

“It wasn’t there before.” Kitty’s voice pitched higher. “It wasn’t there yesterday. I touched it yesterday and it wasn’t there and now it’s there and you want to take it away but that’s different too, that’s changing it again, that’s—“

Her words dissolved into sobs. Nell stood frozen between the gate and her daughter, one hand still raised toward the notice, the other hanging useless at her side. She had seen Kitty frightened before. She had seen her distressed by small changes—a meal served at a different time, a chair moved from its usual place. But this was different. This was not a chair or a meal. This was the gatepost, the one fixed point in Kitty’s world, the one thing she touched every single day, and now it carried something that had not been there before.

Nell crossed back to her daughter. She did not try to touch her. She stood at a distance that felt safe and spoke in the low, steady voice she used with spooked animals.

“Kitty. Listen to me.”

“I can’t. I can’t, it’s wrong, it’s all wrong.”

“The gatepost is still there. The wood is the same wood. The gate still swings and latches.”

“But the paper. The paper is on it. The paper is different.”

“I know.”

Kitty’s grip on the fence loosened. She began to rock—a small motion, back and forth—her eyes fixed on the notice as if it might leap from the wood and attack her. “Why is it there? Why did someone put it there?”

Nell’s jaw tightened. She thought of the letter in her pocket, the one she had not shown Kitty, the one she had folded and refolded until the paper had grown soft and the creases had started to tear. She thought of Danvers and his reasonable tone, his talk of drainage and improvement and the greater good. She thought of the long field, saturated and heavy, and the dyke that would be cut through it whether she agreed or not.

“Someone wants to change the land,” Nell said. “They think they can make it better by moving water from one place to another.”

“But it’s our land.”

“It is.”

“And they just… they just put their paper on it? Without asking?”

“They left a notice. It’s how they do things.”

Kitty’s rocking slowed. Her gaze stayed fixed on the white rectangle, and Nell could see her daughter’s mind working, trying to fit this new object into the pattern she had built, finding no place where it belonged. The gatepost had been touched. It had been altered. The world had shifted, and Kitty had not been asked either.

“It’s not right,” Kitty said. Her voice was quieter now, but it carried a weight that surprised Nell. “It’s not right that they can do that. Put their paper on our gate. Change our things without asking.”

“No. It isn’t right.”

“Are you going to let them?”

The question hung in the air between them. Nell felt the weight of it settle against her chest, pressing down on the breath she tried to take. Kitty’s eyes had left the notice now and were fixed on Nell instead, waiting for an answer. There was something in her daughter’s face that Nell had not seen before. Not just fear. Not just distress at the broken pattern. Something else was rising beneath it, something that looked almost like anger.

“I’ve been fighting them,” Nell said. “In my way. I’ve been trying to find a way to stop it.”

“Does the paper mean you lost?”

“It means they’re moving forward. It doesn’t mean I’ve stopped.”

Kitty’s hands released the fence. She stood straighter, and her gaze drifted back to the notice. The tears had dried on her cheeks, leaving tracks that caught the morning light. Nell watched her daughter study the paper, taking in its shape, its placement, the way it moved in the wind. Kitty was counting again—Nell could see it in the way her lips moved. Counting the inches between the nail and the edge of the post. Counting the breaths she took. Counting something that Nell could not see.

“Can I touch it?” Kitty asked.

“The paper?”

“The gatepost. Where the paper isn’t.”

Nell’s throat tightened. “You can touch whatever you want. It’s still your gate.”

Kitty moved slowly, each step deliberate, her eyes never leaving the white rectangle. She approached the post from the side opposite the notice, her hand extending until her fingertips brushed the weathered wood. Nell saw her daughter’s eyes close, saw the tension in her shoulders shift as she felt the familiar grain, the splinters that had been there for years, the groove worn into the surface by decades of hands passing over it.

“It’s still here,” Kitty said.

“The wood?”

“The gatepost. Underneath.”

Nell stepped closer. She stood beside her daughter and looked at the notice—really looked at it—for the first time. The words were formal, dry, the kind of language that drained meaning from the things it described. Land parcels and drainage channels and rights of access. Nothing about the woman who had worked this soil for forty years. Nothing about the daughter who counted her steps and touched the gate each morning because the world was too big and too changeable and she needed something to stay the same.

“They’re going to dig a dyke,” Nell said. “Through the long field. They say it will help drain the fen.”

“Will it?”

“I don’t know. Maybe. But not for us. For the farms downstream, the ones closer to the river. We just happen to be in the path.”

Kitty’s hand stayed on the gatepost. Her eyes opened, and she looked past the notice, past the gate, to the long field beyond. Nell followed her gaze. The field lay fallow this season, its soil dark and heavy, the remains of last year’s celery crop still visible in the rows. In the distance, the fen stretched toward the horizon, a flat expanse of reed and water and sky that seemed to hold the light before it ever reached the farm.

“They can’t just take it,” Kitty said.

“They have authority. The Board. They can file notices, hold hearings, make decisions. It’s all legal.”

“But it’s ours.”

“I know.”

Kitty turned to face her mother. There was something new in her expression, something that had not been there when she had come down the stairs this morning. The fear was still present, lingering at the edges of her eyes, in the way her fingers continued their restless movement against the seam of her cardigan. But beneath it, Nell saw something that looked almost like resolve.

“What are we going to do?”

The question was not the same as the one Kitty had asked before. Not are you going to let them, but what are we going to do. The shift was small, barely perceptible, but Nell felt it land against her heart like a stone dropped into still water.

“I’ve been to see Danvers,” Nell said. “The man who came to measure the field. He’s with the Board. He’s the one who decides where the dyke goes.”

“What did he say?”

“That the path is set. That the surveys are done. That there will be a hearing, but it’s a formality.”

“A hearing?”

“Where we can object. Make our case. But I don’t know what case there is to make. They’ve already decided.”

Kitty’s gaze returned to the notice. She studied it for a long moment, and when she spoke again, her voice was steady. “Then we need to find something they haven’t decided.”

Nell looked at her daughter. The words were unexpected, coming from someone who had spent her life trying to keep the world from changing. But Kitty’s eyes were clear, and her jaw was set, and the hand that had been trembling at her side was now still.

“What do you mean?”

“I mean they’ve decided about the dyke. They’ve decided about the path. But they haven’t decided about us. About how hard we’ll fight.”

The morning light had strengthened while they stood at the gate. The grey had begun to lift from the sky, and the fen was emerging from the mist, its reeds catching the first gold of the sun. Nell could hear the birds waking in the withy bed, their calls threading through the silence. The world was moving forward, as it always did, indifferent to the small struggles of the people who lived within it.

“I’ve been so afraid of hurting you,” Nell said. The words came out before she could stop them, an admission she had not meant to make. “Of making things worse. You need things to stay the same, and I’ve been trying to hold everything together—the farm, the land, us. But I can’t hold it all. The water keeps coming. The ground keeps softening. And now this.”

“I know,” Kitty said.

“You know?”

“I see it. The way you watch the fields. The way you come in at night with your boots caked in mud and your hands shaking from the cold. I see you trying to hold everything up by yourself.”

Nell’s eyes burned. She blinked against the sensation, unwilling to let the tears fall. She had not cried since her husband had died, and she did not intend to start now, not here, not in front of Kitty.

“I don’t know how to stop them,” Nell said.

“Maybe we don’t stop them. Maybe we just make them see us.”

“They don’t want to see us. We’re just obstacles. Pieces of land in the way of their improvement.”

“Then we become obstacles they can’t ignore.”

Kitty’s voice was quiet, but it carried a certainty that Nell had never heard before. Her daughter, who counted steps and touched gateposts and needed the world to stay in its proper shape, was standing before her and speaking of resistance. It made no sense. And yet it made all the sense in the world.

“You’re not afraid?” Nell asked.

“I’m terrified.” Kitty’s hand found her mother’s, their fingers intertwining. “But I’m more afraid of watching you carry this alone.”

The wind shifted, carrying with it the smell of the fen—the sweetness of rot and growth and water. Nell touched the pocket where the notice now rested, its edges worn from being pried away. She had taken it down herself that morning, and still she felt the hatred rise in her chest. Not for the paper itself, but for what it represented. A world that could change without asking. A power that could reach into her home and alter the shape of her days.

She thought of the letter in her pocket, and the notice she had pried from the gate, the one that held the formal language of the Board’s intent. She thought of the gate that hung crooked on its hinges, the post that was softening in the wet ground, the fence that needed mending and the ditch that needed clearing and the hundred small tasks that waited for her each day. She thought of the long field, saturated and heavy, and the dyke that would cut through it like a wound.

“We should go in,” Nell said. “It’s cold.”

“We should have taken that paper down.”

“It’s evidence. For the hearing. It’s here in my pocket.”

“It was on our gate.” Kitty’s grip tightened on her mother’s hand. “I know you took it. But we should write down what it says. What it meant. We shouldn’t forget.”

Nell looked at her daughter. Kitty’s face was pale, her jaw set, her eyes fixed on the gate where the notice had hung. This was the same woman who had gone silent at the sight of the paper, whose measured counting had faltered, who had stood rooted and trembling when the change itself was too much to bear. And now she was asking to face it.

“Are you sure?”

“I don’t want it there. Every time I walk to this gate, I don’t want to see it. I don’t want to count it. I don’t want it to be part of my morning.”

Nell released Kitty’s hand. She crossed to the gatepost, and this time she did not hesitate. Her fingers found the nail that held the paper in place, and she pulled. The wood resisted for a moment, the nail embedded deep, but Nell had been working this land for forty years, and her hands were strong. The nail came free with a sound that seemed too loud for the quiet morning, and the notice fell into her palm.

She looked at it one last time. The words that had weighed on her for weeks now seemed smaller—just ink on paper, just a thing that could be removed and folded and put away. She tucked it into her pocket alongside the letter, two pieces of paper pressing against her hip, two weights that she could carry.

When she turned back to Kitty, her daughter was already walking toward the farmhouse, her steps measured and deliberate, counting under her breath. Nell watched her go, and then she looked at the gatepost—at the small hole where the nail had been, at the patch of wood that was slightly lighter than the surrounding grain.

The gatepost was still there. The wood was still solid beneath her touch. The morning was still cold, and the fen was still waiting, and the Board would still come with their machines and their plans. But for now, this small thing had been restored. For now, the pattern had been mended.

Nell followed her daughter inside, and she did not look back.
