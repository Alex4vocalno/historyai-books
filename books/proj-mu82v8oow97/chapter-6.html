Chapter 6

Amoss Last Pump

────────────────────────────────────────

The knock came from inside the engine—a hammer striking wet wood, then a shriek of metal that flung Nell’s hand off the throttle wheel. The pump house filled with the stench of burning oil and friction-heat. In the silence after, she heard the water already winning, the field outside drinking the news.

Amos was already crouched by the housing, palm flat against the hot iron. He jerked it back, shook the burn off his fingers.

“Throw the belt,” he said.

She didn’t argue. The leather loop hung from its nail by the door, and she pulled it down, fed it over the flywheel, felt its weight as she dragged it clear of the motor. The whole assembly had run hot for two days—she’d felt it in the shudder of the floorboards, seen it in the way the exhaust smoked—but she’d told herself it would hold until the season turned drier. Now the piston rod had cracked. Beyond the pump house door the long field lay black and sodden from the spring rains, and if the water table rose another inch it would take the gate, and after the gate the lower acre where the celery crowns were just beginning to swell.

Amos stood. He wiped his hands on his trousers, leaving dark streaks. “Cracked clean through. I can mend it.”

“How long?”

“Hour. Maybe two.” He didn’t look at her. His eyes stayed on the machine, reading its failure the way a ploughman reads weather.

“The forge?”

“Best way.”

She nodded. The forge was a lean-to behind the barn, a relic from when the farm kept its own smithing. Amos had brought it back to life the previous summer, patching the chimney, banking the hearth with river clay. She’d watched him work once or twice, struck by the care in his hands—the way he’d test the heat with a strip of paper, the way he’d turn the iron at just the moment it flowered orange. He’d never offered an explanation for where he’d learned it, and she’d never asked. You didn’t ask a man about his past in the Fens. You watched what he could do, and you trusted the work or you didn’t.

They lifted the piston rod together, a dead weight of iron that had been spinning a thousand times a minute. Amos carried it on his shoulder, his gait steady despite the burden. Nell walked behind, her eyes on the back of his neck where the sun had bleached the hair to the color of dried thatch.

The forge was dark inside, smelling of old ash and the mineral tang of the anvil. Amos set the rod on a workbench scarred with gouges and built the fire. Nell found the blower handle and worked it while he arranged kindling, then coal. The flame caught, and the heat began to build against her face, a dry pressure that pushed back the damp living in her bones.

When the fire was hot enough, Amos lifted the cracked section into the coals with the tongs. He didn’t speak. He never spoke much when he worked—a habit she’d come to appreciate, the way he saved his breath for the task. She’d known men who filled every silence with complaint or speculation, men whose voices ground on the ear like a dull blade. Amos was different. His silence had a weight to it, a presence, as if he were listening to something she couldn’t hear.

She stepped back, giving him room, and her hand found the letter in her pocket. The paper was soft now from carrying, the fold-lines worn to creases. She’d read it a dozen times since it came from the Drainage Board, each reading a small erosion. The words hadn’t changed. They never did. Formal notice of proposed works. The new dyke would cut through the long field along a surveyed line, and her field would become a conduit for water from three farms upstream. Compensation would be assessed. Objections might be lodged within thirty days. The language was careful, polite, and utterly indifferent to what it meant to stand in a kitchen at dawn and wonder if your daughter would have a home in a year.

She hadn’t told Kitty the full of it. The girl knew something was wrong—she’d have had to be blind not to see the stakes Danvers had driven into the peat, the bright flags of surveyor’s tape fluttering at each marker—but Nell had kept the letter folded, kept the worst of it pressed between her palm and her thigh whenever Kitty watched from the window. There was no point in naming a fear before it had teeth.

The iron in the forge began to glow. Amos turned it with the tongs, his movements slow and deliberate, and she saw sweat darken the collar of his shirt. The heat in the lean-to built, a thick pressure that made the air feel close, and she thought of Kitty in the kitchen, the cup of tea she’d been holding when Nell left, the way her face had looked through the glass—pale and watchful, the face of a child who had learned too early that the world could shift beneath your feet without warning.

“Hand me the stock,” Amos said.

She passed him the rod of mild steel he’d use to reinforce the weld. He positioned it against the crack, his hammer already in his other hand, and when he struck the first blow the sound rang out against the anvil face. Again. Again. The rhythm established itself, a steady pulse that seemed to mark time in the lean-to, and she watched the metal yield under his attention, the crack beginning to close.

He was working faster now, the hammer rising and falling with a kind of urgency she hadn’t seen in him before. The heat from the forge was intense enough that she had to step back toward the door, but he didn’t seem to feel it. His jaw was set, his breathing audible, and she realized with a start that there was something almost violent in the motion, as if he were driving the iron back together by force of will.

Then he missed a strike.

The hammer glanced off the anvil edge, sending a spark skittering across the dirt floor. He swore under his breath—a sound she’d never heard him make. His free hand came up to his face, pressing against his temple, and for a moment he stood frozen, the hot iron forgotten in the tongs, his whole body rigid.

“Amos?”

He didn’t answer. His breathing had gone shallow, rapid, and she could see the tendons standing out in his neck.

“Amos. The iron.”

He blinked. His hand dropped. He looked at the glowing metal as if he’d never seen it before, and then his voice came, low and rough, not directed at her or at anything she could see.

“Shells falling like rain at Passchendaele.”

The words hung in the heat. She didn’t move. Outside, a bird called from the withy bed, a thin sound that seemed to come from another world.

Amos shook his head once, sharply, and bent back to the work. The hammer resumed its rhythm, but something had shifted—the silence between the strikes was different now, charged with something she couldn’t name. She wanted to ask what he’d meant, where the words had come from, but the set of his shoulders told her the door had already closed. He’d given her something without meaning to, and the only way he knew to bear it was to pretend it hadn’t happened.

She understood that. She’d spent two years practicing the same discipline.

The iron was cooling. He lifted it from the anvil and carried it to the slack tub, thrusting it into the water with a hiss of steam. The sound filled the lean-to, white vapor rising and curling, and when he pulled the rod out it was whole, the crack fused and reinforced, a ridge of new metal running along the break like a scar.

They walked back to the pump house without speaking. The field stretched out on either side, black and wet, and she could see the water standing in the low places, bright as glass where the afternoon light caught it. The gate was visible from here, the new post they’d set still leaning slightly despite the bracing. Another thing that wouldn’t hold forever. Another thing that needed mending.

Inside the pump house, Amos knelt by the engine and began to reassemble the housing. She handed him tools when he reached for them, the two of them moving in the wordless coordination that comes from years of shared work, and when the piston rod was back in place he stepped back and nodded at the throttle.

“Try it.”

She turned the wheel. The engine coughed, caught, and settled into a rhythm—a chug and wheeze louder than before, that would need more oil and more attention, but that ran. The belt took up its rotation. The pump began to draw, and she heard the water moving through the pipes, a low steady sound that meant the field was draining, that the long field would dry, that for today at least the boundary held.

Amos was already at the door. He didn’t look back, didn’t offer a word about what had passed in the forge. He simply walked out into the afternoon, his shadow stretching across the wet peat, and she watched him go with the engine’s rhythm beating against her ribs.

She stood in the doorway. The heat from the motor pushed against her back, and the smell of exhaust mixed with the fen’s damp breath. Somewhere beyond the field, the surveyor’s stakes marked the line where the new dyke would run, bright flags in the grey air.

Her hand found the letter again. The paper was warm from her body, soft as cloth, and she thought of Kitty at the kitchen window, watching the field as if she could hold it steady by sheer force of looking. The girl feared change the way the peat feared the sun—a deep, instinctive recoil from anything that might dry out the shape of her days. Nell had seen it in the way she clung to routines, the way she still set a place at the table for the dead, the way she flinched whenever talk turned to Ely or the future. The drainage board’s notice was more than a threat to the land. It was a crack in the foundation of the only world Kitty had ever trusted.

The pump chugged on. The water rose and fell. And Nell stood in the doorway with the letter in her pocket and the sound of Amos’s words still hanging in the air, unanswered, a weight she hadn’t asked to carry and couldn’t now set down.

She found Amos still by the pump, his back to her, wiping his hands on a rag. The machine’s rhythm had steadied, but the repair was temporary—they both knew it. He’d seen to it without being asked, the way he always did, and Nell had let him because the alternative was watching another piece of their infrastructure surrender to the damp.

“Bearing’s patched,” he said without turning. His voice carried the flatness of a man who had learned to report damage without emotion. “She’ll hold for now. But we’ll need a proper replacement before long.”

Nell stepped closer, her boots sinking slightly in the mud that seemed to creep up around the pump’s base. “How long before long?”

“Month. Maybe two if we’re lucky.” He paused, still facing the machine. “I knew a fellow once. Could’ve fixed this in an afternoon. Had hands like—well. Doesn’t matter now.”

She waited. Amos rarely spoke of before, and when he did, the words came out clipped, accidental, as if his mouth had betrayed him.

He turned finally, wiping his palms on his trousers. In the gray light, his face looked older than she remembered, the lines around his eyes cut deeper. “We were in France together. The fellow with the hands. He could fix anything—engines, radios, the mess tins when the handles broke. Called him the Engineer, though none of us knew his real rating.” Amos’s jaw tightened. “Shrapnel got him in ‘seventeen. Same day I took the shrapnel in my own leg. Funny how that works.”

He wasn’t looking at her anymore. His gaze had drifted past the pump, past the field, to something Nell couldn’t see. She recognized that look. She’d seen it on her husband’s face, in the last year, when the pain was bad and his mind wandered to places she couldn’t follow.

“I didn’t know,” she said. “About your leg.”

“Never came up.” He shrugged, the gesture too casual for the weight it carried. “Doesn’t bother me most days. Just when the damp sets in.”

The pump knocked again, a sound like a heart struggling against something that wouldn’t let it stop. Nell thought of all the things men carried home from war—the visible wounds and the invisible ones, the memories that surfaced in fragments, half-told and quickly buried.

“Can you still fix it?” she asked. “The pump.”

“Can fix anything that wants fixing.” He met her eyes then, and something in his expression shifted—a recognition, perhaps, that she understood what he wasn’t saying. That some things couldn’t be repaired, only held together for a little while longer.

“Then do it,” she said. “We’ve got a month.”
