Chapter 10

Frost and the Barley

The cold came down out of a clear sky just after sunset, settling over the fen like a hand pressed flat against the earth. Nell felt it the moment she stepped out of the farmhouse door — a stillness in the air that had nothing to do with the absence of wind, a brittleness that made the distant sound of the pump seem sharper than it should. She stood on the threshold with her hand still on the latch, reading the evening the way she had learned to read it forty years ago, when her father had shown her how to taste the air and know what the morning would bring.

Frost. A killing frost, and not the gentle kind that silvered the grass and melted by mid-morning. This was the sort that came on clear nights in late spring, when the ground had already given up its winter heat and the tender shoots had begun to believe in warmth.

She thought of the barley in the lower field. Early-sown, pushing up through the black peat in pale green rows that had no business being so trusting. Two weeks of mild weather had coaxed them into growth, and now they stood exposed — ankle-high and utterly defenseless against what was coming.

Nell did not waste time cursing. She went back inside and reached for the lantern that hung by the door, her fingers finding the matches without looking. Kitty sat at the table with her hands wrapped around a cup of cold tea, her face still carrying the particular stillness that had settled over her since the morning’s upheaval. The notice was gone from the gatepost, folded into Nell’s pocket alongside the letter, but something in her daughter had shifted and not yet settled back into place.

“Barley needs covering,” Nell said. “Frost coming.”

Kitty looked up. The firelight caught the part in her hair, the precise plait that had not been disturbed even by the morning’s terror. “I’ll get my coat.”

“Stay inside. It’ll be a long night.”

“I can carry straw.”

Nell almost argued. The words rose in her throat — something about Kitty’s hands, about the way her face had crumpled in distress before anger took hold, about the fragility that seemed to run through her daughter like a crack in good china. But she stopped herself. Nell had pulled the notice from the gate herself. Kitty had demanded it come down. Something in her had found a spine that Nell had not known existed, and to send her inside now would be to deny what had happened.

“Bring gloves,” Nell said instead. “The good ones.”

She went out to the barn without waiting to see if Kitty followed. The straw stack rose against the back wall, golden and dense, giving off the faint sweet smell of last summer’s sun. Nell had forked this straw herself, had watched Amos bind it into sheaves during the harvest, had stored it here against the winter’s need for bedding. Now she would spread it over the barley like a blanket, and if the frost was as hard as she feared, it might just be enough.

Amos found her there before she had finished gathering the forks. He came in through the small door at the back, his shoulders already hunched against the cold, and he did not ask what she was doing. He simply crossed to the far wall and began gathering the spare lanterns, checking the oil levels with the practiced efficiency of a man who had spent his whole life reading the needs of a farm before they were spoken.

“How much?” he asked.

“Lower field. All of it.”

He did not flinch at the scale of the task. He only nodded, once, and reached for a fork.

They worked in silence. There was nothing to say. The frost was coming, and the barley was vulnerable, and the work needed doing. Nell had never been one for speech when action would serve, and Amos had matched her in this for the years he had worked her land. He was not a young man anymore — his hair had gone grey at the temples, and his hands moved more stiffly in the cold than they once had — but he worked with a steadiness that needed no comment.

Kitty appeared at the barn door with her cardigan buttoned to the throat and her work gloves pulled tight. She carried a third fork, and when Nell looked at her, she only said, “Three is faster than two.”

It was. Nell saw this immediately — the way the work divided itself into parallel rows, the way the straw could be forked and spread in a rhythm that covered ground twice as quickly as she had hoped. She had expected to work alone, or at best with Amos, and the hours ahead had stretched out in her mind like a road with no end. But Kitty’s presence changed the mathematics of the night, and for the first time since the notice had appeared on her gate, Nell felt something like hope.

They loaded the straw onto the cart and drove it down to the lower field in the grey light of early evening. The barley rows stretched out before them, pale against the dark peat, and the sky above was already showing the first stars. No clouds to hold the earth’s heat. No shelter. Just the endless clarity of a fenland night, and the cold sinking down through the air like sediment through water.

Nell lit the first lantern and set it at the field’s edge. Amos lit the second and placed it thirty yards down. The third went to Kitty, who held it high and looked out across the rows with an expression Nell could not quite read.

“We start at the far end,” Nell said. “Work back toward the cart. Cover every row.”

They began.

The work was simple in its nature — fork straw, spread it thin, move on — but brutal in its execution. Each forkful had to be lifted from the cart, carried to the rows, and laid down in a layer thick enough to hold the day’s warmth but thin enough not to crush the shoots beneath. The barley was too young to bear weight, too tender to survive exposure, and the margin between salvation and destruction was measured in inches.

Nell’s shoulders burned within the first hour. Her lower back ached with the rhythm of the work — bend, lift, spread, step — and her fingers had gone numb inside her gloves despite the friction of movement. The cold pressed against her from all sides, seeping through her coat and settling into the spaces between her bones. She could feel the frost beginning to form on the fabric of her sleeves, could see it crystallizing on the straw as they laid it down.

Amos worked in the next row over, his movements steady and economical. He did not hurry. Hurry led to mistakes, to thin patches and crushed shoots, to the kind of carelessness that cost more than it saved. He worked at a pace that could be maintained, and Nell matched him, and behind her, Kitty matched them both.

She heard her daughter’s breathing, slightly ragged with effort, and the scrape of the fork tines against the cart. She heard Amos’s boots on the frozen ground, the creak of the lantern as wind stirred the flame, the distant sound of water moving through the ditches. The fen was alive with small sounds, even in the darkness, and Nell found herself listening for the pump — the rhythm of it, the steady pulse that meant the water was being moved, that the land was being kept.

The Board wanted to cut a new dyke through her lower field. The notice had said as much, in the careful language of compulsory powers and drainage improvements. They had drawn their lines on maps, had sent their surveyor to walk her land with his stakes and his chain, had nailed their intentions to her gatepost like a claim of ownership. And now she worked in the dark to save a crop that might be underwater within the year, spreading straw over rows that might be dredged away before the harvest.

The thought should have broken her. It should have made the fork feel heavier in her hands, should have turned the cold deeper into her chest. But instead, she found herself working with a kind of fury, each forkful laid down with a precision that felt like defiance. Let them come with their maps and their notices. Let them bring their machines and their legal powers. Tonight, she was saving barley. Tonight, she was winning.

Hours passed. The cart emptied and was refilled. The rows behind them grew covered, pale gold against the dark earth, and the rows ahead shrank with each pass. Nell lost track of time, lost track of everything except the rhythm of the work and the shape of the task. Her body had gone beyond aching into a kind of hollow exhaustion, but her hands kept moving, and her feet kept stepping, and the work continued.

At some point, Amos appeared at her side with a cup of something hot. Tea, she realized, though she had not seen him go back to the farmhouse. She drank it without stopping, the warmth spreading through her chest like a small fire, and then she handed the cup back and returned to the work.

“Two rows left,” he said.

She looked up. The eastern sky had begun to lighten, not with sunrise but with the grey promise of it, and the field behind them stretched out in ordered rows of straw. Two rows remained, and then the barley would be covered, and then they could rest.

Kitty had slowed. Nell could see it in the way her daughter’s shoulders had begun to sag, in the slight tremble of her fork as she lifted each load. She had been working for hours beyond what her body was accustomed to, beyond what her quiet routines had prepared her for, and the cost was showing.

“Kitty.” Nell kept her voice low. “Go sit in the cart. We’ll finish these last rows.”

“I can do it.”

“I know.” Nell did not argue. She simply waited, and after a moment, Kitty nodded and walked back to the cart, climbing up onto the seat with her hands in her lap and her face turned toward the lightening sky.

Nell watched her go. The morning before, Kitty had screamed at the sight of a notice on a gatepost. Tonight, she had worked through frost and darkness to save a field she had never shown any particular interest in. The transformation was not complete — Nell could see the trembling in her daughter’s frame, the exhaustion that bordered on collapse — but it was real. Something had shifted in Kitty when Nell had pulled that notice from the wood, and Nell did not yet know what it meant.

She turned back to the work. Amos had already started on the next row, and she fell in beside him, their movements synchronized without discussion. The straw went down in thin layers, and the barley disappeared beneath it, and the sky grew lighter with each passing minute.

The last forkful landed as the sun cleared the horizon. Nell straightened and felt her spine crack in three places, and she let out a breath that hung in the air before her. The field was covered. Every row, every shoot, every inch of vulnerable green had been blanketed in straw, and the frost that crystallized on the surface would not touch the growing things beneath.

Amos leaned on his fork, his face grey with exhaustion but his eyes steady. He looked out across the field with the measuring gaze of a man who had seen enough harvests to know what success looked like, and after a long moment, he nodded.

“It’ll hold,” he said.

Nell did not trust herself to speak. She only nodded back. She had sent Amos to fetch the cart while she finished the last row, and Kitty had come with him to check on the morning’s work. Now her daughter sat on the bench seat with her head against the side rail, her breath coming in small white clouds, her face peaceful in the early light.

Amos took the reins. They drove back to the farmhouse in silence, the cart creaking over the frozen ground, the sun rising behind them in a pale wash of gold. The pump was still running in the distance, and the water was still moving through the ditches, and the Board’s notice was still folded in Nell’s pocket. The barley was safe for now, but the water was still rising. The land was still sinking. The fight was not over.

But it was not lost, either. Nell looked back at the field, at the straw-covered rows that held her crop and her hope, and she felt something settle in her chest. Not peace, exactly. Not relief. Something harder, more durable. A kind of resolve that had been forged in the cold and the darkness, in the ache of muscles and the silence between workers.

She thought of the hearing that would come, of the arguments she would have to make, of the evidence she would have to present. She thought of the letter in her pocket and the notice that had been nailed to her gate, of the surveyor’s stakes that still marked the path of the proposed dyke. She thought of Amos working beside her through the night, and Kitty waking to find the world changed and herself capable of facing it.

The farm had been here for three generations. Her grandfather had drained the peat and planted the first willows. Her father had built the farmhouse and dug the ditches. Her husband had worked beside her until the land had claimed him, and now she was alone with the weight of it all, carrying the burden that should have been shared.

But she was not alone. Not entirely. Amos had worked through the night without being asked. Kitty had found a strength that Nell had never suspected. And the land itself — the peat and the water and the willow roots that held the boundaries together — was still fighting, in its slow and patient way, against the forces that would reshape it.

Nell climbed down from the cart at the farmhouse door and went inside to put the kettle on. The morning stretched ahead of her, full of the ordinary work of the farm, and she would need to check the banks and tend the pump and see to the hundred small tasks that kept the water at bay. But first, she would make tea, and she would sit for a moment, and she would let herself feel the full weight of what they had accomplished.

The frost had come. They had met it. And in the morning light, with the straw-covered field behind her and her daughter sleeping in the cart, Nell Askew allowed herself to believe that they might survive this after all.
