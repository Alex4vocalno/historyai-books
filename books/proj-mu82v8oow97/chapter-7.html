Chapter 7

Nell and Amos, Against the Water

The pump rattled on behind them, a mechanical heartbeat in the grey morning, but Amos’s words from the night before hung in the damp air unanswered. Nell stood at the edge of the lower field, her boots sunk to the ankle in ground that had no firmness left to give. The dark water of the ditch ran high, nearly level with the bank, and somewhere beneath that still surface she could feel the slow, patient pressure of the fen pushing back against every ditch they had ever cut.

She had come out before first light, drawn by something she could not name—a weight in her chest that had nothing to do with the letter folded in her pocket. The drainage board’s notice had arrived three days ago, and she had read it through twice before setting it on the kitchen shelf, as if placing it there could contain what it meant. Now she stood watching the water, and the water did not watch her back. It simply waited.

Behind her, the farmhouse kitchen showed a pale square of light. Kitty would be moving soon, putting the kettle on, looking out the window to see where her mother had gone. Nell did not turn. She kept her eyes on the ditch bank where it curved along the lower field, holding back the brown water that wanted nothing more than to spread itself across her celery beds.

The ground gave a little as she shifted her weight. A soft, wet sound. The kind of sound a body makes when it is no longer quite alive.

Amos found her there when the light had strengthened enough to show the full extent of what the night had done. He came across the farmyard with his spade over one shoulder, his breath making small clouds in the cold air. He did not ask what she was looking at. He had learned, over the years, that Nell Askew would say what needed saying when she was ready, and not before.

They stood together in silence, watching the water.

Then Amos took a step forward, testing the ground near the ditch edge with the toe of his boot. The earth held for a moment, then gave with a suddenness that made him pull back. Water seeped up around the impression, filling the small hollow with brown that reflected the pale sky.

“Peat’s sinking faster this side.”

Nell said nothing. She had seen it too—the way the bank had slumped overnight, the upper edge now hanging over the water like a lip that had forgotten how to close. The ditch was winning. It had been winning for years, and she had known it, and she had kept digging and pumping and patching because that was what you did when the alternative was to watch everything go.

“We’ll need to brace it.”

“Before noon.” Amos turned his head, measuring the sky. “Rain coming.”

As if the fen needed any more water. Nell bent and picked up a handful of the saturated earth. It ran through her fingers like something between mud and soup, leaving her palm stained dark. She wiped it on her skirt without thinking. The fabric was already marked from yesterday’s work, and the day before that, and the day before that.

“Get the spades.”

They worked in the way of people who had done this work together for too many years to need discussion. Amos took the ditch side, where the ground was most uncertain, and Nell took the field side, where the peat still had some grip left. They cut turves from the higher ground—thick, heavy squares of sod that came up wet and dark, smelling of the deep time that had made them. The smell of peat was a particular thing, vegetable and mineral at once, the scent of something that had once been alive and was now becoming something else entirely.

The first hour passed in the rhythm of cut and carry and place. Nell’s shoulders burned with the effort of lifting the waterlogged turves, each one heavier than it looked. Her boots made a sucking sound each time she pulled them free of the mud, and by the second hour the cold had crept through the leather and into her feet, so that each step sent a dull ache up through her ankles.

Amos worked without complaint, his breathing steady and deep. He was ten years older than Nell’s husband would have been, if her husband had lived, and his face had the kind of weathering that came from decades of fen work—skin creased at the corners of the eyes, hands that had permanent stains at the knuckles. He did not talk while he worked. He simply cut and lifted and placed, his movements efficient and sure.

But the ground was not sure. The ground was tired.

Nell saw it first—a darkening of the earth near the ditch edge, a wetness that was more than wet. She straightened, her hand tightening on the spade handle.

“Amos.”

He looked up, followed her gaze. The bank was giving way. Slowly, almost gently, as if apologizing for the inconvenience, a section of the turf they had just placed began to slide downward, taking with it a wedge of the underlying peat. Water rushed into the gap, brown and eager, and the sound it made was a soft, wet gulp that seemed to come from the throat of something hungry.

They moved at the same time. Amos threw himself sideways, spade out, trying to catch the falling sod before it hit the water. Nell dropped to her knees in the mud, her hands grabbing at the edge of the collapsing bank, feeling the earth give beneath her fingers. For a moment they were both still, frozen in a position that had no stability, waiting for the ground to decide whether it would hold or go.

It held. Barely. The slide stopped a few inches down, the turf wedged at an angle against the peat below. Water still seeped through, finding new paths around the obstruction, but the worst of the breach had paused.

Nell’s knees were soaked through. Her hands were covered in mud to the wrist, and when she pulled them free, the peat clung to her skin like it wanted to keep her. She looked at Amos, who was crouched on the ditch side with his spade planted in the unstable ground, his face grey with the effort of not moving.

“Not enough.” His voice was flat. Not complaining. Just stating what they both knew.

“No.”

They looked at the breach together. It was perhaps four feet across, a wound in the bank that let the ditch water bleed through into the lower field. Beyond it, the celery beds stretched in dark, parallel rows—the celery that was supposed to pay this year’s rates, the celery that was supposed to keep the farm whole for another season. If the water reached those beds, if it sat there for more than a day, the roots would rot and the stalks would yellow and all of the spring planting would be for nothing.

Nell thought of the papers in her pocket. The drainage board’s words, formal and careful, explaining that the new dyke would cut through the long field for the improvement of the district. The improvement of the district. As if the district were a single thing that could be improved, and not a hundred different lives, each one holding onto its own piece of ground.

“Cut from the withy bed.” She heard her own voice as if someone else were speaking. “The roots there hold better.”

Amos nodded once, without questioning how she knew or whether it would work. He turned and began making his way along the ditch path toward the withy bed that grew at the boundary of her land. The willows there had been planted by her grandfather, or so her mother had said—a dense thicket of whip-thin branches that sent their roots deep into the wet ground, holding the earth together in a way that bare soil never could.

Nell stayed by the breach, watching the water. It was still now, but not still enough. She could see the current moving beneath the surface, a slow brown flow that had no patience and no mercy. The fen had been here before her grandfather came, before the first drain was cut, before anyone thought they could make this land serve them. It would be here after she was gone. That was the thing about the fen—it did not care about improvement schemes or drainage boards or the stubborn pride of one woman who refused to let go.

But she was not gone yet.

She knelt in the mud and began to dig with her hands, carving a deeper channel where the water could pool without spreading. Her fingers went numb almost immediately, the cold biting through her skin and into the bone. She kept digging. The mud was dense and heavy, and each handful required effort that her tired arms could barely give. She kept digging.

When Amos returned, he carried six turves of withy-bound sod across his shoulders, the roots dangling dark and wet. He had cut them from the edge of the bed, where the willows grew thickest, and the earth there was tangled with pale root-threads that looked almost like hair.

“Start at the bottom.” Nell did not look up from her digging. “Work up.”

They placed the first turve together, Nell guiding it into the hole she had carved, Amos pressing it down with the flat of his spade. The roots caught against the peat, tangling, holding. The second turve went above it, and then the third, each one overlapping the last like shingles on a roof. Water pushed against their work, finding gaps, forcing its way through. They pressed harder. Amos braced his shoulder against the sod and leaned his whole weight into it, and Nell did the same from the other side.

For a long moment, nothing happened. The water pushed, and they pushed back, and the ground beneath them trembled with the strain of holding two forces that would not yield.

Then the flow slowed. The gap began to close.

They worked without speaking. There was no breath for words. Each turve had to be placed and pressed and held until it settled, and by the time they reached the top of the breach, Nell’s arms were shaking so badly she could barely lift her hands. The last turve went in crooked, but it held. The water rose behind it, dark and sullen, and the new bank held it back.

Nell sat back on her heels, mud-soaked and shaking. The cold had gone past her feet and up through her legs, so that her whole lower body felt like something that belonged to someone else. She looked at what they had made—a rough patch of darker earth against the lighter peat, a scar that would show until the grass grew back, if the grass ever grew back.

Amos stood with one hand on his spade, his chest moving with deep, slow breaths. His face was grey with exhaustion, and there was mud in his hair where he had pushed it back with a soiled hand. He did not look at Nell. He looked at the repair, testing it with his eyes, looking for the flaw that would undo what they had done.

“It’ll hold.”

“For now.” Nell’s voice came out rough, as if the words had to drag themselves through her throat. “Until the next rain.”

“Or the next.”

“Or the next.”

They sat together in the wet field, surrounded by the smell of peat and the sound of the pump still rattling behind them. The morning had grown while they worked, and the light was stronger now, showing the full extent of the damage— the scarred bank, the mud-smeared grass, the water that had crept into the lower rows of celery before they had stopped it. Not much. A few inches of seepage that might drain away if the sun came out. But the sun showed no sign of coming.

Amos moved first, pushing himself upright with visible effort. He offered Nell his hand, and she took it, and he pulled her to her feet. Her knees buckled for a moment, and she had to catch his arm to stay upright. They stood like that for a breath, two people leaning on each other because neither could stand alone.

Then Nell let go and bent to retrieve her spade from where it had fallen. The metal was caked with mud, the handle slick. She wiped it on her skirt without thinking, adding another stain to the many that already marked the fabric.

“The ground’s getting softer.” Amos was looking past her, toward the long field where the drainage board’s stakes still stood, marking the path of the new dyke. “Even the withy bed. The roots aren’t holding like they used to.”

Nell followed his gaze. The stakes were small from here, thin wooden markers that looked almost harmless against the grey sky. But she knew what they meant. A new dyke meant a new depth, a new cut that would change how the water moved through all of this land—her land, the land her husband’s family had drained and her father’s family had defended and she had spent her whole life trying to keep.

“The board says it will lower the water table.”

Amos made a sound that might have been a laugh, if it had had any humor in it. “The board says what it needs to say.”

She thought of the letter in her pocket, the formal language that reduced her life to a parcel on a map. She thought of the gate post they had braced three days ago, still standing but leaning a little more each time she checked it. She thought of Kitty in the kitchen window, watching her mother stand in a wet field at dawn, not knowing what to do but unable to stay inside.

“We’ll manage.” The words came out before she had thought them through, but she meant them. She had to mean them. “We always have.”

Amos looked at her, his face unreadable. He had worked for her family since before her husband died, had stood beside her at the funeral with his hat in his hands, had come back the next day and every day after to dig and cut and pump and repair. He knew what managing cost. He knew what it took.

“Until we can’t.”

It was not cruel, the way he said it. Just honest. Just true.

Nell bent and picked up a piece of the sod they had cut, turning it over in her hands. The roots were pale and thick, tangled together in a desperate grip. She pressed her thumb into the earth and felt it give, soft and wet, no resistance left.

“The field feels like a sponge.”

“Drink enough water, anything will swell.”

She looked at him. “And burst.”

Amos did not answer. He had already said what he meant to say, and he was not a man who wasted words. He simply waited, his hand resting on the spade handle, his eyes on the horizon where the rain was beginning to gather.

Nell turned back toward the farmhouse. Through the kitchen window, she could see Kitty’s face, pale and watchful, a cup of tea held forgotten in her hands. Her daughter had come to the window at some point while they worked, had stood there watching without coming out, knowing that some things could not be helped from the outside.

The pump still rattled behind her, pulling water from the ditches, sending it out to the river. It had been running all morning, and it would run all day, and tomorrow it would run again. The fen did not stop giving water, and they could not stop pumping it away. That was the arrangement they had made with this land, and they would keep making it until the land decided otherwise.

She began walking toward the house, her boots sinking with each step. The ground had no firmness left, and she could feel each footfall pressing down into something that would not press back. The whole field was like that now—wet and soft and slowly giving way, held together by roots and hope and the stubbornness of people who refused to leave.

Behind her, Amos began gathering the tools. She heard him moving, the clink of metal against metal, the sound of his boots in the mud. They would go inside soon, both of them, and sit at her kitchen table with tea that would go cold in their hands, and they would not talk about what the ground had almost taken from them this morning.

But she would remember. She would remember the way the earth had slid away, the sound of the water rushing in, the feeling of her knees sinking into mud that had no bottom. And she would remember what Amos had said, the quiet truth of it, the thing she had known for months but had not let herself speak.

The ground was failing. The water was winning. And the letter in her pocket was only the beginning of what was coming.

She reached the kitchen door and paused with her hand on the latch. Inside, Kitty would be waiting with questions she could not answer. Outside, the field stretched grey and wet to the horizon, the new dyke’s path marked by stakes that looked like crosses in a graveyard.

Nell opened the door and went in.
