Chapter 18

Defeated by Rising Waters

The sucking sound of boots pulling free from deep mud followed Nell across the dark field, each step a negotiation with ground that no longer remembered how to hold firm. Rain drove against her face in sheets, cold and relentless, the kind of rain that had been falling since before she slept and would keep falling long after. The truth she had learned pressed against her chest—not the truth of water and soil, which she had known all her life, but the truth of men and their schemes. The drainage board’s new dyke had nothing to do with drainage. The realization sat in her gut, cold and heavy.

The lower ditch had overflowed in the night. She could hear it before she saw it—the strange, flat sound of water where water should not be, spreading across the barley field in a dark sheet that caught the first grey light of dawn. The pump house stood at the field’s edge, its small window glowing with lantern light. Amos was already there.

Nell’s boots found the plank bridge over the inner drainage cut, the wood slick and treacherous. She crossed with her weight forward, one hand out for balance though there was nothing to grasp. The rain had softened everything. The planks dipped beneath her feet. Below, the cut ran full and fast, brown with peat and rising.

The pump house door stood open. Amos crouched beside the engine, his back to her, his shoulders hunched forward in a way she had not seen before—not weariness, exactly, but something closer to recognition. The lantern threw his shadow against the far wall. Tools lay scattered on the concrete floor: wrenches, a grease gun, rags black with oil. The air inside smelled of machine oil and something burnt.

She stopped in the doorway. Water dripped from her coat onto the threshold.

Amos did not turn. His hands moved over the engine casing, slow and deliberate, touching each component as if taking leave of it. The piston housing had been opened. Inside, the metal gleamed dully in the lantern light, scored with deep scratches.

“How bad?” Nell asked.

Amos’s hands stopped moving. He remained crouched, his face turned toward the machine rather than toward her. When he spoke, his voice was flat, drained of the usual observation.

“Crankshaft’s cracked. Bearing spun. The whole lower assembly.”

Nell stepped inside, out of the rain. The pump house felt small, cramped, the walls pressing inward. She moved to stand beside Amos, looking down at the engine’s exposed guts. The damage was visible even to her untrained eye—metal that should have been smooth was scored and warped, the parts no longer fitting together as they should.

“Can you fix it?”

A long pause. Amos reached out and touched the cracked crankshaft, his fingers tracing the line of the break. Then he stood, slowly, his knees cracking as he rose. He turned to face her. His face was masked with mud and exhaustion, but his eyes were what held her—dark, flat, emptied of the quiet competence that had always been there. He shook his head once.

Nell looked back at the engine. Outside, the rain drummed against the roof, a steady, patient sound. Water was rising in the lower field. Without the pump, there was nothing to stop it.

“What do we have?” she asked.

“Burlap in the barn. Sand by the bridge. The old willow props.”

She nodded. There would be no repairing the pump today, perhaps not for weeks—replacement parts would have to come from the city, ordered through channels that moved slowly and cost money she did not have. The field was what mattered now. The barley could withstand some flooding, but not this, not a sheet of water spreading inch by inch across the low ground.

They worked in silence. The barn stood at the farmyard’s edge, and Nell walked ahead through the rain while Amos followed with a wheelbarrow. The burlap sacks were in the back, behind the seed drill, folded in a stack that smelled of mice and old grain. She pulled them out one by one, her hands working automatically while her mind turned over the problem.

The lower field was the lowest point of her land. The drainage cut that should have carried water away ran backward now, the fen’s water table risen so high that the cut itself overflowed. Without the pump to push water up and out, the field would fill like a bowl. The barley would drown. And if the barley drowned, there would be nothing to sell at harvest, nothing to pay the labor, nothing to hold back the board when they came with their compulsory purchase and their claims of improvement.

She grabbed an armload of sacks and carried them out into the rain. Amos had the wheelbarrow ready. They loaded it together, working without speech, each reaching for the next sack without needing to ask.

The sand pile stood near the bridge where the farm track crossed the inner cut. Nell had ordered it months ago, a precaution against just such a spring. The pile had settled under the winter rains, the outer layer crusted hard. She took the spade from where it leaned against the fence post and drove it into the mass. The sand came away in wet, heavy clumps.

They filled the sacks in the rain. Nell held each one open while Amos shoveled, then tied the neck with twine and stacked it. The work was simple, rhythmic, the kind of work that required no thought. Her hands knew what to do. Her body moved through the motions. But the rain kept falling, and the water kept rising.

The first sandbag went into place at the lowest point of the field’s edge, where the drainage cut had overflowed its bank. Nell knelt in the mud to set it, feeling the cold water seep through her trousers, feeling the weight of the bag settle into the soft earth. Another bag beside it. Another beside that. Amos brought them as fast as she could place them, the wheelbarrow squeaking across the rutted track.

The water was already ankle-deep in the field. It moved slowly, almost gently, spreading across the low ground in a dark sheet. The barley stalks stood above it, their green tips catching the rain, but the water was rising. In an hour, it would be to their stems. In two hours, to their roots.

Nell worked faster. Her hands were numb with cold, her fingers stiff as she tied each sack. The mud sucked at her boots when she moved, each step requiring effort to pull free. Rain ran down her face, dripped from her nose, collected in the hollow of her throat. Her coat had soaked through long ago. She could feel the water against her skin, cold and relentless.

“More by the willow,” Amos said, his voice low.

She looked up. He stood beside the wheelbarrow, his face streaming with rain, his eyes on the far corner of the field where the withy bed marked the boundary. The willows there had been planted generations ago, their roots woven through the soil like fingers holding hands. The ground was firmer there, the water slower to rise.

“Take what we have,” she said. “I’ll bring more from the barn.”

He nodded and began loading sandbags into the wheelbarrow. Nell walked back toward the barn, her boots slipping in the mud. The rain was falling harder now, the drops striking the ground with a sound like thousands of small stones. The sky had lightened to a uniform grey, no sun, no horizon, just the endless fall of water.

The barn was dark after the rain. She stood in the doorway for a moment, letting her eyes adjust. The smell of hay and old grain surrounded her, familiar and stable. Her spade leaned against the wall where she had left it. She picked it up and turned to go back out.

The letter from the board was still in her pocket. She could feel it there, a folded weight against her hip, the paper softened now from days of carrying. The compulsory purchase notice. The claim that her land was needed for drainage improvement. She had believed it, or had tried to believe it, had tried to work within the bounds they had set. But now she knew. The dyke was not about drainage. The dyke was about consolidation, about moving boundaries, about someone somewhere who wanted her land for reasons that had nothing to do with water.

She stepped back into the rain. The truth had not freed her. It had only made the fight clearer—and the fight was losing.

Amos had placed the sandbags in a line along the willow boundary. The water there was shallower, held back by the woven roots beneath the surface. Nell joined him, taking each bag from the wheelbarrow and setting it in place. They worked without speaking, the rhythm of their movements synchronized by years of labor together.

But the water kept rising. She could see it creeping up the sandbags, darkening the burlap, finding gaps where the bags did not quite meet. Each time she placed a new bag, the water had moved a little higher. Each time she looked up, the flooded area had spread a little further.

The rain showed no sign of stopping.

They had been working for two hours when Amos stopped. Nell felt the absence of his movement—the wheelbarrow no longer squeaking, the sandbags no longer arriving at her side. She straightened and turned.

He stood at the field’s edge, looking out across the water. His spade hung from his hand, the blade trailing a thin line of mud. His shoulders were slumped, his head bowed forward. Rain streamed down his face and dripped from his jaw.

Nell walked over to stand beside him. Together they looked out at the lower field.

The water had spread across half the barley now, a dark sheet that reflected the grey sky. The stalks that remained above the surface bent under the rain, their tips weighted with water. In another hour, perhaps two, the entire field would be submerged. The barley would drown. The work they had done—the sandbags, the willow props, the hours of labor in the cold and the wet—had held back nothing.

“The pump,” Amos said, his voice barely audible above the rain. “If it were working—“

“It’s not.”

He did not argue. He stood beside her, his breathing heavy, his chest heaving with the effort of the morning. His hands were black with oil from the engine, the grease streaked and smeared by rain and mud.

Nell looked down at her own hands. They were numb, the fingers white and wrinkled from the water. Mud had packed under her nails. Her coat hung heavy and sodden from her shoulders. She could feel the cold in her bones now, a deep ache that no movement could warm.

They could keep working. They could fill more sandbags, place them along the edges, try to hold back what could not be held. The sand pile was not yet exhausted. The burlap sacks were not yet gone. There was more they could do.

But Nell knew, with a certainty that settled into her like the cold, that it would make no difference. The water was rising. The pump was broken. The field was lost. And somewhere, in a room she had never seen, men were signing papers that would take this land and call it improvement.

“Amos.”

He turned his head toward her. His eyes were flat, empty of the stubborn hope that had always characterized him. He looked, she thought, like a man who had finally seen the shape of the thing that would defeat him.

“We’ll come back,” she said. “When the water recedes.”

He did not answer. He looked back out at the field, at the dark water spreading across the barley, at the rain falling on a landscape that no longer belonged to anyone but the fen itself. After a long moment, he nodded.

They stood together in the rain, two figures at the edge of a drowning field. The water rose around their boots, seeping up through the mud, filling the prints their feet had left. The sound of it was everywhere—the rush of the overflow, the drum of rain on water, the slow, patient gurgle of drainage cuts running backward. The fen was taking back what had been taken from it.

Nell thought of the letter in her pocket. She thought of the notice that had been nailed to her gate, the stakes that had been driven into her fields, the surveyor who had walked her land with his instruments and his maps. She thought of Kitty in the kitchen, waiting for her mother to return, waiting for the world to make sense.

The world would not make sense. The world was water and mud and the slow, patient pressure of a land that had never wanted to be drained. The men who had sent the letter, who had planned the dyke, who had decided that her farm would serve their purposes—they were somewhere else, dry and comfortable, signing papers that described this field as a necessary sacrifice for progress. They would not see the water rising. They would not smell the peat and the rot. They would not feel the cold seeping into their bones.

But Nell felt it. She felt it in her hands, in her feet, in the ache that had settled into her spine. She felt the weight of the work she had done and the work she had failed to do. She felt the truth she now carried—the truth that this fight was not about drainage, not about improvement, not about anything she could see or touch or stand against.

The rain kept falling. The water kept rising. And Nell stood at the edge of her field, defeated by the thing she had fought every day of her life, knowing that the real battle had not yet begun.

Amos shifted beside her. He lifted his spade and turned toward the farm track, his movements slow, heavy with the weight of the morning. He did not look back at the water. He did not say anything. He simply began to walk, his boots sucking free of the mud with each step.

Nell watched him go. Then she turned and looked out one last time at the flooded field, at the barley drowning in dark water, at the rain falling on a landscape that had been hers and was slipping away. She reached into her pocket and touched the folded letter, the paper soft and damp against her fingers.

The fen waited. The water rose. And somewhere beyond the rain, the real enemy was counting on her defeat.
