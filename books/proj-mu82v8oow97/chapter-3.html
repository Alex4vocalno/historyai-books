Chapter 3

The Gate and the Letter

The stakes stood in the long field like a mouth full of broken teeth, and Nell Askew had been staring at them since before the mist began to lift. Her breath clouded before her face. The wooden markers the surveyor had driven into her soil—her soil—caught the first grey light, and each one seemed to hum with a frequency only she could hear. She had slept little. The weight of yesterday’s discovery had settled behind her eyes, a dull pressure that no amount of rubbing could ease. The field was no longer simply a field. It was a grid of potential destruction, a map of what would be taken.

She turned away at last, her boots sucking free of the wet ground with a sound like a wound opening. The pump needed seeing to before dusk. The thought had been with Nell since first light, a second pulse beneath the day’s demands, and now she carried it along the ditch path with her tools slung over one shoulder. The morning mist lay thick across the Fen, softening the straight lines of the drainage cuts, turning the world into a grey expanse where sky and earth had lost their boundary.

Her boots found the familiar rut between the withy beds, the ground giving slightly under each step, that characteristic give of peat that had drunk the winter rains and would not release them until the sun grew strong. The handle of the hammer bumped against her hip with each stride. A cold rivulet of air slid down her collar, and the smell of the ditches rose to meet her—wet earth, decaying reed, something faintly metallic where the iron of the pump pipes bled into the water.

The gate to the long field hung crooked on its hinges, the bottom corner dragging a furrow through the grass. She had been meaning to mend it for a week, and now the neglect seemed to reproach her, another small failure in a season of small failures. She set down her tools and crouched to examine the hinge. The iron had rusted where the pin met the strap, the metal swollen with corrosion, the wood beneath it soft and splintered from years of rain.

The morning pressed against her from all sides. Mist curled through the withies, and somewhere beyond the field a lapwing called, its thin cry folding back on itself. The air was thick with moisture, each breath coating her throat. She could taste the peat on her tongue, that distinctive richness that meant the land was alive, drinking, breathing. Water collected on the gate’s weathered planks, darkening the grain, and when she ran her thumb across the surface the wood felt slick and cold. The rust on the hinge flaked under her touch, staining her skin the color of dried blood. She pulled the hammer from her belt, tested its weight in her palm, and knelt in the wet grass to drive the pin free.

The metal screamed against metal. The sound travelled across the empty field, sharp and startling, and she paused with the hammer raised mid-swing, listening to the echo die against the distant treeline. The pin loosened. She worked it back and forth until it slid free, warm from friction despite the cold air, and she set it aside on a flat stone where she would not lose it in the grass. The hinge itself was sound, the iron thick enough to hold for another season if she could find a way to secure it to wood that was not already rotting from within.

That was when she saw it.

A square of paper, nailed to the gatepost above the hinge, its edges curling where the damp had already begun to soak through. Not a leaflet, not some advertisement for seed or machinery. The seal at the top was official, pressed into the paper with enough force to leave an impression she could see even at a distance.

The hammer lowered to her side. Her hand came up, almost of its own accord, fingers reaching toward the paper as though it might burn her. The nail that held it was new, its head bright and unweathered, a wound in the old wood that had not been there yesterday.

She pulled it free with a single sharp motion.

The paper crackled between her fingers, stiff with the weight of officialdom. She unfolded it, and the damp air immediately began to soften the creases, the ink bleeding slightly where a drop of mist landed near the margin. The words were printed in a careful hand, the letters small and precise, each sentence followed by a clause that led inevitably to the next, building a structure that admitted no argument.

NOTICE OF INTENT TO CONSTRUCT DRAINAGE WORKS

The Isle of Ely Drainage Board hereby gives notice that, pursuant to the powers conferred upon it by the Land Drainage Act of 1861 and subsequent amendments thereto, it intends to excavate a new dyke through the lands designated as Plot 47 and Plot 48 on the parish map of Littleport, being part of the holding known as Askew Farm.

The dyke will follow a course from the northern boundary of Plot 47 in a south-south-easterly direction for a distance of approximately four hundred and twenty yards, thence turning east-north-east for a further one hundred and fifty yards to join the existing drainage channel known as Silt Dyke.

The works will commence not earlier than sixty days from the date of this notice.

She read the words twice. Three times. The measurements meant nothing to her in that moment, the distances mere numbers on a page, but the direction—the direction she could picture without effort. South-south-easterly from the northern boundary. Through the long field. Her best field. The field that had grown the finest celery in the county two years running, the field where the soil was deep and black and rich, where the peat held the moisture through even the driest summer.

She looked up. The mist was beginning to thin, burning off under the weak sun, and the stakes the surveyor had driven into the ground emerged from the grey like markers on a grave. She had not understood their purpose when she first saw them. Now she understood. Each stake marked the path of the dyke. Each stake marked the place where her field would be cut in two.

The paper trembled in her hand. She folded it, pressed it flat against her thigh, folded it again. The nail lay in the grass at her feet, its bright head catching the light. She picked it up, weighed it in her palm, and slipped it into her pocket alongside the paper. Evidence. Proof that something had been done to her land without her knowledge, without her consent.

The hinge repair would have to wait. She gathered her tools and walked back along the ditch path, but her feet carried her past the turning that led to the pump, past the gate that opened onto the farmyard, past the house where Kitty would still be sleeping. She walked instead along the boundary of the long field, tracing the line of the stakes with her eyes.

The peat squelched beneath her boots. Water pooled in her footprints, dark and glossy, reflecting the pale sky. The smell of the earth rose up around her, that deep organic richness that had drawn her husband’s father to this land, that had kept the family here through three generations. The peat was alive. It held the history of every crop, every harvest, every season of rain and drought. It held the memory of her husband’s hands working the soil, of his voice calling across the field at dusk, of the weight of him in the bed beside her through the cold winters.

She stopped at the approximate center of the field, where the stakes formed a line that cut directly through the heart of the planting. The celery would go in next month. She had already prepared the seedbeds, had already ordered the plants from the nursery at Wisbech. But if the dyke came through, half of those seedbeds would be destroyed. The spoil heap alone—the earth excavated from the channel—would spread across yards of good soil on either side, burying the peat under a ridge of sterile clay dug from the subsoil beneath.

A bird rose from the withy beds at the field’s edge, its wings beating the air with a sound like cloth tearing. The lapwing circled once, its cry thin and mournful, before disappearing over the treeline toward the river. Nell watched it go. The silence that followed seemed to press against her ears, thick and expectant.

She crouched in the grass and pressed her palm flat against the earth. The soil was cold against her skin, dense with moisture, yielding slightly under the pressure of her hand. A worm twisted through the dark crumbs near her thumb, blind and indifferent to the notice in her pocket, to the stakes that marked its home for destruction. This was what the board saw when they looked at her land. Not the years of work. Not the memory of her husband. Not the future she had planned for Kitty, a future built on the solid ground of a working farm. They saw only a line on a map, a problem to be solved, a path of least resistance for water that needed somewhere to go.

The water. That was what it always came back to. The Fens were made of water, held back by ditches and dykes and pumps that never slept. The land itself was a kind of illusion, a trick of engineering that let people believe they could live below sea level, that they could farm what had once been marsh and would be marsh again if the pumps ever stopped. She knew this. Had always known it. But knowing it in the abstract was different from standing in her field with a notice in her pocket that said her land was needed for the greater good.

She stayed crouched in the grass for a long time. The sun climbed higher, burning away the last of the mist, and the field emerged in full detail around her. The dark soil, rich and even. The straight lines of the seedbeds, waiting for the celery plants. The withy beds at the boundary, their pale stems catching the light. The stakes that marked the path of the dyke, bright and new against the ancient peat.

When she rose at last, her knees ached from the cold and the damp had soaked through the fabric of her trousers. She brushed the soil from her palms, feeling the grit work its way under her nails, the earth marking her as it always did. She was of this land. It had claimed her long before the drainage board had thought to claim it.

The walk back to the house seemed longer than usual. Each step required effort, her boots dragging through the wet grass, the tools heavy on her shoulder. The pump would have to wait. Everything would have to wait. She needed to think, to plan, to find some way to fight this. But first she needed to be alone with what she had learned, to let the reality of it settle into her bones.

When Nell entered the kitchen, Kitty sat at the table with a cup of tea cradled between her hands. The girl’s hair was still plaited from sleep, loose strands framing her face, and she looked up as the door closed with an expression that mixed sleepiness with concern.

“You’re late. The pump—“

“I know.” Nell set her tools by the door and crossed to the range, filling the kettle from the water bucket without turning around. “The pump needs seeing to. I’ll go after breakfast.”

“Is something wrong?”

Nell’s hand paused on the kettle. She could feel the weight of the letter in her pocket, pressing against her thigh like a stone. She thought of the stakes in the field, of the bright new nail she had pulled from the gatepost, of the words that had already begun to blur in her memory, their precise meanings slipping away even as she tried to hold them.

“The gate hinge needs replacing,” she said at last. “The wood’s rotten through. I’ll need to cut a new post.”

It was not a lie. Not exactly. The gate did need mending. But the words felt strange in her mouth, a shape that did not match the weight of what she carried. She turned from the range and looked at her daughter, at the girl’s sleep-soft face and questioning eyes, and felt the letter like a secret that had already begun to distance her from the life she had built.

“Kitty.” She pulled out a chair and sat across from her daughter, the table between them scarred with years of use. “The drainage board—they’ve sent a notice.”

“What kind of notice?”

“They want to cut a new dyke. Through the long field.”

Kitty’s hands tightened around her cup. The girl said nothing, but her face changed, the sleepiness falling away to reveal something sharper beneath. Fear, perhaps. Or something harder than fear.

“Can they do that?”

Nell reached across the table and covered her daughter’s hand with her own. The girl’s fingers were cold, the skin thin and pale against the worn wood of the table.

“I don’t know,” she said. “But I mean to find out.”

The morning stretched ahead of them, full of work that needed doing, of ditches that needed checking and a pump that would not run itself. But for a moment they sat together in the quiet kitchen, the letter folded in Nell’s pocket, the stakes standing in the field like sentinels, and the weight of what was coming pressing against them from all sides.

Nell rose at last. She folded the letter more tightly into her pocket, the paper stiff against her leg, and reached for her tools.

“The pump needs seeing to before dusk,” she said, and the words felt like a promise, a thread of continuity in a world that was threatening to unravel. “We’ll manage.”

She walked out into the morning, the sun warm on her back, the water gleaming in the ditches, and the long field waiting for whatever would come next.
