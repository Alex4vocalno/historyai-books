Chapter 13

The Lamb and the Ditch

The ewe stood alone in the corner of the long field, head low, breath smoking in the grey air. Nell saw her from the ditch path and changed direction. Her boots sucked against the wet peat. The spade dragged at her arm, heavy as a second limb.

Yesterday’s work had held. The drain still ran clear. The water level sat two inches lower than the day before. But the ground under her feet told a different story. Soft. Spongy. The whole field sinking back into the fen from which it had been wrested. The white stakes the surveyor had placed still marked the path of the coming dyke, their neat, official precision a row of teeth in the mist.

She had meant to check the lower bank where Amos had woven the willow supports. Instead she walked toward the ewe, whose flanks trembled in a way that had nothing to do with cold.

A lamb lay on the ground. Still wet, unmoving, its legs splayed at angles that looked wrong. The ewe turned her head to watch Nell approach but did not move away. Too exhausted to flee or fight. Nell knelt in the mud, setting the spade aside, and reached for the lamb.

Cold. The wool was cold and slick with birth fluid, the small body limp in her hands. She pressed her fingers to the chest. A heartbeat—faint, rapid, irregular—but there. Still there.

“Come on,” she said. Her voice came out rough, scraped from somewhere low. She began to rub the lamb’s chest with the heel of her palm, working the fluid from its lungs, stimulating the blood to move. “Come on now.”

The ewe watched with dark, patient eyes. She had done this before. Nell knew that. The ewe knew what it meant when a lamb did not rise. But Nell kept working, her hands moving in steady rhythm, her knees soaking in the cold mud. The mist thickened around them, the sun a pale smear behind the clouds. Somewhere behind her, the ditch water ran, a low, constant whisper against the banks.

The lamb coughed. A thin, wet sound. Then again. Its head lifted an inch from the ground, and Nell felt the small body shudder with the effort of drawing breath.

“That’s it.” She kept rubbing, feeling the warmth slowly return to the limbs. “That’s it, little one.”

She was so focused on the lamb that she did not hear Amos approach. She only became aware of him when his shadow fell across the lamb, blocking what little light there was. She looked up. He stood at the edge of the field, his face grey with exhaustion, his spade in hand. Mud masked his jaw. His sleeves streamed water. He did not speak. He only turned his head toward the ditch and then back to her, his eyes meeting hers.

The lamb shivered in her hands. Behind Amos, she could see the ditch bank where he had been working. A section of it had collapsed inward, a raw wound in the earth, water seeping through the gap. Not rushing. Not yet. But seeping. Finding the weakness. Testing it.

“How long?” she asked.

“Half hour.” His voice was flat. “Maybe less. The willow’s not holding.”

Nell looked down at the lamb. It was breathing now, but shallowly, its eyes half-open, unfocused. It needed warmth. It needed to be dried and brought to the barn, kept by the stove until it could stand. The ewe would follow, or she would not—there was no predicting the instincts of a mother in distress.

The letter pressed against her thigh through the fabric of her pocket. The notice she had pulled from the gatepost, folded and refolded until the creases were soft as cloth. The Board’s words, precise and legal, informing her of their intent to cut the dyke through her land. She had carried it for days now, a weight she could not set down, a reminder that this fight was not only against the water.

“Can you hold it?” she asked Amos.

He looked at the lamb, then at her. He did not ask why. He did not point out that he had been fighting the ditch all morning, that his arms were shaking with exhaustion, that his boots were full of water. He simply set down his spade and knelt in the mud beside her, reaching out to take the lamb from her hands.

“Go,” he said. “I’ll bring it.”

She hesitated. The lamb’s thin heartbeat still echoed in her palms. But Amos’s face was steady, his hands gentle as he cradled the small body against his chest.

“The barn,” she said. “Get it warm. Kitty will help.”

He nodded once, already rising, already turning toward the farmhouse. Nell watched him go—the lamb a pale bundle in his arms, the ewe following at his heels—then turned back to the ditch.

The water was higher than it had been yesterday. She could see that now, standing at the edge of the bank. The collapse Amos had pointed out was small, perhaps two feet across, but the water had found it. A steady trickle ran through the gap, dark with peat, carrying pieces of the bank with it. If she left it, the trickle would become a stream. The stream would become a flood. And the flood would take the lower field, the celery beds, the work of years.

She picked up her spade and stepped down into the water.

The cold shocked her, even through her boots. The ditch was only waist-deep, but the mud at the bottom was soft and greedy, pulling at her legs as she waded toward the collapsed bank. Water seeped into her clothes, her skin, her bones. Yesterday’s exhaustion rose up to meet her, and she pushed it down.

The clay was heavy. She dug her spade into the bank above the collapse, cutting squares of sod and clay, lifting them one by one to pack against the gap. Each shovelful weighed more than it should, the waterlogged earth clinging to the blade. Her shoulders burned. Her lower back ached from the angle. But she kept working, methodical, steady, the way she had done this work for thirty years.

From the barn, she could hear Kitty’s voice. Not words, exactly—just the high, thin sound of her daughter speaking to the lamb, the soft murmur of reassurance that Kitty used with animals. A sound Nell knew well. Kitty had always been that way, even as a child, talking to the chickens, the cats, the stray dogs that wandered onto the farm. It was one of the reasons Nell had never worried about leaving her alone with the animals. Kitty understood them in a way that required no words.

But the lamb was weak. Nell could hear that in the silence between Kitty’s murmurs—the pause, the held breath, the waiting for a sound that might not come.

She drove the spade into the clay again. And again. The gap was closing, the water slowing to a seep, then a drip. She packed the sod tight against the edges, pressing it into place with her hands, feeling the cold mud squelch between her fingers. The willow withies Amos had woven were still there, but they had pulled loose from the bank, their roots dangling in the water. She would need to re-tie them, re-anchor them, but that was work for another day. For now, she just needed the wall to hold.

The water level in the ditch had dropped slightly, the flow redirected. She had bought time. Hours, perhaps. A day, if she was lucky. But the weakness was still there, hidden beneath the surface, waiting for the next rain, the next tide, the next moment of inattention.

She climbed out of the ditch, her boots heavy with water, her clothes clinging to her skin. The mist was thick now, obscuring the farmhouse, the barn, the white stakes that marked the coming dyke. She could barely see her own hands in front of her face.

In the silence, she heard Kitty call out, her voice thin and frightened.

“Mother!”

Nell started forward, her feet slipping on the wet ground. The barn loomed through the mist, the door hanging open. She moved toward it as fast as she could, which was not fast enough, her legs heavy, her breath coming in gasps.

Kitty stood in the doorway, the lamb in her arms. Her face was pale, her eyes wide, but her voice was steady when she spoke.

“It’s not breathing right.”

Nell reached her and knelt, taking the lamb from Kitty’s arms. The small body was warmer now, dried with straw, but its breath came in gasps, its chest heaving with effort. She pressed her ear to the lamb’s ribs and heard the heart—still there, still fighting, but slower than before.

“What did you do?” she asked.

“I wrapped it. I kept it by the stove. I—“ Kitty’s voice cracked, and she stopped, swallowing hard. “I didn’t know what else to do.”

Nell looked up at her daughter. Kitty’s hands were shaking. Her cardigan was buttoned to the throat, her hair plaited precisely in the middle. She looked as she always looked—neat, controlled, contained—but there was something else in her face now. Something fierce. Something that had not been there before.

“You did right,” Nell said.

Kitty’s eyes filled with tears, but she did not cry. She only nodded, once, and knelt beside Nell, her hand resting on the lamb’s flank.

They stayed like that for a long time, the two of them, the lamb between them. Nell could feel the small heart beating against her palm, weaker than before, but still present. Still fighting. Outside, the mist pressed against the walls of the barn, and the ditch water whispered against the banks, and the white stakes stood in their neat, official row, waiting for the surveyor to return.

The lamb shivered in Kitty’s arms, and Nell rose to check the bank once more.

The walk back to the ditch was shorter than she expected. The mist had a way of compressing distance, of making the familiar strange. She could hear the water before she could see it—the low, constant whisper that had been the background to her life for as long as she could remember. The sound of the fen, slowly, patiently reclaiming what had been taken from it.

Amos was there before her. He had returned to the ditch while she was with the lamb, and he was standing waist-deep in the water, his back to her, his shoulders moving in the steady rhythm of digging. He had not gone to rest. He had not gone to eat. He had simply come back to the work, as if it were the only thing that mattered.

She climbed down into the ditch beside him. He did not look up, but he shifted to make room for her, and they worked side by side in silence, their spades striking the clay in a rhythm that needed no words. The gap was smaller now, the water slower. They packed the sod tight against the edges, pressing it into place with their hands.

When the wall held, when the water had slowed to a trickle, Amos finally stopped. He leaned on his spade, his breathing heavy, and looked at her.

“It’ll need stone,” he said. “The willow won’t hold another tide.”

“I know.”

“Where will you get stone?”

She thought of the letter in her pocket. The notice. The Board’s precise, legal words. They had offered compensation, but the amount would not cover the stone she needed, the labor, the years of work that would be lost. They had offered to buy her out, but the land was not theirs to buy. It was hers. It had been her mother’s, and her grandmother’s, and her grandmother’s mother’s before that.

“I’ll find it,” she said.

Amos looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and climbed out of the ditch. He offered her his hand, and she took it, pulling herself up onto the bank. They stood together in the mist, two figures in a landscape that was slowly disappearing, the water whispering at their feet.

“The lamb?” he asked.

“Alive. For now.”

He nodded again and turned toward his cottage. His shoulders were slumped with exhaustion, his steps heavy. But he had come back to the ditch when he could have rested. He had worked beside her without being asked. She had known Amos for twenty years, and he had always been a man of few words, but today she saw something new in him. A quiet partnership. A shared recognition that they were fighting something larger than either of them could name.

She watched him go, then turned back toward the barn. The mist was thinning now, the sun beginning to break through. She could see the farmhouse in the distance, the windows glowing with the light of the stove. She could see Kitty standing in the doorway, the lamb still in her arms, watching her mother walk across the field.

Nell thought of the letter in her pocket. The notice. The Board’s words, precise and legal, informing her of their intent. She had carried it for days, a weight she could not set down. But standing there, looking at her daughter, looking at the land that was still hers, she felt something shift inside her.

She reached into her pocket and pulled out the letter. The paper was soft now, worn thin from being handled. The words were still there, still precise, still legal. But they were only words. They were not the land. They were not the water. They were not the lamb’s thin heartbeat or Kitty’s fierce, steady hands.

She folded the letter again, carefully, precisely, and put it back in her pocket. Then she walked toward the barn, toward her daughter, toward whatever came next.

The ditch held for now. The lamb shivered in Kitty’s arms. And the fen waited, patient and eternal, for the next tide to come.
