Chapter 20

Memory of a Distant War

────────────────────────────────────────

The silence woke her before the light did. Nell lay still in the dark, listening for the low, rhythmic clatter of the pump house, that mechanical heartbeat that marked the hours of a fenland night. Nothing. Just the drip of rain from the eaves and the slow creak of timber settling into damp earth.

She was pulling on her boots before her mind had fully formed the thought. The lower field. The celery rows barely holding in soil that had been too wet for planting, now drowning under water that had nowhere to go.

The yard was slick with last night’s downpour, puddles reflecting the first grey wash of dawn. She could smell it—that particular fen smell of peat turned sour under standing water, of rot beginning where it shouldn’t. The pump house door hung open, and Amos was already there, a lantern held low, its yellow light catching the black water that lapped against the engine’s foundation.

“Mag.” He didn’t look up. His voice was flat, the word a statement rather than a question.

She came to stand beside him, and the sight struck her like a blow to the chest. The lower ditch had overflowed sometime in the small hours, and now a dark sheet of water covered the field’s lowest third, swallowing the first two rows of celery entirely. The engine sat silent, its flywheel still, the whole machine looking somehow smaller in the dim light, as if it had shrunk overnight into something fragile and inadequate.

“How long?” she asked.

“Hour. Maybe two.” He set the lantern on a shelf and began rolling up his sleeves, revealing forearms already marked with old grease stains. “Could hear it from the cottage when it stopped.”

She should have heard it too. She should have been awake, should have been watching. But exhaustion had pulled her under, and the rain had drummed so steady against the tiles that she’d slept through the failure of the one thing keeping her land alive.

Amos waded into the water without another word, the black mud pulling at his boots with each step. The pump’s housing rose waist-high from the flood, iron dark with age and oil. He set his hand on the flywheel and gave it an experimental turn. It moved an inch, resisted, stopped.

“Seized?”

“Something inside.” He was already reaching for the toolbox on the shelf, his movements economical, practiced. “We’ll need to open the valve housing. You’ll hold the lamp.”

She took the lantern and stepped into the water beside him. The cold bit through her trousers instantly, and the mud sucked at her feet, soft and bottomless. This was the fen’s secret—the ground that looked solid was always ready to open and swallow you if you stood still too long. She planted her feet wide and raised the lantern.

He worked in silence, his large hands surprisingly delicate as they unbolted the housing. Grease blackened his fingers, and water dripped from his elbows into the flood, each drop making a small, concentric circle that vanished into the dark. The lantern threw shadows across his face, hollowing his cheeks, catching in the pale stubble along his jaw.

“Pass me the smaller wrench.”

She handed it to him, and their fingers touched briefly—his cold and slick, hers warm from holding the lamp. He didn’t acknowledge the contact, just took the tool and turned back to the engine.

“The water came up fast,” she said.

“It does, when there’s nothing pushing it down.”

She watched him work. The bolt was stubborn, seated in a housing that hadn’t been opened in years, and he had to lean his weight into the wrench. A muscle jumped in his forearm. The bolt turned with a groan of metal on metal.

“Cracked,” he said, pulling the housing free and holding it up to the light.

She leaned closer. A thin, dark line ran through the brass valve seat, barely visible, but unmistakable. Steam or pressure, maybe just age—the kind of failure that came without warning and stopped everything.

“Can you braze it?”

“Not here. Not with the water this high.” He turned the piece in his hands, examining it from every angle. “We need a replacement, or we need to get creative.”

“Creative how?”

He didn’t answer immediately. Instead, he set the cracked housing on the shelf and reached for a length of leather packing, the kind used to seal joints. His hands moved with the confidence of a man who had done this before—not this exact repair, but something like it. Something that required improvisation when the proper tools weren’t available.

“I’ve seen engines worse than this,” he said, his voice low, almost to himself. “Outside Ladysmith. We kept them running on wire and prayer.”

The name landed between them like a stone dropped into still water. Ladysmith. She knew that name, knew it from the newspaper headlines years ago, from the maps her husband had studied, from the letters that came back to some families and not to others. A siege. A war on the other side of the world.

She looked at him, but he didn’t look back. His attention was fixed on the engine, on the leather packing he was winding around a thread of brass. His jaw was set, and she could see the muscle working beneath the skin—the effort of pushing the memory back down, of returning to the present task.

“I didn’t know you were there,” she said.

“There’s a lot I haven’t mentioned.”

He said it without bitterness, without invitation to ask more. Just a fact, offered up and then withdrawn, like a hand extended and then pulled back. But something had shifted. She had worked beside this man for two years, had trusted him with her land and her machinery, and she had never known that he had been part of that distant, brutal war.

He fitted the packing into place, his fingers working by feel now, the lantern light barely reaching the interior of the valve housing. Water lapped against their legs. Somewhere in the dark, a lapwing called—a thin, sharp cry that carried across the fen.

“Will it hold?”

“Long enough.” He turned to face her, and for a moment, in the lantern’s unsteady light, she saw him clearly—not just the hired man who appeared at dawn and left at dusk, but someone who had seen things he couldn’t unsee. His eyes were pale blue, watchful, and there was a weight in them that had nothing to do with the broken pump. “Long enough to get us through today.”

She nodded, accepting the limitation. That was the fen’s teaching: you didn’t fix things forever. You fixed them for now, and then you fixed them again tomorrow, and the day after that, until either you or the land gave out.

He began to reassemble the housing, each piece fitting back into place with a satisfying click. She held the lamp steady, and they worked in the rhythm they had established over seasons of shared labor—him reaching for tools without asking, her handing them over before he could ask. The water rose and fell against their legs with each movement, small waves breaking against the pump’s foundation.

“Turn the flywheel,” he said, and she set down the lantern and gripped the iron wheel. It was cold and slick with spray, and she had to plant her feet in the mud to get purchase. She pulled, felt the resistance, pulled again.

The engine coughed.

“Again.”

She pulled, and this time the engine caught—a shudder running through the iron, then a steady rhythm as the pistons began their work. The sound was louder than she remembered, or maybe she had just never listened so closely before. It was the sound of survival, of water being pulled from the land and pushed toward the river, of another day earned.

Amos wiped his hands on his trousers, leaving dark streaks on the fabric. His face was unreadable in the lantern’s glow, but she could see the exhaustion in the set of his shoulders, in the way he stood with his weight shifted to one side, favoring the leg that always bothered him in damp weather.

“The lower field’s gone,” he said. “Whatever was planted there won’t come back.”

“I know.”

“Upper field might hold, if we can keep this running.”

“I know that too.”

He looked at her then, and something passed between them—not sympathy, exactly, but recognition. They were both standing in water that shouldn’t be there, working on a machine that shouldn’t have broken, fighting a battle that they both knew might be unwinnable. The drainage board wanted her land. The fen wanted to take it back to water. And here they were, two people with cracked valves and leather packing, holding the line for one more day.

“The water’s rising everywhere,” he said, and his voice was different now—not flat, but heavy, as if each word carried more weight than it should. “Not just here. The whole fen’s sinking. The peat’s collapsing in on itself where they’ve drained it too deep. I’ve seen it before, in other places.”

“Where?”

“Different country. Different water.” He shook his head, a small, sharp movement. “Doesn’t matter. What matters is this pump stays running, or we lose more than a few rows of celery.”

She understood what he wasn’t saying. The drainage board’s new dyke—the one they wanted to cut through her long field—would change everything. The water would move differently, would pool in new places, would find new ways to undermine what had been solid ground. And the men who wanted to build it, who claimed it was for improvement, for efficiency—they didn’t have to live with the consequences. They didn’t have to stand in the dark at dawn, ankle-deep in water that should have been earth, listening to an engine that might fail at any moment.

“You should have told me,” she said. “About Ladysmith.”

“What would it have changed?”

She considered the question. He was right, in a way. Knowing wouldn’t have made him any better at his work, wouldn’t have made the engine run more smoothly or the water recede any faster. But it would have changed something between them—a wall she hadn’t known was there, now revealed by the cracks running through it.

“It would have told me you understand about holding on,” she said.

He didn’t respond. He was looking past her, toward the grey light seeping through the pump house’s open door, toward the water that stretched across the lower field in a dark, silent sheet. The engine throbbed behind them, a heartbeat in the darkness.

“I’ll check the upper drains,” he said finally. “Make sure the water’s moving where it should.”

“I’ll stay with the pump.”

He nodded, collected his spade from where it leaned against the wall, and walked out into the dawn. She watched him go, his silhouette dark against the lightening sky, his steps slow and deliberate in the flooded field.

The lantern had burned low, and she blew it out, setting it back on the shelf. In the grey light, the pump house looked smaller than ever, a fragile wooden structure clinging to a foundation that was slowly sinking into the fen. The engine’s rhythm seemed louder now, or maybe she was just listening harder, hearing every hesitation, every small vibration that might signal the next failure.

She thought about what he had said—outside Ladysmith—and tried to imagine him there, younger, in a different kind of mud. She had seen photographs in the papers: men in khaki, thin and hollow-eyed, standing in lines that stretched toward a horizon of veldt and sky. She couldn’t picture Amos among them. He belonged here, in the fen, with his spade and his silence and his hands that knew how to fix what was broken.

But then, she supposed, he had belonged there too. That was the thing about men like him—they went where they were sent, did what was asked, and then came home and tried to forget. Some things couldn’t be forgotten, though. They came out in small ways—in the way he checked the gates at night, in the way he never spoke of what he’d seen, in the way he could look at a cracked valve housing and remember another engine, another failure, another life.

The water outside was beginning to recede, pulled away by the pump’s steady work. She could see the tops of the drowned celery rows now, pale and wilted, but still anchored in the soil. Lost, but not entirely gone.

She reached into her pocket and felt the folded letter there—the one from the drainage board, with its formal language and its polite threats. She had carried it for weeks now, a weight against her hip, a reminder of what was coming. Beside it was the notice she had torn from the gatepost, its edges soft from being handled too often. Two pieces of paper, and between them, everything she had.

The engine coughed, and her heart stuttered, but it caught again, settling back into its rhythm. She laid her hand on the housing, feeling the vibration travel up through her arm, into her chest. It was a heartbeat, this machine. Her land’s heartbeat. And as long as it kept running, she could keep fighting.

Outside, she heard the spade striking earth, a steady, rhythmic sound that carried across the water. He was digging the upper drains, clearing the channels that would carry the flood away from what remained of her crop. Working in silence, as he always did. But now, when she thought of him, she would think of him differently—not just as a hired man, but as someone who had stood outside a besieged city on the other side of the world and kept engines running when everything else was falling apart.

The light was strengthening. Dawn gave way to morning, and the fen emerged from darkness in shades of grey and brown and green. The water still covered the lower field, but it was shallower now, retreating toward the ditches. The engine drummed on. And Nell stood in the pump house doorway, watching the land she loved emerge, inch by inch, from the flood.

She thought about what Amos had said. The whole fen’s sinking. The peat collapsing where they’d drained it too deep. She had known this, in her bones, had felt it every time she walked the fields and felt the ground shift beneath her feet. But hearing it from him—from someone who had seen land fail before—made it real in a new way.

This was what she was fighting. Not just the drainage board, not just the compulsory purchase notice, but the fen itself, turning against the people who had tried to tame it. And maybe she couldn’t win. Maybe the water would take her land in the end, one way or another. But she wasn’t going to let it go without a fight.

She pulled the letter from her pocket and looked at it in the growing light. The drainage board’s seal was crisp at the bottom, official and final. She had read it so many times that she knew the words by heart—the dates, the requirements, the polite but unmistakable threat of legal action if she did not comply.

But there was something else in her pocket now. Something new. A name from a distant war, spoken in the half-dark by a man who never spoke of himself. Ladysmith. It hung in the air, changing everything and nothing.

She folded the letter again and put it back. The engine throbbed. The water receded. And somewhere out in the fen, a lapwing called, its thin cry carrying across the land that was hers, for now, for today, for as long as she could hold it.
