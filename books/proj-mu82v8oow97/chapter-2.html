Chapter 2

Surveying the Flooded Field

────────────────────────────────────────

A stranger with a tripod stood in her long field, and Nell Askew watched from the kitchen window as he unfolded his instruments with the careful deliberation of a man unaccustomed to being observed. The morning mist had not yet burned away. His figure appeared ghostly at the edge of the pasture, a gray shape moving through gray air. She had known this day would come since the notice arrived a week ago, but knowing and seeing were different things. The notice had been paper, words arranged in the careful language of officialdom, and she had read it twice before setting it on the shelf above the sink, where it still sat, its edges curling in the damp. Now the paper had sprouted legs and a theodolite and was standing in her best pasture, turning her forty acres into a problem to be solved.

She pulled her shawl tighter and went to the door. The pump needed seeing to before dusk, and the ditches along the lower boundary were still backed up from last night’s rain, but none of that would matter if she let this man plant his markers unchallenged. She stepped out into the yard, and the cold air pressed against her face like a wet cloth.

Philip Danvers—he had introduced himself in the letter, though she had not expected him to introduce himself in person—looked up as she approached. He was younger than she had imagined from the formality of his correspondence. Thirty, perhaps, with the kind of face that blended into a crowd: neat moustache, spectacles, a cap that sat squarely on his head. His coat was city-made, too light for the Fenland wind, and she saw the way he shifted his weight from one foot to the other, feeling the peat give beneath his boots.

“Mrs. Askew.” He touched the brim of his cap. “I do apologize for intruding upon your land without prior arrangement. The board felt it best to proceed with the survey while the weather holds.”

“The weather never holds here.” She stopped a few feet from his tripod, her arms folded across her chest. “It merely pauses.”

“Yes. I’m beginning to see that.” He gestured toward the instrument, brass fittings glinting dully under the gray sky. “I’ll try not to take up too much of your time. The survey should be complete by this afternoon.”

“And then?”

“Then the board will review my findings and determine the optimal path for the new drainage channel. You’ll receive notification of their decision within the month.”

Nell looked past him to the long field, forty acres of black peat that her husband had drained acre by acre, ditch by ditch, in the fifteen years he’d worked this land. The soil here was deep and rich, holding moisture like a sponge, and in a dry summer the potatoes came up clean and smooth, the sort that won prizes at the Ely market. The field had been Thomas’s pride. Now a stranger stood in the middle of it with a brass instrument on a tripod, drawing lines on a map.

“The notice said the dyke would cut through the lower quarter.” She kept her voice level. “That’s where the peat is deepest.”

“Yes. The survey will confirm the exact grade.” Danvers turned back to his theodolite, adjusting a screw with deliberate precision. “The board’s engineers believe the natural fall of the land supports that route. But I prefer to verify such things myself.”

“And if the fall is different than your engineers believe?”

He paused, his hand still on the adjustment screw. “Then I note it in my report.”

“Will it matter?”

The question hung between them. Danvers did not look at her. He peered through the instrument’s eyepiece, his fingers making a small correction to the angle.

“The board’s decision takes many factors into account. Land quality, drainage efficiency, cost of construction. The survey provides data. What the board does with that data is not my province.”

“But you’re the one drawing the line.”

He straightened and met her eyes for the first time. Behind his spectacles, his gaze was careful, neutral, the look of a man who had learned to keep his opinions separate from his work.

“I’m marking what’s already been decided, Mrs. Askew. The board has the authority to cut the dyke. My task is to find the most sensible path. If there’s a better route than the one proposed, I’ll note it. But the dyke will be cut. That much was settled when Parliament approved the scheme.”

“So my land is no longer mine when the board needs it.”

“Your land remains yours. The board will compensate you for the acreage taken and any damage to the remainder during construction. The notice outlined the terms.”

“The notice was written by the same people who want my land.”

Danvers said nothing. He reached into his coat and withdrew a small notebook, its pages warped from the damp air, and began to write something in pencil. The scratch of lead on paper was the only sound besides the wind moving through the bare branches of the willow by the ditch.

Nell watched him write. She thought of the notice on the shelf above the sink, its careful paragraphs, its references to Acts of Parliament, its offers of compensation that assumed she would accept. She thought of Thomas standing in this same field fifteen years ago, his boots sinking into the mud as he explained to her how the peat held water, how the ditches had to follow the natural fall, how a man could make something of this land if he understood it. He had understood it. She had learned from him, watched him, worked beside him in the last years when his chest began to fail and the heavy work passed slowly into her hands. This field was not just land. It was the shape of her life for the past decade and a half.

Danvers closed his notebook and tucked it back into his coat. “I’ll need to walk the full length of the proposed route. If you have no objection.”

“I have objection. But you’ll walk it anyway.”

He hesitated, and something shifted in his expression, a flicker of discomfort that he quickly smoothed away.

“I’m sorry. Truly. I know this isn’t easy. But the work has to be done, and it falls to me to do it. I’d rather do it well than poorly.”

“Doing it well means cutting through my best soil.”

“Doing it well means cutting through the soil that will give the best drainage to the greatest number of acres. The board’s mandate is regional, Mrs. Askew. Your field is part of a larger system.”

“A system I’m not part of deciding.”

“No. I don’t suppose you are.”

He turned and began to walk across the field, his steps careful and measured. Nell followed, matching his pace, staying a few yards to his left. She would not stand aside. If he was going to mark her land for cutting, he would do it with her watching.

They walked in silence for a hundred yards. The peat squelched beneath their boots, dark water welling up around each footprint. The field had been wetter than usual this spring, the ditches struggling to carry away the rain that fell nearly every day. Thomas would have been out here with the spade, clearing the channels, making sure the water moved. Thomas was two years in the ground now, and the ditches were her responsibility, and she had been out here herself yesterday, knee-deep in cold mud, because Amos had been busy with the pump and there was no one else.

Danvers stopped at a point near the center of the field and drove his heel into the ground to test the firmness. Then he reached into the satchel at his hip and withdrew a wooden stake, sharpened to a point, about two feet long.

Nell’s hands curled into fists at her sides.

He knelt, positioned the stake, and raised a small mallet he had drawn from the same satchel. The first blow was tentative, the wood sinking a few inches into the soft peat. He struck again, harder, and the stake drove home with a muffled thud that seemed to Nell to echo across the empty field.

She flinched. The motion was small, involuntary, a tightening in her shoulders that she could not control. The stake stood in her field now, pale wood against black soil, a marker that said: this land is no longer whole.

Danvers rose and brushed the peat from his knees. He did not look at her.

“The first marker,” he said. “The line runs northeast from here, following the natural fall toward the main drain.”

“How many markers?”

“Twelve. Perhaps fourteen. Enough to give the construction crew a clear line to follow.”

“And then the crew comes.”

“Within the month, yes. Weather permitting.”

Nell stared at the stake. It looked like a grave marker, though she knew that was foolish. It was wood, nothing more. But it stood in her field now, and soon there would be thirteen more like it, and then a ditch, and then a permanent scar across the land Thomas had spent his life healing.

“Mrs. Askew.” Danvers’s voice was gentle, though she had not asked for gentleness. “The board’s offer is fair. You’ll be compensated for the acreage, and the new drainage may benefit your remaining fields. The peat holds water too well in wet years. A proper channel could lower the water table across your entire farm.”

“You’re telling me the board is doing me a favor.”

“I’m telling you what the engineers believe. I’m not here to argue the board’s case. I’m here to survey the land.”

“Then survey it.” She turned away from him and began to walk back toward the house. Her boots left deep prints in the wet ground, and she knew she would have to smooth them over later, fill them in, keep the field whole for however many more days it remained hers.

She did not go inside. Instead she stood by the gate that led from the yard to the field, her arms wrapped around herself against the wind, and watched Danvers move across the pasture. Every so often he stopped, knelt, and drove another stake into the ground. Each thud carried on the air, a soft report that marked another point on the line.

She did not move until he had placed six stakes. Then she turned and went into the house.

The kitchen was warm from the banked fire in the range, and the smell of yesterday’s bread still lingered in the air. Nell stood at the sink and pumped water into a basin, watching the clear stream wash over her hands, carrying away the peat stain from her fingers. The water was cold, and she let it run until her skin ached, and then she pumped more.

The door opened behind her.

“Mother?”

Kitty stood in the doorway, her dark hair tangled from sleep, her doll clutched against her chest. She was twelve, small for her age, with the same watchful eyes Thomas had had, the same tendency to silence. She had been quiet since her father’s death, quieter than a child should be, and Nell sometimes did not know how to reach her.

“What is it, love?”

Kitty did not answer immediately. She crossed the kitchen and climbed onto the bench by the table, settling the doll in her lap. The doll’s name was Margaret. Kitty had named her three years ago and had not let her out of her sight since.

“There’s a man in the field.”

“I know. He’s from the drainage board. He’s marking where the new dyke will go.”

“What’s a dyke?”

“A ditch. A large ditch to carry water away.”

Kitty’s fingers stroked Margaret’s yarn hair, the motion repetitive and slow. “Why does there need to be a new ditch?”

“Because the board thinks the land needs better drainage. They’re cutting a channel through the lower field to help the water move.”

“Through our field?”

“Yes.”

Kitty was quiet for a moment. Then she looked up, her eyes meeting Nell’s with an intensity that made Nell’s chest tighten.

“Will we have to move?”

The question landed like a blow. Nell turned from the sink, drying her hands on the cloth that hung by the range, and crossed to the table. She sat across from Kitty and forced her voice to stay steady.

“Of course not. The dyke is just a ditch. It doesn’t change anything about the house or the farm. We’ll stay right where we are.”

“But if they take the field—“

“They’re not taking the field. They’re cutting a channel through part of it. The rest stays ours. Nothing changes.”

Kitty studied her mother’s face, looking for cracks in the reassurance. Nell kept her expression still, her hands folded on the table, her breathing even. After a long moment, Kitty nodded and looked back at Margaret.

“Will we have potatoes again tonight?”

“If you like.”

“Good. I like potatoes.”

Nell rose and went to the range, though the pot was already warm and there was nothing to do but stir the contents and replace the lid. Her hands needed motion. Her body needed something to do while her mind turned over the conversation she had just had.

A child’s fear, she told herself. A child’s question, born of not understanding. Kitty did not know about the notice on the shelf or the compensation that would not equal what the land was worth or the way a dyke would split the field and make the work harder and the drainage less certain. Kitty only saw a stranger with stakes and heard words she did not fully comprehend.

But the question had been asked. And Nell had answered with a lie, because the truth was that she did not know what would happen. The board’s notice had spoken of compensation and construction, but it had not spoken of what came after. A farm cut in half was harder to work. A field divided by a dyke was harder to drain. The water table might lower, as Danvers had said, or it might rise in the fields on either side of the channel, pooling where the peat had been cut. She had seen it before on other farms, where the board’s schemes had brought more water, not less, and the farmers had sold and left because the land would no longer hold a crop.

She did not know. She could not know. And Kitty had asked, and she had lied, because what else could she do?

The door opened again, and this time it was not Kitty but Philip Danvers who stood on the threshold, his cap in his hands, his face pale with cold.

“Mrs. Askew. I’ve completed the first pass. I wonder if I might trouble you for a moment’s warmth before I continue.”

Nell straightened. “You’re not finished?”

“The light is still sufficient for another hour’s work. But my hands have gone numb, and I cannot adjust the instrument properly when I cannot feel my fingers.”

She hesitated. Every instinct told her to send him away, to refuse him the smallest comfort, to make him feel in his body what he was doing to her land. But Thomas would have offered shelter to a stranger, even one who brought trouble. And the house was warm, and there was tea in the pot, and she was not so far gone in anger that she could turn a man out into the cold.

“Sit.” She gestured to the bench across from Kitty. “I’ll pour tea.”

Danvers sat, placing his cap on the table beside his notebook. Kitty watched him with open curiosity, her thumb stroking Margaret’s head.

“Are you the ditch man?” she asked.

Danvers blinked at her, then smiled, a small, tired expression that softened the formality of his face.

“I suppose I am. Though I prefer surveyor.”

“What’s a surveyor?”

“Someone who measures things. I measure the land so that other people know where to dig.”

“Do you dig the ditch?”

“No. I only mark where it will go.”

Kitty considered this. “Then you’re not the ditch man. You’re the marker man.”

Danvers laughed, a short sound that seemed to surprise him. “I’ve never been called that before. But I suppose you’re right.”

Nell set a cup of tea in front of him, the ceramic chipped at the rim, the tea dark and strong. She did not sit. She stood at the end of the table, her arms crossed, and watched him drink.

“The girl is your daughter?” Danvers asked.

“Kitty. Yes.”

“She looks like you.”

Nell did not respond. She let the silence stretch, and after a moment Danvers set down his cup and opened his notebook.

“I’ve marked the route the board proposed. I should tell you that the fall is not quite what the engineers believed. The land drops more steeply in the eastern quarter than the maps suggest.”

“Will that change the route?”

“It may. I’ll note it in my report. The board may adjust the line, or they may not. But I can only report what I find.”

“And what have you found?”

He looked up at her, his eyes steady behind his spectacles.

“I’ve found that your soil is excellent. Some of the best peat I’ve seen in this district. Deep, well-drained, good structure. Whatever you’re doing here, it’s working.”

“We work hard.”

“I can see that.”

He closed the notebook and rose, leaving the tea half-finished. “I should continue. The light won’t hold forever.”

Nell followed him to the door. He paused on the threshold, pulling on his gloves, and for a moment he seemed about to say something more. Then he shook his head, touched the brim of his cap, and stepped out into the gray afternoon.

She watched him walk back to the field, his figure growing smaller against the flat horizon. At the edge of the pasture, another figure appeared, distant and still, a dark shape against the lighter sky. Amos. He had been working the back fields all morning, and now he stood at the boundary between the long field and the lower pasture, watching Danvers move across the land.

Nell could not see his face at this distance, could not read his expression. But she saw the way he stood, shoulders squared, hands at his sides, a stillness in his posture that seemed to hold something in check. He watched for a long moment, and then he turned and walked away, disappearing behind the barn, returning to his own work without a word or a gesture.

Danvers continued his survey, driving stake after stake into the wet ground. Nell counted them from the doorway. Seven. Eight. Nine. The line was taking shape, a pale scar across the dark field, marking the path of the dyke that would cut her land in two.

She went back inside and closed the door.

The kitchen felt smaller now, the walls pressing in. Kitty had moved to the window and was watching Danvers with the same intensity she had given to Margaret a few minutes before.

“Mother.”

“What is it?”

“The marker man is putting stakes in our field.”

“I know.”

“Will they stay there?”

“Until the ditch is dug. Then they’ll take them away.”

Kitty turned from the window and looked at Nell. “I don’t like it.”

“I know, love.”

“It feels like the field is being cut.”

Nell did not answer. She crossed the kitchen and pulled Kitty into an embrace, holding her daughter against her chest, feeling the small body tense and then relax. Margaret was pressed between them, her yarn hair tickling Nell’s chin.

“Nothing is being cut,” she said, though she did not believe it. “The field is still ours. The house is still ours. We’re not going anywhere.”

Kitty pulled back and looked up at her mother. “Promise?”

Nell opened her mouth to answer. The word promise sat on her tongue, bitter and false. She swallowed it down.

“We’ll manage,” she said instead. “We always have.”

She released Kitty and went to the sink. The basin still held water from earlier, and she pumped more, watching it fill, watching the surface ripple and settle. The light was fading outside. Danvers would finish soon, and then the markers would stand in her field all night, pale sentinels marking the path of the coming destruction.

She thought of Amos, standing at the edge of the field, watching without speaking. He had not come to the house, had not offered words of comfort or protest. He had simply watched, and then turned away, and gone back to work. That was Amos. That was what he did. He worked, and he watched, and he kept his own counsel.

She pumped water into a cup and drank, the cold sharp against her throat. Then she set the cup down and went to the door.

“Kitty. Stay inside. I need to check on something.”

She stepped out into the yard and walked toward the barn. The wind had risen, and the sky was darkening toward dusk, and somewhere in the back field she could hear the sound of Amos’s work, the rhythmic scrape of a spade against peat. She would go to him, and they would stand together, and they would not speak of the stakes or the survey or the future that was closing in around them.

But she would not stand alone. That was something. That was enough for now.

She passed the edge of the long field, and the stakes stood in a row before her, pale wood against black soil, marking the line that would not be erased. She did not stop. She kept walking, her eyes fixed on the barn, on the fields beyond, on the work that still needed doing.

The pump needed seeing to before dusk. The evening feeding would not wait. And tomorrow there would be more stakes, and tomorrow the survey would be complete, and tomorrow the next phase would begin.

But today there was still work to do.

The pump handle stuck twice before the water came, and Nell cursed under her breath at the rust that had gathered at the joint. She would need oil. She would need a dozen things before the week was out, and now there would be survey fees and solicitor’s letters and all the quiet costs of fighting something that could not be fought.

She was still at the trough when Philip Danvers emerged from the far field, his notebook tucked under one arm, his boots caked with the same black soil that had held her family for three generations. He moved with an efficiency that bordered on indifference—each step measured, each motion economical. He did not look at the house as he approached, did not glance toward the barn where Amos had resumed his digging. He looked only at the path ahead, as if the land were already a map and not a place.

“Mrs. Askew,” he said, and his voice was neither warm nor cold. “I’ll have the preliminary survey notes filed by week’s end. The full report will take another fortnight, weather permitting.”

He was not cruel. She understood that now. He was simply a man doing a job, and the job required stakes in her fields and lines across her past. He would lose no sleep over it. He would remember none of their names by next month.

“Will you take tea before you go?” she heard herself ask. The words came from somewhere distant, from the part of her that had been trained to hospitality before she had been trained to grief.

“That’s kind, but no. I’ve miles yet today.”

She watched him go, and when she turned back toward the house, she saw Kitty at the window, her small face pale and watchful. Behind her, in the back field, Amos drove his spade into the earth again and again, his back to the house, his back to everything.
