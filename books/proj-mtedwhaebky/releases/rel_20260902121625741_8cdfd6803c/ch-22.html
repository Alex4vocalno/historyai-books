Chapter 22

The Empire of Empty Calories

The true measure of an empire’s victory is not when it plants its flag on foreign soil, but when its logic becomes the invisible architecture of everyday life. By the final quarter of the twentieth century, the economic logic of the sugar plantation—the relentless production of ever-cheaper sweetness for distant consumers—achieved this ultimate form of dominion. It ceased to be a geographical empire of owned islands and enslaved laborers, and became a metabolic empire of owned stomachs and engineered cravings.

Its territory was the global supermarket aisle; its subjects were every consumer who, often without a second thought, reached for a product whose very existence and profitability depended on a new, invisible caloric base. This chapter asks how a molecule, once a rare luxury, became the foundational infrastructure of modern eating, and what it meant that this final triumph required no ships or soldiers, only food scientists and marketing plans.

The pressure that forged this new empire was global, sudden, and brutal. The 1970s shattered the postwar consensus of cheap and stable plenty.

The OPEC oil embargo of 1973 did more than cause gasoline lines; it sent a shockwave through the intricate, oil-dependent system of industrial agriculture and global food transport. Fertilizer, tractor fuel, and shipping costs soared.

Simultaneously, the world commodity markets convulsed. The price of sugar itself, that old benchmark, skyrocketed in 1974 to heights not seen in generations.

For the vast, integrated complex of American and European agribusiness and food processing, this double crisis was an existential threat. Their entire postwar model was predicated on predictable, low-cost inputs: cheap oil to power the system, cheap corn and wheat from subsidized farms, and cheap cane sugar from stable (or controlled) tropical sources. Now, that model was broken.

The system faced a stark choice: retreat and simplify, finding cheaper, local ways to feed people, or innovate forward into a new, even more integrated and controlled paradigm of food production. The industry’s choice was a pivot so profound it would redefine the human diet.

This new resource fundamentally re-engineered the food landscape from the ingredient level upward. With HFCS-55 now flowing reliably from Midwestern processing plants, food manufacturers could reconceive entire product categories. The liquid sweetener blended seamlessly into beverages, eliminating the graininess of sucrose and creating a smoother mouthfeel that itself became a sensory expectation. It extended the shelf life of baked goods by retaining moisture, allowing breads and snacks to survive cross-country truck journeys and weeks under supermarket fluorescents without staling. Most importantly, it provided a predictable, low-cost input that insulated manufacturers from the gyrations of the international sugar market. This stability was not a side benefit but the core of the new empire’s economic logic; it allowed for the precise calculation of profit margins on a massive scale, turning the production of breakfast cereals, soft drinks, and packaged snacks from a culinary art into a volume-driven engineering discipline.

The architectural expression of this discipline was the modern supermarket’s “center aisle.” While the perishable departments—dairy, meat, produce—remained on the store’s perimeter, the vast interior space became a cathedral to stability and shelf life. Here, in brightly colored boxes and bottles, sat the empire’s true products: not foodstuffs in a traditional sense, but engineered caloric delivery systems. Their formulations followed a now-standardized template: a refined carbohydrate base (often wheat or corn flour) providing cheap bulk, a dose of HFCS for palatability and browning, fat for mouthfeel and flavor carrier, and salt to enhance and balance the sweetness.

This holy trinity of sugar, fat, and salt, pioneered by food scientists studying “bliss point” dynamics, was not a culinary accident but a calculated neurological trigger, designed to maximize consumption and, by extension, volume sales. The center aisle was no longer a place for provisioning but for stimulation, its layout a “planogram” meticulously optimized to guide the consumer from one craveable, high-margin item to the next.

This manufacturing and retail revolution required a parallel revolution in marketing, one that shifted from advertising specific products to normalizing a new mode of eating. The narrative of convenience, initially targeted at time-pressed homemakers in the 1950s, evolved into a narrative of fun, freedom, and individuality for a younger, broader demographic. Sugared cereals were no longer just breakfast; they were “part of a complete breakfast” and, more importantly, a toy-delivery mechanism. Soft drink campaigns moved beyond mere refreshment to associate their products with peak athletic performance, youthful rebellion, and global harmony. The marketing genius lay in decoupling the product from its nutritional reality, framing the consumption of empty calories as an expression of personal choice and modern lifestyle. This ideological project was as crucial as the enzymatic isomerization process; it transformed a dietary shift driven by corporate cost-cutting into a perceived consumer liberation.

The empire’s logic was inherently expansionary, and its next frontiers lay beyond the borders of its American heartland. The same multinational corporations that had consolidated control over the U.S. food supply—companies like Coca-Cola, PepsiCo, Nestlé, and Kraft—now wielded a potent new toolkit for global conquest. They possessed the capital, the patented HFCS-based formulations, the brand power, and, critically, the experience in managing vast, efficient distribution networks. As trade barriers fell under neoliberal policies in the 1980s and 1990s, these corporations moved into emerging markets in Latin America, Asia, and Eastern Europe not as mere exporters, but as local producers. They built bottling plants in Mexico, snack factories in Thailand, and distribution hubs in Poland, sourcing cheaper local labor and often local agricultural inputs, while retaining control over the proprietary blends of flavors, sweeteners, and stabilizers that defined their products.

This expansion followed a classic imperial pattern of creative destruction, dismantling existing foodways to create demand for new ones. In markets where small-scale street vendors or family-run shops had dominated food distribution, the arrival of multinationals, backed by massive advertising budgets and sleek packaging, presented processed foods not as foreign imports but as symbols of modernity and progress. Traditional diets, often built around whole grains, legumes, and seasonal vegetables, were subtly framed as “backward” or “poor,” while the consumption of sugary sodas, packaged chips, and instant noodles signaled participation in a global, aspirational middle class. The transformation was particularly rapid in urban areas, where the pace of life and the breakdown of extended family structures created a ready-made demographic for the empire’s promise of convenience and consistent taste.

The human metabolic system, however, proved less adaptable than global supply chains. By the 1990s, the public health consequences of this dietary transition, pioneered in the United States, began to manifest as a silent, global epidemic. Rates of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome, once considered diseases of affluence, began to climb sharply not only in the developed world but in developing nations adopting Westernized diets. The correlation was unmistakable: where the processed food complex established its distribution channels, public health metrics began to deteriorate. The empire’s infrastructure of cheap sweetness and fat was performing its function flawlessly—delivering dense, palatable calories efficiently to populations—but human biology, evolved for scarcity, was buckling under the constant caloric onslaught. This created a profound irony: the same economic forces that had solved the problem of calorie scarcity for millions were now generating a new, more insidious crisis of caloric overabundance and malnutrition.

The industry’s response to this growing health critique was not a retreat but a further refinement of its imperial logic. It deployed its food science prowess to create a new frontier: “functional” foods and diet products. Sugar was replaced with artificial sweeteners like aspartame; fats were engineered out and replaced with starch-based substitutes; products were fortified with vitamins and minerals to offset their inherent nutritional poverty. This pivot allowed the core architecture to remain intact—the center-aisle product, the heavy marketing, the global supply chain—while donning the cloak of health consciousness. The message shifted from “fun” to “wellness,” from “indulgence” to “smart choice,” even as the underlying economic model continued to prioritize shelf stability, low-cost inputs, and high profit margins over nutritional integrity. The empire demonstrated a chameleonic ability to adopt the language of its critics without surrendering an inch of its metabolic territory.

The volatility of the 1970s forced food manufacturers into a new, more ruthless calculus. With profit margins squeezed between rising ingredient costs and consumer price sensitivity, the industry’s research and development divisions were tasked with a singular mission: reformulation.

The goal was no longer merely to make foods tastier or more convenient, but to make them fundamentally cheaper to produce without sacrificing—and indeed, while enhancing—their palatability. This led to a systematic audit of every ingredient line on a balance sheet.

Sugar, once a celebrated flavor, became a cost problem to be solved. The quest was not for a mere substitute, but for a strategic substitute—one that would transfer economic risk from the unpredictable global tropics to the politically stable, subsidized heartland of American corn. High-fructose corn syrup was the elegant answer to this corporate dilemma, but its adoption required more than just a patent; it required a reimagining of food itself as a system of interchangeable, engineered components.

This shift in manufacturing philosophy had a direct and visible impact on the grocery cart.

Product developers discovered that HFCS did more than sweeten; it altered the very physics of food. In soft drinks, its liquid state allowed for more efficient, high-speed bottling line integration, reducing costs per unit in a business where a fraction of a cent mattered. In condiments like ketchup and barbecue sauce, its hygroscopic nature—its ability to attract and hold moisture—created a glazy, clingy texture that consumers came to associate with quality.

Perhaps most insidiously, in baked goods and snacks, HFCS’s fructose component, which is more reactive than glucose, produced deeper Maillard browning and more complex flavor notes during baking, allowing manufacturers to use less of the more expensive flavor-building ingredients like butter, cocoa, or real fruit. The sweetener was not just an additive; it was a multifaceted tool for cost control and sensory manipulation, enabling the creation of a universe of products that were at once strikingly consistent, incredibly shelf-stable, and remarkably cheap.

The normalization of this new dietary paradigm was not left to chance. It was underwritten by a marketing apparatus that learned to speak not to the stomach, but to the psyche. As the center aisle filled with these novel, engineered creations, advertising narratives evolved to make them indispensable.

The concept of “snacking” was transformed from an occasional, perhaps guilty, pleasure into a sanctioned, even necessary, component of the daily routine. Television commercials depicted a world where grabbing a candy bar was an act of fuel for an on-the-go lifestyle, where drinking a soda was synonymous with taking a refreshing break, and where serving pre-sweetened yogurt or cereal to children was a demonstration of caring modernity.

This marketing did not argue for the nutritional virtue of these products—an impossible task—but instead skillfully associated them with energy, happiness, social connection, and efficient living. The consumption of empty calories was thereby framed not as a passive submission to corporate product lines, but as an active, affirmative choice in step with the accelerating tempo of late-century life.

This cultural work was essential for the empire’s domestic stability, but its true test was in export.

The globalization of the American diet in the 1980s and 1990s was a process of layered substitution. First, multinationals substituted their own products for local ones. A street vendor’s fresh fruit juice or herbal tea was displaced by a bottled, branded soda. A homemade snack was replaced by a packaged chip.

Second, and more fundamentally, they sought to substitute the values associated with food. The traditional meal, often a social institution with seasonal and culinary rhythms, was juxtaposed against the marketed ideal of instant, individual gratification. The message, delivered through the powerful new medium of satellite television advertising, was that modernity meant freedom from the kitchen, from seasonality, and from the slow pace of traditional preparation. The processed food was not just a commodity; it was a token of membership in a new, global consumer class.

The success of this campaign created a feedback loop that further entrenched the empire’s logic.

As demand grew in new markets, it justified the construction of local bottling plants and manufacturing facilities, which in turn lowered costs and increased availability, creating more demand. National governments, often eager for foreign investment and tax revenue, facilitated this expansion through favorable trade agreements and deregulation.

The result was a rapid, often dramatic, dietary transition. In countries like Mexico and Brazil, per capita sugar-sweetened beverage consumption began to rival, and in some cases surpass, that of the United States. In Eastern Europe following the collapse of the Soviet bloc, the arrival of Western snack foods symbolized the end of austerity and the promise of capitalist abundance. The empire of empty calories did not conquer through coercion, but through the powerful allure of its own, meticulously crafted image—an image of sweetness, ease, and progress that proved irresistible across astonishingly diverse cultural landscapes.

It looked at the volatility of oil and tropical sugar and saw not a reason to abandon sweetness, but a reason to reinvent its very source.

If distance and politics made cane sugar vulnerable, the answer was to eliminate the distance. If the price was unstable, the answer was to create a cheaper, more controllable substitute. The pivot was not away from sugar, but deeper into its economic principle, using a different raw material: corn. Here, the historical arc bent back on itself. The American Midwest had been transformed into a monolithic sea of corn by decades of federal policy and technological innovation, creating a surplus so vast it was a political and economic problem in search of a solution.

The scientific answer was high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS). The key breakthrough was not the initial discovery, but the perfection of enzymatic isomerization processes in the mid-1970s that made the production of HFCS-55—a syrup nearly as sweet as table sucrose—efficient and cheap on an industrial scale. This was not merely a new sweetener. It was a strategic resource.

It was cheaper than imported sugar. It was a liquid, perfect for automated pumping and blending in vast factory tanks. It was derived from a massively subsidized, politically secure domestic crop. This final, invisible triumph of the plantation logic—the total externalization of cost and risk—now sat in every supermarket aisle, asking, as it always had, who would pay the true price of cheap sweetness. The answer, written in the body’s ledger of insulin and fat, was already coming due. Yet on the shelf, the economic logic was flawless: a bag of sugar made from this new empire of empty calories cost less than a bag of apples.
