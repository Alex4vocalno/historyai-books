Chapter 10

Alibi Verified

The keypad numbers glowed green in the afternoon gloom of Nora’s office, each digit a small, steady eye watching her decide. The phone receiver sat in her palm like a stone pulled from a riverbed—heavy, cold, unwilling to be lifted. Chen’s dismissal still burned behind her teeth. The system’s door had shut. The official case was closed, filed under “accidental,” and the detective’s silhouette had swallowed itself back into the bureaucracy that protected men like Daniel Reed.

She dialed.

Three rings. The office air conditioner hummed its sterile, institutional drone, pushing dead air across the clutter of her desk. Her notepad lay open, the word OPPORTUNITY underlined twice in blue ink, the pen hovering above it like a scalpel waiting for the first incision.

“Ridgewood Country Club, this is Gerald speaking, how may I assist you today?”

The voice was polished, warm, lacquered with the kind of professional ease that cost thousands in customer-service training. Nora could hear the ambient murmur of the dining room behind him—the low clatter of silverware, the tinkle of ice settling in a highball glass somewhere near the phone’s cradle.

“Mr. Gerald, good afternoon. My name is Nora Voss, I’m with Hartwell Insurance Adjusters.” The lie came out smooth, practiced, wearing the skin of a real woman who existed somewhere in a corporate directory. “I’m conducting a routine review of a claim involving one of your members. A Daniel Reed. I need to verify his attendance on the evening of—“She checked the date she’d circled on her desk calendar, the red ink like a wound on the page. “September the fourteenth.”

“Oh, certainly, Ms. Voss. Let me pull up the entry log.” A keyboard clattered. Nora’s pen tip touched the paper and held, a dark point of pressure. “Yes, September fourteenth. Mr. Reed—he’s a regular, of course. Swipes in around… let’s see… six-eighteen p. m. Our digital system logs the key fob at the main entrance.”

Six-eighteen. Nora wrote the number and drew a line from it to the fire’s reported start time. The gap was comfortable. Too comfortable.

“And he was seen inside the club after that time?”

“Oh, absolutely. I was on the floor myself that evening—we were short-staffed on the dining side, so I was helping seat guests.” Gerald’s voice carried the cheerful, unselfconscious detail of a man who remembered his work the way others remembered their morning commute. “Mr. Reed had his usual table in the lounge. He arrived a little after six-thirty, ordered a drink, stayed through dinner. I’d say he was with us until… oh, past ten, easily. I recall because we were closing the kitchen at ten and he’d already settled his tab by then.”

Through the receiver, the sound of ice clinking against glass punctuated Gerald’s words— someone at the bar, enjoying an afternoon drink. The mundanity of it was devastating. Nora’s knuckles whitened around the pen. The single line she’d drawn under OPPORTUNITY now looked like the desperate mark of a child playing detective.

“He was alone?” she asked.

“For most of the evening, yes. A few members stopped by his table to chat—you know how it is. But he dined solo.”

“Is there anyone who might remember specifics? A server, perhaps?”

“I can give you the name of our floor supervisor. But honestly, most of the staff who were on that night remember Mr. Reed. He’s quite personable. Always asks about people’s families.”

Personable. The word landed in Nora’s mouth like a coin from a stranger’s pocket. She thanked Gerald, her voice holding its professional shape through muscle memory alone, and set the receiver down. The dial tone purred in the silent office. The air conditioner cycled off, and the sudden quiet pressed against her eardrums like deep water.

She stared at the notepad. Six-eighteen. Past ten. Every minute accounted for, stamped and logged and witnessed by a man who had no reason to lie. The fire had been called in at nine-forty-seven. Daniel Reed had been eating dinner twelve miles from the Glass House while his wife’s lungs filled with smoke.

Nora pulled the singed diary page from the folder beneath her desk. She kept it there, between two case files, where no one would think to look. The paper’s edge was brown and curled, the ink surviving in patches like a message half-erased by the very thing that killed its writer. She’d read it so many times the words had begun to feel like her own thoughts.

The smell is back. Burnt plastic, something electrical, in the study. I told Daniel and he said I was imagining it. He said I should see someone. But I know what I smelled. I know what it means..

Nora had built her theory on this entry. The smell Eleanor reported, the one Daniel dismissed—she’d read it as evidence. He knew the smell because he’d caused it. He was testing something, preparing the method, and when Eleanor noticed, he’d gaslit her into silence. It was the skeleton of the case: Daniel had opportunity because the smell proved premeditation, and premeditation meant he would have engineered a window of absence.

But the alibi held. The alibi was perfect.

She read the entry again. The word imagining sat on the page like a trapdoor. What if Daniel was right? What if the smell was nothing—a faulty outlet, old wiring in a house full of glass and steel? Eleanor had been anxious, isolated, afraid of her husband. Fear made people hypersensitive. A person could smell smoke where there was none if she’d been conditioned to expect fire.

Nora pressed her thumb against the singed edge. The paper was warm, as if it still held a ghost of the heat. She’d been so certain Eleanor’s fear was evidence. Now the certainty crumbled like the diary’s burnt margin, and beneath it yawned a different, uglier possibility: that she’d taken the terror of a frightened woman and weaponized it, reading murder into a marriage that might have been merely unhappy.

The coffee shop on Mercer Street smelled of burnt espresso and wet coats. Rain had started on the drive over, a thin, persistent drizzle that fogged the windows and turned the sidewalk into a mirror of gray light. The hiss of the steam wand behind the counter punctuated the low chatter of the afternoon crowd. Nora sat in a corner booth, a cup of cold coffee untouched in front of her, watching the young man across the table fold and unfold a paper napkin with nervous fingers.

He was in his mid-twenties, lean, with the slightly hollow look of someone who worked on his feet for ten-hour shifts. He’d been easy to find—Gerald had mentioned the floor supervisor, who’d mentioned a waiter who’d specifically served the lounge that night. A twenty-dollar tip and a plausible story about an insurance questionnaire had brought him here.

“So you remember Daniel Reed,” Nora said. She kept her voice level, conversational. The coffee’s bitter smell cut through the warmth of the room.

The waiter scratched his jaw. “Yeah, actually. The scotch guy. I mean, we got plenty of guys order scotch, but this one—yeah. He came in, sat at table fourteen by the window. Ordered a double Macallan, neat. Then halfway through, wanted one of those big ice cubes, you know, the hand-cut kind? Said it opened up the flavor profile.” The waiter’s mouth quirked. “Which, like, okay, dude. It’s ice. But sure.”

Nora’s pen moved. Double Macallan. Ice cube. The specificity was agonizing. “Did you talk to him?”

“Oh, for sure. He was chill, not demanding at all. We got to chatting about the Ryder Cup—I’m a huge golf guy, and he noticed I was watching the highlights on my phone during a lull. He knew all the stats. We talked for like ten minutes about Koepka’s driving accuracy. It was, like, weirdly detailed? Like he really followed it.” The waiter shrugged. “Nice tipper, though. Left me forty on a hundred-twenty tab.”

Nora’s fingers went still on the pen. The tip pressed a dark blot into the paper, a small wound spreading through the fiber. She could see the scene: Daniel Reed, charming and relaxed, discussing golf scores, ordering premium scotch with the confidence of a man who had nowhere to be and nothing to hide. Twelve miles away, the Glass House would have been dark, Eleanor already dead or dying, the fire building in the walls. But Daniel had been here, in this ordinary world of ice cubes and Ryder Cup stats, building an alibi as solid as the granite bar top.

“You’re sure about the timing?” Nora asked. The words came out thin.

“Yeah, pretty sure. I remember because my shift ended at ten and he was still there when I clocked out. My buddy took over table fourteen. Oh—and the kitchen sent out his lamb at like eight-fifteen, because I was the one who ran it. He said it was perfect.” The waiter’s face shifted—not dramatically, just the subtle relaxation of someone who’d said all he had to say. “Is this guy in some kind of trouble?”

“No,” Nora said. “Routine review. Thank you for your time.”

She watched him leave, the bell above the door chiming once as he stepped into the rain. The steam wand hissed behind the counter. A woman laughed somewhere near the window. The sounds of a world that functioned normally, indifferent to the woman who sat in the corner booth with cold coffee and a theory bleeding out on the table in front of her.

Nora drove back to her office in the dark. Rain streaked the windshield in long, diagonal threads that the wipers caught and flung aside, over and over, a rhythm as mechanical and futile as the thoughts turning in her head. The alibi was verified. Gerald’s key-fob log. The waiter’s scotch and golf scores. The lamb at eight-fifteen. The tab settled before ten. Every detail mundane, specific, and mutually corroborating. There was no crack in it, no seam where a lie could be inserted. Daniel Reed had been at Ridgewood Country Club the entire evening Eleanor died.

Back in her office, Nora stood before the case board she’d pinned to the wall behind her desk. Index cards connected by red thread. Photos of the Glass House ruin. Eleanor’s diary entries transcribed on yellow legal paper. And in the center, a card that read ALIBI in thick black marker, circled three times.

She picked up the pen. Her hand trembled—a fine, visible vibration that traveled from her wrist to her fingertips, as if the bones themselves had gone uncertain. She wrote VERIFIED across the card in block letters, each one a nail hammered into the coffin of her theory.

The chair beneath her creaked as she sat. It felt unsteady, as if one leg had shortened, as if the floor itself had tilted a degree she couldn’t see but could feel in the pit of her stomach. She pressed her palms flat on the desk and stared at the board.

If not the alibi, what was the evidence?

The singed diary page lay where she’d left it, between the case files. She reached for it again, her thumb finding the burnt edge by habit. The smell is back. Eleanor’s words. Eleanor’s fear. Nora had read that fear as a warning ignored. But what if she’d misread it entirely? What if Eleanor wasn’t documenting a threat but simply recording a wife’s growing anxiety in a house with old wiring? What if the smell was real but innocent, and Daniel’s dismissal was callous, not criminal?

The rain had stopped. Through the office window, the streetlight cast a pale orange glow across the wet pavement. Nora stared at the ALIBI card, now marked VERIFIED, and felt the floor of her certainty shift beneath her like tectonic plates grinding toward a fault line she hadn’t known was there. Somewhere in the ruin of the Glass House, in the bones of a house that had been a home, the truth sat in the ash. But it wasn’t where she’d thought it was. It wasn’t in the alibi. It wasn’t in the smell.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Daniel Reed, her client, the man whose grief she’d been hired to process: Nora, I think it’s time we talked. Can we meet tomorrow? I have something I need to tell you about Ellie.

She set the phone face-down on the desk. The screen’s light bled through the gap between the case and the wood, a thin line of white in the dark room, like a door cracked open in a house that should have stayed locked.
