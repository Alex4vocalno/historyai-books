Chapter 8

Crossing the Line

The summons to Dr. Evans’s office was printed on clinic letterhead, formal and cold, and it had been sitting in Nora’s inbox for two days before she found the courage to open it. The words “Mandatory Case Review” sat in bold type above her name, stamped with a date and time that left no room for negotiation. She had read it three times in her car before her hands stopped shaking enough to grip the steering wheel.

Now she stood outside his door, adjusting her blazer, pulling the fabric straight across her shoulders as if the right crease could hide the fact that she hadn’t slept more than four hours in any night since she’d crawled through the broken window of the Glass House. The leather bag on her shoulder held the singed diary page tucked between a notepad and a dog-eared copy of The Bereaved Self, the spine cracked from use. She could feel its weight like a stone pressing against her hip, a gravity that pulled her posture off-center.

The corridor smelled of old books and reheated coffee, the clinic’s permanent atmosphere of quiet competence. A receptionist passed behind her, heels clicking on linoleum, and Nora’s shoulders flinched before she caught herself. She pressed her thumbnail into the pad of her ring finger, a small private pain to anchor the performance she was about to give.

She knocked.

“Come in.”

Dr. Evans’s office was exactly as it always was: oak desk polished to a dull shine, bookshelves lined with clinical manuals and framed photographs of hiking trips, a spider plant that had outgrown its pot. Sunlight cut through the window blinds in clean slats, catching the dust motes her entry stirred from the carpet. The light fell across his desk in bars, and behind them, a printed sheet of paper waited face-up, its heading visible from the doorway: Boundary Violations & Projection.

He sat with his sleeves rolled to the elbows, a fountain pen resting beside his right hand. His eyes, usually the warm brown of someone who’d spent decades listening to broken people without judgment, caught the light and held none of that warmth now. They were flinty, appraising, the eyes of a man who had noticed a crack in the foundation and was deciding whether to condemn the building or shore it up.

“Nora. Sit down.”

She sat. The chair was the same guest chair she’d sat in a hundred times before, during supervision sessions that felt like conversation, like mentorship. Today the leather felt stiff, unyielding. She placed her bag on the floor beside the chair, carefully, as if the diary inside might rattle.

“Thank you for coming in on short notice,” Evans said. He picked up the pen, not to write but to hold, a prop for his hands. “I wanted to discuss the Reed case. Your documentation has been thorough.”

“I’ve been careful.”

“Thorough isn’t the word I’d lead with.” He opened a folder—her folder, she realized, the clinical file she’d been maintaining—and turned it so they could both see. “Your session notes have tripled in length over the past three weeks. You’ve requested two unscheduled follow-ups with the client. And your language in the most recent entry—“He paused, reading. “’ The client’s affect suggests rehearsed grief, consistent with performance rather than processing.’ That’s a clinical observation, Nora, but it’s framed like an accusation.”

“It’s a hypothesis. Complex grief often presents with—“She stopped. Her voice had risen half a register. She brought it back down. “I’m maintaining appropriate clinical distance.”

Evans set the pen down. The tap of metal on wood was small and precise, a period at the end of a sentence he hadn’t spoken yet.

“Are you?”

The silence that followed pressed against her eardrums. She could hear the clock on the wall, its second hand clicking with mechanical indifference. The spider plant rustled in the draft from the vent above, a sound like pages turning.

“I’ve been thorough because the case warrants it,” she said. “Daniel Reed’s grief presentation is atypical. I’d be negligent not to document the inconsistencies.”

“Inconsistencies.” Evans leaned back in his chair, and the leather creaked beneath him. “That’s an interesting word for what reads like an investigation. You’ve noted his word choices, his timing, his clothing. You described the way he folded his hands during one session for an entire paragraph. That’s not clinical observation, Nora. That’s surveillance.”

Her thumb pressed harder into her ring finger. The nail left a white crescent in the skin. “I’m building a profile.”

“You’re building a case.” He said it without anger, without judgment, and that was worse. “There’s a difference, and you know it. The difference is the difference between your role and a detective’s. You’re a grief counselor. Your job is to sit with the bereaved and help them carry the weight. It is not to determine whether they deserve to carry it.”

He slid the printed policy across the desk. She didn’t look at it. She didn’t need to. She’d read the boundary policy in her first year at the clinic, signed it, filed it. Boundary Violations & Projection. The words sat in the sunlight between them, clean and institutional, a document designed for exactly this moment.

“I understand the policy,” she said, and her voice came out flat, controlled, a voice she didn’t recognize as her own. It was the voice of someone who had rehearsed this conversation in the shower at 2 a. m., who had practiced the shape of the lie until it fit her mouth like a retainer.

Evans studied her. The pen sat untouched on the desk. He picked it up, turned it between his fingers, set it down again. The rhythm was deliberate, a metronome.

“The Leo case, Nora.”

Her fingers went still on the notepad she’d brought. The clinical words she’d prepared—complex grief, atypical presentation, therapeutic concern—evaporated like steam off a hot surface. The room contracted. The sunlight bars on the desk narrowed to a single bright line.

“We’re not repeating that.”

The pen’s tap echoed like a heartbeat in the sudden silence, the ghost of her past failure now sitting visibly between them. Leo. The name filled the room like smoke. Leo, whose grief had been real, whose wife Clara had died in a fire ruled accidental, whose story Nora had misread because her own guilt had been louder than his pain. Leo, who had sat across from her in this same chair, in this same office, and she had failed to see what was in front of her because she’d been too busy projecting her own damage onto his.

Leo, who had taught her the cost of getting it wrong.

“That was different,” she said. Her voice was quieter now, stripped of the professional armor she’d walked in wearing. “Leo’s case involved a misdiagnosis. I misread his grief as performative when it was genuine. This is the opposite. Daniel Reed’s grief is performative, and I can prove—“She caught herself.

Prove.

The word hung in the air. Evans heard it. She watched him hear it, watched the slight shift in his posture, the way he leaned forward half an inch, the way his eyes sharpened.

“Prove,” he repeated. “Nora, you’re not a prosecutor. You don’t prove things. You observe them, you document them, you refer them. If you have a genuine clinical concern about a client’s involvement in a death, the protocol is clear: you contact the authorities and you step back. You do not investigate.”

“I haven’t investigated.” The lie came out smooth, practiced, and it tasted like copper on the back of her tongue. “I’ve observed. Everything in my notes comes from the sessions.”

Evans held her gaze. The warmth that had lived in those eyes for years was gone, replaced by something harder, something that looked like the expression a doctor wears before delivering a diagnosis.

“I’m going to issue a formal verbal warning,” he said. “It goes on your record. If there are further signs of boundary violation or personal projection in this case, I will recommend a mandatory leave of absence and a full review of your caseload. Is that clear?”

“Crystal.”

“I need you to hear me, Nora. Not agree with me. Hear me.”

“I hear you.” She stood. Her legs were steady, which surprised her, because the rest of her felt like it was held together with tape and willpower. She picked up her bag. The diary shifted inside it, the weight settling against her hip like a hand pressing down.

“Nora.”

She stopped at the door.

“Whatever happened with Leo—it doesn’t give you permission to correct it through the next client who walks in. You can’t balance that ledger. That’s not how this works.”

She didn’t turn around. “I know how it works.”

The door closed behind her with a soft click, and the sound of the lock engaging—imagined or real, she couldn’t tell—followed her down the corridor like a footstep.

The parking lot was half-empty. Late afternoon light stretched across the asphalt in long amber planes, and the air through the cracked window of her car smelled of hot tar and someone’s cigarette smoke from the sidewalk. She sat in the driver’s seat with both hands on the wheel, gripping it the way a person grips a ledge. The leather was warm from the sun, tacky under her palms. Her reflection stared back from the rearview mirror, and the face looking back at her had the kind of stillness that comes after something has broken and the pieces haven’t started falling yet.

Formal verbal warning. On her record. The words should have landed like a hammer, and they did, but the blow didn’t break what she expected. It broke the thin wall between the woman who followed the rules and the woman who had crawled through a broken window at midnight to steal a dead woman’s diary from a crime scene.

She pulled her bag onto the passenger seat. The zipper stuck, then gave. Her fingers found the diary’s edge—the singed page she’d pulled from the Glass House, its corner blackened and curled like a leaf that had been told to burn and had only half-obeyed. She slid it free and held it under the dim glow of her phone’s flashlight.

The paper smelled of old smoke. Not the sharp chemical bite of a recent fire, but something deeper, settled into the fibers, a ghost of heat that had seeped in and never left. The ink was Eleanor’s, unmistakable—the same looping script she’d been reading for weeks, the handwriting of a woman who had written in the dark, in secret, in the margins of a life she couldn’t control.

She’d read the entries about Daniel’s silences, about the locked cabinet, about the smell of smoke that came and went before the fire. She’d read about the fear that lived in the house like a fifth season, always coming, never quite arriving. But she hadn’t read this page.

The singed corner had hidden it. She’d been afraid to unfold it, afraid the paper would crumble, afraid of what Eleanor had written in the part of the diary that had been closest to the flame. As if the fire had tried to consume this particular truth first.

She unfolded it now, her thumbs careful on the brittle edges, and the phone’s blue-white light caught the first line.

Who do you tell when the person you should trust is the threat?

Nora read it again. The words didn’t change. They sat on the page in Eleanor’s careful hand, steady and unadorned, the question of a woman who had looked around at every door she could open and found each one locked from the outside.

The next lines filled the page in tight, compressed script, as if Eleanor had written quickly, as if the words were escaping before she could stop them:

He brought home a fire extinguisher last week. Put it in the kitchen, next to the stove. He showed me how to use it, smiled while he demonstrated, said “just in case.” Daniel has never cooked a meal in his life. He doesn’t know what the stove looks like. But he knows what fire looks like. He’s been learning, and he wants me to know he’s been learning, and the lesson isn’t about safety. The lesson is that he’s ready.

Nora’s hand trembled. The paper shook in her grip, a small seismic event that started in her wrist and traveled up her arm. She tightened her fingers until the tremor stopped.

Who do you tell? The police would need proof. My friends would need a reason. My family would need me to be someone they recognize, and I don’t recognize myself anymore. I am a woman who sits in her own house and counts the exits. I am a woman who smells smoke and wonders if it’s tonight.

The only person who might believe me is someone who doesn’t know him. Someone whose job is to listen. Someone who might hear what I’m saying between the lines of what I’m writing and understand that this is not a diary. This is a record. This is evidence.

If you’re reading this, please. See it for what it is.

The phone light flickered. The battery icon pulsed red in the corner of the screen. Nora didn’t move. She sat in her car in the clinic parking lot, the formal warning sitting on her record like a brand, the diary page trembling in her hands, and she understood something she hadn’t understood before.

Eleanor hadn’t just been documenting her fear. She’d been writing to someone. She’d been writing to a grief counselor she’d never met, a stranger who might one day sit across from her husband and see the thing she’d seen, and she’d left this page in the house knowing—hoping—that someone would come looking.

Nora folded the page along its original creases, slid it back into the bag, and zipped it shut. The warning from Evans was a wall. The diary was a door. She could not walk through both.

She turned the key in the ignition. The engine caught, and the vibration traveled through the steering wheel into her palms, into her wrists, into the bones of her arms. The dashboard glowed pale blue in the fading light, and the clock read 5: 47.

Detective Chen’s number was in her phone. She’d saved it weeks ago, the day she’d first let herself think the word murder without flinching. It sat in her contacts like a loaded round in a chamber, and every day she hadn’t dialed it was a day she’d chosen the wrong door.

Evans had told her to step back. The policy was clear. The warning was formal. Her professional license, her career, the life she’d built around the work of sitting with grief—all of it balanced on the edge of a decision she’d already made and was only now admitting to herself.

Eleanor had written: This is evidence.

Nora pulled out of the parking lot. She didn’t drive home.
