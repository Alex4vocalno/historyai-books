Chapter 9

Official Dismissal

The precinct door buzzer sounded like a verdict.

Nora pressed through before the lock could reconsider. The lobby’s fluorescent tubes cast everything in the color of old milk—faces, floors, the plastic chairs bolted in rows like pews in a church that had given up on God. She carried a manila folder against her chest, the edges digging into her forearms where she gripped too hard. Two weeks of obsession lived inside that cardboard: Eleanor’s fear, transcribed in careful handwriting; Daniel’s polished grief, documented in session notes; a phrase—perfect accident—that had burned itself into the space behind her eyes.

What it did not contain: the singed page she’d pried from beneath a loose floorboard in a dead woman’s kitchen at two in the morning, fingers black with charcoal, breath held until her lungs screamed. That page sat in her jacket’s inside pocket, folded into quarters, the paper’s rough edge catching against her shirt with every breath like a second heartbeat.

“Ms. Voss?”

Detective Chen stood in the doorway to the back offices. Shorter than she’d expected. Brown jacket, no tie, the kind of face that had been trained to absorb information without endorsing it. He held a clipboard against his hip.

“Detective. Thank you for making the time.”

“Your message said it was urgent.” He stepped aside, guiding her down a corridor lined with corkboards and memos. “This way.”

The interview room was small. A scarred metal table, three chairs, a one-way mirror that probably hadn’t fooled anyone in decades. The fluorescent buzz was a living thing, a frequency that settled in her molars and stayed.

“Sit.” Chen closed the door. The latch clicked with a sound like a bone setting. “You want coffee? Water?”

“I’m fine.” She set the folder on the table. Her palms left faint damp prints on the manila surface. She wiped them on her slacks.

Chen sat across from her, pen uncapped, notebook open. He didn’t look at the folder yet. He looked at her.

“Ms. Voss, I want to be straightforward with you. I looked into your background before you arrived. Licensed grief counselor. Eleven years in private practice. A client roster that, until recently, included Daniel Reed.”

“Until recently?”

“You discharged him as a client three days ago. Before that, you were seeing him weekly for bereavement counseling.” He tapped the pen against the notebook’s edge. “Now you’re here, asking for a meeting with the detective who worked his wife’s death investigation. Help me understand the timeline.”

Nora straightened in her chair. The metal groaned beneath her shifting weight. “I discharged Mr. Reed because I had concerns. Professional concerns. About his account of his wife’s death.”

“Concerns.” Chen wrote the word down.

“Eleanor Reed was afraid of her husband. Documentedly, consistently, and in her own handwriting.” Nora opened the folder. Her fingers, steadier than she’d feared, drew out three photocopied pages—transcriptions she’d made of the diary entries she could safely reveal. She laid them on the table between Chen’s notebook and the salt shaker someone had left by the window. “These are from a diary Eleanor kept in the months before the fire. She wrote about Daniel’s behavior—controlling patterns, cold anger, a specific dinner party where he—““Where did you get these?”

“The diary was in Eleanor’s belongings. I was given access through the therapeutic relationship.” The lie was smooth, practiced. She felt a muscle tighten in her jaw and forced it to relax. Let him think it came from Daniel.

Chen picked up the first page, held it at arm’s length, and read. His expression stayed neutral, the professional blankness of a man who’d heard a thousand stories that didn’t go anywhere.

“’He came home smiling. The same smile he wears at the club. I used to think it meant he was happy. Now I know it means he’s decided something.’” Chen set the page down. “This is evocative writing, Ms. Voss. What does it prove?”

“It establishes a pattern. Eleanor was documenting her husband’s behavior because she was afraid of what he might do.”

“People write in diaries for all sorts of reasons. Venting. Processing. Working through anxiety.” Chen slid the page back toward her. “A woman expressing fear in a private journal isn’t the same as a woman predicting her own murder. You know that. You’re a counselor.”

Nora pulled the second page forward. “This entry is from six weeks before the fire. Eleanor wrote that Daniel had been researching ‘safety improvements’ to the house. New wiring in the kitchen. She said he was unusually focused on the electrical panel.”

“Unusual for a homeowner to upgrade wiring?”

“Unusual for a man whose wife describes him as calculating every move like a chess player.” Nora tapped the page. “She was specific. She wrote about the smell.”

Chen’s pen stopped. “What smell?”

“Burning plastic. Ozone. She describes it in the diary—‘the smell that comes and goes, that Daniel says is nothing, that I know is something.’” She leaned forward. “She wrote about it three times in two months. She was documenting it, Detective. She was building a record.”

“Of a house with old wiring having electrical issues.” Chen leaned back in his chair. “Which is what the fire department found.”

Nora pulled the third page. Her hand lingered on the paper a half-second too long. She saw Chen notice.

“This entry is from ten days before the fire.” She flattened the page on the table. “Eleanor writes: ‘I think he’s planning something. Something perfect. Something that would look like an accident. He said the word perfect twice at dinner, talking about a colleague’s retirement party, but I could see he was thinking about the house. About the wiring. About the smell I keep complaining about and he keeps dismissing.’”

Silence. The fluorescent tube above them flickered once, a brief stutter in the hum.

Chen read the entry. His eyes tracked the lines, and his expression shifted—not toward belief, but toward the careful recalibration of a man who’d been presented with something he hadn’t expected.

“This is interesting,” he said.

The word interesting sat between them. Nora heard what lived underneath it—not interesting as in compelling, but interesting as in a therapist constructing a narrative from fragments that confirmed her own hypothesis.

“She’s describing a premeditated plan,” Nora said. “In her own words, ten days before she died in a fire that the official report attributes to the exact wiring she was afraid of.”

“Ms. Voss.” Chen closed his notebook. He opened a drawer, pulled out a thicker manila folder—official, stamped with a case number in red ink—and set it on the table between them. “I want to be transparent with you. Based on your message, I reopened this case file. I pulled the full investigation report this morning and had a junior officer cross-reference it with the diary excerpts you provided in your email. I went through it page by page.”

He opened the folder. Inside were photographs, diagrams, typed reports. He flipped past the first several pages to a technical drawing near the back—a detailed sketch of the Glass House’s wiring layout, with annotations in red ink marking junction points and load calculations.

“The fire originated here.” Chen’s finger landed on a circled junction point in the kitchen, near the sink. “An overloaded connection in the electrical panel behind the kitchen wall. The wiring was original to the house—twenty-two years old, cloth-insulated, installed before modern code requirements. The fire marshal’s report concluded that thermal degradation over time caused insulation failure, arc fault, ignition.”

He pulled out a photograph. The kitchen, gutted. The sink visible as a porcelain skeleton. The wall behind it opened like a wound.

“Right here,” Chen said. “This is where Eleanor Reed says, in your diary excerpt, that she smelled burning. And this is where the fire started.”

Nora stared at the diagram. But the diary first mentioned the study, she thought. Had the fault migrated? Or had Eleanor’s fear simply followed the smell to its source? The circled junction point was precisely where Eleanor had described the smell in later entries—under the sink, behind the wall, the place she’d written about with the specificity of a woman who kept checking because no one else would listen.

The precision of it made her stomach drop. Not because it disproved her theory, but because it made the theory indistinguishable from the official explanation. A woman afraid of a smell near the sink. A fire that started near the sink. One explanation was coincidence. The other was design. The diagram couldn’t tell the difference.

“I want to be clear about what you’re showing me,” Nora said. “The fire started exactly where Eleanor feared it would.”

“I’m showing you that the fire started where an electrical fault developed. The same electrical fault that any competent inspector would have flagged during a routine assessment.” Chen closed the folder. “Eleanor Reed smelled burning because there was burning. The wiring was degrading. That’s not evidence of a crime—that’s evidence of a house that needed maintenance.”

“Daniel was working on that wiring. He told Eleanor he was making improvements.”

“According to the diary. According to a woman who, by your own description, was afraid of her husband and may have been interpreting ordinary behavior through a lens of anxiety.”

Nora’s jaw tightened. “You’re dismissing her fear as neurosis.”

“I’m distinguishing between a woman’s emotional experience and a provable criminal act.” Chen’s voice stayed level. “I’ve been doing this for fourteen years, Ms. Voss. I’ve seen domestic violence cases that started with less. I’ve also seen grief counselors and family members construct elaborate theories from ambiguous evidence because the alternative—random, meaningless tragedy—is unbearable.”

He held her gaze. “Which is it?”

The question landed like a slap, not because it was cruel but because it was fair. Nora’s fingers curled against the table. The singed page in her jacket pressed against her ribs, its presence a weight she couldn’t explain and couldn’t surrender.

“I’m not constructing a theory because I can’t accept tragedy,” she said. “I’m presenting a documented pattern of fear from a woman who died in the exact manner she predicted.”

“Predicted.” Chen almost smiled. “That’s the word that concerns me. You’ve decided Eleanor predicted her death. What I see is a woman who had a reasonable fear of old wiring, wrote about it, and died because the wiring was old. The diary doesn’t prove intent. It proves awareness.”

He stood. The chair scraped against the floor.

“Ms. Voss, I reopened this file because you asked me to. I reviewed the report. I compared the diary entries to the investigation findings.” He placed both hands on the table, leaning forward. “The conclusion stands: accidental electrical fault. There is no physical evidence of arson. There is no witness testimony contradicting the timeline. There is no forensic indicator of a crime. The diary entries you’ve shown me are a woman’s private reflections, which, while concerning, do not constitute probable cause.”

He picked up the official folder and held it against his chest like a shield.

“I’m going to be direct with you now, and I need you to hear it as professional guidance, not a threat. You were Daniel Reed’s therapist. You discharged him. You’re now coming to me with diary entries you obtained through a therapeutic relationship, asking me to investigate your former client for murder based on his dead wife’s journal. Do you understand how that looks?”

Nora said nothing. The fluorescent hum filled the space her voice left behind. She didn’t correct him. Let him think it came from Daniel. It hides the break-in.

“If you continue pursuing this—contacting witnesses, gathering what you believe is evidence, inserting yourself into a closed investigation—you could face professional consequences. Licensing board complaints. Civil liability. I’d strongly recommend you step back.”

Chen opened the door. The corridor’s air hit her face, cooler than the interview room, carrying the smell of stale coffee and floor cleaner.

“Thank you for your time, Detective.”

“Ms. Voss.” He caught her at the threshold. “If you find something concrete—physical evidence, a witness, documentation of a direct threat—you bring it to me. That’s the system. You go outside it, and you’re on your own.”

She walked down the corridor. Her heels clicked on linoleum that had been mopped a thousand times and still looked dirty. The lobby’s plastic chairs were empty. The buzzer let her out into gray afternoon light that stung her eyes after the fluorescent interior.

The cold hit her face. She stood on the precinct steps, folder under her arm, the singed page still folded against her chest. Traffic noise washed over her. A bus wheezed past at the corner. Somewhere, a car alarm chirped twice and went silent.

She needed to get to her vehicle. She needed to think.

A dark sedan was parked across the street, two lots down from the precinct entrance. Engine off. No movement inside that she could see through the windshield’s glare. But the silhouette was wrong for an empty car—someone sat in the driver’s seat, still.

The posture, the stillness—it made her breath stop in her chest.

Nora’s hand went to her keys. The metal teeth bit into her palm.

The sedan was a common make. A common color. Half the cars in the city matched its profile. She couldn’t see the license plate from this distance, couldn’t make out the driver’s features through the afternoon light bouncing off the glass.

The posture, the stillness—it made her breath stop in her chest.

Inside her car, she locked the doors. Her breath came in short pulls. The dashboard clock read 3: 47. She sat with her hands on the wheel, the folder on the passenger seat, the singed page in her jacket, and the official report’s conclusion ringing in her skull like a bell that wouldn’t stop.

Accidental electrical fault. No physical evidence of arson. No probable cause.

The wiring diagram burned in her memory—the circled junction point, the red annotations, the precise location matching Eleanor’s fear with the clinical accuracy of an autopsy report. Eleanor had known. She’d written it down. She’d smelled it. And the system had looked at her fear and called it reasonable, looked at the fire and called it inevitable, and closed the file.

Nora pulled out her phone. Her thumb hovered over the screen, over a contact she hadn’t called in two years. A private number. A person who operated where the system’s light didn’t reach.

She’d sworn she wouldn’t go there again. The last time had cost her, a debt paid in the currency of trust and sleep, leaving a blueprint of damage she’d promised never to retrace.

The sedan might have been Daniel’s. It might have been anyone’s. The ambiguity was a knife with two edges—if he was watching her, he knew she’d gone to the police. If he wasn’t, she was inventing shadows in daylight.

She pressed the call button.

Two rings. Three. A voice answered—low, cautious, familiar.

“It’s Nora.” She watched the precinct steps in her rearview mirror. No one came out. No one followed. “I need help with something. Something outside the usual channels.”

A pause. “You sound like you already know the answer to the question you’re about to ask.”

“I need access to Daniel Reed’s financial records. Construction permits. Insurance policies. Anything that shows a pattern of preparation.”

“Nora.” The voice carried a weight of shared history. “You know what this costs.”

“I know.”

The line held silence for three seconds. Four.

“Send me what you have. I’ll call you back.”

The call ended. Nora set the phone on the passenger seat beside the folder. The car smelled like old coffee and the ghost of Eleanor’s diary, though the diary was miles away, locked in her desk, and the singed page was here, against her ribs, a secret she was choosing to carry into the dark.

She started the engine. In the rearview mirror, the sedan was still there. Still. Still watching, or still empty, or still a car that happened to be parked across from a police precinct on a gray afternoon while a grief counselor drove away with a dead woman’s last warning folded against her heart.

She pulled into traffic. The precinct shrank in the mirror. The sedan didn’t move.

But it could have.
