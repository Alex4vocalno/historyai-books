Chapter 2

The Ghost in the Room

The words “consistent,” “composed,” and “rehearsed” stared back at Nora from her own handwriting, circled three times in red ink, pressed deep enough to scar the page beneath.

That had been six days ago. The circles remained, three small wounds in the paper. She’d told herself, repeatedly, with the kind of conviction that only works when nobody is listening, that she was overreacting. That the dread pooling in her stomach—thick, chemical, wrong—was a relic, not a signal. That Daniel Reed was just another client, and she was just another counselor who hadn’t slept well.

Now Daniel was back. She could see him through the frosted glass of her office door, already seated, his silhouette motionless. He’d arrived twelve minutes early. She knew this because she’d been watching the clock with the attention she usually reserved for crisis assessments, her thumb worrying the callus on her forefinger until the skin burned.

Nora closed the notebook, slid it into her desk drawer, and pushed the drawer shut with her hip. The metal runners caught and scraped. She smoothed her blouse, checked her reflection in the darkened computer screen—professional, composed, nothing circling behind her eyes—and opened the door.

“Daniel. Come in.”

He was already in. Back straight, hands folded in his lap, coat draped over the arm of the chair with a precision that suggested placement, not tossing. He looked up at her with an expression of quiet readiness, like a man who had been waiting for this appointment the way someone else might wait for a train —patiently, knowing it would come.

“Thank you, Nora.” His voice was warm. Not performatively warm. The kind of warm that made her question whether she’d forgotten what genuine kindness sounds like. “I appreciate you making time.”

“Of course.” She settled into her own chair, the leather creaking in the silence. She picked up her pen, then set it down. Picked it up again. “How has this past week been for you?”

“Long,” he said. “The nights are long.”

She waited. In her experience, silence was the most useful tool in a counselor’s arsenal. People rushed to fill it. They stumbled into truth in the gaps between sentences. They contradicted themselves. They circled back to what mattered, or they fled from it, and either way, you learned something.

Daniel didn’t rush.

He let the silence sit between them like a guest he was comfortable with. He looked at the window, then back at her, and smiled—not broadly, just a slight softening at the corners of his mouth that conveyed gratitude for being asked.

“The house is quiet now,” he said. “Eleanor always had music playing. She’d leave the radio on in the kitchen, just low enough that you couldn’t quite make out the song. Just enough to know it was there.” He paused. “I keep turning it on, hoping it will feel like she’s still here. It doesn’t.”

Nora wrote: Idealization of deceased. Sensory detail—music. Longing for presence..

Her pen stopped. The detail was beautiful. Specific. The kind of small, intimate observation that felt plucked from a real marriage. And yet something about the delivery—the measured cadence, the pause placed precisely where a listener’s empathy would peak—made the hair at her nape lift.

She looked up. “You mentioned last week that you were in the study when the fire started. Can you walk me through that evening again?”

Daniel’s expression shifted to something more somber. “Of course.” He took a breath. “I’d been reading. Eleanor was in the kitchen— she’d been trying a new recipe. Something with lamb, I think. She was always trying new things.” A faint, fond shake of his head. “I heard the smoke alarm, and by the time I got downstairs, the kitchen was—“He swallowed. “It was bad.”

Nora nodded slowly. Her pen was still. She remembered, from last week’s session, that he’d said Eleanor was cooking chicken. Not lamb. A small thing. Inconsequential, maybe. People misspoke. People forgot details under emotional strain. She’d done it herself a hundred times in sessions, said one word when she meant another, and it had meant nothing.

But she hadn’t misspoken last week. She was almost certain.

“I’m sorry,” she said, leaning forward slightly, keeping her tone gentle. “Last week you mentioned she was cooking chicken, I think. Do you remember?”

Daniel looked at her. His expression didn’t flicker. “Did I? No—it was lamb. I’m sure of that. Eleanor had been planning it for days. She was so particular about getting the seasoning right.” He offered a small, self-deprecating smile. “I’m afraid I wasn’t paying close enough attention to my own story last week. Grief does that, doesn’t it? Makes everything feel a bit like a dream.”

“Of course,” Nora said. “That’s completely normal.”

She wrote nothing. Her hand was still.

He’d corrected the inconsistency without hesitation. Without a single crack in composure. Not a flush, not a stammer, not a micro-expression of being caught. He’d simply adjusted, the way someone adjusts a tie—smoothly, reflexively, without any indication that the tie had been crooked in the first place.

Nora felt something shift inside her. A door opening onto a dark room she’d been trying to keep locked.

The late afternoon sun had moved while they spoke, and now it cut across the rug in a long, warm rectangle, illuminating dust motes that drifted between them like slow, aimless confetti. The light caught the side of Daniel’s face, and Nora could see the fine grain of his skin, the careful shave, the faint lines around his eyes that suggested a man who had spent years smiling in all the right photographs.

He was speaking again. His hands rested calmly on his knees, and his voice had settled into a lower register—intimate, confessional, calibrated for the space between two people in a room where one of them was paid to listen.

“The hardest part isn’t the big things,” he said. “It’s the small ones. The way she’d hum when she was concentrating. The way she’d leave her reading glasses on top of the refrigerator and then spend twenty minutes looking for them.” He laughed softly, and the laugh curdled into something fragile. “I keep finding her glasses in places I’ve already checked three times.”

Nora watched him.

A single tear emerged from his left eye. It tracked down his cheek in a slow, perfect line, catching the light as it went, arriving at his jaw just as he said the word lonely.

The tear was a period at the end of a sentence. It arrived precisely on cue..

And the scent of his cologne—expensive, subtle, the kind that suggests careful selection—suddenly smelled to Nora like a locked room. A room with no windows. A room where the air was too still and everything had been arranged just so.

She looked away. Focused on a smudge on her notebook, a faint crescent of ink from weeks ago. She breathed.

And then Leo was there.

Not in the room. In her. Behind her eyes, sudden and blinding—Leo’s face, flushed and rigid with a rage that made his features look carved from something harder than flesh. She could see the tendons in his neck. She could see the way his jaw locked. She could hear his voice, that terrible, measured calm that was worse than shouting: If I did it, they’d say you should have seen it coming. Would they be wrong?

She flinched.

It was small—a twitch at the corner of her mouth, a slight pull of her shoulders—but it was there, and it broke something inside the session. She lost the thread of what Daniel was saying. His words became sound without meaning, the way a foreign language sounds when you’re too tired to parse it.

“Nora?” Daniel’s voice, concerned. “Are you all right?”

She blinked. The office reassembled around her. The dust motes. The light. The client chair and the man in it, watching her with an expression of gentle worry that she didn’t trust and couldn’t explain not trusting.

“I’m fine,” she said. “I apologize. I—lost my train of thought for a moment.” She straightened. “You were saying. About Eleanor’s glasses. ““Her glasses,” Daniel said, and something in the way he picked the thread back up— seamlessly, without confusion, without the awkwardness of someone whose counselor had just zoned out—made Nora’s stomach tighten. “I found them again yesterday. On the nightstand. I just… sat there holding them for the longest time.”

“That’s grief,” Nora said. “It lives in the objects they leave behind. ““It does.” He looked at her with something open and undefended, and she felt the full weight of her own suspicion press against the inside of her chest like a trapped bird.

What was she doing? This man had lost his wife. He was sitting in her office, doing the work, telling her about reading glasses and kitchen music and lamb instead of chicken—or chicken instead of lamb, and who the hell cares, Nora, who the hell cares except you, who can’t stop seeing monsters in the dark because you missed one in the light?

She knew what this was. She’d been here before, in this exact posture of hypervigilance, scanning a client’s face for signs of performance, cataloguing inconsistencies, building a case that didn’t need building. She’d done it with Leo. She’d done it and she’d been right, and then she’d done nothing, and then it was too late, and now every client who sat in that chair was Leo, and every smooth sentence was a lie, and every perfect tear was evidence.

But Daniel was not Leo. Daniel was a man whose wife had burned to death in their kitchen while he read in the study, and he was trying to survive it.

Wasn’t he?

She looked at him again. The tear had dried. His hands were still. His eyes were steady and sad and exactly the right amount of both.

“Daniel,” she said, and her voice was softer now, more careful, because she was no longer probing. She was retreating. “Thank you for sharing that. I know this isn’t easy.”

“It isn’t,” he agreed. “But it helps. Talking about her. It makes her feel less…” He searched for the word. “Less gone.”

Nora nodded. She wrote: Client displays textbook complicated grief. Idealization, sensory reminiscence, emotional lability within expected range. Session productive.

She did not write performative. She thought it. The word surfaced and she pushed it down, drowned it in the ink of what she actually wrote, because the word performative was her word, her pathology, her scar tissue masquerading as clinical observation.

The session continued for another fifteen minutes. Daniel spoke about the insurance paperwork, the neighbors who kept bringing casseroles he couldn’t eat, the way the house smelled different now—like absence, he said, which was such a precise way to put it that Nora almost admired the construction. She listened. She nodded. She asked follow-up questions that were gentle and open-ended and exactly the questions a competent counselor would ask.

When the hour was up, Daniel stood. He shrugged on his coat with the same careful precision. He thanked her. He said he’d see her next week. He left.

The door closed behind him with a soft click.

Nora sat in the silence.

The indent in the client chair cushion was still visible—a shallow compression in the foam, already beginning to rise back. She stared at it. She stared at it the way you stare at a space where someone was, looking for the ghost they left behind.

The office was quiet. The hum of her computer was the only sound, a low, persistent drone that she usually filtered out but which now seemed to fill the room like tinnitus. The late sun had shifted again, leaving the rug in shadow. The dust motes were gone, or invisible, or had never been there.

She picked up her pen. Opened her notebook to a fresh page. And began to type—not write, type, because writing was personal and typing was clinical, and she needed clinical right now, she needed the distance of a keyboard and a screen and the formal structure of a session note that would go in a file that would be reviewed by nobody, because nobody reviewed her work anymore, because she was careful, because she was fine.

Session Notes — Client: Reed, Daniel. Session 2.

Client presents with complicated grief following the death of his wife, Eleanor Reed, in a house fire approximately six weeks ago. Affect is appropriate—sad, reflective, with moments of warmth when discussing the deceased. Client provides specific, sensory-rich memories of his wife (music in the kitchen, misplaced reading glasses, cooking lamb). Emotional expression is within expected parameters, including one tearful moment when discussing loneliness.

Client corrected a minor inconsistency from the previous session (type of meat Eleanor was cooking on the night of the fire) without defensiveness or distress, attributing the error to his own emotional state. This is consistent with the cognitive effects of acute grief.

Assessment: Client is engaged in the therapeutic process and displays genuine grief responses. No indicators of complicated grief pathology beyond expected bereavement patterns. Recommend continuing weekly sessions.

She looked at what she’d written. It was clean. Professional. True.

She highlighted the word genuine and stared at it until the letters stopped looking like English.

Then she deleted the word and replaced it with appropriate.

Then she deleted that and wrote observed.

Then she closed the file.

The screen went dark, and in its reflection she could see herself—her face pale, her eyes too bright, her mouth set in a line that was trying very hard to be firm and was instead just thin. She looked like a woman arguing with herself. She looked like a woman who had lost an argument with herself and was pretending the other side had forfeited.

She reached for the notebook. The circled words were still there, visible through the page like bruises through skin. Consistent. Composed. Rehearsed.

She tore the page out. Folded it once, twice, three times, and dropped it in the trash. The words disappeared beneath a crumpled tissue and a coffee-stained receipt.

Nora stood, picked up her bag, and turned off the lamp. The office dimmed. The chair was empty. The indent was gone.

She locked the door and walked down the hallway, her heels clicking on the linoleum in a rhythm that sounded, if you listened carefully, like someone trying very hard to convince herself of something.

The ghost in the room had been her own. She was sure of it. Almost sure. The kind of sure that sits in your stomach like a stone you swallowed and can’t quite cough up, that you carry with you and tell yourself is just a feeling, just a memory, just the residue of an old failure that has nothing to do with the man in the chair and his perfect tear and his lamb that used to be chicken.

She was sure.

She was sure.

She pushed through the lobby doors and stepped into the evening, and the air was cool and clean and smelled nothing like smoke, and she told herself that was enough.
