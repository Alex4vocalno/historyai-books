Chapter 15

Stevenson’s Own Account

In April 1824, the publisher William Blackwood issued a memorandum for the sale of a new volume, An Account of the Bell Rock Lighthouse. The bound ledger, priced at one guinea, contained 328 pages of text and a frontispiece engraving. Its publication marked a precise moment in a longer campaign concerning the historical record of the lighthouse’s construction, arriving three years after the death of consulting engineer John Rennie in October 1821.

The book’s physical construction announced its argumentative purpose. The preface, addressed “to the Public,” promised a documentary rather than a literary achievement: a record of “difficulties encountered and the means employed to overcome them.” The body divided into four chronological sections corresponding to the four working seasons of 1807 through 1810, each opening with preparatory arrangements and closing with progress achieved. This structure imposed a logic of necessity upon contingent events. The September 1807 stranding of thirty-two men when the tender Smeaton broke her moorings appeared not as catastrophe narrowly averted but as demonstration of organizational capacity under pressure. The construction of the temporary wooden beacon in 1808 emerged as inevitable response to the demonstrated inadequacy of ship-based operations. The laying of the first granite courses in 1810 marked the culmination of systematic preparation. The narrative moved from crisis to adaptation to triumph with the apparent inevitability of technical progress itself.

Yet this inevitability was manufactured through selection. Stevenson’s account devoted seventeen pages to the beacon’s construction: the prefabrication of timbers at Arbroath, their transport to the rock, the raising of the six-legged structure that would house workmen through subsequent seasons. It allocated twenty-three pages to the foundation course, explaining the system of dovetail joints and iron bolts that locked each granite block to its neighbors, the method of drilling and blasting the rock surface, the precautions against tidal damage during mortar curing. By contrast, the visits of John Rennie—three documented inspections in 1808, 1809, and 1810—occupied a single paragraph. Rennie appeared as a consulting engineer who had approved the general design and occasionally inspected progress. The description was technically accurate. It was also strategically minimal, reducing a figure of contemporary eminence to a formal checkpoint in a process directed elsewhere.

The disparity in narrative attention carried an implicit theory of engineering achievement. Stevenson’s account consistently located decisive agency in proximity to physical difficulty. The men who mattered were those who endured the conditions: masons working waist-deep in seawater during two-hour windows of low tide, smiths forging bolts in cramped beacon quarters, sailors maneuvering tenders through breaking seas. Stevenson placed himself in this company, not above it. His narrative recorded his presence on the rock during 147 of 262 working days across the four seasons, a statistic calculated with evident satisfaction. He described his own labor with the same concrete specificity applied to workmen: examining fits of interlocking courses, supervising mortar mixing, taking soundings to predict daily working conditions. The message required no explicit statement. The Bell Rock had been built by men who were there, and he had been there more consistently than anyone else.

The treatment of crisis moments reinforced this positioning. The Smeaton stranding received extended narrative reconstruction through preserved log entries: the rising wind of September 17, 1807, the parted hawser at 3 a. m., the dawn discovery that the ship had drifted beyond sight, thirty-two men left on a rock that would be submerged by returning tide. Stevenson emphasized improvised survival: temporary beacon construction from salvaged timber, provision rationing, morale maintenance during thirty-two hours of exposure. When rescue arrived, it was Stevenson who had directed emergency operations and preserved order among stranded men. Rennie was not present. The absence spoke without requiring commentary.

The technical explanations served parallel functions. Stevenson devoted careful attention to the “courses”—precisely dimensioned granite blocks cut at Arbroath to interlock without mortar above high-water line. He described dovetail joints, wrought-iron bolts countersunk into recesses, the tower’s taper from forty-two feet diameter at base to fifteen feet at lantern stage. These passages established command of engineering particulars. They also, implicitly, established command of engineering decisions. The text noted that “several improvements” had been made to John Smeaton’s Eddystone design, but did not specify whose improvements. The passive construction allowed technical authority without explicit self-attribution, a rhetorical modesty that was itself power’s instrument.

The celebration of workmen’s endurance operated in similar register. Stevenson’s narrative named individual masons, smiths, and sailors with frequency unusual in contemporary technical writing. John Bonnyman, who lost a finger to the beam crane in 1808, appeared as representative sacrifice, his later appointment as lighthouse keeper offered as institutional recognition. Peter Logan, master mason supervising granite cutting, received extended description for Arbroath yard management. Francis Watt, principal smith, was credited with innovations in salt-resistant ironwork. These attributions acknowledged human cost in concrete terms. They demonstrated capacity to mobilize and retain skilled labor. They constructed a community of achievement with the resident engineer as natural center—the figure who knew names, remembered injuries, distributed recognition.

The silences were as calculated as emphases. The correspondence with Rennie during construction years, including eighty-two letters requesting instructions on matters already resolved, received no mention. The strategy that had transformed Rennie’s nominal authority into paper trail of consultation—a tactic Stevenson’s grandson would later describe as deliberate “system of worrying” to keep the consulting engineer at distance—was entirely absent. The frustrating 1809 visit, when Rennie proposed beacon modifications that would have delayed work by weeks, appeared as routine inspection. The fundamental uncertainty about whether tower proportions and structural system originated with Rennie or Stevenson was addressed only through indirect assertion. The design was described as approved by Rennie, a formulation conceding formal precedence while implying substantive independence.

The publication of 1824 thus represented culmination of position-taking that had begun with first foundation stone. Stevenson had understood from outset that the Bell Rock would be remembered, and that memory would require management. His preservation of documents—logbooks, correspondence, financial accounts, daily tallies of stone landed and courses completed—had been systematic from first season. The 1824 account drew upon this archive with confidence of a man who had known, seventeen years earlier, that he would someday need to prove what happened and who made it happen. The narrative was not retrospective reconstruction but planned disclosure, execution of strategy conceived in press of immediate difficulty.

Contemporary reception confirmed effectiveness. The Edinburgh Review, whose judgment carried decisive weight in Scottish professional circles, praised the account as valuable contribution to practical engineering literature, noting particularly the clear and unpretending narrative of difficulties overcome. The Quarterly Review in London, less friendly to Scottish self-assertion, nonetheless acknowledged documentary value. More significant than journalistic notice was institutional response. The Northern Lighthouse Board, meeting November 1824, ordered purchase of fifty copies for distribution to commissioners and engineers. Minutes recorded no discussion of historical claims; the order proceeded as routine administrative recognition. Stevenson had become, by 1824, the Board’s established voice. His account was received as official history because he had made himself indispensable to the institution that would preserve it.

Parallel activities of Rennie’s sons demonstrated what Stevenson had pre-empted. Sir John Rennie, assuming father’s practice, had begun collecting materials for memoir asserting senior Rennie’s primacy in great engineering achievements of previous generation. The Bell Rock appeared in this projected narrative as Rennie project executed through subordinate agency, a characterization eventually published in the 1870s. But delay proved costly. By the time Rennie counter-narrative reached print, Stevenson’s 1824 account had been absorbed into reference literature, cited in parliamentary papers, incorporated into training of lighthouse engineers. The historical field had been occupied.

The mechanism of this occupation repaid examination. Stevenson’s narrative achieved effects through distinctive fusion of personal testimony and institutional documentation. First-person voice appeared only intermittently, in passages of direct observation: “I was on the rock,” “I examined the joint,” “I directed the men.” These insertions established presence without demanding attention. They allowed reader to forget that narrative was shaped by single perspective, even as that perspective structured every page. Extensive quotations from Board minutes and workmen’s records created appearance of multi-vocal documentation. Reader encountered voices of commissioners, contractors, sailors, masons, all apparently speaking for themselves. Only selection and arrangement of these voices remained entirely in Stevenson’s control.

Technical passages operated with similar indirection. Explanation of interlocking granite courses moved between description levels with skill that concealed argumentative purpose. Paragraph on geological properties of Aberdeen granite established material constraints any design must accommodate. Paragraph on cutting tolerances at Arbroath demonstrated execution precision. Paragraph on assembly process showed integration of design and construction. Cumulative effect made completed tower appear as necessary outcome of coherent technical logic, with Stevenson’s management as thread connecting successive stages. Rennie’s name appeared only as formal approval, checkpoint passed rather than direction given.

Human costs acknowledged served equally strategic purposes. Account of 1807 stranding did not minimize danger; it detailed danger with specificity testifying to narrator’s own exposure. Men had been left without shelter on rock that would be submerged. Tide was rising. Ship that should carry them to safety had disappeared into stormy darkness. Narrative power derived from restraint: simple statement of facts, absence of rhetorical heightening, implied contrast between terror of situation and calm of its recording. Reader was invited to measure achievement by risk entailed, and to identify achievement with man who survived to describe it.

Later career of 1824 account demonstrated success in shaping institutional memory. When Northern Lighthouse Board recorded formal tribute to Stevenson on his death in 1850, resolution drew language directly from his narrative. Honor of conceiving and executing the Bell Rock lighthouse was attributed to him alone, formulation that Board minutes preserved and subsequent historians quoted. Rennie claim had not been refuted; it had been rendered irrelevant by prior establishment of alternative record. Dispute over credit, begun in correspondence of 1812 and continued through postwar years, was effectively concluded by publication of 1824. Files remained, letters could be consulted, but narrative that organized them had achieved precedence that mere documentation could not displace.

Stevenson’s own subsequent career confirmed the strategic value of historical investment. He remained engineer to the Board until 1843, directing construction of fourteen additional lighthouses and establishing technical standards governing Scottish lighthouse engineering for the remainder of the century. Authority derived partly from accumulated expertise, partly from institutional position, but partly also from a published record that made him author of the greatest engineering achievement in the Board’s history. The 1824 account was not retrospective vanity. It was a forward-looking instrument, a claim on future recognition that shaped the possibilities of present action. When Stevenson advised on Skerryvore lighthouse in the 1830s, his recommendations carried weight because he had already written the definitive account of how such impossible projects could be accomplished.

Book’s final effect was to transform Bell Rock from contested achievement into settled monument. Stevenson’s narrative made tower speak with single voice, his own, and made that voice appear as natural expression of structure itself. Interlocking granite courses, iron doors and shutters, lantern first lit February 1, 1811—all material facts were organized into story of sustained individual effort overcoming natural obstacle. Collaborative complexity of actual construction, network of contractors, commissioners, sailors, masons whose labor was extracted and coordinated, was simplified into drama of representative heroism. Tower stood as evidence of what one man, properly situated and sufficiently determined, could achieve.

This simplification was not false in particulars. Stevenson had been present, had directed, had preserved records and written account. The falsity, if it could be called that, lay in what narrative made invisible: structure of institutional support enabling his presence, financial arrangements sustaining work through four seasons, political negotiations securing parliamentary authorization and public funding. The 1824 account treated these enabling conditions as background, natural features of landscape rather than achievements in own right. Result was model of engineering achievement as individual mastery, model serving Stevenson’s interests and those of profession he represented, but obscuring collective and institutional dimensions of work described.

Endurance of this model testified to effectiveness of Stevenson’s narrative strategy. More than century after publication, his 1824 account remained standard source for histories of Bell Rock lighthouse. Engineers cited technical descriptions. Biographers drew anecdotes. Tower itself, photographed and described in countless publications, carried Stevenson’s interpretation in visible form. Dispute over credit had been resolved not by argument but by prior occupation of historical field. Rennie’s claims persisted in family papers and occasional polemics, but could not displace established narrative. Stevenson had written book that defined what Bell Rock meant, and meaning, once established, proved harder to move than granite.

In 1824, the year of publication, Stevenson inscribed a presentation copy to the Commissioners of Northern Lighthouses. The inscription was formal, acknowledging their patronage and support through the construction years. The book that followed made a more substantial claim. It established the author as the necessary figure without whom the lighthouse could not have been built, and established this claim through the very density of documentation. Minutes, letters, financial accounts, daily tallies—all materials, presented with apparent transparency, composed a portrait of managerial indispensability. A reader working through the 1824 account emerged with a single clear impression: that Robert Stevenson had been present at every decisive moment, had directed every critical operation, had preserved the record that proved it. The impression was accurate to the documents. It was also, in the selection and arrangement of those documents, a construction as deliberate as the interlocking granite courses described.

The volume’s appearance in 1824 carried temporal significance that Stevenson could not have failed to calculate. John Rennie had died in October 1821, leaving his reputation in the hands of executors and heirs who would require years to organize his papers and construct their competing narrative. Stevenson’s publication arrived while Rennie’s voice remained silenced and his partisans remained unprepared. The three-year interval between Rennie’s death and the book’s release suggests deliberate preparation rather than hasty composition—time enough to review seventeen years of accumulated documents, to select and arrange materials, to establish through careful ordering what direct polemic might have weakened. The date on the title page thus marked not merely completion but strategic opportunity seized.

The physical specifications of the published volume reinforced its documentary authority. William Blackwood, established Edinburgh publisher with strong connections to Scottish professional and scientific circles, lent institutional credibility that a London house might have complicated. The price of one guinea positioned the book within reach of engineers, commissioners, and educated readers without cheapening its claims through mass accessibility. The frontispiece engraving, showing the completed tower rising from its rocky base, offered visual confirmation of narrative achievement before reader encountered a single page of text. These material choices—publisher, price, illustration—constituted a rhetoric of their own, asserting that the account within deserved serious attention from serious men.

Stevenson’s preface, with its modest promise of documentary utility, deployed conventions of scientific writing that masked literary craft. The disavowal of literary ambition was itself literary device, establishing narrator as engineer concerned solely with practical record. Yet the prose that followed demonstrated considerable narrative skill in managing reader’s attention and inference. Chronological structure, apparently simple organizing principle, actually imposed interpretive framework through division into four seasons. Each section opened with obstacles—weather, tide, material shortage, labor difficulty—and closed with progress measured in courses laid, feet of elevation gained, days of productive work tallied against days lost. This rhythm of difficulty and advance created momentum carrying reader toward inevitable completion, with Stevenson as constant presence guiding each transition.

The treatment of technical innovation revealed careful management of intellectual property claims. Stevenson’s description of the interlocking granite courses explained the system of dovetail joints and iron bolts with precision sufficient to guide subsequent construction, yet avoided explicit assertion of design authorship. The text noted that “the principle of dovetailing the stones together was adopted from the Eddystone Lighthouse,” acknowledging Smeaton’s precedent, then described “several improvements” incorporated into the Bell Rock execution without specifying their origin. This formulation allowed reader to infer Stevenson’s creative contribution while providing deniability against charges of self-promotion. The passive construction—“improvements were made,” “modifications were introduced”—distributed agency ambiguously between consulting engineer and resident superintendent, preserving formal deference while implying substantive direction.

The narrative’s handling of the 1808 beacon construction exemplified this technique of implied authorship. Stevenson devoted extended description to the six-legged wooden structure that would house workmen through subsequent seasons, explaining its prefabrication at Arbroath, its transport and assembly on the rock, its strategic positioning to withstand winter gales. The beacon represented crucial innovation, transforming Bell Rock from inaccessible reef to habitable workplace. Yet the text described its design as emerging from practical necessity rather than individual conception: “it became necessary to erect a temporary beacon,” “the plan adopted was,” “the structure consisted of.” Reader encountered completed solution without explicit architect, though every detail of description testified to Stevenson’s intimate knowledge of its construction. The beacon appeared as inevitable response to circumstances, with Stevenson’s management as natural expression of circumstances rather than decisive intervention.

This narrative method extended to financial and administrative matters, where Stevenson’s account demonstrated command of resources without claiming credit for their acquisition. The text recorded parliamentary authorization of £15, 000 in 1806, subsequent supplemental grants, careful accounting of expenditures against progress achieved. These passages established Stevenson’s reliability as steward of public funds, a qualification essential to continued Board employment and future commissions. They also, implicitly, established his responsibility for success: the man who controlled expenditure was the man who controlled outcome. Rennie’s role in securing initial authorization, in parliamentary negotiations, in Board politics, received no mention.

The physical book itself, with engraved frontispiece and careful typography, embodied this constructedness. It was not a manuscript journal, spontaneously produced in the aftermath of achievement. It was a manufactured object, produced through the same institutional channels—Board, publisher, network of professional readers—that had enabled the lighthouse’s construction. Its publication in 1824 marked the completion of a process begun with the first foundation stone: the transformation of a precarious, collaborative, materially contingent enterprise into a stable narrative of individual authorship. The tower on Bell Rock had been built against the sea. The book describing it was built against time, against the fading of memory and the competing claims of the dead. Both structures required patience, precision, and a willingness to work within severe constraints. Both, in the end, achieved a permanence their builders could not have fully anticipated.

When Northern Lighthouse Board met in 1825 to plan next major project, Skerryvore reef in Hebrides, they turned naturally to author of definitive account of how such work was done. Stevenson’s narrative had successfully begun to harden into accepted history.
