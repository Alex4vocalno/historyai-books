Chapter 20

The Keeper of the Workmen’s Roll

On the morning of February 1, 1811, Peter Logan prepared a final memorandum, closing the daily roll he had kept for the artificers on the Bell Rock. The ledger was a single sheet ruled in columns, recording who rose, who fell ill, who received the ration of lemon juice, and which hour the tide permitted labor. The lighthouse would be lit that day, a date fixed since autumn, but what remained to be settled was not masonry. It was bookkeeping: the final reckoning of men, tools, provisions, and time. The work that would outlast Robert Stevenson’s four summers on the rock began on paper, in the beacon house and the moored tender, where the proof of what had been done, by whom, and at what cost, lived.

The roll was a single sheet, ruled in columns, that covered one week. Down the left margin ran the names of the artificers: masons, smiths, sailors, the cook, the two pilots. Across the top, the days of the week, each subdivided by tide times. The entries were spare. One line might read that W. Smith was absent with fever. Another noted that J. Dove worked at the forge for four hours. A third recorded that gales prevented any landing at all. The handwriting changed when Logan’s quill tired or when the clerk from the Smeaton took his place. The paper itself bore the stains of salt and coal smoke, and at the edges where it had been folded for passage to the mainland, the fiber had worn thin.

The roll was not a diary. The Northern Lighthouse Board had authorized the beacon house and the tender on condition that Stevenson account for every shilling spent and every man employed, and the roll served that audit. It served something else as well. Eleven miles from the nearest harbor, with the rock submerged twenty hours of each day, the sixty men who worked the site had no recourse to law, to church, to the ordinary structures of authority. The roll became the structure. It recorded the distribution of beef and biscuit, the rotation of sleeping berths, the punishment of men who drank. When a mason challenged Stevenson’s command, the foreman’s entry marking a dispute as settled closed the matter as effectively as a magistrate’s warrant. The paper regime substituted for the absent state.

Logan had begun keeping the roll in 1807, the first season, when the men still slept aboard the Smeaton and worked from boats. He was a mason by trade, promoted to foreman because he could read and write, because he had worked with Stevenson at the Pentland Skerries, and because he possessed the temperament to count and remember while the sea rose. The roll from that first summer shows the learning curve. The columns wander. Weather observations intrude into the space reserved for work done. The names of men appear with variant spellings, as if Logan had never asked how a man spelled himself. By the second season, the entries had tightened. The roll had become a system.

The system had a purpose beyond survival. It created a continuous thread of evidence connecting the rock to Edinburgh. Every fortnight, when the weather allowed, a boat carried the sheets to Arbroath and thence by post to the Board. Stevenson could thus manage the works from the rock while justifying his decisions to commissioners who had never seen the reef. When he ordered the construction of the beacon house in 1808—a wooden fortress on six legs, raised above the highest spring tides—he cited the roll’s record of lost time to prove that the Smeaton’s cramped quarters were costing the project more than the new structure would. The Board approved the expense because the paper demonstrated its necessity.

The beacon house changed everything. Where the Smeaton had been a ship, moored and rolling, the beacon was fixed to the rock itself. It contained a kitchen, a sleeping room for the artificers, a workshop for the smith, and a signal mast for communication with the tender. Logan moved his headquarters there, and the roll moved with him. The entries from this period show a new precision. The foreman could now observe the men continuously, could note the exact hour when the tide uncovered the work site, could track the consumption of candles and coal against the output of the forge. The beacon house was a factory built on a reef, and the roll was its time clock.

Francis Watt managed the supplies. A carpenter by training, he had expanded into the procurement and distribution of everything the works required. The roll recorded his deliveries: so many hundredweight of iron, so many gallons of whale oil for the lamps, so many pounds of soap. Watt’s own handwriting appears in the margins when Logan was absent, noting shortages and requisitions. Foreman and quartermaster mirrored each other through the paper, each confirming the other’s count. The enterprise ran on this ensemble: not the solitary engineer of legend, but a distributed system of observation and record, bound together by the daily practice of writing things down.

The roll’s most important column was the last. The remarks section occupied the right-hand edge of the sheet, a space reserved for entries that did not fit the other categories. Here Logan recorded sickness, injury, conflict, and the small human failures that accumulate when men live in confinement. W. Brown received lemon juice for scurvy. T. Anderson was reprimanded for insolence. A mason named Keith, his hand crushed by a crane, was sent back to the tender. These entries were brief, but they were not neutral. The distribution of lemon juice—an expensive preventive, imported from Mediterranean ports—went only to men whose symptoms Logan had judged severe enough to warrant the cost. A reprimand, a sending away, a note of insolence: these were decisions that shaped careers and, in the closed world of the rock, determined survival.

Stevenson read the roll. The materials do not specify how often, or in what detail, but the pattern of his own correspondence suggests continuous attention. When the Board questioned his management, he cited Logan’s figures. When he negotiated with contractors on the mainland, he used the roll’s record of productivity to set rates and deadlines. The paper was his armor against doubt. And when, after the lighthouse was finished, he came to write his Account of the Bell Rock Lighthouse, the roll provided the chronological spine of his narrative. He could place events to the day, sometimes to the hour, because the foreman had written them down.

The Account, published in 1824, is a work of administrative art. Stevenson does not merely describe the construction; he demonstrates it, through extracts from the roll, from his own letters, from the Board’s minutes. The reader follows the progress of the works through documents that were themselves instruments of that progress. This was not hindsight. Stevenson had understood from the first that the lighthouse would be judged by its paper trail as much as by its granite. The roll was the project in another form: solid, interlocking, built to withstand inspection.

But the roll also concealed. Its categories determined what could be recorded and what could not. The remarks column might note that a man had drowned when his boat overturned, but it could not capture the sound of that death, the particular silence that followed, the conversations in the beacon house that night. The roll counted hours of labor but not the quality of that labor, the ingenuity with which a mason fitted a course, the exhaustion that slowed a man’s hands in the fourth hour of a tide. A technology of abstraction, it transformed human effort into exchangeable units: days, shillings, courses laid. This abstraction was necessary to the enterprise. It was also, for the men whose names filled its columns, a form of erasure.

Consider the entry for September 1807, when the Smeaton broke her moorings and drifted away with the tide, leaving thirty-two men stranded on the reef. Logan’s record of that day—if he kept one—has not survived. Stevenson’s Account describes the event from memory, supplemented by the testimony of others. The roll resumes, in the surviving fragments, with the aftermath: the names of the men who remained, the distribution of reduced rations, the days of waiting until rescue. The gap in the record is itself significant. The system that claimed to document everything had failed, at the moment of greatest crisis, to document itself. The paper regime, like the wooden beacon it administered, was vulnerable to the sea.

The stranded men had lived for three days and nights on the rock, with no shelter but the incomplete beacon house and no food but what they had carried in their pockets. Stevenson, who was among them, later minimized the danger, noting that the weather remained calm and that the men had kept their discipline. But the roll’s silence suggests otherwise. For three days, no one counted. The categories of authority—foreman, clerk, engineer—had dissolved into the brute fact of survival. When the rescue boats finally arrived and the roll resumed, the entries had a new quality, tentative and detailed, as if Logan were reasserting control over a world that had proved its fragility.

The experience shaped the system. The beacon house that rose in 1808 was stronger than its predecessor, built to withstand gales that would have destroyed the earlier structure. The roll, too, acquired new columns. Logan began to record the state of the moorings, the condition of the boats, the weather forecasts received from the mainland. The paper regime learned from its failure, becoming more comprehensive, more defensive, more determined to anticipate the sea’s violence. The pattern held for the whole enterprise: each crisis generated not retreat but elaboration, an expansion of the administrative apparatus that might prevent the next crisis or, failing that, document it for future justification.

The final season, 1810, brought a new intensity to the record-keeping. The tower was rising above the beacon house now, the granite courses interlocking without mortar, the precision of the Arbroath yard tested against the irregularities of the rock. Logan’s roll tracked the delivery of each stone, the name of the mason who set it, the hour when the tide forced a halt. The entries grew longer, more detailed, as if the foreman sensed that he was recording history. And in a sense he was. The tower that rose from his entries would outlast every man who built it. The roll was the only account they would leave.

Stevenson understood this. In his winter preparations for that final season, he had allocated space in the stores for anvils and bellows alongside the masons’ food, but he had also arranged for additional paper, additional ink, additional hands to copy and collate the records. The lighthouse would be lit on February first. The proof of its construction would be complete by then, ready for submission to the Board, ready for the judgment of posterity. The work of building and the work of documentation would end together.

The men who filled the roll were not passive subjects of this regime. They learned its categories, its vulnerabilities, its possibilities for advantage. A mason who knew that Logan recorded sickness could time his ailment to coincide with the worst weather, ensuring dry quarters in the beacon house while others worked in the spray. A sailor who understood the importance of the mooring entries could exaggerate the danger of a frayed rope, securing new equipment and lighter duties. The roll was a language, and the men spoke it with varying degrees of fluency. Not resistance, exactly, but accommodation: the adaptation of human desire to administrative form.

But the form was coercive. The roll determined who ate, who slept, who was sent away. It translated physical presence into legal status: a man recorded was a man employed, entitled to wages and rations; a man omitted, for whatever reason, risked destitution. The foreman’s pen could erase as well as inscribe. Stevenson’s system depended on this power. The isolated worksite, the irregular tides, the dangerous labor: all required a discipline that could not rely on the ordinary incentives of free labor. The roll supplied that discipline, making the men visible to authority and therefore controllable.

The cost was human. The entries for sickness cluster in the winter months, when the cold and damp of the beacon house bred fever and rheumatism. The scurvy cases, marked by the distribution of lemon juice, suggest a diet inadequate to the labor demanded. The entries for insolence and dispute hint at conflicts that the roll’s brevity cannot capture—anger, perhaps, or despair, or the small brutalities of men confined together beyond the reach of law. Stevenson, in his Account, acknowledges none of this. The roll, in his hands, becomes a record of triumph, its human costs absorbed into the narrative of engineering progress.

Yet the costs are there to be read. The roll’s very comprehensiveness—its determination to record everything—preserves what it cannot interpret. One entry notes that J. McDonald suffered fever for fourteen days and returned weak. Another records that a mason named Wilson injured his hand with a pickaxe and claimed compensation. A third states that W. Thomson drowned, his body not recovered. These are not statistics. They are the traces of individual lives, compressed into the form that the system required. The historian who reads the roll against Stevenson’s Account can recover something of what the official narrative suppressed: the fragility of the enterprise, the vulnerability of the men, the dependence of the whole structure on human bodies that could sicken, break, and die.

The final entries, from January 1811, show the system at its most elaborate. Logan recorded the installation of the lantern, the testing of the lamps, the training of the keepers who would maintain the light. The roll expanded to include new categories: the consumption of oil, the rotation of the light, the state of the glass. The lighthouse was becoming its own administrative entity, no longer a construction site but a permanent installation, requiring permanent record-keeping. The foreman’s work would continue, though Stevenson would depart. The paper regime had outlasted the engineer who created it.

On February first, the light was lit. The roll for that day—if it was kept—has not survived in the fragments that remain. Stevenson’s Account describes the event in his own voice, without documentary support. The lighthouse, he writes, exhibited a steady and brilliant light, visible from the coast of Fife, to the great satisfaction of all who had been engaged in the erection of the building. The satisfaction was real enough. But it was also, in part, the satisfaction of completion, of a system that had achieved its purpose and could now rest.

The project was finished. The paper remained.

In the Arbroath yard, the master masons had kept their own records: the drawings of each course, the templates for the granite blocks, the accounts of wages and materials. These documents, too, were gathered and preserved, bound into volumes that Stevenson would deposit with the Board. The lighthouse existed now in three forms: as a physical tower on the reef, as a set of administrative records in Edinburgh, and as a narrative in Stevenson’s Account. Each form supported the others. The tower proved the records accurate; the records justified the expense; the narrative gave meaning to both.

But the three forms were not identical. The tower bore the irregularities of its construction: the courses that did not quite match, the repairs made necessary by storm damage, the wear of twenty years of waves. The records smoothed these irregularities into a coherent progress, each difficulty overcome, each season advancing toward completion. The narrative went further, transforming the records into a story of individual genius and collective effort, with Stevenson at its center. The foreman’s roll, in this transformation, became supporting evidence, its authorship subordinated to the engineer’s design.

This was the mechanism of credit. The lighthouse had been built by a distributed system—men, tools, paper, tides—but its value, in the economy of reputation, required concentration. Someone must be named as responsible, as author, as genius. The dispute that would follow, between Stevenson and the consulting engineer John Rennie, concerned this concentration. Rennie had designed the tower’s foundation and advised on its construction; he claimed, or his supporters claimed, the principal credit for the work. Stevenson, armed with his Account and the foreman’s roll, argued for his own primacy. The documents would be decisive. They recorded who had been present, who had decided, who had managed the daily labor of the enterprise.

The roll, in this dispute, served a new function. No longer merely a tool of management, it became an instrument of adjudication. Its entries could be cited to prove Stevenson’s continuous engagement, his command of details, his personal direction of the works. The foreman’s handwriting, the clerk’s marginalia, the weather observations that only a resident could have made: all supported the claim that Stevenson had been not merely an overseer but the author of the lighthouse. The paper regime, created to manage risk, now managed reputation.

This transformation was not accidental. Stevenson had designed his record-keeping with posterity in mind. The roll’s comprehensiveness, its precision, its survival through the hazards of the reef: all served the future as much as the present. The engineer who understood that the lighthouse would be judged by its paper trail had also understood that he would be judged by the lighthouse. The foreman’s daily entries were, in this light, a form of self-documentation, the creation of evidence for a controversy that Stevenson anticipated but could not yet name.

The project’s complete documentary record now exists as a finished artifact. The volumes in the Board’s archives, the bound Account, the fragments of Logan’s roll preserved among Stevenson’s papers: these constitute a second lighthouse, built of paper rather than granite, more durable in some ways and more vulnerable in others. The tower on the reef would weather storms for two centuries. The documents would weather interpretation, citation, dispute. Logan had helped forge tools whose final use he could not have imagined. Those tools were about to be turned against an enemy Stevenson had not yet declared.
