Chapter 21

The Judgment of the Board of Inquiry

The ledger arrived at the Northern Lighthouse Board’s Edinburgh rooms in the spring of 1812, bound in calf and stamped with the date of the lighthouse’s completion. It was not a large book, perhaps two hundred pages of ruled paper, but its weight exceeded its physical mass. Inside lay the complete financial record of the Bell Rock construction: every wage paid to every workman, every invoice from the Arbroath yards, every disbursement for timber, iron, rope, and canvas across four years of intermittent labor. The Board’s clerk placed it on the table before the assembled Commissioners with the same care a court officer might exhibit when presenting evidence. The lamp had been lit on February first, 1811. Now the reckoning began.

Robert Stevenson understood what this moment required. He had spent the winter of 1811–1812 arranging his papers, copying his journals, ensuring that the documentary trail of his four years on the reef would survive scrutiny. The Board had authorized the audit in its March meeting, responding to a memorial Stevenson himself had submitted requesting a thorough investigation of the whole expenditure. This was not modesty. It was strategy.

The dispute with John Rennie had already escaped the private channel of their correspondence; it had appeared in engineering conversations in London, in the Edinburgh learned societies, in the newspapers that served the shipping interest. Rennie, the consulting engineer, continued to receive credit in some quarters for the design and execution of the work. Stevenson, the resident engineer who had lived on the rock through two hundred working tides, found this intolerable. He had learned from the reef itself that survival depended on preparation. The audit was his prepared ground.

The Commissioners who gathered to examine the ledger were men of commercial and maritime standing: shipowners, merchants, naval officers, the practical elite of Scotland’s eastern coast. They had authorized the lighthouse in 1806, after Stevenson’s original proposal had been shelved and then revived following the wreck of HMS York in 1804. They had released funds through the years of construction, had received Stevenson’s progress reports with their mixtures of technical detail and barely suppressed alarm. Now they faced a different kind of report. The Act of Parliament that had enabled the work required accountability; the Board’s own regulations demanded it. But beneath these formal obligations lay a question that no statute could frame precisely. Who had built the Bell Rock lighthouse? Whose mind had conceived it, whose hand had executed it, whose authority had carried it through the disasters and improvisations of its making?

The mechanism of official judgment began with the simplest of procedures: the matching of records. The clerk read aloud from the workmen’s rolls, compiled by Peter Logan, the clerk of works whose daily entries had tracked every hour of labor on the reef. These figures were checked against Stevenson’s journals, which recorded expenditures and the conditions under which they had been incurred: the tides that permitted work, the gales that suspended it, the accidents that interrupted it. The rolls showed that John Bonnyman, the stonemason, had lost a finger to the beam crane in September 1808; they showed also that he had been compensated with appointment as one of the first lighthouse keepers. This was not abstract bookkeeping. It was the conversion of physical sacrifice into institutional memory.

Against these records of presence and labor, the Board set the documents that Rennie had generated from his London office. There were his original reports on Stevenson’s proposed design, dating from 1806, approving the general scheme and the cost estimate that had secured parliamentary authorization for what became known as the Cape Rock Lighthouse (Scotland) Act 1806. There were his invoices for consultations, for drawings, for the periodic visits he had made to the site—four in total across the construction period, each brief, each occurring when weather permitted a landing. The Board’s secretary arranged these papers in chronological order, creating a visual narrative of distance and proximity. Rennie’s handwriting appeared on sheets that grew increasingly occasional as the years advanced. Stevenson’s filled volume after volume.

The comparison was not, in strict terms, a competition. The Board had engaged both men for different functions. Rennie was the consulting engineer, retained for his established reputation and his mastery of the Eddystone precedent; Stevenson was the resident engineer, employed for his availability and his willingness to endure the reef.

Yet the distinction between these roles had proven unstable in practice. Stevenson had modified Rennie’s suggested foundation details after the first season’s experiments proved them impractical. He had designed the temporary beacon house without reference to London. He had improvised solutions to problems (pump failures, crane breakages, the September 1807 disaster when the Smeaton broke her moorings and stranded thirty-two men) that no consulting engineer could have addressed from a distance. The question before the Board was whether these improvisations constituted faithful execution of another man’s design, or the emergence of a design authority that the original arrangement had not anticipated.

Stevenson attended the Board’s sessions in person, as his position required. He answered questions about specific expenditures with the precision of a man who had kept his accounts for audit and for vindication. When asked about the eighty-two letters he had sent to Rennie in 1809—a documented strategy to ward off further visits—he explained them as necessary consultations on technical matters. The figure hung in the room. Eighty-two letters suggested either exhaustive collaboration or deliberate documentation, depending on how one interpreted the tone of their contents. The Board did not request to see the letters themselves. They were not, strictly speaking, financial records. But the number entered the minutes, where it would remain for future historians to assess.

Rennie did not attend. His health, always precarious, had declined in the years following the lighthouse’s completion; he would die in 1821, still engaged in major engineering works but increasingly removed from the controversies of his past projects. His son, also John Rennie, had begun to assume his father’s correspondence, and it was through this channel that the Rennie interest was represented. The younger Rennie submitted a memorial emphasizing the original design contribution, the structural improvements suggested by his father, the general superintendence that the consulting role had entailed. The phrase was carefully chosen. It claimed neither exclusive credit nor subordinate status, but a collaborative authority that the documentary record could not quite confirm or deny.

The Board’s deliberation extended across several meetings, a duration that itself indicated the difficulty of their task. They were not equipped to judge engineering questions in the abstract; they were equipped to assess whether moneys had been properly spent and whether the persons who had spent them had acted within their commissions. Yet the credit dispute forced them toward a judgment they had not sought. In their April session, they directed their secretary to prepare a formal statement of findings, addressing both the financial audit and the respective services of the engineers.

The mechanism of institutional judgment operated through a characteristic procedure: the reduction of complex human activity to categories that could be recorded and compared. The Board’s final report, completed in June 1812, allocated the work into phases. The design phase, it noted, had been initiated by Stevenson’s proposals, reviewed and approved by Rennie—a structure based upon John Smeaton’s Eddystone Lighthouse but with several improvements made by Stevenson. The construction phase had been executed by Stevenson with unremitting personal exertion and constant residence upon the spot. The financial phase, the audit’s proper territory, showed expenditures within the authorized totals, with some savings attributable to Stevenson’s management of the workyard and his negotiation with suppliers. The report’s language was measured, its conclusions distributed. It affirmed that the lighthouse had been designed under the direction of Mr. Rennie and executed under the immediate superintendence of Mr. Stevenson.

This formula satisfied no one completely, which may have been its purpose. Rennie received acknowledgment of his foundational role; Stevenson received recognition of his executive authority. The Board had avoided choosing between them, had in effect created a joint custody of the achievement. Yet the distribution of credit was not symmetrical. The phrase immediate superintendence carried weight in the engineering culture of the period. It implied management and invention, the continuous adjustment of plan to circumstance that distinguished the practicing engineer from the consulting theorist. Stevenson’s allies, and he had cultivated them carefully in the Edinburgh establishment, could read the report as vindication. Rennie’s partisans, and he had his own network in the London societies, could note that the design attribution remained intact.

The financial consequences of the judgment fell more clearly in Stevenson’s favor. The Board’s audit confirmed his accounts in full, authorizing final payment of his salary and expenses through the construction period. More significantly, it established a precedent for his future employment. When the Board considered its next major project, the lighthouse on the Skerryvore reef, even more remote and dangerous than the Bell Rock, it would turn to Stevenson without the formality of competitive consultation. The judgment had created a career.

Rennie’s compensation was also authorized, his invoices paid according to the terms of his engagement. But the audit revealed what his role had cost in comparative terms. His total remuneration, while substantial, represented a fraction of the expenditure on resident labor. The proportions were not in themselves discrediting; they were, indeed, standard for consulting arrangements. Yet they entered a record that could be consulted, compared, cited. In the accounting of credit, as in the accounting of money, relative magnitude carries meaning.

The Board’s judgment did not conclude the dispute. It crystallized it, gave it a documentary form that would outlast the immediate controversy. Stevenson preserved the report among his papers, referenced it in subsequent correspondence, would eventually incorporate its findings into the narrative he published in 1824—an account that gave little or no credit to Rennie. Rennie’s memorials entered his family’s archive, to be consulted by his biographers and defenders. The two documentary trails ran parallel, never quite intersecting, each sustaining a version of the achievement that the other could not fully acknowledge.

What the Board had created, in its deliberate institutional fashion, was a template for historical memory. The judgment established that the Bell Rock lighthouse could not be attributed to a single author, that its making had involved a distribution of labor and intelligence that resisted heroic simplification. This was not the conclusion either protagonist had sought. Both Stevenson and Rennie had advanced claims to comprehensive authority; both had framed their contributions in ways that minimized the other’s essential role. The Board’s refusal to choose between them, its insistence on a collaborative verdict, reflected caution and accuracy. The lighthouse had been built by a system, not a person: by the interaction of resident and consulting engineers, of clerks and workmen, of parliamentary authorization and tidal opportunity.

Yet systems do not write history. Persons do, and the persons most capable of writing, those with access to archives, to publication, to the networks of learned society, shape the record that survives. Stevenson understood this with the clarity of a man who had spent four years watching the sea erase his daily progress. He began, in the years following the Board’s judgment, to assemble the materials for his own account: his journals, the workmen’s rolls, the Board’s minutes, his correspondence with Rennie. The official verdict provided a foundation; his narrative would build upon it, extending its recognition of his immediate superintendence into a more comprehensive claim to authorship.

The mechanism of power that the Board’s inquiry exemplified, institutional judgment as the adjudication of personal dispute, operated with a characteristic delay. The audit of 1812 addressed expenditures incurred years before; its findings would influence reputations for decades after. This temporal displacement was inherent in the nature of administrative process, which must wait for events to conclude before it can assess them, and which generates records that outlast the circumstances of their creation. The Board’s clerk, copying the final report into the minute book, was producing a document that would be consulted in 1824, in 1850, in the scholarly controversies of a later century.

The immediate consequence of the judgment was more concrete. Stevenson received his final payment in August 1812, a substantial sum that recognized his salary and the risks he had assumed. The workmen whose rolls the Board had audited received their wages according to the established rates; Bonnyman and his fellow keepers began their service in the completed tower. The lighthouse itself, visible from the shipping lanes eleven miles distant, performed its function without reference to the disputes of its making. Its light swept the horizon; its mechanism turned; its keepers recorded the weather and maintained the apparatus. The achievement had become independent of its authors.

This independence was, in a sense, the ultimate judgment. The Board’s inquiry had been necessary because the lighthouse’s completion had not settled the question of who had built it. The structure’s continued operation would gradually render that question historical rather than practical, a matter of record and reputation rather than of employment and payment. Yet the historical question mattered intensely to the men who had staked their identities upon the work. Stevenson and Rennie were not merely disputing credit; they were negotiating their places in a profession that was itself being defined through such projects. The consulting engineer and the resident engineer, the designer and the executant, the theorist and the practitioner: these categories were not fixed. They were being established through contests like this one, through the judgments of boards and the narratives of participants.

The Board’s formula, designed under the direction of Mr. Rennie and executed under the immediate superintendence of Mr. Stevenson, would be quoted, analyzed, challenged, and defended for years to come. It entered the literature of engineering as a case study in collaborative attribution. It informed the practices of the Northern Lighthouse Board in its subsequent projects, encouraging clearer definition of roles at the outset to avoid the disputes that retrospective judgment could not fully resolve. And it provided Stevenson with a documented basis for the more comprehensive claim he would advance in his 1824 Account, where the Board’s recognition of his immediate superintendence would be extended into a narrative of comprehensive authority.

The mechanism of institutional judgment had not concluded the controversy. It had transformed it, given it a form that could be perpetuated and contested through subsequent documentary production. The audit of 1812 was itself a kind of construction, an assembly of records into a structure that would endure. Like the lighthouse, it required maintenance; like the lighthouse, it would be subject to the erosions of time and the interventions of those who used it. Stevenson’s preservation of his papers, his eventual publication of his narrative, his grandson’s later defense of his reputation: these were the keepers of the documentary lighthouse, ensuring that its light continued to reach across the distance between the event and its historical record.

The Board’s clerk filed the completed audit in the summer of 1812, closing the ledger with a final entry that balanced expenditure against authorization. The physical object, calf binding, ruled pages, inked figures, was placed in the Board’s archive, where it remains. Its contents had been copied into the minutes, summarized in the annual report, distributed to the Commissioners for their approval. The procedure was complete. Yet the document’s true work was only beginning. It would be consulted by Stevenson as he prepared his Account, by Rennie’s defenders as they composed their memorials, by later historians attempting to reconstruct the making of the lighthouse from the records that survived. The judgment of 1812 had established a verdict, but it had also established a contested terrain, a field of documentation within which the struggle for historical credit would continue long after the original participants had ceased to dispute it.

The lighthouse stood. The lamp burned. The papers accumulated. And the mechanism of power that the Board’s inquiry represented, institutional procedure as the arbiter of personal achievement, continued to operate, converting the fluid experiences of construction into the fixed forms of record and judgment. Stevenson had sought this conversion, had prepared for it through four years of meticulous documentation. He understood, as the reef had taught him, that survival in a hostile environment required effort and evidence. The Board’s audit provided the official recognition; his own subsequent narrative would provide the enduring claim. Between these two productions, the judgment of 1812 occupied a crucial position: the moment when institutional authority confirmed what individual documentation had prepared, establishing a documented verdict that would shape all subsequent histories, including the one Stevenson himself would publish.

That verdict arrived at a particular cost. The Board’s formula, with its careful balance of design and execution, had required the reduction of four years of improvisation, danger, and daily decision to a pair of phrases that could be entered in minutes and cited in disputes. The men who had worked the rock, who had lost fingers and hearing and months of their lives to the tidal regime, appeared in the record only as figures in rolls, as entries to be audited and approved. Bonnyman’s amputation was noted because it had occasioned a pension; the other injuries, the exhaustion, the psychological toll of the beacon house months, left no trace in the documents that survived. The mechanism of institutional judgment was also a mechanism of selective memory, preserving what served the interests of accountability and credit while discarding what did not fit its categories.

Stevenson, in his subsequent narrative, would partially restore this human dimension, describing the conditions of work with a vividness that the Board’s minutes could not accommodate. Yet his restoration was itself selective, shaped by the same imperatives that had governed the audit. The workmen appeared in his Account as participants in a heroic undertaking, their labor subordinated to the narrative of engineering achievement. The Board’s judgment had established the framework within which this narrative would operate: the lighthouse as a triumph of professional authority, its making a demonstration of what resident supervision could accomplish when directed by adequate design. The human cost of that accomplishment, the toll extracted from bodies and spirits by the tidal discipline, remained at the margins of the record.

The Bell Rock lighthouse, near the entrance to the Firths of Forth and Tay, was built during 1807 and 1810. Rennie was, by some, credited with the design and execution—a claim that would be championed by popular engineering author Samuel Smiles—but there seems little doubt that he was only nominally responsible for the great undertaking. Robert Stevenson, on the other hand, was continuously present, directing every detail of the work through its most perilous phases. This assessment, which would be repeated and elaborated in subsequent accounts, drew its authority from the documentary foundation that the Board’s audit had established. The judgment of 1812 had not merely settled a dispute; it had created the evidentiary basis for a historical narrative that would outlast both participants.

The final entry in the audit ledger, made by the Board’s clerk on a summer afternoon in 1812, recorded a balance: funds authorized, funds expended, funds remaining. The figures were correct; the arithmetic was sound; the Commissioners affixed their signatures. Yet the document that resulted from this procedure was something more than a financial statement. It was a claim upon the future, a bid to shape how the achievement would be remembered. Stevenson, who had prepared the ground for this claim with four years of journals and letters and rolls, understood its value. He collected his copies of the minutes, his authorization of payment, his recognition by the Board, and placed them in the archive from which he would later construct his Account. The mechanism of power had operated; its product was now his to use.

The Board’s judgment provided an official verdict, but it also crystallized the contested narrative into a form that would now shape all subsequent histories, including Stevenson’s own 1824 Account.

The Northern Lighthouse Board’s verdict was not its last word on the matter. When Stevenson died in 1850, the Commissioners would put on record in their minutes that to him was ‘due the honour of conceiving and executing the Bell Rock lighthouse.’ But Rennie’s son, Sir John Rennie, would continue to claim that his father’s advice entitled him to rank it among his own achievements. The judgment of 1812 had established a template for historical memory; it could not prevent its future contestation.
