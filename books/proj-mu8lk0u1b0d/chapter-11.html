Chapter 11

The Race Against the Equinox

The pressure is now structural: the second course would have to follow. The first granite blocks, laid in June 1810, had proved that the tower could rise. But proof was not progress. Each course above would sit upon the one below, and each required the same precision, the same tidal window, the same convergence of stone, men, and still water. August had come, and with it a fact that no engineer could alter: the equinox approached, and after it, the winter gales that had smashed the earlier wooden beacon in 1808 and nearly killed the men upon it.

Late in August, a squall caught a loaded boat halfway between the Smeaton and the reef. The vessel had left the ship with a cargo of cut granite, the stones lashed in netting for the hoist. The wind came from the east without warning, as it often did eleven miles offshore, where no landmass broke the fetch of the North Sea.

The crew faced a choice that would repeat itself through the remaining weeks of the season: jettison the load and save the boat, or hold course and risk swamping. They held the stones. Water shipped over the gunwales. The men bailed with their hands and whatever containers they could seize. The boat survived, but the incident entered the record as more than a near-miss. It demonstrated that the margin between progress and loss had narrowed to a single decision, made in seconds, without consultation with Stevenson on the Smeaton or Dove on the rock.

The 1810 campaign had begun with ambitions that now looked like liability. Stevenson had planned to raise multiple courses before the weather turned. The first course sat secure on its leveled bed, the interlocking granite proving the value of the Arbroath workyard’s exacting preparation. But height multiplied difficulty. Each successive course required longer hoisting, more complex rigging, and greater exposure of men and materials to the open water between ship and reef. The tide still allowed only two hours of work, sometimes less when the swell ran high. The men still rowed from the Smeaton to the rock, still climbed onto wet granite streaming with weed, still worked until the water rose around their knees and forced them back to the boats.

Francis Watt had joined the masons that season. He was not a young man, and he had worked stone long enough to know that speed and precision were rarely compatible. The Bell Rock demanded both. The granite blocks arrived from Arbroath with their dovetail joints already cut, each course designed to lock into the one below without mortar. Watt’s job was to guide the lowering, to check the fit, to ensure that the horizontal joints were true. A single misaligned block would compromise every course above it. The cost of error was not merely material. It was the time required to lift, recut, and relay—a time that the season no longer possessed.

The work rhythm had intensified beyond what the first season had established. Aboard the Pharos, which had replaced the original lightship, crews now worked in overlapping shifts. Men snatched meals between tides, slept in the cramped quarters when sleep was possible, and woke to the call of the watch marking the ebb. The beacon house, the six-legged wooden structure that Stevenson had erected in 1808, served as the forward base. It was safer than the open rock, but not safe. Gales that would have sent the men back to the Smeaton in earlier seasons now pinned them inside, the structure shaking on its iron stanchions, the sea breaking against the legs below.

Stevenson managed this compression through James Dove, his deputy on the rock. Dove had authority to decide which operations to attempt as the working window contracted. Each morning, or what passed for morning in the perpetual calculation of tide tables, Dove assessed the sea state, the visibility, the condition of the men who had slept—or not—through the hours of darkness. He sent word to the Smeaton, or received Stevenson’s instructions, or made the call himself when communication failed. The chain of command was clear in design but frayed in execution. Stevenson retained ultimate responsibility, yet he was often a mile away, dependent on Dove’s judgment and the willingness of men like Watt to work through conditions that would have halted any shore-based construction.

The shortening days were not merely atmospheric. They reduced the number of tides that could be worked in daylight, and daylight mattered. Lanterns could extend operations into dusk, but not into the full dark of an August night at latitude 56 degrees north. The masons needed to see the joints, to test the fit with their hands and eyes, to ensure that the granite settled true. Each lost hour of sun was a lost tide, and each lost tide was a course unlaid, a week of work that could not be recovered.

The storms came more frequently as September advanced. A squall on the twenty-third pinned the boats to the Smeaton for thirty hours, the men watching the reef disappear and reappear through curtains of rain. Another on the first of October drove the Pharos from her station, forcing a search that consumed another day and night before she was found riding to sea anchors twenty miles to the northeast. These were not yet the equinoctial gales, but they warned of what was coming. Stevenson knew the pattern from his earlier lighthouse work, from the Pentland Skerries in 1798 and the endless waiting for weather that would not come. The difference at Bell Rock was the investment already made, the first course standing proof that completion was possible, and the impossibility of resuming in spring with the structure half-built and exposed to the winter’s full violence.

The psychological strain on the men in the beacon house became, by October, a factor in its own right. Francis Watt and his fellow masons lived in a wooden box six feet above the rock, listening to the sea surge beneath them. The structure had been designed to withstand the conditions, and it had survived two winters. But survival was not comfort. Gales shook the frame until the ironwork sang. Sleep came in fragments, between the noise and the cold and the constant awareness that the sea surrounded them, waiting. The men who had chosen this work—chosen it for the wages, for the challenge, for the prospect of being named in the achievement—now faced the accumulated cost of that choice.

Stevenson’s own position had shifted with the progress of the work. In 1807 and 1808, he had been the promoter of a project that many considered impossible. The Board’s minutes from those years show repeated requests for funds, for patience, for authority to proceed. By 1810, he was the custodian of a project that had proved its feasibility and now demanded its completion. The first course changed the political calculus. It demonstrated that the tower could rise, that the Arbroath preparation had been worth the expense, that the engineering was sound. But it also committed the Board to finishing what had been started. A half-built lighthouse was worse than none at all: a navigation hazard, an advertisement of failed ambition, a standing charge against the account without the benefit of the light.

John Rennie’s correspondence from this period, preserved in the Board’s papers, shows the consulting engineer pressing for information and offering advice that Stevenson accepted or modified according to conditions on the rock. The division of authority remained unresolved in the documents, as it would remain unresolved in the historical record. Rennie had approved the design and cost estimate in 1806. He had visited the site in 1808. But he was not present for the daily decisions of 1810, the choice to hoist or wait, to send the boats or hold them, to trust the beacon house in a rising wind or evacuate to the Smeaton. Stevenson made those choices, or delegated them to Dove, and bore the consequences.

The near-disasters multiplied as the season compressed. A stone slipped during hoisting on the eighth of September, crashing back into the boat that had carried it and splintering the gunwale. No one was injured, but the incident halted work for a tide while the damage was repaired and the stone inspected for cracks. A boat did swamp on the twenty-ninth, in sight of the reef, the men clinging to the overturned hull until the Smeaton’s launch reached them. The granite was lost, the work delayed, the men returned soaked and shaken to quarters where no dry clothes awaited them.

These incidents accumulated in the record as they accumulated in experience. The Board’s minutes for October 1810 show Stevenson reporting progress in courses laid and stones landed, but also requesting additional funds for damaged boats, for medical attendance, for the replacement of a foreman who had collapsed from exhaustion. The human cost was becoming visible in the accounts, translated into pounds sterling and days lost. The mechanism of the project—its dependence on tidal discipline, on the physical capacity of men to work in wet cold and interrupted sleep, on the luck of weather that remained fundamentally beyond control—was producing outcomes that no planning could prevent.

The shortening days brought a final pressure that shaped the closing weeks of the season. Each course laid required the one below it to have cured, or settled, or simply to have been verified. The interlocking design meant that errors propagated upward; a joint that was tight at course two would bind at course four, or fail to seat at all. Watt and his fellow masons worked with increasing deliberation even as the available time decreased. The contradiction was not resolvable. It could only be managed, day by day, tide by tide, with Dove judging when the conditions allowed precision and when they demanded haste, and with Stevenson on the Smeaton weighing the reports from the rock against the reports from the sky.

By mid-October, the decision to cease work for the season was no longer a choice but a recognition. The equinox had passed, the gales were establishing their autumn pattern, and the tower stood at a height that would have to suffice. Stevenson ordered the securing of the site: the removal of tools and temporary works, the sealing of the beacon house against the winter, the final departure of the Smeaton for Arbroath. The record shows twelve courses laid, a structure rising fifteen feet above the rock, proof of concept transformed into partial achievement.

The men who left the reef that October carried with them the knowledge of what remained. The tower was not half-built; the difficult work of the higher courses, the lantern stage, the lighting apparatus, all lay ahead. But they had also carried, many of them, physical damage that would not heal with rest. John Bonnyman’s finger, amputated in 1808 after a crane accident, was the most visible marker of the project’s cost, but not the only one. The men who had slept in the beacon house through the gales of September had absorbed a strain that would manifest in later years, in shortened lives, in the silence that working men maintained about hardship they had chosen to endure.

Stevenson’s own reckoning came in the form of a report to the Board, composed in the weeks after the Smeaton’s return. He documented the courses laid, the stones prepared for the following season, the repairs required to the boats and the beacon house. He also, in language more reserved than his private correspondence would justify, noted the difficulty of the work and the risk of continuing beyond the point where safety and precision could be maintained. The report was designed to secure continued funding and authority for the final campaign. It was also, though Stevenson would not have framed it thus, an account of power exercised under constraint: the power to mobilize men and materials, to extract work from the sea’s brief mercy, to convert tidal discipline into stone that would outlast the lives of its builders.

The season ended with work secured but unfinished. The tower stood visible from the Smeaton’s deck, a geometric intrusion on the natural horizon, promising completion and demanding it. Stevenson had the winter to plan the final push: the courses that would bring the light within reach, the apparatus that would make the structure functional, the withdrawal of men and equipment that would mark the project’s end. He also had the winter to consider what the season had revealed about the nature of the work itself. The race against the equinox had not been won; it had been survived. The cost of that survival was recorded in the Board’s accounts, in the medical receipts and the replacement of boats, in the silence of men who would return to the rock in spring knowing what awaited them.

The Arbroath workyard waited with its stock of granite, the stones cut and numbered for courses still unlaid. The quay there, built of uncemented stones in a broad arc against the open sea, had served its purpose through three seasons of loading and departure. It would serve again. The foundation stone of the new harbor, laid by the Marquess of Downshire in 1821, was still eleven years in the future; the quay itself would be removed for local wall building about 1833, appearing in the drawing of 1832 but vanished from the first Ordnance Survey map of 1834. These later facts do not alter the condition of 1810, but they frame it. The temporary structures of great projects disappear. What remains is the tower, and the record of how it was raised.

Stevenson spent the winter of 1810–11 in calculation and preparation. The Board’s minutes show him requesting funds for a larger complement of masons, for improved hoisting gear, for the final shipment of granite required to complete the structure. The requests were granted, or modified, or questioned, in the ordinary negotiation of institutional power. What the minutes do not show, because it was not recorded in that form, was the personal reckoning that the season had imposed. Stevenson had staked his reputation on a project that many had considered impossible. He had proved the impossibility wrong. He had not yet proved that the project could be finished.

The tower stood fifteen feet above the Bell Rock, locked granite rising from a reef that remained underwater for twenty hours of every day. The men who had laid those courses had done so in conditions that no shore-based construction could match, and they had done so under a discipline that extracted maximum effort from minimum time. The human cost of that extraction was not yet fully visible. It would become so in the final season, when the height of the work multiplied the danger, and the completion of the tower brought with it the reckoning that Stevenson had deferred. For now, the structure waited, and the sea waited, and the men who would finish what had been started prepared to return to the rock they had learned to fear and to need.
