Chapter 14

The Dispute in Correspondence

In the archives of the Northern Lighthouse Board, a memorandum dated February 1812, bearing the seal of the consulting engineer John Rennie, was received and preserved. Its phrasing asserted an overarching design authority for the Bell Rock undertaking, claiming the conception, structural innovations, and successful completion as vindication of his engineering principles. The lighthouse’s structure was based upon the design of the Eddystone Lighthouse, built by John Smeaton, but Stevenson had made several improvements. Copies were dispatched to members of the Scottish engineering fraternity and to publications. The physical achievement was complete; what remained was the narrative of who had achieved it.

Stevenson was forty years old in that winter of 1812. He had spent four seasons on the reef, had directed the laying of granite courses in two-hour windows of tidal exposure, had adapted designs to conditions that no prior experience had prepared. The Board paid him as its engineer. It had not, however, secured his reputation against competing claims. He reached for his files and began to draft a reply that same morning, establishing the pattern of correspondence that would occupy three years and generate dozens of letters between the two engineers and the institution that employed them both.

The dispute that emerged was not personal in the ordinary sense. Stevenson and Rennie had worked together throughout the construction, had exchanged technical recommendations, had maintained the forms of professional courtesy that enabled collaboration between a salaried engineer and a consulting authority. What divided them was a fundamental question about the nature of engineering achievement. Rennie represented a model of professional authority derived from design: the engineer as architect, whose contribution consisted in conception and calculation, whose physical absence from the site marked elevated status. Stevenson represented a competing model: the engineer as manager, whose contribution consisted in adaptation and execution, whose presence on the reef was not incidental but essential. The Bell Rock had required both. The correspondence would reveal that neither model could comfortably accommodate the other in a single narrative of success.

Stevenson’s reply of March 1812 did not dispute Rennie’s role in approving the design. It disputed the nature of that role.

Rennie had visited the works twice during four seasons of construction. He had approved modifications suggested from the site. He had reviewed estimates and adjusted proportions. What he had not done, Stevenson insisted, was confront the daily reality of the reef: the drilling of foundation holes through seaweed and slime while the tide receded, the fitting of interlocking granite blocks by masons standing in water to their knees, the management of men whose lives depended on calculations of wind and current that no theory could predict.

Stevenson named the men who had executed the work under his direction. Peter Logan, the foreman who had directed the actual laying of stone. Francis Watt, the master mason who had cut the courses in Arbroath to tolerances that would allow assembly without mortar.

These were not abstractions. They were the individuals who had made the tower possible, and Stevenson had been among them, on the rock, in the boats, through the nights when the wooden beacon housed the work party above the returning sea.

Rennie responded with his own particulars. He cited the structural modifications he had imposed upon Stevenson’s original design, the calculations of stress and stability that had determined the tower’s final proportions, the professional reputation he had risked in lending his name to so hazardous an undertaking. He noted his study of the Eddystone Lighthouse, which Stevenson had adapted, and his prior consultations with the Board on other projects. He appealed to established convention: the consulting engineer was traditionally credited with the works he approved. The letters grew longer. The claims grew more precise. By 1813, both men were attaching supporting documents to their correspondence—extracts from Board minutes, copies of earlier letters, memoranda on specific technical decisions whose authorship they disputed.

The Northern Lighthouse Board found itself in an uncomfortable position. It had employed Stevenson as its engineer, had paid him for his services, had accepted his reports and recommendations throughout the construction. According to the later account of Stevenson’s grandson, Robert Louis Stevenson, the Board paid Stevenson alone when the lighthouse was completed. This suggested that an institutional judgment of contribution had already been rendered in one form. But institutional payment was not public reputation. The Board’s minutes from this period record no formal resolution of the dispute between its own engineer and the consulting authority whose approval had enabled the project. The Commissioners were gentlemen of property and shipping interest, not engineers. They could evaluate accounts and inspect finished works. They could not adjudicate competing claims to technical innovation.

What the Board could do was preserve the record. Its minutes accumulated the correspondence, noted the visits of both men to its Edinburgh chambers, recorded the testimonials submitted by workmen and captains and keepers. This archival function was not neutral. Every document entered into the minutes became available to subsequent inquiry, to historical judgment, to the grandson who would one day construct his own narrative from these materials. The Board’s clerks performed their duty without awareness that they were building a case. But the duty, performed consistently across years of dispute, created possibilities for future resolution that neither Stevenson nor Rennie could fully anticipate.

The correspondence gradually exposed a deeper question about the nature of engineering itself. Rennie claimed the future: the principles that would inform subsequent projects, the methodology that could be transferred to other sites, the professional standards that the Bell Rock had helped establish. Stevenson claimed the past: the specific decisions made on specific days, the men who had executed them, the conditions that had shaped their execution. Both claims were true. Both were incomplete. The letters revealed what neither man could fully acknowledge—that the lighthouse had been built by a system of collaboration so intricate that its disaggregation into individual contribution was itself a distortion, a necessary fiction imposed by the requirements of professional reputation and historical memory.

Stevenson’s letters from 1813 and 1814 grew increasingly detailed in their reconstruction of the construction seasons. He described the September 1807 morning when the tender Smeaton broke her moorings and drifted away, leaving thirty-two men on the reef with insufficient boats to carry them off. He described his own decision to remain with the stranded party, the calculations of tide and wind that determined their survival, the eventual rescue that arrived with hours to spare. He described the building of the wooden beacon in 1808, the six-legged structure that had allowed men to live on the rock through the working months, the adaptations required when winter storms proved more severe than anticipated. These were narratives of choice under pressure, of judgment exercised in conditions that no prior experience had prepared.

Rennie’s letters emphasized the structural principles that had made such adaptations possible. The interlocking granite courses, derived from Smeaton’s Eddystone design but modified according to his own calculations. The proportion of base to height, determined by his analysis of wind stress and wave impact. The decision to proceed with construction despite the hazards, which he had supported and which he now claimed as evidence of his professional courage. Rennie had not been present for the September 1807 stranding. He had not helped haul the beacon’s timbers up the reef in the two-hour windows of low water. But he had lent his name, his reputation, his engineering authority to a project that many had considered impossible. His claim rested on the magnitude of that risk and the vindication of his judgment in its success.

The dispute was, in this sense, a dispute over time itself. Rennie could point to the enabling legislation of 1806, to the approval that had made construction possible, to the principles that would outlast the particular conditions of the reef. Stevenson could point to the daily logs of construction, to the testimony of men who had worked under his direction, to the adaptations that had transformed approved designs into physical reality. The correspondence accumulated both kinds of evidence without resolving their competing claims. Each man constructed a narrative that made his contribution indispensable and the other’s supplementary. Neither narrative could accommodate the full complexity of what had occurred.

The institutional context shaped what could be claimed. The Northern Lighthouse Board was a public body, established by statute, responsible for the safety of shipping rather than the profit of shareholders. This meant that its engineers could not claim property in their innovations. The designs they produced, the methods they developed, belonged to the Board and, through the Board, to the public. What remained to be claimed was reputation: the acknowledgment by peers and posterity that one’s judgment had been decisive, one’s contribution essential. Reputation was not trivial. In an emerging profession without formal licensing or standardized training, it was the only currency that mattered. It determined who would be consulted, who would be employed, who would be remembered.

Stevenson understood this with increasing clarity as the correspondence proceeded. His letters to the Board grew more strategic, less defensive. He began to assemble materials that would support a comprehensive claim: the original design he had submitted in 1804, the modifications required by Rennie’s consultation, the further modifications imposed by conditions on the reef, the daily records kept by foremen and masons, the testimony of men who had worked under his direction. He was building a case for his indispensability. The argument was that Rennie could have approved a hundred designs and the lighthouse would not have been built; Stevenson, by his presence and adaptation, had made the approved design into a physical reality.

This claim had implications beyond the immediate dispute. It proposed a new model of engineering authority, one that located genius not in the conception alone but in the management of execution under impossible conditions. The Bell Rock had been built not by the application of established principles but by the invention of new methods in response to daily emergencies: the joggles cut to lock granite block to granite block, the iron rails laid to guide the courses, the entire system of tidal discipline that extracted construction from the sea’s own rhythm. These innovations belonged to no existing professional category. They required a new kind of recognition that the correspondence was attempting to establish.

Rennie, for his part, began to recognize the vulnerability of his position. His standing rested on consultation, on the authority of judgment exercised at a distance. But distance was also absence. He could not claim to have seen what Stevenson had seen, to have decided what Stevenson had decided in the moments when the tide turned and the wind rose and the granite courses had to be fitted or abandoned. His letters grew more insistent on the magnitude of his contribution, more detailed in their reconstruction of technical decisions, more explicit in their appeal to professional convention. The consulting engineer, he repeated, was traditionally credited with the works he approved. The Bell Rock should be no exception.

The appeal to convention revealed its own limitations. The profession of civil engineering was still emerging, its standards still contested, its hierarchy still uncertain. There was no established rule for allocating credit between the consulting engineer who approved a design and the resident engineer who executed it under conditions that transformed the design in execution. Rennie was attempting to establish such a rule by asserting it. Stevenson was attempting to establish a competing rule by documenting the actual process of construction. Both men understood that the Bell Rock would be a precedent, that its allocation of credit would shape subsequent allocations, that the correspondence was constructing the professional future as much as recording the professional past.

By late 1814, the exchange had reached an impasse. Neither man would concede priority. Neither could compel the other’s acknowledgment. The Northern Lighthouse Board continued to preserve the record without rendering judgment. The public, for the most part, remained unaware of the dispute. The lighthouse itself continued its nightly operation, its beam sweeping across the entrance to the Firths of Forth and Tay, its mechanism maintained by keepers who knew nothing of the correspondence in Edinburgh. The physical achievement was complete and, in its completeness, indifferent to the claims it had generated.

But the indifference was temporary. Stevenson had begun to plan a more comprehensive response. The correspondence had revealed what could be disputed; it had also revealed what could be documented. If Rennie could appeal to professional convention, Stevenson could appeal to the record itself: the letters, the minutes, the testimony of workmen, the daily logs of construction that he had preserved. The dispute in correspondence was preparation for a longer campaign, one that would extend beyond Rennie’s death in 1821 and Stevenson’s own death in 1850, into historical accounts written by men who had not been present for any of it.

The memorandum’s arrival in February 1812 was not, in retrospect, unexpected. Rennie had observed the growing public attention to the completed tower with the eyes of a man who understood how reputations were consolidated in the engineering press and the drawing rooms of Edinburgh and London. The Bell Rock represented the most ambitious offshore construction yet attempted in British waters, and its success offered a platform from which to assert the primacy of design-centered engineering. His letter to the Board therefore carried an assertion of contribution and an implication of hierarchy: the consulting engineer stood above the resident engineer in the chain of creative authority, much as the architect stood above the builder. The phrasing was careful, calibrated for subsequent quotation, designed to enter the permanent record as the definitive account of professional responsibility.

Stevenson’s recognition of this strategy shaped his response in ways that would become apparent only as the correspondence accumulated. Where Rennie wrote for posterity from the first, Stevenson wrote initially for the Board and only gradually learned to write for history. His March 1812 reply contained the raw materials of a counter-narrative—the naming of Logan and Watt, the inventory of daily hazards—but lacked the systematic documentation that would characterize his later submissions. The difference in rhetorical maturity between the two men reflected their different positions in the professional world. Rennie at sixty-two had spent decades cultivating reputation through publication and consultation; Stevenson at forty had spent his working life in the practical administration of lightkeeping and construction, with little occasion for public self-presentation. The correspondence would become, for him, a tutorial in the arts of professional advocacy.

The Board’s silence in the face of these competing submissions was itself a form of institutional speech. Its minutes recorded receipt of correspondence without recording evaluation, creating a documentary trail that preserved dispute without resolving it. This procedural neutrality served multiple purposes. It avoided the embarrassment of choosing between two men whose continued cooperation the Board might require for future projects. It protected the Board itself from charges of partiality toward its salaried employee or its consulting authority. And it acknowledged, perhaps unconsciously, that the Commissioners lacked the technical competence to adjudicate claims about engineering methodology. Their expertise lay in navigation, in commerce, in the political management of Scottish maritime interests. The technical transformation of granite into lighthouse exceeded their jurisdiction.

The surviving correspondence reveals how both engineers adjusted their strategies in response to this institutional vacuum. Rennie, finding that his initial assertion had not been formally endorsed, began to attach increasingly elaborate supporting documentation: copies of his 1806 recommendations, calculations of structural load, references to his prior consultations with Smeaton’s successors at the Eddystone. He was constructing a paper trail that could survive independent examination, that could be cited by allies and successors, that could outlast any particular institutional judgment. Stevenson, recognizing the effectiveness of this documentary multiplication, began to assemble his own archive. He requested copies of Board minutes that mentioned his service. He solicited letters from Peter Logan describing specific construction decisions. He preserved the daily journals kept by the master of the works, documents that recorded weather conditions, tidal states, and the progress of individual courses with a granularity that no consulting engineer’s papers could match.

The asymmetry between their documentary positions shaped what each man could claim. Rennie’s papers demonstrated authority; Stevenson’s papers demonstrated presence. When Stevenson described the morning of September 1807, he could cite the master of the tender Smeaton, the names of the thirty-two stranded men, the hour of the rescue. These particulars served a rhetorical function beyond their evidentiary value.

The irony was that their dispute would outlast their own versions of it. Rennie’s son, Sir John Rennie, would continue to assert his father’s priority in publications and professional forums. Stevenson’s grandson would construct his own narrative from the papers that his grandfather had assembled. The Northern Lighthouse Board, in its minutes of 1850, would finally record its judgment: that to Robert Stevenson was “due the honour of conceiving and executing the Bell Rock lighthouse.” But this judgment, rendered when both engineers were dead, could not resolve the fundamental tension that the correspondence had exposed. The lighthouse had been built by a system that required both conception and execution, both distant authority and present adaptation. The credit assignable to individuals was necessarily partial, a simplification imposed upon a collaboration that had been, in its actual occurrence, too complex for singular attribution.

Stevenson filed Rennie’s 1812 letter with his reply, and the exchange became the first entry in a dossier that would grow across the remaining decades of his life. He did not know, in 1814, that he would outlive his rival by nearly thirty years, that he would have time to refine and repeat his claims, that he would eventually publish his own comprehensive account. He knew only that the correspondence had established the terms of a conflict that would continue as long as the lighthouse stood, and that the weapons in this conflict were documents: the letters written, the minutes recorded, the testimony preserved. The mechanism turned. The light swept its arc across the water. The dispute over who had made it possible remained unresolved in the files, waiting for a judgment that would require another decade of preparation and another decade of writing before it could be delivered.
