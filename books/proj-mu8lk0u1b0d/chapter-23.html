Chapter 23

The Public Claim to the Work

The question of whose name would attach to what had been made had moved beyond private letters and the closed minutes of board meetings. The documents that each man had gathered through these years of reckoning would now be tested in open circulation, where professional reputation was made and where historical memory took its first fixed shapes.

Robert Stevenson sat at his desk in the Edinburgh offices of the Northern Lighthouse Board, the autumn of 1814 settling into the stone walls around him, and began to compose the first of what would become a series of official reports that carried his name alone. The act was deliberate, even surgical. He selected which facts to include, which to omit, and in what proportion. The daily working of the tides appeared in patient detail. He recorded the precise number of stones laid each season. The names of the foremen who had executed the interlocking courses filled his pages.

The initial consultation with John Rennie he mentioned with careful brevity. The approval of the design appeared as a procedural necessity, stemming from the 1806 Act passed after Stevenson had sent his design to the renowned engineer for validation. The consulting engineer’s two brief visits to the reef he noted in passing, as administrative formalities rather than creative contributions. The document was not a memoir. It was a claim staked in the language of institutional duty.

Stevenson had learned this method from the machinery he served. The Northern Lighthouse Board did not deal in sentiment. Its minutes recorded decisions, expenditures, and outcomes. Its annual reports consolidated these into a narrative of steady, collective purpose. What Stevenson understood, and what he now put into practice, was that the Board’s apparatus of record-keeping could be turned to personal account. By submitting his Bell Rock reports as the engineer’s required documentation, he ensured that his version of events entered the official archive with institutional authority behind it. The Board would circulate these reports to the Trinity Houses, to the Admiralty, to any member of Parliament who inquired about the extraordinary expense. Stevenson’s name would appear not as a petitioner seeking recognition, but as the designated voice of a completed public work.

The first such report, completed in late 1814, ran to forty printed pages. It opened with a history of the reef’s navigational hazards, drawn from the petitions of shipowners and the wreck records of the previous century. This established the problem as ancient and urgent, long predating any individual engineer.

It then moved to the legislative history: the act of 1806, the enabling of construction, the appointment of Stevenson as the Board’s engineer. Here Rennie appeared, but as a consultant whose approval had been sought and obtained, not as a partner in conception.

The bulk of the document concerned the execution: the moored ship, the wooden beacon, the courses of granite cut in the Arbroath yard to fit without mortar. Stevenson described the September morning in 1807 when the tender broke loose with thirty-two men on the reef, not as a tale of collective endurance, but as a test of the engineer’s organizational preparation. The stores had been laid in. The beacon had been stockpiled. The discipline of his arrangements had prevented disaster.

The lamp’s first lighting on 1 February 1811 received three paragraphs. The engineering particulars of the light apparatus, designed by Francis Watt, filled six.

The report was read aloud at the Board’s December meeting and entered into the minutes without dissent. No commissioner remarked on the distribution of credit. The document had accomplished its work silently, by structure rather than argument. Anyone consulting the Board’s records henceforth would find Stevenson’s name attached to every decision of consequence, his presence established at every critical juncture. The consulting engineer’s role had been acknowledged and simultaneously diminished, reduced to a procedural footnote in a story of sustained, on-site command.

John Rennie, in London, recognized the maneuver for what it was. At sixty-four, he occupied a different terrain. Where Stevenson had built his reputation within a single, specialized institution, Rennie had constructed a national practice across canals, bridges, docks, and waterworks. His name carried weight in the Royal Society, in the Society of Civil Engineers, in the correspondence columns of the agricultural and philosophical journals that were consolidating the new profession’s public identity. He did not need the Northern Lighthouse Board’s minutes to establish his standing. He had other platforms, and he began to use them.

In 1815, Rennie contributed a paper to the Philosophical Magazine on the principles of lighthouse construction. The Bell Rock appeared as one of several examples, but it appeared as his. The lighthouse upon the Bell Rock, he wrote, was executed upon a plan which he had furnished, and under his direction as consulting engineer. The phrasing was precise. Plan and direction claimed intellectual authorship; consulting engineer acknowledged the formal arrangement without conceding subordination. He noted the difficulties of the site, the twenty hours of daily submersion, the four summer seasons of construction. He did not mention Stevenson’s name. The paper was reprinted in the Edinburgh Philosophical Journal, circulating in the very city where Stevenson worked, and where the Board’s own reports were read by a narrower, specialized audience.

The asymmetry mattered. Rennie’s claims reached the emerging public sphere of engineering discussion, a sphere that was defining its heroes, its methods, its history. Stevenson’s claims remained enclosed within the administrative apparatus that had produced the work itself. Both men understood that posterity would draw upon both kinds of record. The question was which would predominate.

The conflict escalated through institutional channels before it reached print again. In 1816, the Royal Society of Edinburgh elected Stevenson a fellow, a recognition that placed him among the scientific establishment of his own city. The citation mentioned his improvements in lighthouse architecture, with particular reference to the Bell Rock. Rennie, already a fellow of the Royal Society of London, made no public response. But in his correspondence with professional colleagues, he began to speak more pointedly of the lighthouse as a demonstration of his own design principles: the interlocking stone, the hollow column, the economy of material that he had pioneered in his earlier works. The Bell Rock, he suggested to one correspondent, represented the extension of Eddystone’s lessons to a more formidable site, and Eddystone had been his teacher’s masterpiece, not Stevenson’s.

Stevenson could not match Rennie’s national reputation directly. What he could do was consolidate his institutional base. Through 1816 and 1817, he prepared a more comprehensive report on the Bell Rock, incorporating drawings, specifications, and a detailed cost accounting. This document, printed by the Board in 1818, ran to ninety pages and included engraved plates of the tower, the light apparatus, and the beacon house. It was the most elaborate technical publication the Board had yet produced. Where the 1814 report had established presence, this one established authorship through sheer comprehensiveness. No consulting engineer’s brief visits could have generated such detail. The cumulative effect of the specifications, the exact dimensions of each course, the angles of the dovetail joints, the weight and provenance of every major timber, was to make the work inseparable from the man who had measured, tested, and recorded it all.

The preface made the claim explicit without making it contentious. The following pages, Stevenson wrote, contain a full account of the Bell Rock Lighthouse, from the period of its first projection to its final completion. Projection was a carefully chosen word. In engineering usage, it could mean either the initial conception or the forward planning of execution. Stevenson allowed both meanings to resonate. He acknowledged the assistance of Mr. John Rennie, who, in the capacity of consulting engineer, examined and approved of the original design. The formulation was technically accurate and strategically minimal. Rennie had examined. He had approved. The design remained, grammatically and substantively, Stevenson’s own.

The report circulated through engineering circles in Edinburgh, London, and the provincial societies that were beginning to form. It was reviewed in the scientific press, generally favorably, with the Bell Rock increasingly cited as a landmark of Scottish engineering. Rennie’s name appeared in these notices, when it appeared at all, as the consultant who had validated a younger man’s ambitious scheme. The reversal was not yet complete, but the momentum had shifted.

Rennie responded in the arena where his authority remained unchallenged. In 1818, he delivered a series of lectures at the Royal Institution in London on the history and principles of civil engineering. The Bell Rock figured prominently. He described it as one of the most arduous and important works of the kind ever executed, and he described it as his. The design, he explained, had been founded upon his study of Smeaton’s Eddystone, with modifications he had specified to meet the particular difficulties of the Scottish site. The execution he attributed to the resident engineer, a formulation that reduced Stevenson to a functionary carrying out instructions devised elsewhere. The lectures were published in 1819, reaching a wider audience than any of Stevenson’s Board reports. They established a competing narrative that could not be ignored, only countered.

The two accounts could not be reconciled. In Stevenson’s version, Rennie had approved a design already substantially formed. In Rennie’s, he had furnished the design that Stevenson merely executed. Both versions contained elements of truth, selected and arranged to support opposed conclusions. The private correspondence of the construction years, with its gradual shifts in understanding between the two men, had not been published. The Board’s minutes recorded decisions without recording the conversations that preceded them. Each man possessed documents that supported his case. Neither possessed documents that definitively refuted the other.

The stalemate hardened into structure. In Edinburgh, Stevenson continued to produce technical papers on the Bell Rock’s operation: the ventilation of the light room, the maintenance of the apparatus, the behavior of the granite in Atlantic storms. Each paper reinforced his identification with the work through ongoing, practical engagement. In London, Rennie incorporated the lighthouse into his broader claims for the rational, scientific principles of modern engineering, using it to illustrate the triumph of systematic design over natural obstacle. Their audiences overlapped only partially. The Board’s constituency was functional and local: keepers, shipmasters, coastal authorities. Rennie’s was national and theoretical: the emerging profession that would soon call itself civil engineering, and that needed founding fathers and exemplary monuments.

The question of credit thus became a question of historical genealogy. Whose engineering was the Bell Rock? The answer would determine not merely personal reputation, but the shape of a new discipline’s self-understanding. Rennie represented the tradition of Smeaton: the gentleman-engineer, the designer whose comprehensive plans were executed by others, the theorist whose mastery of principles transcended particular sites. Stevenson represented something more recent and less easily categorized: the institutional engineer, embedded in a bureaucratic structure, present at every stage of construction, accumulating authority through sustained administrative labor rather than charismatic design. The Bell Rock was the test case because it was unprecedented. No existing category could fully contain it. Both men were claiming to have invented what the other had merely assisted.

The competition took on material form in the honors each sought and received. In 1818, the year of Rennie’s Royal Institution lectures, Stevenson was elected to the Wernerian Natural History Society of Edinburgh, a recognition of the geological and meteorological observations he had accumulated during the construction years. He presented a paper on the tidal patterns of the Bell Rock, based on the watch logs he had preserved. The data was original. The presentation was deliberate. He was establishing a domain of expertise that Rennie could not enter, founded on facts that only long residence at the reef could have gathered.

Rennie’s response came through his institutional weight. He was consulted on lighthouses for the Irish coast, for the Channel Islands, for overseas stations of the expanding empire. In each case, he mentioned the Bell Rock as the foundation of his authority in this specialized field. The references accumulated. In the emerging reference works of engineering, the biographical dictionaries, the historical surveys, the technical encyclopedias that began to appear in the 1820s, Rennie’s claim had the advantage of prior establishment. His career was complete. Stevenson’s was still in progress. The dead or retired engineer could be summarized. The active one remained open to revision.

The public positioning of both men thus created a suspended judgment. Two incompatible narratives of creation circulated in parallel, each with its documentary support, each with its institutional base. The Board’s minutes and reports sustained Stevenson in the administrative world that had produced the work. Rennie’s publications and professional eminence sustained him in the wider sphere that would determine the work’s place in engineering history. Neither could defeat the other. Neither could afford to acknowledge the other on equal terms.

The cost of this stalemate fell unevenly. Stevenson, still serving the Board, could not openly dispute Rennie’s claims without appearing to disparage a consultant whom the institution had honored. His strategy of comprehensive documentation required patience, the accumulation of detail that would eventually outweigh Rennie’s broader assertions through sheer mass of specific evidence. Rennie, for his part, could not directly attack Stevenson’s reports without appearing to belittle a colleague who had carried through a difficult execution. His strategy of principled abstraction allowed him to claim the Bell Rock as an instance of his design philosophy without engaging Stevenson’s particulars.

Both men were trapped by the forms of professional civility that their conflict was helping to define. The emerging etiquette of engineering credit required acknowledgment of collaborators, but it provided no rule for determining priority among claims that could not be simultaneously true. The result was a silence where direct confrontation might have clarified the record. In public, each man praised the work. In print, each man claimed it. The contradiction was visible to anyone who read both accounts, but few did. The audiences remained separate, the narratives parallel, the resolution deferred.

The Bell Rock lighthouse, near the entrance to the Friths of Forth and Tay, was built during 1807 and 1810. In the wider engineering world, Rennie was, by some, credited with the design and execution, a view sustained by his publications and his preeminent national reputation. Yet among those who knew the work’s practical history, there seemed little doubt that he was only nominally responsible for the great undertaking. This divergence between practical knowledge and public record was itself a product of the stalemate. Stevenson had won the documentary battle within the administrative world that had produced the lighthouse. Rennie had won the narrative battle in the wider sphere that would determine its place in engineering history. The two victories could not be reconciled.

Stevenson understood, as he approached his sixtieth year, that administrative reports would not suffice. The documents he had gathered, the watch logs, the workyard accounts, the correspondence with contractors, his own journals from the seasons on the reef, constituted an archive that no one else possessed. To let that archive remain scattered in Board files and private papers was to leave the verdict to future compilers who would work from Rennie’s more accessible publications. The unresolved public stalemate, two competing narratives in circulation, created the necessity for Stevenson to engineer a final, definitive narrative to control history.
